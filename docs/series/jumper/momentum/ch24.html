Chapter 24 of 49

Cent POV: You're going to land in that?

The instructor display gave me the same forward view Iverson and Childs had inside the simulator. Truman had loaded the record of their real landing into the machine, every bad instrument and every second they had spent not knowing which part of the shuttle was lying to them. I knew the summary. Endeavour had come down in Los Angeles. Everyone had lived. The vehicle had not.

Knowing the last line turned out not to prepare me for any of the lines before it.

The guidance needles stayed centered while the terrain outside became a city. Iverson said the ground track was wrong. Houston took long enough to confirm it that I found myself leaning closer to the screen, as if I could identify Los Angeles faster from the top of the atmosphere.

Then Houston said they had two minutes of glide left.

I had crossed that much distance in less time than it took to think about crossing it. Endeavour could not. It had no engines left, no place to stop, and an entire city underneath it full of people who had nothing to do with what was happening overhead.

Childs found the river.

The visual system rolled with Iverson's turn, and the flood-control channel came into view. It was concrete, narrow, and crossed by bridges that looked much too close together even before the simulator put a shuttle between them.

"You're going to land in that?"

"Watch," Truman said.

I did.

Childs built the approach while Iverson flew it. Their voices got shorter as the altitude came off. The first bridge filled the screen. The main gear struck hard enough that I felt the simulator through the floor under my shoes. Endeavour stayed upright, moving between walls with both wings reaching over them.

Then another bridge appeared.

The chute could not open without catching it. The gear could not stay down without hitting it. Iverson ordered it retracted after touchdown, and the wheels folded while the orbiter was still rolling.

I caught the edge of the console with both hands.

Endeavour dropped onto its belly and went under the bridge in a storm of simulated metal and concrete. I had watched orbital machinery tear itself apart. I had stood alone on Mars. Neither had looked as wrong as a spacecraft pulling its landing gear up on the ground because that was the only way the people inside it might live.

"They did this?"

"Every part you're seeing," Truman said.

The controls stopped mattering. Endeavour slid. Another bridge passed over it. Then a scaffold and the worker standing on it appeared in the forward view.

The orbiter stopped directly underneath him.

For a few seconds nobody spoke. Iverson finally called for status. Childs and Timmins answered. The simulator marked the vehicle destroyed beyond repair and all three crew alive.

I looked from the frozen scaffold to the cockpit hatch.

"Okay," I said. "I thought I knew what that landing meant."

"I was so wrong."
