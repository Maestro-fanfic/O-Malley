Chapter 20 of 49

Cent POV: That's more mass than I can push. At all.

Mars won by the plainest argument anybody made in the whole planning process.

There'd been other candidates on the table before that meeting. The Moon came up first and died fast: too close, Sterling pointed out, too easy to reach the ordinary way, not enough of a proof to justify the resources, a stunt rather than a demonstration. Venus came up once, briefly, and everyone in the room looked at the surface temperature and pressure figures and moved on without much discussion. Someone floated a longer-duration target further out just to test the room's appetite for ambition, and Petrov reminded everyone that significantly higher cruise velocity was a separate problem nobody had solved. Solving two hard problems at once was how programs failed instead of succeeded.

"It's closest," Sterling said, at the meeting where it actually got decided, which took about eleven minutes once everyone stopped dancing around the obvious. "Everything else is further, colder, harder to characterize, or all three. We're not picking Mars because it's romantic. We're picking it because it's the nearest real proving ground for a method that needs proving, and you've already stood there once, alone, which means we already know the destination's survivable. We just need to prove we can bring more than one person, and bring them back."

"Works for me," I said, and meant it. I'd stood on the Melas Chasma dunes with a sample bag and come home in time for dinner. There wasn't anything symbolic left in Mars for me to feel about. It was just the practical next step, same as everything else had been since the tank.

"You don't sound like a woman being offered the chance to lead the first crewed Mars mission in human history," Truman observed, dry, watching me across the table.

"I already led the first Mars visit in human history. It just wasn't crewed, and nobody wrote a headline about it because I didn't tell anybody until months later. This is the part where it actually matters to more people than me. I'm glad to do it. I'm just not going to pretend it feels like a bigger deal internally than the tank did, because it doesn't. It's the next practical step. I've gotten good at taking those."

The ship itself nearly went wrong before it went right, and it went wrong in a way that could've cost months if I hadn't caught it in one sentence.

Cory's team, working off Kristen Station's own precedent, defaulted to a hull design shielded the way the station was shielded: a continuous outer shell of water around the crew compartment, thick enough to handle the radiation dose of a long transit the old-fashioned way, mass be damned. It worked for a station assembled in orbit and meant to stay there. It did not work for a carrier I had to accelerate.

I looked at the mass figure in the design review and said the only thing there was to say about it.

"That's more mass than I can push. At all."

The room went quiet the way rooms did whenever I said something flat like that and meant it literally instead of dramatically.

"Define 'can't,'" Osei said. "Slower, or actually impossible?"

"Actually impossible. Every kick's a short contact, a fraction of a second, and the force it takes to move something scales with the mass for that fixed contact time. Past a certain number, the force needed exceeds what either the payload or I can survive per kick, no matter how many kicks I string together. It's not a patience problem. There's no number of tries that gets you there. It's a wall, not a hill."

"So we need less mass."

"You need a lot less mass, and I don't have a number for you yet, just the fact that this number is on the wrong side of the wall."

It took two weeks of actual engineering to find the other side of the problem, and the answer, once somebody said it out loud, was almost embarrassingly simple.

"Radiation dose is a function of time in transit," the mission's radiation specialist said, a quiet man named Petrov who'd spent his whole career worrying about exactly this problem for exactly the opposite reason: too little propulsion, not too much. "Your method shortens the transit time from months to days. The total dose drops with it, proportionally. You don't need to shield against a six-month trip anymore. You need to shield against an eight-day one. That's a completely different, and much smaller, engineering problem."

"So a thinner hull."

"A thinner hull, plus something concentrated and small for the actual danger: a solar particle event, a real spike, rare but survivable if you've got somewhere to hide from it for a few hours. A storm shelter, not a full jacket. Built from consumables you're already carrying anyway: water, food stores, waste tankage, arranged around one compartment instead of the whole hull."

"That I can move," I said, once they ran the new numbers. "That's on the right side of the wall."

The architecture split there, because the carrier still couldn't climb out of a gravity well just because we'd made its radiation shell thinner. Heavy-lift rockets would place its three hull sections, supplies, and the compact landing capsule in orbit. We would assemble and tether the carrier at Kristen. The carrier would remain in orbit at Mars. Only the much lighter capsule, carrying crew, minimal life support, science load, and a dedicated compact version of my two-axis brace fixture, would descend and climb again. The capsule used the same interchangeable battery cassettes as the carrier, sized for descent, surface contingency, ascent, and reserve, then recharged after docking. Surface cargo carried its own isolated banks built around the same cassette standard. Cargo went in separate packages, each one under the mass limit I had already proven.

The carrier's hold was not the upper limit on the base. It only set what accompanied the first crew. Rockets could keep placing standardized habitat sections, power equipment, consumables, and tools at Kristen after we left. Once I knew the carrier and the Mars site as destinations, I could return to Earth orbit and ferry those packages one at a time. Earth still had to lift every kilogram out of its gravity well, and each package still had to stay under my proven limit, but the carrier did not have to contain the final settlement before departure. Nobody hid an impossible ascent inside a sentence about landing.

The carrier chair needed two rotational axes. Its heavy primary bearing could carry me through the full fore-and-aft reversal; an orthogonal trim trunnion supplied the smaller out-of-plane corrections needed as gravity bent the trajectory, the center of mass shifted, or navigation refined the vector. Together they gave guidance pitch-and-yaw control of the thrust vector. Both axes locked solid before a kick. Osei called the whole assembly a gimbaled bearing and designed the load spine for the lateral component instead of asking the attitude-control system to turn the entire ship for every correction.

The smaller fixture became a separate development program immediately. Mars had an atmosphere, thin or not, and a crosswind or bad density forecast could move the required vector faster and farther than the carrier's limited trim axis was built to follow. Osei specified a compact two-axis gimbal with wider travel and faster slew in pitch and yaw, still locked solid before the next kick. NASA authorized a full-size capsule simulator on a six-axis motion base before anyone cut metal for the flight unit.

The transit profile came together after that, worked out in a room with more whiteboards than I'd ever seen in one place, until it stopped being an abstraction and became an actual, specific plan. Kick-coast-kick: a burst of discrete changes on a nominal three-second cadence, each interval nudged by the control model far enough to stay clear of the carrier's live structural modes, building speed for a few hours at departure; then eight days of pure freefall, no pushing at all, just coasting the roughly hundred and eighty million kilometers Mars and Earth happened to have between them at the point we'd be launching; then a mirrored burst at the far end. The carrier would not flip. The primary axis of my chair would rotate through one hundred eighty degrees while the trim axis followed the smaller out-of-plane correction; both would lock and let me bleed velocity along the commanded reverse vector while Iverson and mission control watched the full solution.

"Cruise velocity works out to right around two hundred sixty kilometers a second," Petrov said, running the final numbers. "Comfortably under a tenth of a percent of light speed. Boring, by the standards of what you're actually doing, which I mean as the highest compliment I know how to give an engineering result."

"And for most of the eight days," I said, working through the actual shape of it, "I'm not doing anything. I'm just there. The boosts are hours long at each end. The middle's just coasting."

"That's correct."

I mulled that over, because it meant something I hadn't quite expected: that the middle of this entire mission, the part where I was supposedly the engine making the whole thing possible, was going to be the part where I did the least. Passenger, most of the way there. Driver, only at both ends.

"That's fine," I said, once I'd turned it over enough to actually believe it. "I've spent my whole life being the one who does something. Might be good for me to just be there, for a change, and let the ship do the being-there part on its own for a while."
