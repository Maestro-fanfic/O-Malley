Chapter 25 of 49

Childs POV: The engine says we finished the burn. The stars say we didn't.

Flight medicine cleared us the next morning. Iverson still moved carefully through his shoulders, and I rolled stiffness out of my neck before I put on the headset. Neither of us carried yesterday's shock into the checklist. Sterling watched the second run from simulation control beside Truman and Cent. It began in orbit, twenty minutes before de-orbit ignition.

I took the left seat. Iverson took the right. Timmins configured the orbital maneuvering system behind us. Iverson loaded the burn solution and checked it against the inertial platform while I held attitude and the simulated Earth turned below us.

"De-orbit burn in ten," Houston said.

Both engines ignited on time. Chamber pressure read nominal. Propellant flow agreed. The burn timer counted down without complaint.

The inertial velocity trace did not.

At first the difference was small enough to be sensor noise. Then it kept growing.

"Left engine acceleration is low," Iverson said.

Timmins checked the engine page. "Chamber pressure says matched thrust."

"Inertial says we're short," Iverson said.

"Which inertial?" I asked.

"All three."

The burn timer reached zero. Both engine controllers reported a complete maneuver and shut down. The stars outside settled into the expected attitude. The velocity numbers did not settle with them.

"The engine says we finished the burn," Iverson said. "The stars say we didn't."

Timmins pulled the raw data. A chamber-pressure transducer on the left engine had frozen at its nominal value while the thrust itself decayed. The right engine had delivered its full impulse. The left had not.

"Missing delta-v is twenty-three point eight meters per second," Iverson said. "Current perigee stays above entry interface. If we wait for ground confirmation, we miss the correction window."

I did not look at the green engine summary again. "Can we finish on the right?"

"Yes," Iverson said. "Eight-point-six-second single-engine burn, same attitude. It shifts the footprint west."

"Load it."

Timmins isolated the left engine. Iverson gave me the corrected cutoff. I fired the right engine and stopped it on his mark.

This time the inertial platform, the star reference, and the engine data agreed.

The correction brought us into the atmosphere, but not toward the runway the first plan had promised. By the time Houston finished rebuilding the footprint, our primary field was almost two hundred miles beyond the vehicle's remaining crossrange.

"White Sands is inside the western edge," Houston said. "Dry lake surface, sufficient dimensions. Winds light."

"Energy?" I asked.

"High, but manageable," Iverson said. "We start drag early and stay at the top of the corridor."

We did. The entry loads built, held, and came off. Nothing else failed. I put Endeavour onto the gypsum lakebed with miles of empty surface ahead of us.

The landing was ordinary.

The point of the run was that they reached an atmosphere containing a landing site at all.

Three independent inertial units had told the truth. Iverson listened to them. I acted before the correction window closed. Sitting to my right did not make the decision his or mine alone; it changed which one of us found the answer and which one had to trust it quickly enough to fly.

Truman kept us through the technical debrief, then sent us home. This time Iverson stood as soon as the motion base released us. My hands were steady when I passed my notes across the table. The next run went on the schedule forty-eight hours later.
