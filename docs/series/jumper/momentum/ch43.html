Chapter 43 of 49

Cent POV: We're already far closer to Europa. Let's finish the trip.

We spent the first two weeks building, which surprised nobody who'd watched the continuous inner ring of Kristen Station turn from an empty shell into a lived-in place one room at a time.

The interplanetary carrier remained parked in Mars orbit. The surface base came from separate cargo packages, each light enough for me to manage alone. During the coast I had given those packages slightly less velocity than the carrier, not more, so they arrived behind us instead of becoming unguided projectiles ahead. Once the crew was safely down, I jumped back to each package, killed its remaining velocity, and lowered it to the landing site one careful increment at a time.

Those packages made the starter base, not the final inventory. When assembly used more sealant than the estimate and Ochoa wanted a second field shelter, NASA did not have to fit either one into a ship already at Mars. Rockets lifted replacement stock and the packed shelter to Kristen. I went back, brought each load to Mars separately, and lowered it after the surface crew cleared the landing area. Base construction was limited by launch schedules, package mass, and how much work people on Mars could actually finish. It was not limited by what Momentum had carried on departure day.

Joe made the first delivery I did not bring myself.

We had pressure in the starter habitat by then. I was inside sorting electrical couplers when he appeared on one knee in a pressure suit, two packs clipped tight against his chest and thighs: sealant cartridges, cable fittings, and the drill heads Iverson had worn out faster than the estimate. Nothing he carried extended much beyond the shape of his body.

I caught his arm before Mars gravity finished introducing itself.

"You did that on purpose."

"First time I tried to." His voice came through the suit radio too fast. He looked past me at the curved wall, the red light through the temporary viewport, and then back at my face. "Dad talked me through it from Kristen. I thought supplies made a better excuse than showing up empty-handed."

"You don't need an excuse."

"I know." He smiled then, small and nervous. "Supplies still seemed useful."

He stayed long enough to help Iverson inventory what he had brought and to stand at the viewport with his hand against the glass. I took him home myself. One deliberate jump to Mars was enough for his first day.

The lightweight capsule became our ascent vehicle, not a building block. Iverson and Childs ran surface assembly with the same unhurried competence they'd flown the carrier with. Ochoa disappeared for hours at a time and came back each night with samples that made her forget to eat until somebody reminded her. Reyes kept everyone's bodies honest about what Mars gravity actually asked of them after months of none at all. Truman worked wherever someone handed him a tool and discovered that on Mars nobody cared how many people he'd once commanded if he was holding the wrong end of a power coupling. Grebenchekov worked steady, unhurried, a man entirely at peace with exactly how much life he had left in him and determined to spend it building something that would outlast this trip.

It was on the ninth day, elbow-deep in a habitat section's power coupling beside Grebenchekov, that I said the thing I'd been turning over since before we ever left Earth.

"I want to talk to NASA about something."

"About Mars?"

"About after Mars." I sat back on my heels, looking out past our half-built structure at a sky that, for the first time in my life, had two moons in it instead of one. "Sterling. Truman. Everyone. I want to talk about not going home first."

The conversation happened two days later, patched through to a room I couldn't see but could picture perfectly by now: Sterling, Petrov, and Osei crowded around whatever screen carried my voice across the growing distance. Truman sat beside me at the habitat table, part crew now instead of the man at the far end of the call.

"Say it plain," Sterling said. "You always do. Don't stop now."

"Mars sits about one and a half astronomical units from the sun. Earth's one. Jupiter's out at five point two. That means right now, standing here, we're already about half an astronomical unit closer to Europa than Earth ever is. And this ship doesn't need a launch window the way a chemical rocket would: we make our own thrust, whenever we're ready to make it. There's no reason to burn eight days coming home first just to turn around and burn more than that again going somewhere we're already most of the way toward."

The silence on the line ran long enough that I wondered if the delay had eaten the whole exchange.

"You're proposing we extend the mission," Truman said finally, "on the spot, off the plan, because the math happens to work out in your favor."

"I'm proposing we finish the trip we already started." I thought about a phone call months back, a general's voice cracking with something that wasn't quite composure, a line that had become the whole reason any of this existed. "You asked me once what I actually wanted all this for, and I told you Europa, before Mars was even the practical answer. Mars was always the stepping stone. We're standing on it. We're already far closer to Europa. Let's finish the trip."

Another silence, shorter this time, and when Sterling spoke again I heard the warmth that had built between us over two years and one very long flight.

"Give us the numbers. Real transit time, real consumables math, real risk assessment. If it holds up the way you say it does, I am not going to be the one who tells you no."

"It'll hold up."

"Then get me the numbers," he said, "and let's find out."

It took four days to run the first pass properly: Petrov and Osei working the transit math from Earth, Ochoa and Reyes running science load, consumables, and risk from Mars, Iverson and Childs auditing carrier maintenance, everyone converging the way NASA's people always seemed to converge once a real problem showed up in front of them.

That first pass exposed a twenty-seven-day transit at our validated Mars speed.

"At two hundred sixty kilometers a second, the current alignment puts you just over four point one astronomical units and roughly twenty-seven days from Jupiter," Petrov said. "That is more than three weeks longer than the Mars transit. We can accept it, or test whether the method safely supports a higher cruise velocity. We do not assume."

The G-limit per kick had not changed. What had changed was how precisely I could place each kick and how quickly I could correct my own drift between them. If I tightened the nominal spacing without making any single change larger, while still letting the structural scheduler vary the individual intervals, I could build velocity faster without approaching the wall.

Petrov wanted numbers instead of confidence. The test program took twelve days because every sortie had to prove acceleration, coast, full deceleration, and return.

First sortie: nominal two-and-three-quarter-second spacing, outbound and back. Inspection at Mars orbit.

Second: two-and-a-half, farther out, longer turnaround, full medical review.

Third: two-and-a-quarter. A chair-gimbal sensor drifted outside Osei's tolerance during deceleration and lit the amber warning exactly as designed. I held the next kick. The reading never crossed the abort threshold, so the alarm stayed quiet. We replaced the sensor, repeated the leg, and earned the number instead of averaging the warning away.

Only on the fourth sortie did we test nominal two-second spacing end to end. Reyes monitored every change; Sterling's consoles tracked the departure distance and return solution; Iverson and Childs verified every swivel angle. The scheduler stretched or shortened each actual interval around the live hull modes. The payload numbers stayed clean. My biometrics remained unremarkable in the specific, boring way that meant everything was working.

"Two-second nominal holds," I reported when the data was in.

Osei ran the numbers overnight. "Four hundred ten kilometers a second, sustainable, inside every margin we've validated. That cuts the transit from twenty-seven days to just over seventeen."

"This is a harder problem than Mars was," Petrov said. "It required real testing and asks more of a method you have already proved. It is still a solved problem, not an excuse to pretend the next scale will solve itself."

Nothing went to the President until the last test brought us safely back to Mars orbit.

The conclusion was not "go." It was a mission-control package: transit, radiation, landing-capsule margins, crew medical gates, resupply, abort branches, and the explicit fact that the Mars cargo had consumed almost everything packed for surface construction. Before the proposal went anywhere near the President, Sterling made every console lead sign the part belonging to them.

I solved the supply problem by going home.

NASA had already staged Europa science gear and fresh consumables in a secured hangar against the possibility that the idea survived review. I made four round trips from Mars orbit, bringing water, food, medical stock, replacement suit consumables, a pressure-rated sampling system, and the specialized sensors Ochoa wanted for the ice shell. I could not lift those pallets from Earth's surface. Rockets had delivered them to Kristen first. From the station, each pallet was a known place and a manageable load.

The original installed power capacity did not close with the same failed-bank margin once Europa's instrument heaters, sampling system, and longer contingency time were included. We had not carried future battery hardware all the way to Mars in case we someday wanted it. NASA staged two additional cassette frames, isolation contactors, handling gear, and charged cassettes at Kristen. I brought each piece to Momentum in Mars orbit separately, one load at a time.

Then I went back for Osei. She came aboard in a pressure suit with the installation kit clipped to her harness, installed the new frames and contactors herself, and led the crew through connecting the additional banks. We isolated one bus, expanded the installed capacity without taking the ship dark, failed each new contactor on purpose, and exchanged cassettes under full life-support load. The extra banks gave us peak capacity and a longer reserve. They did not create electricity. During the Europa coast I would keep returning depleted cassettes to Kristen and bringing charged ones back, more often than I had on the Mars run. I took Osei home after she signed the installation into service.

Only after the carrier was restocked and mission control agreed the plan closed did the proposal go to the White House.

The President took the delayed call from the Oval with Halloran and Baines beside him. Sterling delivered the risk case. Petrov delivered the transit. Reyes delivered the medical objection and then, when asked, the medical conditions under which she would withdraw it.

"You're telling me," the President said, "that the crew is already closer, the carrier is resupplied, mission control supports it, and the only thing standing between the United States and the first expedition to Europa is my appetite for signing the paper."

"That's an oversimplification," Sterling said.

"General, I have people for whom oversimplification is a profession. Is it wrong?"

"No, sir."

The President grinned in a way I'd never seen in the Situation Room. "Then my appetite is excellent. Approved, subject to every abort gate in the package. Go finish the trip."

I gathered everyone at the half-finished structure on the thirtieth night, six faces lit by work lamps against a darkening Martian sky, and asked the only question that actually mattered.

"Hey," I said. "Who wants to come to Europa with me?"

Grebenchekov answered first, without hesitating, the way he'd answered everything since the day he volunteered before anyone finished asking him.

"I told you already. I know what my limits are, and this is not one of them."

Iverson and Childs looked at each other for exactly as long as it took to confirm they were already thinking the same thing, and didn't bother answering in words at all, just turned back to the ship like the question had already been settled the moment I asked it.

Ochoa was still holding a sample bag from that afternoon's work, and she looked at it, and then at the sky, and then at me, hungry the way a scientist gets when she's just been offered a second impossible thing in the same year as the first.

"Try and stop me."

Reyes was quiet a moment longer than the rest, thinking it through the careful way she thought through everything, and then nodded once, plain, complete.

"Somebody has to keep all of you alive out there too."

Truman waited until everyone else had answered.

"I did not come all this way to become the man who stays on Mars because the itinerary changed," he said. "Mission command has a better objection than I do, and Sterling just signed the package."

I looked around at all of them, at the structure half-built behind us, at the two moons rising together over a horizon none of us had ever expected to call ordinary, and felt something settle into place that had been building since a tank broke loose near a solar array a lifetime ago: not an ending, not yet, just the next true thing.

I called home that night, before we left, because some things still belonged to the people who'd been in the room for every other version of this conversation.

"Europa," Mom said, once I'd explained it, and I heard her weighing the word the way she weighed everything that mattered. "That's further than anyone's ever been."

"It is."

"Are you scared?"

I considered the question honestly instead of reaching for the easy version. "A little. Mostly I'm ready. There's a difference."

"I know there is," Mom said. "Your father's standing right here trying not to look terrified. Joe already knows, doesn't he."

"He does. He said mission control would stay with us before I finished asking, same as everyone up here."

Dad's voice came through then, close to the receiver, rougher than I was used to hearing it. "Go finish the trip. I've been learning how to be scared for you and let you go since you were born. I'm not going to stop practicing now, when you're doing something this good."

"I love you. Both of you."

"We know," Mom said. "Go build your structure. Call again before you boost."

"Okay," I said. "Then let's finish it."

We loaded the surface base with enough charged cassettes to carry its sealed standby load for months, then left it powered and sealed, climbed in the lightweight capsule, and met the carrier in orbit. Keeping an empty pressure shell warm and monitored took a fraction of the power a working crew had used. The ascent capsule was near my proven mass limit but on the right side of it; the carrier never entered the gravity well at all. Once Iverson had it docked and every Mars sample was secured, I moved back into the main fixture.

Four days later Truman asked for a go/no-go for boost, and all seven of us answered again before either gimbal axis moved or the scheduler released a single kick. Then we boosted for Europa, kick by kick, the carrier falling away from Mars the same patient way it had once fallen away from Earth, seven of us aboard, already farther from home than anyone in history, already closer to where we were actually going than we'd ever been to anywhere else.
