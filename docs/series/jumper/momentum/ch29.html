Chapter 29 of 49

Iverson POV: Second field. Cleaner approach.

The sixth run came after four clear days. Childs and I arrived without stiffness, slept normally the night before, and passed flight medicine with resting numbers back at baseline. I took the right seat for the final evaluation. Childs took the left.

Before the hatch closed, Truman came onto the loop. "This one is the hardest."

"Hit me," Childs said.

I came close to grinning. Truman laughed outright over the loop.

The de-orbit and navigation systems worked. Houston gave us an active commercial airport with a twelve-thousand-foot runway, emergency equipment standing by, and every airliner in the pattern ordered clear. It was the first destination in six runs that looked generous.

Childs built a stable final approach. I verified the runway, winds, and energy. At eighteen hundred feet we were aligned, on profile, and seconds from the gear sequence. The commercial runway filled the windshield.

Then the tower broke in over Houston.

"Endeavour, runway closed. Disabled widebody entering the touchdown zone. Aircraft occupied."

The visual system put it in front of us before the transmission ended: a widebody slewing across the centerline after a rejected takeoff, one main gear collapsed, fire vehicles chasing it, the fuselage still moving into the exact pavement we were about to use.

"Abort this approach," Childs said.

She closed the speed brake and rolled left hard enough to drive us into the harnesses. Timmins's hand was already beside the gear control.

"Gear stays up," she said.

There was no powered go-around. The runway and the occupied airliner slid beneath the right wing while I rebuilt the footprint with our altitude unwinding faster than the display could make it look reasonable.

The other commercial runways required turns we no longer had the energy to complete. A private airfield just beyond the eastern perimeter remained barely inside the boundary. Its centerline lay across our present heading, not ahead of it.

"Second field," I said. "One point four miles east. Immediate heading zero-eight-two. Six thousand one hundred feet by seventy-five. Tower close left at midfield, hangars right, graded dirt beyond the departure end. Not enough runway."

"Still the best ground we've got," Childs said. "Build it."

I put the new line on her display while she was already turning toward it. The footprint boundary closed across the private runway and stopped with the threshold barely on the survivable side. We had enough energy to reach it. We did not have enough to miss the alignment and try again.

The commercial control tower swept past the left wing with ninety feet of lateral clearance. Then the private field appeared late and narrow. We had to approach offset right to clear its smaller tower, then correct left before the hangars became the next problem.

"Hold right. Tower at nine o'clock. Thirty feet wing clearance."

"Visual."

"Clear tower. Correct left now."

Childs crossed the threshold right of center, rolled level, and moved the gear toward the pavement. I called the lateral correction down to feet. She straightened before the mains touched.

"Chute."

Timmins waited until the tail cleared the tower, then deployed it. The canopy opened clean and pulled us straight. Endeavour left the pavement at forty-three knots, crossed the graded overrun, and stopped in packed dirt with the nose gear collapsed.

Nobody aboard was injured.

Childs never tried to save the approach because it had been good a moment earlier. The runway stopped being an answer, and she abandoned it before habit could spend our remaining energy. I rebuilt the reachable geometry with the footprint shrinking under my hands. Childs flew what I gave her. Timmins made the configuration changes that turned our decisions into a survivable vehicle.

After the sixth landing, Truman left the instructor station and came down to the cockpit.

"All right," he said. "You weren't lucky."

"We were lucky there was a reachable piece of concrete," I said. "The rest was work."

Truman nodded once. "That's the answer I wanted." He looked at Timmins first. "I asked for the complete crew because their decisions only mattered because you executed them. This was a crew result. I have two flight billets to fill, and they are pilot billets."

"Understood," Timmins said. There was no resentment in it. He had known what the meeting was when he agreed to come.

Truman looked from Iverson to Childs. "I'm assembling a crew for Mars. The propulsion is real. The procedures are not finished. The vehicle will do things no flight manual has ever covered, and when the instruments disagree with reality I need people who will identify which one is wrong before they act. I would like both of you."

I looked across at Childs. We had not discussed the offer because there had not been an offer yet. We didn't need long.

Childs nodded.

"We accept," I said.

From the instructor station, Cent said, "After watching all the ways you found to make that go wrong, Mars sounds like a field day."

Truman looked back at her. "We haven't started making your part go wrong yet."
