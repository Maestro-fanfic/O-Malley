Chapter 26 of 49

Davy POV: Length is sufficient only if we use the whole finished section.

Cent came home after the Los Angeles replay and tried to describe watching a space shuttle land in a concrete river, pull its wheels back up, and slide under a bridge. She got as far as the scaffold before I told her I wanted to see the next review for myself.

"Good," she said. "I don't want to be the only one in the family who knows what these people can do."

Truman authorized both of us as mission participants, with the explicit rule that observation did not make either of us part of the simulation chain. Two days later, Cent and I stood in simulation control with Truman and Sterling while the third run gave the crew good flight controls and a clean de-orbit burn. Iverson and Childs settled their harnesses before the motion base moved, bodies already anticipating the loads instead of discovering them. The simulator waited until they were below twenty thousand feet to start lying.

The two radar altimeters separated first. One froze at eighteen thousand feet. The other began counting down twice as fast as our inertial descent rate allowed. A few seconds later the air-data system added forty knots to indicated speed.

"Three bad sources," Timmins said. "No common power bus."

"Failure package, not cascade," Iverson said. "What do we still trust?"

"Inertial groundspeed. Barometric trend, not absolute altitude. Visual horizon." Childs checked the footprint against the terrain database. "Ground tracking when Houston has us."

They were over open desert with no NASA runway inside reach. Houston found one continuous prepared surface: an unopened highway bypass, eight lanes poured across a long section of level ground and not yet divided by barriers or light poles. It was close to what Houston had wanted to find in Los Angeles before traffic made the freeways useless.

"Length is sufficient only if we use the whole finished section," Childs said. "Width is marginal. The overpass and both sign gantries are before the usable touchdown zone."

"Wind?" Iverson asked.

"Quartering from the right. Visual estimate only. Air data is still bad."

"Build it."

Childs used inertial groundspeed, visual drift, and the last reliable wind report. The wrong airspeed stayed on the display, forty knots fast and internally consistent. She put the inertial value beside it and called the one we were actually flying.

"Three hundred feet above road grade. Groundspeed two-eighty. Overpass in six seconds."

Iverson held the orbiter above the pavement and cleared the overpass.

"First gantry."

Steel passed under the nose.

"Second gantry. Touchdown zone begins immediately beyond it. Gear down on my mark."

The second sign structure disappeared beneath them.

"Gear down."

Timmins moved the handle. The mains locked just before Iverson put them onto the first clear pavement.

"Chute."

The canopy opened as soon as the tail cleared the gantry. The highway was wide enough for the gear and nothing else. Both wings extended over unfinished shoulders. Iverson kept the centerline under them while the false airspeed insisted they had more margin than the view outside allowed.

"One thousand feet remaining," Childs said.

Endeavour stopped with nine hundred twelve.

I felt the last deceleration through the control-room floor and watched all three of them remain in their seats until the simulator declared the run complete. The motion base had worked harder than it had during the lakebed landing, and so had they, but Iverson's first status call came without the breath he had needed after Los Angeles. Childs was already marking the false indications for review when the hatch opened.

✳

Cent and I sat against the back wall during the review. Truman walked the crew backward through every indication they had rejected and every one they had retained.

"You had three bad sources," he said to Childs. "Why trust inertial groundspeed?"

"Because two independent inertial units agreed with ground tracking and the visual rate over the road," she said. "The air-data number disagreed with all three. I didn't pick the instrument I liked. I picked the explanation that accounted for the evidence."

Cent leaned forward. "And if the view outside had agreed with the airspeed?"

"Then inertial becomes the suspect, and we rebuild from there."

Iverson reviewed the delayed gear extension next. Putting it down earlier would have created enough drag to put them into the overpass. Waiting another second risked an incomplete lock at touchdown. He gave the timing without defending it as if the question were an accusation.

By the end I understood why Cent had wanted me there. The landing was the visible part. The review was where NASA made them prove they could do it again for the right reasons.

Truman put a full recovery day between that run and the farmland evaluation.
