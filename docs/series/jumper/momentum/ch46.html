Chapter 46 of 49

Cent POV: Surface cold like this doesn't feel as if it has a bottom.

The carrier stayed in orbit at Europa for the same reason it had stayed in orbit at Mars: I could not lower that much mass into a gravity well and promise to lift it back out. We transferred into the lightweight capsule with the sample system and surface instruments. The descent ran the same patient, boring way the Mars landing had, kick after kick killing the capsule's velocity against a surface that offered no atmosphere at all to argue with us on the way down, no parachute sequence, no heat shield, just the same method that had worked everywhere else we'd asked it to work.

Nothing about Europa's surface prepared me for how the cold actually felt once we were down and suited up outside the ship, even through insulation Cory's own team had over-engineered specifically for this exact moment.

"Negative one hundred sixty Celsius in this region," Reyes read off, checking suit integrity for the third time before letting anyone stray more than a few meters from the capsule. "Surface cold like this doesn't feel as if it has a bottom. The ice eventually gets warmer with depth because of tidal heating, but up here the environment just keeps pulling heat out of anything we expose."

The landscape itself was unlike anything any of us had stood on before, even after Mars: smooth ice plains broken by long, dark fractures called lineae, some running for what looked like hundreds of kilometers across the horizon, ridges and cliffs of ice thrown up at strange, patient angles by forces that had been reshaping this surface for longer than human beings had existed at all. No wind to speak of. No sound beyond our own suits and our own breathing, a silence so complete it felt less like an absence of noise and more like a physical, tangible thing pressing in from every direction.

"It's beautiful," Ochoa said, quiet, already moving toward the nearest fracture with the careful, reverent haste of a scientist trying not to look like she was rushing. "Genuinely, unexpectedly beautiful. I wasn't ready for that."

"None of the photographs prepare you," Grebenchekov agreed, looking out across the ice with an expression I hadn't seen on him before, something almost young beneath the careful, worn composure he usually wore. "I have looked at pictures of this place for thirty years. They are nothing like standing here."

Ochoa's work came first, the same careful, patient survey she'd run on Mars, cataloging the fracture patterns, taking readings that would tell NASA's scientists more about Europa's ice shell than fifty years of orbital imaging ever had, while Iverson and Childs ran the capsule through its own post-landing checks and Reyes monitored all of us for the particular, insidious ways extreme cold could compromise a suit's systems without announcing itself dramatically first.

It took two days of careful survey work before Ochoa found what she was actually looking for: not a fantasy-thin ice shell, but a young fracture zone with a deep, water-filled conduit that our sounding gear could follow instead of merely infer. The premission models had allowed anything from roughly fifteen to thirty kilometers of ice in most regions. This fracture did not make the ocean shallow. It gave us a path to look down.

"The conduit narrows, widens, and turns," she told me, standing beside the pressure lock Childs and Iverson had anchored over a shallow bore into the sealed fracture. "The descent probe can navigate it, but it cannot bring a sample back. It is a camera and a pressure package on fiber, not a pipe."

The probe took eleven hours to descend. Its armored fiber paid out through bends no drill string could have survived, carrying back darkness, ice, mineral streaks, and pressure readings that climbed steadily. Then the camera cleared the last constriction and the image opened into black water full of slow white particles. The lights reached no wall.

Ochoa looked from the live feed to me. "That is not a model. You're seeing the actual water now."

"Then I can reach it," I said.

"Let's prove that carefully," Ochoa said. "One honest test at a time."
