Chapter 34 of 49

Davy POV: Not a hostile!

Sterling noticed it first, and he noticed it the most boring way possible, which is exactly how these things always start: a login that just didn't work.

Sterling gave me the sequence after lockdown. His own credentials, tested twice, had been denied both times: no useful error message, just a system politely telling the general running mission control he wasn't who he said he was. He called it in as an IT problem, because that's what it looked like, and the first tech who picked up the ticket promised a fix inside the hour.

What made him actually stop and think about it, rather than just filing it under ordinary institutional friction, was the timing: it happened on day two of the transit, with the carrier roughly forty-five million kilometers out and every ground event reaching Cent minutes after it happened. She could jump home. She had proved that by bringing Nasser to surgery. What she could not do was respond to an alarm she would not receive until the people who triggered it had already had time to finish. Sterling had spent thirty years around military and aerospace bureaucracy, and he knew the difference between coincidence and a calendar somebody else had planned around.

The hour came and went. So did three more. And what actually told Sterling this wasn't a glitch wasn't the lockout itself; systems fail all the time; it was the way IT started acting once he pushed on it a second time. Evasive. Slow-walking straightforward questions. A supervisor who wouldn't come to the phone. Sterling had spent thirty years inside enough bureaucracy to know the difference between incompetence and somebody being told, quietly, from somewhere above them, to stall.

He called me before he called anyone else, because he'd met me exactly twice by then and had, for reasons I still don't fully understand, decided I was the right person to call when something started smelling like exactly the kind of thing I used to run from.

"Somebody's pulling my access," he said, flat, controlled, the same voice I imagined he used on people who needed to hear bad news without extra weight added to it. "Not the ship. Nobody's touched the ship. Somebody's trying to pull the standing of the person running mission control, quietly, through channels that are supposed to be beneath politics."

"The Ghost," I said.

"I don't know who it is. I know what it looks like, and it looks exactly like somebody deciding this program's gotten too big, too capable, and too far outside anyone's ability to control it from a comfortable distance, and deciding the fix is to own the access instead of trusting the people already holding it."

I called Hunt inside a minute of hanging up with Sterling.

"You're still in Virginia?"

"Yes."

"Arlington garage. The level we used last time. I'll come get you."

"Give me ten minutes."

He made it in seven, carrying a messenger bag with maintenance coveralls folded inside. I took his arm and jumped us into a locked service room off the mission control hallway, one Sterling had shown me during an earlier visit. Hunt changed, checked a contractor badge against the access list Sterling had sent him, and went out to look while I stayed put. Two strange maintenance men together would have been less useful than one.

Nine minutes later he texted me, and I joined him outside the control room's outer door. He was scanning the hallway the way he always did before he said a word in it.

"Two of them," he said, low. "Badges that don't match anybody's actual clearance list. One's got Sterling's access revocation letter already printed, ready to hand over the second anybody official shows up to make it look procedural instead of what it actually is."

"And if we don't let them?"

"Then we find out how committed they actually are to doing this quietly."

We found out fast. The two men inside weren't soldiers, not exactly, but they moved like people who'd been trained by somebody, and the moment Hunt and I stepped into that hallway without the badges they'd been told to expect, one of them went for something under his jacket before he'd even finished turning around.

Fuck it. The old ways are best.

I caught his wrist and jacket and jumped.

The corridor became open air fifty feet above the water in the pit. I let go and jumped back.

He reacted just like the hijacker had, reaching for his weapon on the way down. I saw his hand start toward his jacket. By the time it got there, I was already gone.

Hunt was still turning toward him. His hand had moved perhaps three inches toward his own jacket. He looked at the empty section of corridor, then at me.

The second man registered his partner's disappearance a fraction faster than Hunt did. He brought a gun clear of his coat.

Before Hunt could react, the second man was gone too. I dropped him over the water on the far side of the pit and came back.

Hunt's hand had reached his jacket. It had not reached his gun.

"What do you need me here for?"

"You found them."

Then Joe appeared.

Not through the control-room door. One instant the corridor between Hunt and me was empty; the next Joe was there on one knee, headset still on, one hand out as if he had been reaching through the video feed itself.

He looked at me. Looked at Hunt. Looked toward the exterior wall in the direction of a retention pond he could not possibly know now contained the second intruder.

"I saw the gun," he said.

Nobody answered.

"I was at the console. I saw it come up and I needed to be here."

Hunt's expression changed first. He knew the shape of it. So did I.

"Joe," I said carefully. "You jumped."

"I don't know how."

"Neither did I the first time."

He got to his feet and crossed the corridor to me, looking me over with both hands on my shoulders.

"Are you hurt?"

"I'm fine. Hunt's fine. The two men aren't here anymore."

His grip loosened. He took one breath.

"Not a hostile," I said.

"Right."

Then he was gone.

Hunt looked at the place he'd been. "Where did he go?"

"I think he went to make sure."

Security arrived inside another minute, drawn by whatever alarm Hunt had apparently tripped on purpose walking in. I went back to the pit for the two men. Both were conscious, barely, treading water on opposite sides of the island with more determination than coordination. I brought them into the service room one at a time, wet and coughing, and security put them in restraints. Neither man was talking, though I doubted that would last once somebody with real leverage sat them down properly.

Hunt looked at me the way he always did after something like this: checking, without asking, that I was actually all right and not just saying so.

"You good?"

"I'm good." I looked at him, at the two empty places in the corridor, at the whole familiar shape of a man who'd once been sent to hunt my family and had spent every year since making up for it in exactly this kind of moment. "Millie's going to want the whole story."

"Make sure she gets all of it," Hunt said.

Sterling was already on the phone, voice tight, giving orders I recognized the shape of even without the specifics: locking down access, pulling records, making absolutely sure nobody quietly finished what these two had started. Then he put that phone down and picked up the Red Phone. Almost immediately, he pulled it away from his ear and winced. A ship was three days out and roughly sixty-seven million kilometers gone. Joe had gone to Cent anyway.
