Chapter 39 of 49

Millie (POV): The old ways are best.

Baines put the attendance roster, the authorization trace, and a security still on the table between us in a secure room in the West Wing. Davy and I had already come through the appointment entrance and cleared screening.

I knew the face before she gave us the name. Devereaux had stood near the rear door in the Oval months earlier and asked how far the government should let one family control a national capability.

The secure phone rang. Baines put the President on speaker.

He told Davy that Devereaux had heard every word of the protection order and repeated it back with everyone else.

"Millie, I'm so sorry that someone in my administration acted this way! More sorry than I can say," the President said.

"David, I found the motherfucker. You want to come see me rip him to shreds?"

"We're down the hall," Davy said.

Baines collected the folders. An agent waiting outside led us down the corridor to the Oval.

Devereaux stood near the fireplace under guard. Davy and I stood against the wall with Baines beside us.

The President did not start quietly.

"Did you order General Sterling's credentials revoked?"

Devereaux tried to call it a continuity review.

"That was not an answer. Did you order it?"

He tried temporary transfer of operational control next. The President stripped that apart too, made him look away from Halloran, and asked the question again until Devereaux said yes.

"Who gave you the authority?"

Devereaux tried to cite the continuity provisions.

"I asked for a name."

There wasn't one. The President made him say that too, then made him say what the administrative language had hidden: he had no authority, and he had sent armed men to enforce his decision.

"Shut the fuck up."

The speaker flattened the volume without making it sound any less like a shout.

"You waited until Millicent Rice was sixty-seven million kilometers away. You calculated the communications delay. Then you used my protection order to map her family, remove the people she trusted from mission control, and send armed men through the door under my authority."

Devereaux said my daughter's capability could not remain subordinate to one family's judgment.

"It was never subordinate to yours!"

The President told him what he had said in that room the first time. Protect, not possess. Partner, not direct. He had made every one of them repeat it so nobody could hide behind a misunderstanding.

"You said it. Then you took my order apart and used the pieces to target her family."

"Both of Millicent Rice's parents are standing in this room because what you did was so far outside my order that they had to hear from me, personally, that their government had not declared war on their family."

Baines looked at me because she saw the decision arrive.

I had watched Davy use the pit for most of our marriage. Terrorists, kidnappers, armed men who believed a weapon entitled them to decide what happened next. When it was my turn, clearing the house where they had held Davy, I used the island differently. No fifty-foot fall. No impact with the water. I put people down on sand and scrub where they could not reach anyone, then went back for the next threat.

The old ways were not gentle.

They were survivable.

I crossed the room.

The agent beside Devereaux moved to block me. I Jumped past his reaching hand and appeared close enough to catch Devereaux by the wrist and the back of his jacket.

He had time to turn his head.

"The old ways are best," I said.

The Oval disappeared.

West Texas heat replaced climate control. The island lay inside the old sinkhole exactly as it had for decades, scrub and pale sand surrounded by dark water, the walls too steep to climb and the rim too far above for anyone below to reach.

I put Devereaux down in the dry center of it.

Not into the water. Not from the rim. On his own feet, though surprise folded one knee and made him catch himself against the sand.

He stared at the walls.

"Where are we?"

"The place my family has put armed men since before my daughter was born."

He looked at me then, and for the first time that day he understood something without first translating it into policy language.

"You wanted to decide where my daughter was allowed to go, who she was allowed to trust, and whether the people who loved her could stay near her. Sit here and think about what that feels like."

"You cannot leave me here."

"I can. I won't. Those are different facts."

I left him on the island and returned to the Oval.

Twelve seconds had passed.

Three Secret Service agents had weapons aimed at the space where I appeared. I kept my hands open and away from my body.

"He's alive," I said. "He's unhurt. He has no phone and nowhere to go. Davy knows the place."

"Turn around."

I turned. The agent secured my wrists behind my back. I did not resist. I had not mistaken the family tradition for legal authority, and I had not come back expecting the President to pretend I had.

Davy looked at me. Fear, anger, and recognition crossed his face in an order too fast to separate.

The President told him to bring Devereaux back.

An agent still had hold of Davy's arm.

"You can come or let go," Davy said. "Those are the choices."

The agent let go.

Davy vanished.

He returned less than a minute later with Devereaux. Wet trouser cuffs, sand on his jacket, no injury I could see. Agents closed around him and restored the federal custody I had interrupted.

"She kidnapped me," Devereaux said.

The President did not soften the word.

"Yes. She did. That does not alter a single question you were answering before she removed you."

Then he looked at me.

"Mrs. Rice," the President said, "you are in federal custody."

"I know."

"You will have counsel."

I nodded and let the agents take me out through the same door my family had used on our first visit.

Davy followed us into the outer office before two agents stopped him.

"Millie!"

"I'm not hurt," I told him.

"I know," he said, which was not the same thing as telling me I had been right.

I would tell Cent what I had done when she was home and standing in front of me. Not through a delayed channel, and not while she still had a mission to fly.

The Secret Service processed me in a room below the West Wing before anyone moved me elsewhere. They photographed my hands, took my fingerprints, bagged my clothes, and asked the same sequence of questions in three slightly different forms. Had Devereaux been injured? Had I left anything on the island? Could anyone else reach it? Was there food, shelter, water, another person waiting for him?

I answered the safety questions and declined the rest until counsel arrived.

The handcuffs were procedural. Everyone in the room knew that. I could have left without moving my hands at all. The agents knew it, the lawyer knew it, and I knew that if I Jumped away, surrender would become a word I'd said rather than a thing I had done.

So I stayed in the chair.

My attorney arrived forty minutes later. She was small, silver-haired, and unimpressed by every person who tried to hurry her. She made the agents leave, checked my wrists, then sat across from me with a legal pad she never once looked down at.

"You removed a person from federal custody inside the White House and transported him across state lines."

"Yes."

"Against his will."

"Yes."

"He was not physically threatening you or another person at that moment."

"No."

"Was he injured?"

"No."

"How long before he was returned?"

"Less than two minutes."

"You came back voluntarily."

"Yes."

She finally wrote something.

"Those facts matter. None makes this trivial. The government does not know what charge it wants yet because the statutes were written for cars, aircraft, doors, and distances a person has to cross. Do not mistake their uncertainty for leniency."

"I don't."

"Good. Do not mistake Devereaux's crimes for legal permission either."

"I don't."

She studied me for a moment. "You may be the first client I've had all year who understands the bad news before I finish giving it."

"Occupational background."

"Counselor?"

"Mother."

Davy was allowed to see me after midnight. They put us in an interview room without glass because glass would have been theater and everyone was tired of theater by then. An agent remained outside the closed door.

He did not hug me immediately.

"You used the island."

"Yes."

"On a man standing beside a federal agent in the Oval Office."

"Yes."

"I spent thirty years teaching people that the pit was how I kept from killing them."

"So did I."

"That doesn't make it ours to use whenever the law is already doing the job."

"I know."

His anger broke then, not louder, just less contained. "Do you? Cent is on her way to Mars, and you may still be in federal custody when she comes home. You didn't only take him out of the room, Millie. You took yourself out of every place she expects you to be."

That was the wound. Devereaux had tried to turn distance into control over my daughter, and I had answered by creating another distance she had not chosen.

"I know," I said again, and this time it nearly failed in my throat.

Davy crossed the room and held me. I let him. Neither of us pretended being loved was the same as being excused.

"Are you going to stay?" he asked into my hair.

He did not mean the next five minutes.

"Yes."

"Even if they put you somewhere stupid and call the walls security?"

"Yes."

"Even if it takes days?"

I thought of Cent receiving a message with Mars filling her windows and no way to answer before the next burn.

"Yes."

The first holding room had a steel door, a narrow bed, and a camera behind smoked plastic. None of it could keep me there.

I stayed until morning.
