Chapter 9 of 49

Cent: The chair was only half the machine.

Osei called me back three days later and erased half her own whiteboard before I had my coat off.

"The chair fixes you," she said. "It doesn't fix the fact that the universe did not standardize its junk for our convenience."

On the board she'd drawn the seat again, this time inside a rectangular frame, and beyond the frame a series of shapes that looked like somebody had emptied a child's block set across the room: cylinders, boxes, trusses, spheres, one lopsided thing I recognized as an old satellite bus.

"Every one of those needs a different place to take load," I said.

"Exactly. If we bolt your seat into one spacecraft, you've got a propulsion system for one spacecraft. If we build a structural spine behind the seat with a common interface, and then build adapters between that interface and whatever load you're moving, you have a propulsion system for anything we can rig safely."

Cory had already filled the far end of the table with hardware: a curved saddle lined with replaceable pads; a split collar that could close around a cylindrical stage; a wide web cradle for structures too fragile to clamp; and a rigid plate with adjustable feet for anything that came with a flat, load-rated surface.

"Universal doesn't mean one magic grabber," he said. "It means one connection on your side and a family of honest attachments on the load side. Same spine, same seat, same sensors. We change the last meter."

"And the handles?"

"Still only for bracing and control," Delgado said. "You close your hands around them so your upper body knows where neutral is. You are not levering the ship with your wrists. The shoulder harness, lap belt, thigh supports, and seatback make you one structural object with the spine. The spine makes you one structural object with the load. Then you change your own momentum and the whole assembly comes with you."

That was the first explanation anyone had given that matched exactly what the PAM-D had felt like. I hadn't pushed the dead stage with my arms. I'd made myself go somewhere else and refused to leave it behind. The chair wasn't an amplifier. It was a way to make the refusal safe and repeatable.

"Build it," I said.

"We are," Osei said. "But you're going to help us decide what fails first."

The engineering room expanded after that. Rigging specialists came over from payload integration. A salvage diver from the Navy spent an afternoon explaining why a clamp that looked secure in air could walk itself loose under repeated load. A robotics engineer brought in the end-effector catalog from three generations of station arms and crossed out almost all of it, because most of those tools were designed to position a load once, gently, not accept dozens of discrete changes in velocity.

Joe sat through the second design review and finally found the thing nobody else in the room had named.

"You're all treating attachment as one event," he said. "Latch it, verify it, fly it. But every kick is another chance for the load to settle differently inside the adapter. The attachment needs to prove it's still good before every kick, not just before the first."

Osei looked at him for a long second, then turned and wrote VERIFY EVERY CYCLE across the top of the board.

"That's why he's in the room," I said.

"Apparently," Truman said from the back, where he'd developed a habit of appearing halfway through meetings and listening until somebody forgot he was a general. "Give him a badge before security gets tired of pretending not to notice him."

One of the payload-integration managers suggested qualifying the chair and harness under static load, then leaving the complete dynamic checkout for orbit.

"We can't reproduce weightlessness on the floor," he said. "At some point the first real push through the complete fixture has to happen at Kristen."

"No," Truman said. "That's the wrong way to build something for space operations. This is NASA. We don't do things the wrong way. Ever."

The manager stopped talking.

"You can't reproduce orbit," Truman continued. "You can reproduce the load going through her body. You can test whether the harness shifts, whether the bearing holds, whether the sensors catch a bad path, and whether she can deliver the impulse you ask for when you ask for it. We do all of that before we put her outside a station with eight hundred kilograms of somebody else's hardware."

Osei built the answer into the floor of a test bay. The remediation-chair simulator did not look like a spacecraft. It looked like the chair and structural spine had been set inside a steel cage, with the load yoke coupled to six hydraulic cylinders that disappeared into a reinforced foundation. Accumulators absorbed the impulse when I pushed. Load cells measured the real magnitude, direction, and timing. The computer applied those numbers to whatever virtual payload Sterling's team had loaded into the model and showed us the velocity change I had actually produced, not the one the test expected.

The simulator used the real harness, index grips, bearing, load cells, warning indicators, and verify-every-cycle logic. The load-side cartridges could change stiffness or settle between kicks, imitating the ways a collar, saddle, or web cradle might redistribute force without pretending a ground rig could reproduce every dead satellite in orbit.

My first target was deliberately easy: one small impulse on the centerline, with a broad timing window. The frame took it without moving anything except hydraulic fluid.

"Magnitude high by six percent," Chu said. "Vector is clean."

"Again."

The next one was light. The one after that arrived late. I had spent months learning that repeated kicks stacked; I had not learned to make each one the same on command. The rig gave me that skill a number at a time. Osei narrowed the acceptable magnitude band. Chu narrowed the timing window. Delgado watched how the loads crossed my shoulders, back, hips, and thighs. Cory changed padding. We rotated the bearing, added lateral preload, changed the modeled payload mass, and made the load cartridges settle after a kick so Joe's verify-every-cycle rule had something real to catch.

On the second day the left shoulder strap walked twelve millimeters under repeated off-axis load. A buckle caught a suit hose when I reached for the release. Osei rejected two quick-release designs: one demanded more hand strength than Delgado would guarantee after a long sequence, and one could open under combined lateral load. None of those versions went to orbit.

By the end of the program I could put repeated impulses inside the requested band, on the requested mark, while the simulator changed the load between them. Amber meant I stopped. Red and the alarm meant I released the grips and got out of the sequence. The frame absorbed every push, and the data showed exactly what the payload and my body would have received.

The load adapters had their own stand. Robotic actuators pulled the collar, saddle, and web cradle through repeated axial and off-axis cycles against representative stage sections while the chair simulator supplied measured impulses from my runs. The first flight collar passed its qualification envelope without shifting beyond tolerance.

The first full-size hardware arrived at Kristen Station in pieces on an ordinary commercial cargo launch. That mattered. I did not lift it through the atmosphere, and nobody pretended I could. Rockets did the part rockets were already good at: getting manageable pieces out of Earth's gravity well. Joe and I caught the crates at the station's external cargo cradle, moved them through the inner ring, and spent two days assembling Osei's structural spine beside the aft service lock.

Kristen itself was still what it had always been: one circular station nested inside a larger circular water shell, its rooms partitioned inside the continuous inner ring rather than built as separate modules. The new rig bolted to a service hardpoint on that ring for checkout. When it wasn't in use, the collar, saddle, plate, and web cradle folded into a rack that looked almost disappointingly ordinary.

The chair did not.

Its harness held me from shoulder to thigh. The two index grips moved through only a few millimeters, enough for Osei's sensors to register my intended vector while keeping my hands in a repeatable neutral brace, not enough for anyone to mistake them for levers that physically shoved anything. Load cells ran under every contact surface. A circular bearing behind the seat let the whole chair rotate relative to the structural spine and lock at a measured angle, a feature Osei called "future-proofing" and refused to explain further until she'd tested it.

The chair could not stop me from jumping. Nothing mechanical could. Osei gave it two ways to tell me not to. A warning-level disagreement lit an amber CAUTION indicator directly above the grips. An abort-level reading turned the indicator red and added a cabin alarm nobody could mistake for advice.

"All right," Cory said, floating in front of me with a tablet. "Tell me what hurts."

"Nothing."

"That's not useful."

"It's extremely useful to me."

We spent the afternoon proving the ground fit survived weightlessness. Delgado checked every harness reference against the marks from Houston. Nothing had shifted. Joe found that the loose tail of one strap floated toward a suit-hose fitting when I twisted left, so Cory added a keeper before we took the chair outside. The release worked in every bearing position with my gloves on. Orbit found one problem the simulator couldn't create. It did not find a chair we had never actually used.

By the time Truman called the review, the rig had acquired a formal name nobody used: Variable Geometry Momentum Transfer Fixture. Joe called it the yoke. I called the whole thing the chair.

"Test article?" Truman asked.

Sterling answered from the wall display. "Centaur upper stage, eight hundred ninety kilograms dry, stable orbit, no hazardous residuals, good tracking history. Cylinder gives us a clean first run for the split collar."

"And if the collar slips?"

"She aborts the cycle, separates, and the stage remains in a safe orbit," Osei said. "No improvising a recovery under pressure. We collect data, come home, fix the rig."

Truman looked at me.

"You heard her."

"I heard her."

"Say it back."

"If the hardware starts lying, I don't save the test. I stop, separate, and let Sterling's catalog keep the stage another week."

"Good. We are not proving you can rescue our engineering from its own mistakes. We are proving the engineering deserves to fly with you."
