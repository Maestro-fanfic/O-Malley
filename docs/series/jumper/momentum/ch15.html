Chapter 15 of 49

Cent: I've never watched anyone build an entire ship around what I can do.

The craft itself started as a shape on a screen and became, over the following weeks, an actual object I could stand next to, which was a stranger transition to watch than I'd expected.

Sterling's "what do you need" had turned into an actual budget line inside of a week, and the budget line had turned into a fabrication floor at a facility outside Houston that NASA cleared for me on a Thursday and expected me to jump directly into on a Friday, which told me something about how fast the machinery of an institution could move once the right person decided it should.

"You don't have to watch every step of this," Truman told me, the first time I stood on that floor and looked at a half-assembled pressure hull sitting on its cradle like the skeleton of something not yet alive. "Most people who commission a spacecraft never see it built. They see the finished thing on a launch pad."

"I'm not most people commissioning a spacecraft. I'm the propulsion system. I'd like to know what I'm going to be riding inside of before I'm riding inside of it."

"Fair point." He said it with the particular satisfaction of a man whose own instinct had been confirmed rather than corrected. "Osei runs this floor. She'll walk you through anything you want walked through."

Osei, it turned out, ran the floor the same way she'd run the whiteboard: fast, precise, and delighted to have someone who actually wanted the details instead of just the summary.

"Pressure hull first," she said, walking me around the cradle. "Standard aerospace-grade aluminum-lithium alloy, welded seams, tested to twice the differential we expect you'll ever see. This part would look the same if we were building it for a chemical rocket. The differences start once we get past the hull."

"Life support?"

"Closed-loop, stand-alone, scaled up from ISS heritage systems and upgraded around everything the station taught us in operation. We still fight the mass budget twice: chemical rockets have to lift every section into orbit, and you still have an upper limit on what the finished ship can carry. What we don't need is interplanetary propellant eating the payload allowance after launch. That buys real margin on consumables, water, air scrubbing, the works."

"Power?"

"The first flight article can live on batteries because every free-flight block is short and it comes back to Kristen to recharge. The carrier can't. Air circulation, carbon-dioxide removal, water processing, thermal control, computers, navigation, communications—none of that stops needing electricity because you solved propulsion. The banks are identical removable cassettes on isolated buses. We can replace or add one without taking the ship dark."

"Solar?"

"Not on this ship. Arrays large enough to carry the continuous load add structure, pointing limits, and another thing you have to protect during every kick. Momentum runs on interchangeable battery cassettes. Two isolated banks. Either bank carries the survival bus while the other is disconnected."

"For eight days?"

"We don't carry eight days of electricity at once. You can return to Kristen. You take a depleted cassette out, bring a charged one back, and the other bank keeps the ship live during the exchange. The installed set gives us seventy-two hours at normal load plus a protected survival reserve. That is more time than any power rotation should need and long enough to diagnose a missed exchange."

"So I'm the part that closes the power budget too."

"You're the reason we can treat energy as resupply instead of payload," Osei said. "The cassette locks, blind-mate connectors, isolation contactors, and handling frame all get tested until a suited crew member can exchange one without exposing a live bus. The ship does not get a green light until a failed cassette can be isolated and replaced without taking life support down."

"So the rocket equation still gets the pieces off Earth. It just doesn't come with us."

"Exactly," she said. "The hull is being match-built and pressure-tested here, then it separates at three launch joints. Heavy-lift boosters put the sections, life support, and cargo into orbit on separate flights. We assemble and tether the carrier at Kristen Station. You do not lift the ship through a gravity well, and nobody designs a mission that quietly assumes you can."

She tapped the center of the cutaway. "The fixture goes in after orbital assembly. Once the hull's sealed and pressurized, once the crew section is livable and every pallet is aboard, we put the chair on its swivel bearing as close to the nominal loaded center of mass as fixed hardware allows. The seat rotates; the ship doesn't have to flip just to reverse the direction of your kicks. The sensor model tracks whatever shifts after that."

I ran my hand along a weld seam, still faintly warm from whatever process had made it, and thought about how strange it was that this whole enormous, precise, expensive undertaking existed because I'd been annoyed one afternoon at how much time I was wasting improvising braces on forty-year-old space junk.

"The chair was one thing," I said, mostly to myself, though Osei heard it. "I've never watched anyone build an entire ship around what I can do."

"Get used to it," she said, not unkindly. "This is going to be the first of a lot of things built specifically because you exist. I'd start making peace with that now."

I came back most days after that, sometimes for an hour, sometimes for most of an afternoon, watching the hull close up around empty space that would eventually be full of instruments and air and, eventually, a person other than me. I learned the names of welders and technicians the way I'd once learned the names of orbital debris, methodically, out of respect for work I couldn't do myself but had come to genuinely admire. A woman named Priya ran the welding rig with a precision that made Osei's own praise sound understated. A retired Air Force sergeant named Boyle fitted the life support ducting with the patient, unhurried care of a man who'd spent thirty years doing the work and had no intention of rushing it now just because the reason for it was strange.

"You know what's funny," Boyle told me one afternoon, running a pressure test on a fitting he'd just finished, "is that this is the most normal spacecraft I've ever built. Every part of it is boring aerospace engineering, done right, the way we always should've been doing it. The only thing that's insane is the reason we're building it, and I don't think about that part while I'm working. I just build the thing in front of me."

"Does that help? Not thinking about the reason?"

"It helps me sleep at night, yeah." He grinned, wiping his hands on a rag that had seen better decades. "I'll leave the thinking-about-it part to you. You're the one who has to actually sit in the chair."

By the fourth week the ground article was sealed, the life support was breathing on its own internal test cycle, and the ship's dedicated copy of my fixture had arrived from the shop. The chair itself held no surprises. It had been machined from the proven Kristen drawings: the same heavily braced seat, the same short index grips, the same support across shoulders, back, hips, and thighs, the same instrumentation. The new hardware was behind it—a ship-specific load yoke mounted on a circular bearing that could turn the whole chair through one hundred eighty degrees and lock it against either direction of travel.

The first time I settled into the ship copy, nobody was watching but Osei and Cory, both of them pretending not to be as moved by the moment as they clearly were.

"How does it feel?" Cory asked.

I closed my hands around the index grips and settled my shoulders and hips into the harness. "Like my chair."

"Good," Osei said. "It is your chair. The experiment is everything we bolted behind it."

She released the bearing. The chair turned smoothly through half a circle and locked with my body facing the opposite end of the hull. Cory checked every harness reference again. Nothing had shifted.

"Same fit," I said. "Opposite vector."

I stayed there a while longer, hands resting on the grips, feeling the whole absurd scale of what had happened in the space of a few weeks: a tank breaking loose, a shoulder wrenched half out of its socket, the chair we'd already proven, and now a faithful copy of it mounted at the center of an actual ship, waiting for the day it would carry someone other than me across a hundred and eighty million kilometers of empty space.

"When do we test the whole assembly for real?" I asked.

"Soon," Osei said. "Once we've picked a target worth the risk, and once Sterling and Truman finish arguing about who gets to make that call."

I looked down at the chair. "Tell them to pick something with good tracking data. I'm tired of eyeballing the interesting part."
