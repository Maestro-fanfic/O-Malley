Chapter 31 of 49

Osei POV: Now we find the next version.

The lander simulator entered integrated training two months after the carrier program began. It stood in the next bay, but I did not treat walking between them as permission to train both in the same day. It wasn't a chair facing a screen either. It was a full-size capsule cabin on its own six-axis motion base, with flight controls at the crew stations, working navigation, the same warning logic, and Cent's full-body chair mounted in the middle on the crossed pitch-and-yaw trunnions planned for the lander. Its gimbal coupled to a second foundation-mounted reaction frame, so Cent could push at any commanded pitch and yaw while the rig absorbed and measured the real impulse.

I showed them why there were two absolute encoders on each axis before I let any of them climb inside. The first ground article had failed on its forty-seventh off-axis load cycle. One pitch lock had back-driven eighteen thousandths of a degree under alternating lateral load. The encoder on the drive still reported the commanded angle because the movement had happened downstream of it. The frame encoder saw the chair move. Yellow stayed on. Green never appeared.

"The system caught it," Iverson said.

"The system caught one version of it," I said. "Then we replaced the single lock with opposed mechanical dogs and gave both sides of the load path an independent vote. Now we find the next version."

My team had already run the replacement through thermal vacuum, cold-soaked lubricant, simulated Mars dust, stalled drives, late locks, and off-axis loads beyond anything the capsule was supposed to see. Now we had to put people inside the loop.

Cent strapped into the chair in her pressure suit. Iverson took command. Childs took navigation. The simulator put Mars across the forward displays and began feeding them a descent that matched the forecast for the first four minutes.

Then the atmosphere changed.

"Eastward drift," Childs said. "Updating correction vector. Pitch down zero point zero-four-seven. Yaw west zero point three-one-two."

The gimbal moved under Cent. Yellow stayed on while the locks reseated.

"Vector changed," Childs said before they finished. "Cancel that. New target coming."

The chair stopped, unlocked, and slewed again. Cent kept her hands closed around the index grips and did nothing. The motion base rolled the cabin two degrees right while the reaction frame held an off-axis preload across the chair structure.

"Both locks seated," Iverson said.

"Position agrees," Childs said. "Load path clean."

Yellow went dark. Green came on.

Cent kicked into the reaction frame. The accumulators absorbed it. The measured impulse landed inside the commanded magnitude band and timing window, and the displays showed the crossrange error collapsing toward zero.

On the next correction, the test director introduced a bad drive encoder. The frame encoder disagreed by twelve thousandths of a degree. Yellow held.

"No go," Cent said.

"Correct," I answered over the simulator loop.

Then the disagreement crossed the abort limit. The indicator turned red and the cabin alarm sounded. Cent released the grips.

"Landing abort," Iverson said. "Hold present attitude."

The run did not stop. The capsule kept descending through the simulated atmosphere while Childs froze the invalid correction, checked the remaining energy and altitude, and identified the last abort gate they had not crossed.

"Gate three remains open," she said. "Return-to-orbit branch. Recenter the gimbal and give me independent position only."

Iverson held the capsule attitude on reaction control. My team isolated the bad drive encoder. The gimbal returned to neutral under the frame encoder, both opposed locks seated, and the load-path sensors agreed. Red cleared. Yellow stayed on while Childs loaded the climb vector and the scheduler rebuilt the sequence.

"Abort vector verified," Childs said.

Green came on.

Cent closed her hands around the grips again and delivered the first recovery kick. The reaction frame took the real impulse. The simulator applied it to the capsule, arresting the descent a piece at a time before building the velocity that carried them back out of the atmosphere. Iverson flew attitude; Childs updated the rendezvous; Cent pushed only when the verified vector and sequence produced green. They climbed to Momentum's orbit, matched velocity, and completed the docking procedure.

Only after the docking capture light came on did I end the run.

We flew three hundred complete landing profiles in that simulator over the next several months: separation from the carrier, cancellation of orbital velocity above the atmosphere, first contact with the density layers, crossrange corrections, terminal descent, touchdown, and the ascent sequence that returned the capsule to orbit. We also flew the aborts from every gate where an abort remained possible. Early aborts climbed back to Momentum and ended only after rendezvous and docking. Later ones abandoned the current landing solution, stabilized the capsule, and continued toward a reachable alternate site. Routine stretches could be compressed, but every kind of correction Cent might have to make was trained with a real push into the reaction frame. Sometimes the computer made a drive lag. Sometimes it changed the atmospheric target before the gimbal locked. Sometimes it failed an encoder, loaded the capsule wrong, or moved the center of mass after we had already agreed on the vector. Green required the commanded pitch and yaw, every mechanical lock, both independent position votes, and the load-path sensors to agree. Cent still had to deliver the requested impulse inside the timing window after it appeared. Anything less became the state the crew had to correct.
