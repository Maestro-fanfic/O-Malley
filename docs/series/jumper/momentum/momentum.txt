Momentum

Book One of the Jumper Universe



Momentum



Chapter 1: Cent: It worked once. That's not the same as knowing it works.



Tuesday mornings on Kristen Station meant net inspections, and net inspections meant floating along sixty meters of truss with a flashlight and a checklist, looking for failures that were never supposed to happen. I'd done it every Tuesday for four months. I was, if anything, getting bored with it, which should have told me something.

"Net one, clear," I said into the comm, working my way hand over hand along the rail. "Net two." I stopped. Looked again. "Net two, clear. Same as last week."

"Copy," Sterling said, from wherever Sterling actually was these days, an office in Colorado Springs I'd never seen and had stopped trying to picture. "Cargo manifest still says water tank, aft cradle three, four hundred kilos nominal."

"Manifest's right. It's sitting pretty."

"Good. COLA's got two objects transiting your altitude band in the next six hours, nothing close, just wanted eyes confirmed before I signed off on the report."

"Eyes confirmed. Tell your board to relax."

"Tell my board yourself sometime. I'd enjoy watching that conversation."

I finished the rest of the inspection the way I finished every Tuesday inspection, which was to say thoroughly and with the specific, low-grade satisfaction of a job that never had anything wrong with it. Kristen Station wasn't finished, wasn't going to be finished for a long while yet, but it was solid, it was ours, and every Tuesday it held together exactly the way it was supposed to.

I was three-quarters of the way back to the hub, thinking about lunch, when the truss jumped under my boots.

Not a big jump. A jolt, quick and hard, more felt than heard, the way anything mechanical announces itself out here where sound has nowhere to travel and vibration does all the talking. I froze, both hands on the rail, and looked back the way I'd come.

Net two was gone.

Not gone-gone. Failed. I could see the shape of it even from sixty meters off, torn webbing trailing off the end of the truss like a flag nobody had ever meant to fly, and behind the webbing, tumbling slow and stupid and completely unbothered by the fact that it wasn't supposed to be able to do this, four hundred kilos of water tank.

"Cent." Sterling's voice again, faster this time, flat in the particular way he got when he was working hard at not sounding alarmed. "Net two just failed. You have a visual?"

"I have a visual." I did the math without meaning to, the way you do math on a falling glass before you've consciously decided to reach for it. Tumbling slow, end over end, drifting off the truss at maybe half a meter a second. Half a meter a second doesn't sound like much. On the ground, it isn't much; the ground eats that energy for you, you stub a toe, you move on with your day. Out here there's no ground to eat anything. There's no air to drag against it, no friction to bleed it off, nothing at all standing between four hundred kilos of momentum and whatever happened to be in its way when it finally arrived somewhere.

The something, in about ninety seconds at that drift rate, was going to be the solar array Joe had spent six hours aligning that morning.

"I see it," I said, and jumped.

I put myself twenty meters out, matched to the tank's tumble, close enough to read the manufacturer's stencil peeling off the side. Slow spin, slight wobble, torn webbing whipping around on every rotation like a rope someone had let go of mid-throw. No way to grab it clean. Get an arm in the wrong place when that webbing came around and I'd lose the arm, or close enough to it that the distinction wouldn't matter much. I'd done that kind of math before, on a different piece of gear, a different afternoon. I wasn't doing it again.

What I actually needed to be was a wall. Something for the tank to push against that could push back without losing a fight to a strip of nylon webbing.

"Talk to me," Sterling said.

"Thinking."

Here's the thing I knew for a fact, the thing Cory had made me demonstrate for him twice in his office with a stepladder and a very worried expression on his face the whole time: I could change my own velocity in a jump. Not carry it. Not cancel it to match a new frame, the way any jumper could. Actually add some, or take some away, out of nothing, the way I'd added twenty feet a second straight up once and never used my legs to do it. Nobody had ever explained where the energy came from. Cory had tried, for about a week, checking numbers against numbers that refused to balance, and then given up and started calling it "the Cent effect" specifically to annoy me. I'd stopped chasing the why a long time before that. You use what works. You don't have to understand a hammer to drive a nail.

I'd done the other thing before, too. The actual push, not just the velocity change. Exactly once. The PAM-D, a year and a half back: a dead Delta third stage, two thousand kilos of tumbling junk nobody on Earth would ever miss, four hundred kilometers up with an entire orbit's worth of time to decide whether I actually wanted to try it. I remembered thinking it through out loud, to nobody, in my own head, the way you talk yourself into cold water: *I could push one of those on Earth, easily, if it were in neutral. In micro-G, if I had something to push against, I could easily move it. Wouldn't know if I didn't try.* And it had worked. Clean, first try, the perigee dropping four hundred klicks in about the time it takes to say so, gone into the mesosphere and gone for good, and me left hanging there afterward feeling like I'd gotten away with something.

That had been on purpose. Borrowed time, an empty sky, and nothing riding on being wrong except my own pride.

This was ninety seconds and Joe's array on the other end of the tumble.

I jumped again, in close this time, close enough that my forearms and shoulder were already set against the tank's shell before I'd finished arriving, no impact, no collision, just suddenly there with my body in contact and the tumble already trying to wrench me sideways with it. My hands steadied the contact; they weren't what moved four hundred kilos. I tightened through my whole body the way I had against the PAM-D, changed my own momentum, and made the tank come with me. Not an arm push. A jump delivered while braced to something else.

The tank stopped tumbling.

Not slowed. Stopped, dead, the way a spinning coin stops when you slap your palm down flat on top of it, except this was four hundred kilos of water and steel and the "palm" was something that didn't have a name I could give Sterling if he asked, which he was about to. I felt the reaction of it come back through my hands into my shoulders, a solid shove, real and physical and entirely too easy for the mass involved.

I hung there a second with my hands still on the shell, breathing harder than the effort should have cost me.

"Cent?" Sterling, careful now, the way he got when the silence on his end had run a beat too long.

"I'm okay. It's not tumbling anymore."

"How."

"Working on that part."

I did it again then, on purpose, body aligned down the truss toward the empty docking port instead of toward Joe's array. I changed my own velocity and kept the contact long enough to bring the tank along for the ride. Felt the moment we separated, its new momentum continuing without me, something that outmassed me eight times over moving exactly where I'd put it and nowhere else, because there was nothing out here to argue with me about it. No air to drag against it. No ground to eat the energy sideways. No gravity trying to bend the path into something curved and complicated. I'd told it where to go, and it went.

On the ground, I could push a truck if I really put my back into it and had good footing and a run at it, and the truck would move about as far as my legs could make it move before friction and the tires and Newton's third law all voted me down. Up here I didn't have legs in it at all. I had my own changed momentum, contact, and intention, and apparently that was still the whole recipe. Same thing I'd done with the PAM-D. Just a smaller truck this time, and about eighteen months less runway to be sure it would work.

"It's headed for the empty port," I said. "Low and slow. Somebody want to catch it before it decides to come back this way?"

Joe's voice, from over by the array, breathless in a way that had nothing to do with exertion: "On it. Cent, what did you just do?"

"Told the tank where to go. It listened. Story for later."

I watched it drift away, tumble-free, obedient, going exactly where I'd sent it at exactly the speed I'd given it, and thought about all the things around the station heavier than me that I'd never once let myself count as things I could just *do*, in the moment, without an orbit's worth of runway first. A section of the inner ring that needed nudging into alignment without burning fuel we didn't have to spare. Debris on a bad vector with nobody's math fast enough to plot an intercept in time. A person, someday, moving too fast in the wrong direction, with nothing between them and a very long fall that technically wasn't falling at all.

I'd known, since the PAM-D, that it wasn't a fluke.

I had not known, until about forty seconds ago, that it would still be there when I didn't have time to talk myself into it first.

* * *

Joe caught the tank at the docking port with both hands and a grunt I heard clean over the comm, and by the time I jumped back to the hub he'd already lashed it down twice over with a fresh net, the kind of overkill that meant he was still running on adrenaline and hadn't found anywhere else to put it yet.

"You want to tell me what that was," he said, not turning around, still checking the lashing a third time.

"I pushed it."

"You pushed it." He did turn around then. Joe had a good face for worry, unfortunately, all the tells right there on the surface where I could read them: a tightness around the eyes, a stillness that wasn't calm so much as held. "Cent, that thing outmassed you by a factor of eight."

"I know what it outmassed me by. I did the math on the way over." I caught the edge of the truss and let myself drift to a stop next to him, close enough that our shoulders touched, which I'd learned over the last year was the fastest way to talk him down off whatever ledge his imagination had climbed. "It worked. I'm fine. The array's fine. The tank's fine. Everybody's fine."

"This time."

"This time," I agreed, because there wasn't a version of that sentence I could argue with honestly. "I've done it before, Joe. Once. The PAM-D, a year and a half ago."

"You never told me you could do that."

"I never told anybody I could do that, except Cory, and only because he made me demonstrate the velocity thing in his office until I got tired of arguing about it." I looked at the tank, lashed down twice over, going nowhere. "I didn't know I could still do it under pressure until about four minutes ago. That's the actual new information here. Not that I can push things. That I can push things when I don't have an orbit's worth of time to decide whether I trust myself."

Joe was quiet for a second, working the last of the adrenaline out of his hands, opening and closing them against nothing. "Are you going to tell Sterling that part? The pressure part?"

"He's going to ask. He always asks the actual question eventually."

"He's good at his job."

"He's annoyingly good at his job." I bumped my shoulder against his again, gentler this time. "Come on. I promised him a full report and my lunch is getting later by the minute."

Sterling's full report, when I finally sat down for it, took about ten minutes and covered everything except the one thing I knew he actually wanted, which he saved, predictably, for last.

"So to be clear," he said, in the voice he used when he already had the shape of the answer and just wanted me to say it out loud for the record. "This is the same thing you did to the PAM-D."

"Same mechanism. Different circumstances."

"Different how."

"The PAM-D I thought about for the better part of an orbit before I tried it. Empty sky, dead junk, nothing on the line but my own pride if it didn't work. Today I had about ninety seconds and Joe's array on the other end of a four-hundred-kilo mistake. I didn't have time to talk myself into it. I just did it."

"And it worked."

"And it worked."

Another pause, and I could picture him exactly, sitting at whatever desk he sat at in Colorado Springs, running the numbers behind his own eyes the way he always did before he let himself say anything he might have to walk back later.

"I want that written up properly," he said finally. "Not for me. For the file. If this is repeatable under time pressure and not just under ideal conditions, that's a different category of fact than the one I've had on record since the PAM-D."

"It's not going in any file that leaves this station."

"I didn't say it was leaving the station. I said I wanted it written down somewhere that isn't just your own head, in case you're ever not around to ask." A beat. "That wasn't meant to be as grim as it just sounded."

"It's fine. You're not wrong." I thought about it a second longer than I meant to, the tank drifting harmless at the empty port, Joe's hands still faintly unsteady an hour later. "I'll write it up. Tonight. You'll have it by morning."

"Good. And Cent?"

"Yeah?"

"Nice work today. I mean that plainly, not as a preamble to a question."

"I know you do," I said, and meant it. "Thanks."

* * *

I called home that evening, the way I did most evenings, mostly out of habit and partly because the secure line into the old hotel still made a family call feel like an event instead of a formality.

"You sound tired," Mom said, before I'd said much of anything.

"Had an interesting day."

"Define interesting."

I told her. All of it, the net failure and the tumble and the ninety seconds and the push, the whole thing laid out plain the way I'd have told it to Sterling if Sterling had been the kind of person you told things to for comfort instead of for the record. Mom listened without interrupting, which was its own kind of tell; she interrupted plenty when she thought I was underselling a risk, and she wasn't interrupting now.

"You're saying this like it's a footnote," she said, once I'd finished. "The pushing-things-fast part. That's not a footnote, Cent, that's a new fact about how you work."

"I know it's not a footnote."

"Does your father know yet?"

"Not yet. You're first."

"He's going to want to talk about it the way he talks about everything that changes the shape of the danger you're in." Which was true, and which I'd been putting off precisely because it was true; Dad's questions always came from the right place and still managed to feel, some days, like being cross-examined by someone who loved you. "I'll tell him gently. Mostly gently."

"Thanks."

"How's Joe?"

"Shaken. He'll be fine by tomorrow. He's better at recovering from being scared for me than he used to be." I thought about his hands, opening and closing against nothing. "He's gotten a lot better, actually, over the last year. He used to spiral. Now he just gets quiet and checks the lashings three times."

"That's growth," Mom said, her smile surviving even the uplink's flat, compressed audio. "Get some sleep. Write your report for Sterling. And Cent, whatever this new thing turns out to mean, take it slow finding out. You don't have to solve all of it in one Tuesday."

"I wasn't planning to."

"You never plan to. It just tends to happen that way around you."

I laughed, because she wasn't wrong about that either, and said goodnight, and stayed for a while afterward in the quiet of my own room with the lights of Earth sliding by underneath, thinking about a tank that had stopped dead the instant I told it to, and a general in Colorado who wanted it written down somewhere that wasn't just my own head, and a boyfriend down the corridor who'd needed three passes at the same lashing before his hands stopped shaking.

I'd known, since the PAM-D, that it wasn't a fluke. Now Joe's shaking hands were part of the data too. I liked that considerably less than the part about the tank.



Chapter 2: Cent: This is free. No fuel. No reaction mass.



I wrote the report Sterling wanted that same night, the way I'd promised, and spent most of the next morning reading it back to myself over coffee and deciding I didn't like how much of it was guesswork dressed up as confidence. *It worked. I don't fully know why it worked, or whether it will work the same way twice, or what the actual limits are.* That wasn't a report. That was a shrug with better grammar.

So I called Sterling before he'd finished his own coffee, by the sound of it.

"I want to try something," I told him. "On purpose this time. Not another tank."

There was a pause on the line, the kind he used when he was deciding whether to ask a question he already knew the answer to. "Define 'try something.'"

"The tank. What I did to the tank. I want to do it again, small, controlled, where nobody's array is on the other end of it if I get it wrong."

"You want a test article."

"I want a lot of test articles. You've got a whole catalog of junk you're already tracking so it doesn't put a hole in something that matters. I want to borrow some of it."

Another pause, shorter. "You're not asking my permission. You're telling me what you're doing so I don't have a coronary when my board lights up with something maneuvering that shouldn't be."

"Pretty much."

"...Send me a window. I'll pull you three candidates, ascending mass order, nothing with an active mission dependency. And Cent, no cover story on this one. If anybody above me asks what Apex Orbital's doing out there, I'm telling them the truth."

"That's all I wanted anyway."

"You realize what you're actually asking me to do, don't you? I'm handing you live tracking data on real orbital debris so you can go push it around by hand, off any established protocol, with no insurance company on Earth that would touch this if I described it to them honestly."

"You could describe it dishonestly."

"I have integrity issues with that plan." He paused long enough to decide to say the rest anyway. "I'm doing it because whatever you did to that tank yesterday, on a ninety-second clock, with real stakes, is either the most useful thing that's happened to my catalog in a decade, or a fluke I need to understand well enough to rule out. Either way I need data, not a war story."

"You'll get data. Give me the window."

The window came through an hour later: three candidates, coordinates and tracking data attached, no fanfare, exactly the businesslike handoff I'd asked for.

The first one was small on purpose. A dead CubeSat, eleven kilos, tumbling lazy over the Pacific, no webbing to catch me across the wrist this time, just aluminum and a stripped solar panel that caught the sun in slow, patient flashes as it turned. I jumped to it slow, took my time lining up, folded my forearms across the frame with my hands only keeping the contact from slipping, and this time I didn't just survive what came next. I watched for it.

The change went out clean, a small flat shove delivered by my whole braced body, no drama, and I felt the reaction arrive a half-second later through my arms, shoulder, and ribs. It would've spun me if I'd let it. I hadn't. I killed it with a follow-up jump before it ever built into anything, the same way I'd cancel a bad fall: jump in place, kill the velocity, land like I'd never had it.

"Talk to me," Sterling said.

"It's not luck. There's a real kickback, every time, and it's not something I have to think hard about correcting. It's like catching a step I already knew was there." I watched the CubeSat drift off, unhurried, exactly where I'd told it to go, spinning gently in a way it hadn't been spinning thirty seconds before. "One push, one measurable shove back at me. Every time."

"Every time is a strong claim after one data point."

"Give me the second one, then."

The second was bigger: a Delta-K upper stage, dead weight north of six hundred kilos, close enough in class to the PAM-D that some part of me relaxed just recognizing the shape of it, the same kind of tank-and-nozzle silhouette, the same rust-orange thermal blanket gone brittle and peeling after however many years up here. I set my shoulder and forearms across the nozzle housing, hands locking the position rather than supplying the force, and changed us the same way I had the CubeSat, no more effort behind it, no less.

The kickback nearly took my arm out of the socket.

Not injury. Not even close to what it would take to actually hurt me. But there was no mistaking that six hundred kilos pushed back harder than eleven did, and pushed back a lot harder, not a little. I hung there a second, working my shoulder, filing that away without chasing it any further yet. There'd be a place where the number stopped being interesting and started being a limit. I hadn't found it. I wasn't looking for it today.

"You okay?" Sterling, fast.

"Fine. Just... bigger pushes back harder. Which, yes, I know, that's the whole idea of mass, I'm not being profound. I mean it's not free. Whatever this is, it costs something on my end too, and the cost scales with what I'm pushing."

"Noted." A beat. "Third candidate's a Cosmos fragment, small, similar mass to your first. Repeat run, if you want it."

I did. Two quick pushes on the same object, thirty seconds apart, both clean, both correctable, and somewhere in the gap between them, not a new test, not a separate thought, just the same breath I was already taking, it landed.

I wasn't burning anything to do this.

No propellant. No reaction mass thrown the other way to buy my half of the equation. Every satellite Apex Orbital had ever nudged into a better orbit, every dead thing Sterling's catalog had ever needed moved off a collision course, every gram of fuel anybody had ever budgeted for doing that kind of work from the ground: all of it existed because moving mass in space normally costs mass. You throw something one way to go the other way. That was the whole tyranny of the rocket equation, the thing that made every mission planner's life miserable, the reason a satellite's usable life ended the day its station-keeping fuel ran dry, not the day its electronics failed.

I didn't have that problem. I had never had that problem. I'd just never stood still long enough to notice I didn't have it.

"Sterling."

"Still here."

"This is free. Whatever I just did to that fragment, it didn't cost anything except me, personally, and a bruised shoulder. No fuel. No reaction mass. Every dead thing in your catalog that's ever needed a nudge: I don't need to bring anything with me to do it. I just need to be there."

The line was quiet long enough that I checked the connection was still up.

"I heard you the first time," he said finally. "I'm doing the math on what that means for the catalog and I don't like how fast the number's moving."

"Yeah."

"Same time tomorrow. Bring a bigger shoulder."

I laughed, actually laughed, hanging there over the Pacific with a dead fragment of somebody else's satellite drifting off exactly where I'd sent it, and thought that it was a strange thing to be tired and thrilled about in exactly the same breath.

* * *

I found Joe in the greenhouse when I got back, which was where he retreated to most evenings lately, tending a row of lettuce that had no business growing as well as it did on a space station and doing it anyway out of pure stubbornness.

"You've got that look," he said, not turning around.

"What look?"

"The one where you've found a new thing to think about and you're going to think about it whether anybody wants you to or not." He pinched a dead leaf off one of the plants and dropped it into the recycler slot. "How'd the testing go?"

"Better than expected. Worse on my shoulder than expected." I flexed it experimentally and regretted the honesty of the gesture when it actually hurt. "Three test articles, three clean pushes, no falloff, no accidents. Sterling's running the numbers on what it means for his catalog."

"And what does it mean?"

I told him the same way I'd told Sterling, except slower, because Joe didn't need the businesslike version; he needed the version where I let the meaning of it show instead of just reporting the result. No fuel. No reaction mass. Every dead thing that needed moving, movable, for the cost of showing up and a sore shoulder.

Joe set down his trowel and finally looked at me properly. "That's not a small thing, Cent."

"I know it's not a small thing."

"I mean it's not small the way yesterday's tank was small. Yesterday was you saving a satellite dish. This is you telling me you've got a free version of the thing every space program on Earth has spent seventy years being poor about."

"I hadn't put it that way in my head yet, but yeah. That tracks."

He was quiet for a moment, turning a lettuce leaf over in his fingers without really looking at it. "Are you going to tell your parents the scale of this? Not the tank-saving part. The part where you've apparently been sitting on a solution to a problem that's shaped the entire history of spaceflight."

"Eventually. Once I understand it better than 'it worked three times and my shoulder hurts.'" I came and stood next to him, close enough that our shoulders touched the way they had after the tank. "I'm not hiding it. I'm just not ready to hand it to anybody bigger than Sterling until I know what it actually is."

"Fair." He went back to the lettuce, and then, without looking up: "I liked the version of you from yesterday. The one who found out something new about herself under pressure and didn't fall apart."

"I liked that version too. I'm hoping to keep being her."

"You will be." He said it simply, like a fact rather than a hope, which was one of the things I'd come to rely on about Joe without ever quite telling him so. "Come on. Dinner. You can tell me about the Delta-K over food instead of over a lettuce row."

I let him steer me toward the galley, my shoulder aching in a way that felt, absurdly, like the good kind of tired, and thought that whatever this turned into, I was glad to have somebody in the room who noticed the shape of the new thing before I'd even finished explaining it to myself.



Chapter 3: Cent: Two kicks. Same object, different frames, no falloff between them.



Apex Orbital ran on ordinary Tuesdays too, same as Kristen Station did, and most of them looked nothing like a tank breaking loose or a general handing over live tracking data on debris nobody else was allowed to touch. Most of them looked like invoices.

"You're billing this as a standard mitigation pass," Dad said, over the business line, going through the week's paperwork the way he did every Monday morning with the particular thoroughness of a man who'd spent years making sure nothing about this family left a trail that could be used against it later. "Same rate as the Iridium job in March?"

"Same rate. It's the same kind of work. Coordinates, jump, push, confirm. I don't charge more just because I understand it better than I used to."

"You could. Nobody else on Earth can do it at all."

"Nobody else on Earth is billing for it either, Dad. Let's not get greedy about a monopoly built on something I still don't fully understand myself."

He laughed, the short, dry sound that meant he agreed but wasn't going to say so outright. "Fair. What's on today's docket?"

"Fengyun fragment. Fast one, in and out before dinner. Close pass with an Iridium replacement, needs the miss distance opened up."

"Routine, then."

"Routine," I agreed, and believed it completely, which tells you something about how little I understood, that morning, about what "routine" was about to stop meaning.

The Fengyun fragment was supposed to be routine.

Sterling's catalog had it flagged three weeks out from the close pass, the kind of job Apex Orbital did four or five times a year now and billed for like anybody else: relayed coordinates, a jump to visual range, a push to open the miss distance up past anybody's nerves. I'd done a dozen of these. I wasn't thinking about the CubeSat or the Delta-K or any of it. I was thinking about getting it done before dinner, and maybe about whether Joe would want to trade the greenhouse duty for cooking tonight, since his lettuce experiment had produced actual, edible lettuce for the first time and he'd want an excuse to show it off.

I jumped to it, matched its tumble, got a hand flat against the housing, and pushed the way I'd push anything: same effort I'd have used on the Cosmos fragment, calibrated by feel more than math. The kickback came back through my palm right on schedule. I killed my own drift with a follow-up jump, same as always, and checked the numbers Sterling read me off the ground track.

Not enough.

"That opens the miss distance to about four hundred meters," Sterling said. "COLA wants six."

"It's not going to get there on one push."

"Can you get more out of it?"

I could've asked what he meant. I could've assumed he meant push harder. Some instinct that had nothing to do with caution and everything to do with efficiency told me harder wasn't actually the question worth asking. Instead I just did the obvious thing, the thing that didn't feel like a decision at all: I let the fragment finish tumbling away from me, jumped after it, same object, new position, a new little sliver of its post-push drift, then got my hand back against the housing, and pushed it again.

The second shove landed the same as the first. Clean. No falloff, no argument from the fragment about already having been pushed once, no sense that it had spent something on the first push that it now owed me interest on. Sterling read the new numbers back a minute later.

"That's six-twenty. You're clear."

"Copy. Heading back."

I jumped home, ate dinner, admired Joe's lettuce with the appropriate amount of ceremony, told him about the array alignment holding steady and not much else, because there wasn't much else to tell. It had been an ordinary Tuesday's worth of work. It wasn't until I was lying in my bunk that night, staring at the same ceiling panel I always stared at, that I actually turned the afternoon over and looked at what I'd done.

Two kicks. Same object, different frames, no falloff between them.

I sat up.

It wasn't that the first push had somehow not been enough and I'd made up the difference by trying harder. It was that there wasn't a difference to make up in any special way at all: the second push worked exactly like the first, full value, no discount for already having been kicked once. Nothing about the fragment's history mattered to what I could do to it now. I could've stopped there and called it four hundred meters and gone home unsatisfied, or I could chase it down again and again and again, a hundred small kicks strung end to end with nothing capping the sum of them except how many times I was willing to jump and how much shoulder I had left at the end of it.

That wasn't about me going fast. I'd already solved going fast, for myself, alone: I'd stood on Mars a year ago and come home the same afternoon, and going fast for one person who can already put herself anywhere she's been was never the hard part.

This was about everything that wasn't me. A tank. A section of station structure. A hundred meters of dumb tumbling junk with nobody aboard to ask if it minded being nudged. Things that had to get somewhere the ordinary way, riding fuel and thrust and the tyranny of the rocket equation the whole way there, except now maybe they didn't, not if I was willing to keep showing up and doing it again.

I lay back down. The ceiling panel hadn't moved. Everything else kind of had.

"You're thinking loud," Joe said from the other side of the bunk, not quite asleep either.

"Just doing math."

"Anything I need to worry about?"

I thought about the fragment, obedient, six hundred and twenty meters clear of anything it might have hit, having taken two kicks from me like it was nothing, like it would've taken twenty.

"No," I said. "I think I need to worry about it. Go back to sleep."

He didn't answer, which meant he had. I lay there a while longer, doing the math anyway.

* * *

I ran the idea past Sterling two days later, on a call that started as routine catalog business and didn't stay that way for long.

"I want to try something with the Fengyun fragment," I said. "Not the moving-it part. The repeating-it part."

"Go on."

"Two kicks, no falloff between them. I want to know if that holds at ten. Twenty. I want to know if there's a ceiling on how many times I can move the same object before something about the process breaks down, mine or its."

The pause sounded like him weighing the request against whatever else was competing for his attention that morning. "You're proposing an endurance test."

"I'm proposing I find out whether 'it stacks' is actually true, or whether I got lucky twice in a row on an object small enough not to teach me anything."

The target he found was a defunct weather satellite, tumbling gently over the Indian Ocean, well-characterized enough that Sterling's team could log its exact drift after every single push and compare it against the last. Every push, the same. Every correction, the same. No falloff at ten kicks. No falloff at twenty. My shoulder ached by the end of it in the ordinary, honest way a muscle aches after real work. Chapter 4: Cent: I know I don't have any right to ask, but...

Chapter 5: Cent: I keep running out of places to brace.



We ran through Sterling's catalog in ascending order, and ascending order turned out to mean something different every week.

Week two was defunct comsats, a Boeing 601 bus the size of a delivery van, no solar panels left to worry about clipping, and I learned that "bigger" didn't mean "harder to find something to hold," because a comsat bus still had edges, ribs, structure built to be handled by a crane arm on the ground once upon a time. There was a retired weather satellite that week too, and an old Intelsat relay that Sterling flagged half-joking as "sentimental value, try not to break it," which I didn't, though I came closer than I told him.

I grabbed the stage by a thruster mount that first time because it was the only thing that looked like it wouldn't tear my hand open, pushed the way I'd pushed everything else, and felt the push go somewhere I hadn't intended: not straight through the object's center of mass, off to one side of it instead, because the thruster mount wasn't anywhere near the center of anything. The stage didn't just move. It started to tumble, slow, in a new axis it hadn't had before I touched it.

"That's not what I wanted," I told Sterling, jumping clear before it could catch me in whatever it was about to do.

"Define what you wanted."

"I wanted it to go that way. It went that way and started spinning on an axis I didn't ask for. I put the push somewhere off-center and it's arguing with me about it now."

"So find the center."

"I know where the center is. Knowing where it is and having two structural points that let me couple my body through it are different problems. There's no brace centered on an Ariane upper stage. There's no reason there should be. Nobody built these things expecting somebody to show up, change her own momentum, and bring the stage with her."

It became the actual work, after that: not the momentum change, the bracing. Every new test article meant a slow first pass, hand-eye, sometimes literally walking my hands across a structure in freefall the way you'd feel along a dark hallway, looking for two places where I could set my forearms, shoulder, or harness close enough to the balance point that bringing the object with me wouldn't also impart a spin I hadn't asked for. Sometimes there wasn't a clean pair. I improvised instead, spreading my body across two structural points and using my hands only to keep the brace from sliding while the real change came from me, all of me, the same way it had with the PAM-D. It worked, mostly. It also meant every test took longer, and every test article that didn't cooperate cost me an extra jump or two just working out how to brace before I ever got to the part where I changed anything.

I called Cory about it one evening, mostly to complain, the way I'd taken to complaining to him about problems that were engineering-shaped even when they weren't strictly suit problems, because he was the only person I knew who found this kind of thing genuinely delightful instead of alarming.

"You're describing a rigging problem," he said, once I'd walked him through it. "Every derelict up there was built for a ground crew and a crane arm, not for somebody showing up in freefall with just two hands. You need a universal attachment point and none of them have one."

"That's exactly the problem. I don't suppose you've got a solution lying around."

"Not for objects that already exist and weren't built with you in mind. That's just going to be slow and annoying forever, unless somebody builds a purpose-made frame that couples your whole body to the load instead of making you improvise contact geometry every time." A pause, and I could practically hear him grinning through the connection. "You're basically doing structural engineering with your bare hands and no instruments. I hope you know how insane that is."

"I'm aware."

"Good. Keep being aware. It's good for you."

By the fourth week I'd stopped thinking of it as testing a superpower and started thinking of it the way I thought about rigging a load on Kristen Station: as an engineering problem with an annoying, specific, structural answer that I kept having to solve fresh every single time, because nobody had built anything up here with me in mind. A Delta-K's tank dome gave me a decent bracing point if I split my hands across two structural ribs. A dead GPS satellite's antenna mount was useless, but the bus underneath it had a service port I could wedge a shoulder against. Every object taught me something new about where engineers of forty years ago had assumed a human being would never need to stand.

I mentioned it to Sterling almost as an aside, at the tail end of a routine call, not because I wanted anything from him, just because it was taking up space in my head and he was the person I talked to most.

"I keep running out of places to brace," I said. "Every test article's a fresh puzzle. I improvise two body-contact points and use my hands to hold the geometry, and it works, but it's slow, and it's not going to scale if I'm ever doing this on something that actually matters instead of some catalog's worth of junk nobody's using anyway."

There was a silence on the line that ran a beat longer than his silences usually ran.

"Sterling?"

"Say that again. The part about improvising the brace."

"I brace across two points instead of one, so the momentum change lands where I need it even without a real center-of-mass fixture. My hands keep the brace indexed; my whole body supplies the change. It works. It's just slow, and it's a new puzzle every time."

Another silence, and when he came back his voice had a different quality to it entirely, careful in a way I hadn't heard from him before, like a man walking toward a conclusion he didn't quite believe he was allowed to reach.

"Cent," he said. "I need you to tell me something, and I need you to tell me straight, because I think I already know the answer and I need to hear you say it instead of guessing at it myself."



Chapter 6: Sterling: Wait, you can do what?

An Ariane stage. A Delta-K. A weather satellite. Each job had stayed in the mental box it arrived in: one dead object, one safer orbit, one line removed from a collision report. Her sentence took the walls off the box.

"Then the frame comes too, as long as the total mass stays on the right side of whatever limit I haven't finished finding."

"A payload frame."

"Sure."

"A pressure hull."

There was a short pause on her end.

I meant every word. I spent the next hour after that call staring at the same spreadsheet, except now it no longer looked like a catalog report. Chapter 7: Cent: Cent! Are you okay?



NASA moved fast once Sterling made the calls, and I mean actually fast, not government fast. It felt like getting hit by a car. Chapter 8: Cent: We've got eleven thousand people at NASA. Let us help.



The room NASA put together to fix my chair, as I came to call it even after everyone else had stopped, had a structural engineer, a biomechanics specialist, a G-force physiologist on loan from the flight surgeon's office, and Cory, who kept trying to introduce himself as "the suit guy" like that undersold what he'd been doing for me for two years. On the satellite bus I had two bad brace points, so the reaction came back through my arms and shoulders before the rest of my body could share it. Past a certain force, the geometry is the bottleneck, not me."

"So the fix isn't making you stronger," said the biomechanics specialist, a soft-spoken man named Delgado who'd spent the first ten minutes of the meeting just watching me move my own shoulder experimentally without saying anything at all. "It's spreading the load so no single joint takes the whole hit."

"That's the whole idea, yeah."

"May I?" Delgado came around the table and, with my permission, walked me through a series of simple motions, arms braced this way, that way, watching where the strain actually traveled. "Your body's already doing something clever without any training for it: bracing through the whole skeleton rather than just the arms, the way a good lifter braces a deadlift. The problem isn't your technique. It's that there's nothing external to brace against except your own joints. Give the load somewhere else to go and you solve most of this without touching your physiology at all."

Osei was already sketching before he'd finished the sentence: a seat braced across the back, hips, thighs, and shoulders, with two short index grips mounted where my hands naturally closed when the rest of me was locked into the structure.

"The handles are indexes, not levers," she said, more to the whiteboard than to any of us. "They tell your hands where the neutral brace is and give you something to close around while the harness and seat carry the actual load. You sit down, your whole body is already coupled to the frame, every time, and the reaction spreads through the seat structure instead of bottlenecking in your arms."

"That solves my point-load problem." I sat up straighter, hearing it. "That's the exact thing I've been fighting for a month. Every test article's a fresh puzzle for how to brace. This fixes my geometry once."

"It also lets us instrument the seat directly," said the physiologist, a brisk, precise woman named Chu who hadn't said much yet but clearly missed nothing. "Real-time load data on you, every push, not just after the fact. We stop reconstructing what happened from bruises and start actually watching it happen."

Cory had been quiet, which for Cory was its own kind of loud, and when he finally spoke it was the other half of the problem, the half that had actually killed the satellite-bus scenario on paper. "Spreading your own load solves your half. It does nothing for the payload's half. You can survive a harder push now. That doesn't mean whatever you're pushing can."

"So don't push harder," I said. "Push smaller, more often."

The room went quiet the way it does when four smart people all reach the same idea within about two seconds of each other.

"Discrete increments," Osei said slowly. "Instead of one change at full effort, a lot of small changes, spaced out. Each one small enough that nothing, payload or you, ever sees a G-spike past what's survivable. You just do it more times."

"How many times, for a given delta-v?"

"Depends on the mass and the target velocity." Chu was already flipping through a small notebook of her own. "But if each kick's small enough, the number doesn't matter, engineering-wise. It's just time."

"I have time," I said. "I don't have a spare shoulder."

That got a laugh, the real kind, and it was the first time the room had felt less like a briefing and more like people actually building something together. We spent the next two hours working out spacing: close enough together that a mission profile didn't take forever, far enough apart that I could self-correct my own drift between each one before the next, which was its own separate skill I'd have to actually practice, not just theorize about. Delgado wanted five seconds, worried about cumulative fatigue on the joints even at reduced load. Chu wanted three, worried more about total mission time than fatigue she was confident the braced seat would already solve. Osei sat between them, sketching load curves for both, until the argument stopped being an argument and became two people converging on the same number from opposite directions.

"Three," Chu said finally. "Provisionally. We test it before either of us gets attached to being right."

"I have time," I said again, "and now apparently I have a research team. This is already better than a month ago."

Truman came in near the end of it, coat still on, having clearly come straight from somewhere else, and stood at the back listening for a few minutes before he said anything.

"How's it looking?"

"Like we solved her half of it," Osei said, flat and certain, meaning it completely. "Braced seat, distributed load, incremental delivery instead of single-shot force. She won't need to improvise her own body position again, and neither the payload nor she will ever see a lethal spike, assuming the numbers hold."

Truman looked at the whiteboard a long moment, at four different specialties' worth of handwriting crowded onto it, at me sitting in a borrowed chair describing a problem that hadn't existed as a category of engineering problem three weeks ago. His jaw unclenched, just slightly.

"This is what I keep trying to tell people," he said, mostly to me, though the room heard it too. "We've got eleven thousand people at NASA, many of them smarter than me and you combined. You come to us with a problem nobody's ever had, and inside three weeks four of them build you a chair for it. Let us help. That's the whole pitch. That's all I ever want anybody to hear when I say it."

"I'm hearing it," I said. "Loud and clear."

"Good." He clapped Osei on the shoulder on his way past, an old, easy gesture that told me this wasn't the first whiteboard he'd walked into like this. "Build her the chair. Then build the load side of it. The satellite bus is not the upper end of this."

Sitting there with four strangers who'd stopped being strangers somewhere in the last two hours, I realized I wasn't going to be improvising braces alone anymore.

* * *

Cory walked me out to the parking lot afterward, in no hurry, hands in his pockets, wearing the particular quiet of a man deciding how to say something.

"You know this changes things for me too," he said eventually. "Two years I've been the only outside expert on 'the Cent effect.' Now I'm one voice in a room of six, and next month it'll probably be a room of twenty."

"Does that bother you?"

"Not as much as I expected it to." He looked up at the flat Houston sky, gray with the particular haze of a city that never quite got a clear night. "I think I spent two years quietly proud of being the only one who understood you, in the ways that mattered for the suits. Watching Osei sketch that chair in about ninety seconds was humbling. It was also the right thing to have happen, and I'd rather be humbled and useful than solitary and slow."

"You're still useful. You've been in every room since this started."

"I intend to stay in every room. I'm just no longer the only chair in it." He bumped my good shoulder, gently, mindful of the other one. "Go home, Cent. Ice the shoulder that's actually still bruised. Let the six of us build you something to sit in before you go finding any more walls."

"Deal."

I jumped home that evening lighter than I'd felt in weeks, not because the problem was solved, exactly, but because it had stopped being only mine to solve. Joe found me in the kitchen, staring at nothing in particular, and asked what I was thinking about.

"Eleven thousand people," I said. "Apparently that's how many NASA's got. I only needed six of them today and it still felt like more help than I've had in my whole life put together."

"Get used to it," he said. "I have a feeling that number's only going to grow on you."



Chapter 9: Cent: The chair was only half the machine.



Osei called me back three days later and erased half her own whiteboard before I had my coat off.

"The chair fixes you," she said. "It doesn't fix the fact that the universe did not standardize its junk for our convenience."

On the board she'd drawn the seat again, this time inside a rectangular frame, and beyond the frame a series of shapes that looked like somebody had emptied a child's block set across the room: cylinders, boxes, trusses, spheres, one lopsided thing I recognized as an old satellite bus.

"Every one of those needs a different place to take load," I said.

"Exactly. If we bolt your seat into one spacecraft, you've got a propulsion system for one spacecraft. If we build a structural spine behind the seat with a common interface, and then build adapters between that interface and whatever load you're moving, you have a propulsion system for anything we can rig safely."

Cory had already filled the far end of the table with hardware: a curved saddle lined with replaceable pads; a split collar that could close around a cylindrical stage; a wide web cradle for structures too fragile to clamp; and a rigid plate with adjustable feet for anything that came with a flat, load-rated surface.

"Universal doesn't mean one magic grabber," he said. "It means one connection on your side and a family of honest attachments on the load side. Same spine, same seat, same sensors. We change the last meter."

"And the handles?"

"Still only for bracing and control," Delgado said. "You close your hands around them so your upper body knows where neutral is. You are not levering the ship with your wrists. The shoulder harness, lap belt, thigh supports, and seatback make you one structural object with the spine. The spine makes you one structural object with the load. Then you change your own momentum and the whole assembly comes with you."

That was the first explanation anyone had given that matched exactly what the PAM-D had felt like. I hadn't pushed the dead stage with my arms. I'd made myself go somewhere else and refused to leave it behind. The chair wasn't an amplifier. It was a way to make the refusal safe and repeatable.

"Build it," I said.

"We are," Osei said. "But you're going to help us decide what fails first."

The engineering room expanded after that. Rigging specialists came over from payload integration. A salvage diver from the Navy spent an afternoon explaining why a clamp that looked secure in air could walk itself loose under repeated load. A robotics engineer brought in the end-effector catalog from three generations of station arms and crossed out almost all of it, because most of those tools were designed to position a load once, gently, not accept dozens of discrete changes in velocity.

Joe sat through the second design review and finally found the thing nobody else in the room had named.

"You're all treating attachment as one event," he said. "Latch it, verify it, fly it. But every kick is another chance for the load to settle differently inside the adapter. The attachment needs to prove it's still good before every kick, not just before the first."

Osei looked at him for a long second, then turned and wrote **VERIFY EVERY CYCLE** across the top of the board.

"That's why he's in the room," I said.

"Apparently," Truman said from the back, where he'd developed a habit of appearing halfway through meetings and listening until somebody forgot he was a general. "Give him a badge before security gets tired of pretending not to notice him."

One of the payload-integration managers suggested qualifying the chair and harness under static load, then leaving the complete dynamic checkout for orbit.

"We can't reproduce weightlessness on the floor," he said. "At some point the first real push through the complete fixture has to happen at Kristen."

"No," Truman said. "That's the wrong way to build something for space operations. This is NASA. We don't do things the wrong way. Ever."

The manager stopped talking.

"You can't reproduce orbit," Truman continued. "You can reproduce the load going through her body. You can test whether the harness shifts, whether the bearing holds, whether the sensors catch a bad path, and whether she can deliver the impulse you ask for when you ask for it. We do all of that before we put her outside a station with eight hundred kilograms of somebody else's hardware."

Osei built the answer into the floor of a test bay. The remediation-chair simulator did not look like a spacecraft. It looked like the chair and structural spine had been set inside a steel cage, with the load yoke coupled to six hydraulic cylinders that disappeared into a reinforced foundation. Accumulators absorbed the impulse when I pushed. Load cells measured the real magnitude, direction, and timing. The computer applied those numbers to whatever virtual payload Sterling's team had loaded into the model and showed us the velocity change I had actually produced, not the one the test expected.

The simulator used the real harness, index grips, bearing, load cells, warning indicators, and verify-every-cycle logic. The load-side cartridges could change stiffness or settle between kicks, imitating the ways a collar, saddle, or web cradle might redistribute force without pretending a ground rig could reproduce every dead satellite in orbit.

My first target was deliberately easy: one small impulse on the centerline, with a broad timing window. The frame took it without moving anything except hydraulic fluid.

"Magnitude high by six percent," Chu said. "Vector is clean."

"Again."

The next one was light. The one after that arrived late. I had spent months learning that repeated kicks stacked; I had not learned to make each one the same on command. The rig gave me that skill a number at a time. Osei narrowed the acceptable magnitude band. Chu narrowed the timing window. Delgado watched how the loads crossed my shoulders, back, hips, and thighs. Cory changed padding. We rotated the bearing, added lateral preload, changed the modeled payload mass, and made the load cartridges settle after a kick so Joe's verify-every-cycle rule had something real to catch.

On the second day the left shoulder strap walked twelve millimeters under repeated off-axis load. A buckle caught a suit hose when I reached for the release. Osei rejected two quick-release designs: one demanded more hand strength than Delgado would guarantee after a long sequence, and one could open under combined lateral load. None of those versions went to orbit.

By the end of the program I could put repeated impulses inside the requested band, on the requested mark, while the simulator changed the load between them. Amber meant I stopped. Red and the alarm meant I released the grips and got out of the sequence. The frame absorbed every push, and the data showed exactly what the payload and my body would have received.

The load adapters had their own stand. Robotic actuators pulled the collar, saddle, and web cradle through repeated axial and off-axis cycles against representative stage sections while the chair simulator supplied measured impulses from my runs. The first flight collar passed its qualification envelope without shifting beyond tolerance.

The first full-size hardware arrived at Kristen Station in pieces on an ordinary commercial cargo launch. That mattered. I did not lift it through the atmosphere, and nobody pretended I could. Rockets did the part rockets were already good at: getting manageable pieces out of Earth's gravity well. Joe and I caught the crates at the station's external cargo cradle, moved them through the inner ring, and spent two days assembling Osei's structural spine beside the aft service lock.

Kristen itself was still what it had always been: one circular station nested inside a larger circular water shell, its rooms partitioned inside the continuous inner ring rather than built as separate modules. The new rig bolted to a service hardpoint on that ring for checkout. When it wasn't in use, the collar, saddle, plate, and web cradle folded into a rack that looked almost disappointingly ordinary.

The chair did not.

Its harness held me from shoulder to thigh. The two index grips moved through only a few millimeters, enough for Osei's sensors to register my intended vector while keeping my hands in a repeatable neutral brace, not enough for anyone to mistake them for levers that physically shoved anything. Load cells ran under every contact surface. A circular bearing behind the seat let the whole chair rotate relative to the structural spine and lock at a measured angle, a feature Osei called "future-proofing" and refused to explain further until she'd tested it.

The chair could not stop me from jumping. Nothing mechanical could. Osei gave it two ways to tell me not to. A warning-level disagreement lit an amber CAUTION indicator directly above the grips. An abort-level reading turned the indicator red and added a cabin alarm nobody could mistake for advice.

"All right," Cory said, floating in front of me with a tablet. "Tell me what hurts."

"Nothing."

"That's not useful."

"It's extremely useful to me."

We spent the afternoon proving the ground fit survived weightlessness. Delgado checked every harness reference against the marks from Houston. Nothing had shifted. Joe found that the loose tail of one strap floated toward a suit-hose fitting when I twisted left, so Cory added a keeper before we took the chair outside. The release worked in every bearing position with my gloves on. Orbit found one problem the simulator couldn't create. It did not find a chair we had never actually used.

By the time Truman called the review, the rig had acquired a formal name nobody used: Variable Geometry Momentum Transfer Fixture. Joe called it the yoke. I called the whole thing the chair.

"Test article?" Truman asked.

Sterling answered from the wall display. "Centaur upper stage, eight hundred ninety kilograms dry, stable orbit, no hazardous residuals, good tracking history. Cylinder gives us a clean first run for the split collar."

"And if the collar slips?"

"She aborts the cycle, separates, and the stage remains in a safe orbit," Osei said. "No improvising a recovery under pressure. We collect data, come home, fix the rig."

Truman looked at me.

"You heard her."

"I heard her."

"Say it back."

"If the hardware starts lying, I don't save the test. I stop, separate, and let Sterling's catalog keep the stage another week."

"Good. We are not proving you can rescue our engineering from its own mistakes. We are proving the engineering deserves to fly with you."



Chapter 10: Cent: The first adapter lasted eleven kicks.



We took the yoke out from Kristen Station on a tether and moved it to the Centaur stage one piece at a time. The chair rode the structural spine. The split collar rode folded behind it. Joe and Cory worked the latches while I held position, and Sterling's team watched every centimeter from the ground.

Once the collar closed around the stage, twelve padded shoes extended inward until each found structure instead of insulation. Green lights came up one after another on Osei's display.

"Attachment verified," she said from Houston. "Load path balanced within two percent. Cent, rotate the chair to zero, lock the bearing, and confirm neutral brace."

I turned the seat until the index mark aligned with the stage's long axis. The bearing locked behind me with a solid mechanical clunk. I settled my shoulders, hips, and thighs into the harness and closed my hands around the index grips.

"Neutral brace."

"Reminder," Delgado said. "Hands are not the force path."

"I know."

"I know you know. Say it anyway."

"Hands index. Harness carries. Spine transfers. Adapter delivers."

"Proceed."

The first kick was so clean I almost laughed. I changed my own velocity and felt the entire assembly, chair, spine, collar, and eight hundred ninety kilograms of dead upper stage, come with me. The reaction spread across my back and hips instead of punching through one shoulder. Three seconds later I corrected my drift and did it again.

At kick six, Joe said, "Pad four's walking."

The number was small, less than a millimeter, and still inside Osei's limit.

"We see it," she said. "Continue at reduced increment."

Kick seven. Pad four moved again. Pad nine took more load to compensate.

Kick eight, nine, ten. The stage's velocity kept building exactly as planned, but the adapter was slowly teaching us that a perfect cylinder drawn on a screen and a forty-year-old rocket stage wrapped in degraded insulation were not the same shape.

Kick eleven landed with a sharp sideways snap.

The collar moved.

Not much. Three centimeters was enough. The stage slewed off-axis, the spine twisted against the chair bearing, and the whole assembly began a slow tumble.

Every useful instinct I had told me to correct it. I knew how. I could feel the new center of mass in the tumble and knew exactly where another change would have to land.

Osei's voice cut across mine before I spoke.

"Abort. Separate the yoke."

I hit the release. The collar opened, the spring arms kicked us clear, and I jumped in place to kill our residual spin. The Centaur continued on the vector eleven kicks had given it, tumbling now but still nowhere near anything that cared.

"I could have caught it," I said.

"I know," Osei answered. No anger, no apology. "That wasn't the test."

Back at Kristen, we found the failure in under an hour and spent nine days fixing it. The collar shoes had measured compression, not shear. They knew how hard they were pressing inward; they didn't know the degraded outer layers were sliding underneath them until the load redistributed far enough to trip the limit.

The second collar had toothed pads that bit only into approved structural zones, shear sensors at every shoe, and a third restraint: a wide web saddle that wrapped the stage and carried any load the hard points couldn't share evenly. Joe's verify-every-cycle rule moved from a note on the whiteboard into software. Before the chair would accept another command, twelve compression readings, twelve shear readings, and the web tension all had to agree that the load path was still the same one we'd started with.

We tested the adapter without me first. A robotic tug pulled it in every direction Osei could justify, then in three directions she couldn't justify but wanted anyway. The first web saddle tore at a stitched junction. The second held but shifted under alternating load. The third used continuous woven bands with no load-bearing seam anywhere near the stage.

Only then did we go back out.

The Centaur was still where Sterling said it would be. We matched its tumble, closed the revised collar around it, tensioned the saddle, and watched every sensor turn green.

"Same mission?" I asked.

"New mission," Truman said. "The first one ended when the hardware failed. This one begins with hardware we've actually earned."

Kick one.

The stage came with me.

Kick eleven.

Every pad held.

Kick twenty, then thirty, each preceded by the same brief sensor vote: compression, shear, tension, geometry. The chair kept the reaction out of my shoulders. The yoke kept the force centered through the stage. Sterling read the new orbit back to us, his voice getting quieter as the number became impossible to mistake.

"Perigee is dropping exactly on the predicted curve. Five more at current increment."

Five more. Then stop.

The Centaur drifted away from us on a clean disposal path, no tumble added, no propellant burned, no improvised handhold anywhere in the operation. It would enter atmosphere days later over an empty stretch of the Pacific, one more large dead object removed from Sterling's board.

Joe floated in front of the chair after we separated and put both hands on the frame, checking it the way he'd checked the tank's lashings three times on the first day.

"How do your shoulders feel?"

"Like I didn't move nine hundred kilos."

"Good."

"How do yours feel?"

"Like I watched you move nine hundred kilos."

That was honest enough that I reached out and pulled him in against the frame until he stopped pretending a systems check was the only reason his hands were shaking.

Houston kept the line open. Nobody made a comment.

Later, Osei sent me a cutaway of the production version. Same chair. Same spine. A catalog of attachments for cylinders, flat-backed payloads, trusses, pressure vessels, and soft-skinned cargo, all of them built around the same rule: fit the load honestly, verify it before every kick, and never ask my hands to do what my whole body was built to do.

The chair had fixed me. The yoke made the fix useful.

At last I could see a straight engineering line from a dead upper stage to a real ship, and nobody on that line had needed to pretend the ship already existed.



Chapter 11: Davy: You don't get to tell me what I can and can't do.



The decommissioned hotel in Los Angeles was quiet in November only by comparison with what it had been built for. The old ballroom still smelled faintly of dust and floor wax. The emptied guest floors carried every closing door farther than it deserved. I'd taken to walking the service corridors and roof most evenings, checking cameras, stairwells, and the locked vehicle entrance, less because anything was actually wrong with them than because the route gave me somewhere to put a worry that had been building for three weeks and hadn't found a proper sentence yet.

I found the sentence that evening coming down the back stairs, watching Cent through the wired-glass kitchen door before she knew I was there: hunched over a tablet at the old banquet table, scrolling through something with NASA's seal in the corner, entirely absorbed, entirely comfortable, the way you get comfortable with a thing only after you've stopped noticing how strange it actually is.

I waited until Millie had put the kettle on before I said anything, because some conversations go better with something to do with your hands, and I'd learned that the hard way, more than once, over more than one kitchen table.

"I want to talk about NASA," I said.

Cent didn't look up from the tablet, still reading whatever engineering update Osei's team had sent that day. "What about them."

"All of it. How deep we've let them in. Three weeks ago it was a debris catalog. Now there's a whiteboard in Houston with your name on a chair design and a general who calls you at home and a man who runs mission crews for the actual government asking what you need built. I want to talk about how far that goes before somebody up that chain decides they'd rather own this than borrow it."

I know what it looks like when an institution decides it owns you instead of trusting you, because I lived it. We ran from exactly that kind of decision made by exactly that kind of person, in a building with exactly that kind of good intentions painted on the outside of it. I still remembered, some nights more than others, exactly what it felt like to realize a file existed on you that you'd never get to read, written by people who'd never met you, deciding things about your life that you'd have no say in at all. Nobody in this family needed the history lesson. They'd all lived versions of it themselves, one way or another.

"They're not the NSA, Davy," Millie said, not sharp, just level, pouring water over tea leaves like the conversation hadn't raised her pulse at all, though I knew her well enough to see that it had. "Sterling's spent two years earning trust the slow way. Truman's spent three weeks building actual hardware instead of asking actual questions. If this were an ownership play, it would look different. It would look like paperwork before help, not the other way around."

"It always looks like help at the start. That's the whole design of it."

"That's the whole design of some of it," Millie said. "Not all of it. You're allowed to have learned the lesson without applying it to everyone who was never in the room that taught it to you."

Cent set the tablet down, finally, and I watched her decide to actually be in this conversation instead of letting it happen around her.

"I hear both of you," she said. "I do. Dad, I know exactly what you're afraid of, and I'm not going to pretend it's not a real thing that happens to real people, because it happened to you, and it almost happened to me, more than once, before I was old enough to understand what almost meant. But I'm not eight. I'm not the girl you had to keep off the grid because a piece of paper with my name on it would've been a leash. I've got a public name. A public station. A public relationship with a general who calls me Cent instead of Space Girl because he decided, on his own, somewhere along the way, that I'd earned that. I already can't go dark the way we did after Daarkon. That door's been closed for two years, and it wasn't NASA that closed it. "No," she agreed. "It doesn't. But it means the choice in front of me isn't 'trust NASA or stay invisible.' That second option's already gone. The actual choice is 'trust NASA, or don't, and either way I'm still the most visible person on the planet who can do what I do.' I'd rather spend that visibility building something real with people who've earned it than spend it hiding from a threat that isn't actually the shape you're picturing."

I wanted to argue with that. I worried at the impulse, because I'd spent most of my adult life being right about dangers like this and I didn't know how to stop being braced for them just because my daughter had grown up believing, reasonably, that the danger had changed shape since my day.

"Tell me what it would actually look like," I said, "if I'm wrong about all of it and you're right. Walk me through it, the way you'd walk an engineer through a spec."

Cent thought about that seriously, which I appreciated more than she probably knew. "It looks like Sterling and Truman getting exactly what they're asking for right now: my time, my ability, my say in how it gets used. It doesn't look like them getting my name on anything I haven't read, or a badge that says I work for them instead of with them, or a version of me that shows up because I'm told to instead of because I decided to. The day any of that changes, I walk. I've walked away from bigger things than a NASA contract."

"And if it changes quietly? The way these things usually do?"

"Then that's exactly what having people watching for it is for," Millie said, before Cent could answer. "That's not a reason to say no to all of it. That's a reason to keep paying attention, which we were always going to do anyway."

"You're grown, and the thing that scared me happened before you existed," I said finally. "I know that. I know you're not her, the girl in the cave, the girl I used to have to hide. I just." I stopped, and made myself say the actual thing instead of the shape of it. "I'm scared of losing you to an institution the way I almost lost myself to one. That's not really about NASA. That's about me."

"I know it is," Cent said, gentler than I deserved. "And I love you for it. But you don't get to tell me what I can and can't do because of something that happened to you before I existed. You get to worry. You get to ask hard questions, same as Mom just did. You don't get veto."

Joe had been quiet the whole time, sitting by the window, still in the way of a man who'd learned, the expensive way, exactly where the line was between raising a concern and trying to own somebody else's decision. He spoke now, careful.

"That's a lesson I had to learn the hard way," he said. "I confused being scared for you with having the right to decide for you, and it almost cost me everything. Your dad's allowed to be afraid. He just can't make the fear your orders. Neither can I."

"I'd feel better if there were some actual boundary drawn somewhere," he said. "Not us pulling back, but you deciding in advance what you're not willing to hand over, so it's your line and not something that gets discovered after the fact because nobody drew it first."

"Give me an example," Cent said. "Concretely. What would you draw the line at?"

Joe thought about it, genuinely, the way he thought about everything technical, working it through before committing to an answer. "Your family's location, obviously, though that's already been off the table since before any of this. Full access to your medical data without you reviewing it first. Any contract language that talks about you in terms of ownership rather than partnership, even in the boring legal boilerplate nobody reads. And a standing right to say no to any specific mission element, even after you've said yes to the program in general."

"That's a good list."

"It's a start," Millie said. "We'll build the rest of it as we go, same as we've built everything else in this family. Nobody expects you to have the whole thing solved at a kitchen table on a Tuesday night."

Cent's shoulders came down half an inch. She'd braced for something with more weight behind it, and this wasn't that.

"That's fair," she said. "That I can do."

Millie set four cups of tea across the old banquet table, the same steady ritual she'd carried through twenty years and more than one kitchen.

"For the record," she said, "I still think it's the right call. Not because I'm not scared too. Because I've watched what Sterling's actually done with two years of chances to betray us, and he hasn't taken a single one of them, and at some point that has to count for something, or none of the last two years of your life have meant anything at all."

I picked up the tea and didn't drink it right away, just held it, warm, the way I'd been holding a version of this fear for longer than my daughter had been alive.

"All right," I said. "I'm still scared. I'm going along with it anyway, because it's not actually my call to veto, and because some part of me has to eventually believe the world's gotten a little bit safer since the last time it tried to eat my family whole." I looked at Cent, at the tablet with the chair design still glowing on it. "Just. Draw the line Joe's talking about. And let me be the one who's still scared, even after you've decided. I've earned that much."

"You have," Cent said. "Always."

We drank the tea. Nobody solved anything else that night, and nothing needed solving further: the actual decision had already been made, the way it always was in this family, by the person it belonged to, with everyone else's fear heard and folded in without being allowed to become a veto. The G-limit per kick had not changed. What had changed was how precisely I could place each kick and how quickly I could correct my own drift between them. We stayed at Europa another five days after the water sample, mostly for Ochoa's sake and partly because nobody wanted to be the one who said it was time to go home when there was still this much left to learn.
