Chapter 21 of 49

Cent POV: Hell yes, I want them.

Truman ran the crew briefing himself, which told me before he said a word how seriously he was taking it: he could have delegated it, and didn't. He'd spent, by his own count, three weeks reviewing personnel files most people in his position would have handed to a subcommittee, because, as he put it when I asked him why, "I've sent people up on my own signature before. I'm not about to start delegating the part where I decide who gets to go somewhere nobody's ever been."

"Grebenchekov, you've already got," he said, sliding the first folder across the table. "He's asked twice now, and I don't think either of us doubts him. Second seat, geologist, flight surgeon: those we'll get to. I want to start with the pair I think you'll like best, because I liked them best the second I saw the file."

The folder had two names in it I recognized instantly, because half the planet recognized them: Commander Robert "Bob" Iverson and Major Rebecca "Beck" Childs, the shuttle pilots who'd brought Endeavour down in the Los Angeles River after a navigation failure corrupted both the orbiter's guidance and the landing beacons, putting them a hundred twenty-nine miles off course before anyone knew it. Childs found the river with two minutes of glide left, before Houston's computers did. Iverson flew them through the bridges and stopped the orbiter in the channel. I'd watched that landing on a screen like everyone else, years back, before any of this was my life yet, and remembered thinking, distantly, that whoever they were, they had to be good.

"Endeavour's instruments told them they were on course while the windows told them they were aimed at downtown Los Angeles," Truman said. "Childs found the only reachable landing geometry and worked it out faster than the ground system. Iverson trusted the answer, committed to it, and flew a shuttle under bridges to make it work. Neither of them has any connection to you or this program: no debt, no rescue, nothing personal. Just two of the best pilots NASA's ever had, available, and exactly the kind of judgment I want on a ship that's going to do things no ship's ever done."

"Hell yes, I want them," I said, before he'd even finished the sentence, and meant every bit of the speed behind it.

"I haven't asked them yet."

"Ask them. I'll take that answer to the bank already."

He laughed, surprised into it, and made a note that I suspected was mostly for show, since I doubted Truman had ever seriously considered not asking them once he'd decided to bring the file to me at all.

The rest of the briefing filled in around them. A planetary geologist named Dr. Priya Nasser, sharp and a little combative in the good way, who spent the first ten minutes of her own introduction interrogating the mission's science objectives instead of her own role in it, which told me everything I needed to know about how seriously she'd take the actual work once we landed.

"I want to be clear about something," Nasser said, cutting off Truman mid-sentence without hesitation. "I'm not coming along to collect a nice bag of rocks for a museum display. I want core samples from at least three geologically distinct formations, and I want enough time on the surface to actually choose them properly instead of grabbing whatever's closest to the ship because somebody's nervous about the clock."

"How much time is enough time?" Truman asked, not defensive, genuinely curious.

"More than anyone's budgeted so far. We'll fight about the actual number later. I just want it on record now that I intend to fight about it."

"Noted," Truman said, and I caught something like real respect crossing his face, the specific respect of a man who preferred people who told him exactly what they wanted over people who nodded along and resented him later.

A flight surgeon, Dr. Reyes, quieter than Alvarez had been but with the same unhurried competence, assigned specifically because eight days each way and a real landing meant real medical risk that couldn't just be monitored from the ground anymore. She said almost nothing during her own introduction beyond confirming her qualifications, and it was only afterward, walking out, that she told me quietly, "I don't need the room to know why I want this. I know why. That's enough."

"Can I ask anyway?"

"My sister died of something a flight surgeon two hours closer could have caught in time. I've spent my whole career trying to be the two hours closer for somebody else, someday. This is about as far from anywhere as a person can be needing that. I'd like to be the reason nobody on this crew finds out what my sister's family found out."

I didn't have anything adequate to say back to that, so I just nodded, and she nodded back, and neither of us needed to make more of it than it already was.

"That's the operating crew," Truman said, once the last folder had gone around the table. "Grebenchekov, Iverson, Childs, Nasser, Reyes, and you."

"And Joe," I said.

Truman's expression didn't change, exactly, but something in it settled, like a decision he'd already half made was just getting confirmed out loud. "Joe's not flying."

"I know he's not flying. I mean Joe's on this somehow. He's been in every room since the chair test, whether anybody gave him a badge for it or not, and I'd rather it be official than have him standing at the back of your control room forever on nobody's authority."

"I noticed him too," Truman said. "Standing there through Grebenchekov's flight, not moving, not needing to be told twice what mattered. I don't have a slot for him on the crew roster. I do have a slot for him in mission command, if he wants it: not running anything, not above anyone, just there, the way he already was, except with an actual chair and a headset and my authorization to speak on the loop when it matters." He looked at me directly. "That's not a favor to you. I've watched enough people crack under this kind of pressure to know what it looks like when someone doesn't, and he didn't, not once, the whole time your life was actually on the line up there."

"He'll take it," I said. "I already know he will."

"Then it's done." Truman closed the last folder and set both hands flat on the table, a small, deliberate gesture that told me the meeting had reached the part he'd been building toward the whole time. "One more name. Mine."

I looked at him. "You're flying."

"The leg kept me out of a shuttle cockpit twenty years ago. This ship does not ask me to work rudder pedals, pull seven G, or climb a launch tower. It asks me to sit in a properly fitted acceleration couch, follow medical limits, and do the job I've spent my whole career training other people to do. I have no honest reason to stay behind except habit, and every reason to believe I've earned the right to go."

"Somebody has to run the room when everything goes wrong."

"Sterling."

Sterling's voice came from the wall speaker. "I have been informed of my promotion from catalog nuisance to the man in the room. I objected for nearly seven seconds."

Truman ignored him. "Seven aboard, counting you. Small enough to work, deep enough to cover what can actually go wrong. Sterling directs from Houston. Joe sits the family console and speaks when the room needs the thing only he knows. I fly."

"You already decided."

"I decided to ask. You're the propulsion system and the person whose trust holds the crew together. I'm asking."

I thought about the way his voice had broken over the comm when the satellite bus hurt me, the way he'd stood in the back of every engineering room without needing to own it, the way he'd spent years sending people toward things he wanted badly enough to go see himself.

"Hell yes," I said. "I want you too."

His smile appeared and was gone almost before anyone could call attention to it.

"Good. Then it's done. This crew is the best thing I've ever gotten to build, and this time I'm going with it. I intend to bring every single one of you home."

Nobody said anything for a moment. It wasn't the kind of thing you answered with a joke, and it wasn't the kind of thing that needed one.

"We know," Grebenchekov said finally, quiet, for all of us. "That is why we said yes."
