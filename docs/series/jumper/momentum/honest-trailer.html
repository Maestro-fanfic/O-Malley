Honest Trailer

Bonus Chapter: The Honest Trailer

A screen lit somewhere outside continuity. A voice dropped into the register reserved for impossible machinery, preventable government conspiracies, and teenagers being asked to solve both.

“In a world where one family has already put up with far more shit than their powers are worth…”

A second voice cut in, brighter and much too pleased with itself.

“…one teenage girl will discover something exciting and new!”

Silence.

“No,” said the first voice. “She’ll discover something that is neither exciting nor new.”

“I was trying to sell it.”

“She’s had enough sold to her. She’s at the point where she’d slam the door on Girl Scout cookies.”

The screen showed Cent Rice, suited and tethered outside Kristen Station, watching four hundred kilograms of water tank drift toward Joe’s solar array.

“She can push things.”

A beat.

“No, seriously. That’s the breakthrough.”

This is Momentum.

Watch as Cent saves the station by doing what every engineer dreams of doing after the seventh design review: putting both hands on the problem and shoving it somewhere else.

Then watch one push become a free reactionless drive in a series of steps whose whole premise defies the word reasonable to its face.

Because in the Rice family, scope creep travels at interplanetary velocity.

Witness Cent’s new power meet the most formidable force in the known universe: NASA certification.

“Eleven thousand people turn ‘girl pushes spaceship’ into adapters, load paths, mass limits, a gimbaled chair, medical tolerances…”

The voice began to fade beneath the accelerating list.

“…structural models, redundant indicators, abort criteria…”

By the end, it was barely audible.

“…and a full operational vocabulary for the precise moment when the chair says green and Cent’s body says absolutely not.”

“The first adapter lasted eleven kicks,” the narrator observed. “Which is both a successful engineering test and a deeply upsetting sentence when the adapter is attached to a person.”

Meet General Sterling, who begins this story tracking orbital debris and ends it helping plan a voyage to Europa, all because he once asked the question that functions as this trilogy’s official motto:

“Wait, you can do what?”

Meet President Holcomb, who responds to the existence of a privately owned, teleporting, reactionless space program by asking what the government owes the girl doing the work.

Then watch everyone discover how many people in Washington heard an entirely different question.

Enter Devereaux, an unelected security official who decides the best way to protect America from a family that can teleport through any wall is to threaten that family from inside a room with walls.

“Bold strategy,” the narrator said. “Let’s see if the man who can appear behind you agrees.”

See Davy deploy decades of trauma, paranoia, and extremely justified pattern recognition. See Cent insist that Dad is fine, the phrase every daughter says when Dad is anything but fine. And see Millie, therapist, mother, survivor, resolve a federal conspiracy using the evidence-based intervention known as:

“Millie’s got a gun.”

The screen held on the aftermath longer than the joke required: Millie in custody, Cent far from home, Davy alive but shaken, and a family discovering that preventing the worst outcome did not make what happened simple.

The narrator’s voice lowered.

“This is where the trailer usually makes the gun the punch line. The book doesn’t. It lets Millie’s choice remain frightening, necessary, arguable, and hers. We noticed. We’re leaving it that way.”

The reel moved on.

Strap into a spacecraft powered by repeated tiny kicks—

“I mean, I feel like I should go get a ‘cup’ before even opening this book,” the other voice said.

—as Cent and a crew of professionals travel to Mars, then Europa, while Dan Truman turns every impossible maneuver into the world’s tensest go/no-go poll.

Meet Childs and Iverson, pilots who have survived everything from bad engines to a cement culvert that people call, for some weird reason, the LA River. Their professional philosophy is simple: the stars outrank the instruments, Los Angeles is not a runway, and green is not a suggestion.

Travel through radiation, cold, orbital mechanics, and one pressure vessel that learns the hard way that “person-shaped opening to an alien ocean” is not the same thing as “faucet.”

The forward cap departed the experiment at artillery speed.

“Who could have predicted this?”

David Rice raised one hand.

“Right. David predicted this. Put his name on the replacement.”

Marvel as the second chamber holds and actual water from Europa crosses a Cent-shaped gap for thirty seconds.

Various people imagine new… stuff, and the President imagines a price tag that weighs more than Europa does. Cent pretends to imagine a validation campaign, and fails miserably.

## Spot the Moment This Stopped Being a Test

The rules are simple: every time somebody insists they are only testing one small thing, put a marker on the next permanent piece of space infrastructure that appears because of it.

Possible starting points include the water tank, the first adapter, the gimbaled chair, the Mars vehicle, and the Europa chamber.

Anyone who chooses Europa is eliminated for failing to recognize scope creep at interplanetary distances.

Anyone who chooses the chair must explain why eleven thousand people were needed for something that was supposedly not a program yet.

Anyone who chooses the water tank is technically closest, but still wrong.

The correct answer is the instant Cent pushed back.

There is no prize. The prize money has been reassigned to the next validation campaign Cent is pretending not to plan.

## Starring

Millicent Rice as Space Girl and the Chamber of OSHA Compliance.

David Rice as Taken: Teleporter Dad Edition.

Millie Rice as Good Will Hunting Rifle.

Joe as The Boyfriend Who Understands “I’m Going to Europa” Is Not a Breakup Line.

General Sterling as Mission Control’s Emotional Support General.

President Holcomb as The President from Armageddon, Once Again Discovering That Giving the Government a Button Was a Mistake.

Dan Truman as Harry Stamper’s Competent Former Boss, Somehow Making All of This Less Weird.

Osei as Load-Bearing Common Sense.

Childs and Iverson as The Pilots Who Read the Checklist After Physics Leaves the Chat.

Devereaux as The Ghost Who Forgot Teleporters Can Check Behind Him.

And Europa’s subsurface ocean as Coming Soon to a Reservoir Near You, Pending Twelve More Validation Campaigns.

The alternate titles appeared on the screen:

NASA’s Punch-Drive.

The Fast and the Methodologically Cautious.

How I Learned to Stop Worrying and Love the Go/No-Go Poll.

The narrator considered them.

“No. The book already found the right one.”

The title card landed.

# MOMENTUM

Directed by: Newton’s Third Law, under protest.

Written by: a test plan that kept becoming a mission architecture.

Propulsion supplied by: one teenager, one chair, and no scientifically acceptable explanation whatsoever.

Special thanks to: every engineer who saw a miracle and responded by asking for the load-cell data.

The screen went dark, then came back once more on Sterling calling Cent late at night.

“So,” the narrator said, “she can push a ship across the solar system now. How far does that go?”

Cent looked past Earth toward the stars.

“Ask me again sometime.”

The narrator sighed.

“Oh no.”
