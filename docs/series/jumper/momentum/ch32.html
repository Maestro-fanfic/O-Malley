Chapter 32 of 49

Cent POV: So he's a Ghost, then.

It was the questions that tipped me off, not any one of them, but the pattern underneath all of them.

A contractor auditing the chair's fixture wanted to know exactly how many increments it took to reach cruising velocity, phrased like idle curiosity, the kind of thing you'd ask if you were simply impressed by the engineering. A visiting engineer from a subcontractor I'd never heard of asked, almost in passing, whether my ability worked the same regardless of who I was touching, or whether it was specific to objects rather than people, a question that had no obvious bearing on anything his subcontract actually covered. A woman introduced to me as a program liaison, whose actual role nobody in the room seemed able to name precisely when I asked afterward, wanted to know how long I could sustain repeated pushes before needing real rest, and whether that limit was physical or something else, phrased so casually I almost answered before noticing how specific it was. A fourth conversation, weeks later, with a man who described himself only as "on loan from another agency" and never elaborated, asked whether anyone besides me in my family could do what I did, and whether that ability had ever been formally tested rather than just observed.

None of them asked anything a single conversation would flag as suspicious. That was the whole design of it, I understood eventually: spread it thin enough, across enough different faces and different plausible reasons to be in the room, that no individual moment ever crossed a line worth reporting. It was only in aggregate, once I started keeping a mental tally the way Sterling kept his catalog, that the shape of it stopped looking like curiosity and started looking like a dossier being built one polite question at a time.

I mentioned it to Dad the way I mentioned most things that worried me lately, at the kitchen table, over tea neither of us was really drinking, and watched his face go hard and cold: not surprised, not panicked, focused in the particular way it got when fear had finished turning into a problem he could work.

"I think somebody's compiling a profile on me," I said. "Not NASA. Somebody using NASA's own access to get close enough to ask, without ever asking anything that would look wrong written down by itself."

I watched him go still the way he did whenever something reached backward into the part of his life he almost never talked about.

"Names," he said. "Give me every name."

I gave him what I had, which wasn't much: first names, mostly, a couple of company affiliations that turned out, when he checked, to be real companies with real contracts and no obvious reason to be asking what their people had been asking.

"This is exactly how it starts," he said, and I heard years of running from something living underneath those six words. "Not a raid. Not a warrant. Just questions, spread out, quiet, until somebody who was never supposed to be watching knows more about you than you'd ever have told them on purpose."

Mom came in partway through, caught the shape of the conversation fast, and started where she usually started: measured, process-first. "Let's not assume the worst shape before we know it's the worst shape. Could be ordinary due diligence on an unprecedented program. Could be a dozen boring explanations."

"Could be," Dad said. "Could also be exactly what it looks like."

I watched Mom's face change when the measured version stopped being enough. It wasn't fast or dramatic. Her expression simply lost all its qualifications. I'd seen it before in the warehouse outside New Prospect, when she jumped in with a red aluminum bat already moving.

"Then we find out which," she said. "Today. Not next week."

We could still vanish. That option never went away. But there was no version of it that meant changing hotels and waiting for the danger to pass. We would have to abandon everything: NASA, Joe, Kristen Station, and Grandmother with it. We wouldn't abandon Grandmother a second time. We called Hunt.

He picked up on the first ring, which he never did, and I heard something shift in his voice the moment Dad laid out what I'd noticed.

"Shit," Hunt said, plain, no performance in it. "Let me put some feelers out. This feels all kinds of wrong, and I don't like that I hadn't already caught it myself."

He called back twice in the first two days with nothing but process: names he was running, doors that weren't opening the way they usually did, a first hint that whoever this was, they'd already thought to make Hunt's own usual channels harder to use than normal.

"Somebody's been quietly making my life difficult for about six months," he told us, on the second call, an edge of genuine surprise in his voice that told me this wasn't performance. "I assumed it was routine bureaucratic friction. I did not assume it was connected to you until about an hour ago, when I tried to pull a personnel record that should have been a five-minute request and got stonewalled by someone who used exactly the kind of vague, procedural language people use when they've been told to stonewall without being told why."

It took him four days total to come back with anything conclusive, and what he came back with wasn't an answer so much as the shape of one.

"Nothing concrete," he said, on a call all three of us sat in on. "No name I can hand you, no organization I can point at and say there. But there's chatter: the kind that tells you somebody real is asking real questions upstream of the people I'd normally lean on for answers, which usually means whoever's asking outranks my usual sources, or scares them, or both."

"Who," Dad said.

"I don't know. Whoever it is hasn't left a name anywhere I can reach. Could be one person. Could be a committee that only feels like one person because nobody on it disagrees with the loudest voice in the room. All I've got is the outline of somebody circling, patient, careful, the exact kind of careful that means they've done this before and know not to leave prints."

"So he's a ghost," Dad said, half to himself, the words coming out before he'd decided to say them.

"No idea who he is," Hunt said, and I heard, even over the phone, something almost like a dry, tired amusement under the words. "So he's a Ghost, then. Capital G. Feels about right."

"Feels about right," Dad agreed, and it wasn't a joke either of them was actually enjoying, just a name for a shape neither of them could see clearly yet, which was, I was starting to understand, somehow worse than a shape you could.

Mom reached across the table and put her hand over mine, the way she used to when I was small enough that a hand over mine was the whole solution to whatever was wrong.

"We watch," she said. "All of us. And if he shows himself for real, he'll find out exactly what happens when he does."
