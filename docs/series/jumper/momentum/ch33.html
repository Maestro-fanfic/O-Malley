Chapter 33 of 49

Cent POV: I've done this before.

Launch day didn't feel like launch day. That was the strangest part.

The actual launches had happened over the preceding month: three heavy-lift boosters for the carrier sections, two cargo flights for the landing capsule, battery cassettes, and supplies, every kilogram climbing out of Earth's gravity well on chemical fire because neither the ship nor I could muscle that mass off the ground. NASA sent trained orbital-assembly crews up with the sections. They built the carrier beside Kristen under Osei's direction. Iverson, Childs, and the mission specialists joined the formal checkout; Joe worked the integration console on the ground; I fetched tools and stayed out of the way whenever a task required an EVA qualification. The completed ship stayed on Kristen's aft tether through pressure tests, life-support trials, and one complete simulated abort.

The electrical checkout took its own week. Load banks stood in for every normal and peak demand before the cabin used any of it. Osei made the controller lose an entire battery bank, transfer the survival bus to the other, isolate a failed cassette, and accept a charged replacement while every life-support load remained live. Momentum did not leave the tether until either bank could keep everyone alive and a suited crew member could complete an exchange without an unstable bus or an exposed connector.

Once the flight capsule reached Kristen, we tested the actual gimbal on the station's aft tether with ballast in every crew and cargo position. Childs called changing correction vectors from the navigation console. The chair moved me in pitch and yaw, locked, waited for both systems, and released a series of low-energy kicks into the tethered load. The last run repeated the downstream encoder fault from the ground program. Yellow held. I kept still. Then they cleared it, the chair found the new vector, and green came up.

The carrier's flight chair got its own acceptance run. We loaded the ship to its departure mass distribution, moved both gimbal axes through the complete insertion schedule, and introduced every hold the simulator program had taught us to recognize. I made low-energy kicks on fore, aft, and trimmed vectors while the tether took the accumulated change. The scheduler, both position votes, both sets of lock sensors, and the load-path model all had to agree before each green. When the test director delayed the trim-axis witness switch that had exposed the display problem in Houston, yellow cleared, amber came on, and green stayed dark.

So there was no countdown in the old sense on our departure day, no fire under a pad, no crowd holding its breath at a fence line miles away. There was a ship already in orbit and seven of us strapped into seats we'd trained in for months. I settled into the fixture with my whole body locked into the harness and my hands closed around the index grips. The grips gave me the brace. The chair and load spine carried the change.

Sterling completed the ground poll, then handed the ship to Truman.

"I need a go/no-go for boost," Truman said.

"Guidance, go," Iverson said.

"Navigation, go," Childs said.

"Vehicle, go," Grebenchekov said.

"Medical, go," Reyes said.

"Payload, go," Nasser said.

I checked the fixture status. The sensor vote was clean. The yellow SEQUENCE WAIT light was on.

"Momentum, go."

"Command, go," Truman said. "Flight, ship is go for boost."

"Copy go for boost," Sterling said. "Release aft tether."

The clamps opened. Kristen began to drift away.

Iverson called attitude and velocity from the pilot's station while Childs cross-checked the independent navigation solution. There was nothing for either of them to steer during the kick sequence, but there was plenty for them to catch if the ship stopped doing what the numbers said.

The sensor vote stayed clean. Yellow stayed on until the scheduler had the first kick sequenced. Only when both systems were ready did yellow go dark and the green GO light come on. Push. Correct my own drift. The next green came a fraction early, the one after that a fraction late, the scheduler keeping my average pace while refusing to strike the carrier like a bell and the sensor vote checking the load path before every one. By the fortieth kick we were up to speed, and the burn, if you could call something with no flame a burn, cut off clean, and Childs, beside Iverson, said the only thing anybody said for a long moment afterward.

"Well. That's the weirdest ascent profile I've ever flown, and I've flown some weird ones."

"Get used to it," I said. "This is the boring part. The next eight days are going to be a lot more boring than this."

They were, mostly, and everybody had told the truth about that being fine. The first day of the coast felt like the longest of my life, in the strange way that stretches of enforced idleness always run longer than stretches of real danger; I kept finding myself drifting toward the fixture out of habit, hands halfway to the grips, before remembering there was nothing to push for the better part of a week. Iverson caught me doing it twice and, the second time, gently steered me toward the galley instead.

"You're allowed to just be a passenger for a while," he said. "That's not a demotion. Childs and I fly a hundred hours of perfectly ordinary transit for every five minutes anybody remembers afterward. Boring is the job, most of the time. You'll get used to it."

"I've never been the boring part of anything before."

"Get used to that too. It suits you better than you'd think."

By the second day the seven of us had settled into something like a rhythm anyway. Nasser spent the coast cataloging every scrap of data anyone had ever gathered on Mars's geology and arguing happily with anyone who'd argue back about which of it was wrong. Reyes ran a light medical schedule, checkups more than treatment. Truman discovered that being a passenger in his own program made him reorganize storage lockers nobody had asked him to touch. Grebenchekov and I talked more in five days than we had in the two years before, easy with each other the way two people get once they've each been genuinely afraid for the other and come out the other side of it still friends. Joe's voice came through from mission control twice a day, steady beside Sterling on the family console, not needing to say much to be worth hearing.

It was day two when Nasser doubled over at the galley station, quiet at first, the kind of quiet that made it worse when Reyes' face changed color reading her own instruments a minute later.

"Acute abdominal pain, right lower quadrant, fever climbing." Reyes' voice had gone flat and fast, professional in the way that meant she was scared and choosing not to sound it. "I can't rule out appendicitis from here, and if it is, this ship doesn't have what she needs. I can manage pain and infection for a while. I cannot do surgery."

Iverson and Childs were needed to fly the ship if anything else went wrong during the time this would take, and nobody was volunteering Grebenchekov's repaired aorta for an emergency medical jump unless there was no other choice. That left the math simple, once anyone said it out loud.

"I'll take her back," I said. "Earth, real surgeons. Mission control already has the medical contingency."

Sterling answered before anyone else could. "Affirmative. Contingency Alpha Medical. Secure surgical team is standing by at the program clinic on Johnson grounds. Cent, you go directly to the marked receiving bay. Dr. Nolan has the room, blood, imaging, and two surgical teams ready. Do not improvise a hospital."

"You don't have live tracking on this ship," Iverson said. "We're two days and roughly forty-five million kilometers into an eight-day coast. How do you find your way back to a moving target with no updated fix?"

"The same way I've always found Kristen Station," I said. "I don't need a fix. I know this ship. I've flown it, lived in it, touched every surface of it for months. I don't chase a set of coordinates. I go to a place I know, the same way I'd go home. It's moving. So am I, relative to it, the second I leave. Doesn't matter. I've done this before."

I took Nasser's hand, felt her flinch at the pain even through the grip, and jumped.

Earth resolved around us inside a cleared receiving bay at Johnson, not a parking lot and not some hospital forced to invent security and space medicine while a patient was already failing. Nolan's team had the first scan running inside four minutes. Appendicitis, complicated enough that the surgeon refused to promise a simple recovery, out before it ruptured.

Nasser woke angry.

"How long."

Nolan did not offer her the comforting version. "Six weeks before I'd consider ordinary strenuous fieldwork. Longer before I clear you for repeated jumps, an interplanetary capsule, and a Mars surface operation. You're off this flight."

"The landing is in six days."

"And you will still be healing when they come home."

NASA had planned for exactly the kind of sentence nobody wanted to hear. Every crew position except mine had two qualified alternates who had trained the full mission profile and stayed medically ready through departure. Dr. Lena Ochoa, the first geology standby, was already in quarantine with her flight bag packed before Nasser came out of anesthesia.

"I hate this," Nasser told her when they spoke, voice thin from pain and medication.

"I know."

"Bring back cores from three distinct formations. Don't let Truman trade surface time for a prettier press schedule. The layered site in sector seven is worth the walk."

Ochoa listened without pretending the handoff made either of them feel better. "I'll bring you the cores."

Nasser looked at me. "Go. If you stand here feeling guilty, I'll find a way to make recovery take longer out of spite."

I left her with Nolan and made the first scheduled power rotation while I was already home. Kristen's crew released one charged cassette to me. I carried it to Momentum by itself, waited while Grebenchekov isolated the depleted cassette and locked the replacement into the open bank, then took the depleted one back to Kristen for recharge. The other bank carried life support through the entire exchange.

Then I returned to Johnson for Ochoa. I took her hand and jumped back to the carrier. The pallet of replacement medical consumables came on a separate trip; neither one of us pretended my mass limit stopped mattering because the schedule had become inconvenient. I didn't have live coordinates. I didn't need them. I knew the ship the way you know your own house in the dark: its air, its surfaces, the exact spatial fact of it. Distance wasn't the thing I was solving.

Ochoa arrived in the galley while Iverson was halfway through explaining to Truman that no, reorganizing the emergency food by flavor was not a flight-director function.

"Dr. Lena Ochoa," she said. "I was told somebody ordered geology."

The laugh that went through the cabin carried more relief than humor.

Nasser would not be back this mission. That mattered, and nobody tried to soften it into a two-day inconvenience. Ochoa took her station, her objectives, and the promise she'd made. The coast continued with seven aboard, one name changed and one person on Earth beginning a recovery that would take longer than the mission itself.
