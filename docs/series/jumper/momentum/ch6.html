Chapter 6 of 49

Sterling: Wait, you can do what?

I was still on the call from the end of her test report, the cumulative-delta-v spreadsheet open beside the catalog. I had watched every target move. I had supplied the coordinates, listened through the kickback, and read the new orbits myself. What I had not done, until she complained about the lack of a repeatable brace, was stop thinking in catalog-sized pieces.

An Ariane stage. A Delta-K. A weather satellite. Each job had stayed in the mental box it arrived in: one dead object, one safer orbit, one line removed from a collision report. Her sentence took the walls off the box.

I looked at the total velocity she had built from small repeated changes. Then I looked at the mass column. Neither number knew or cared that the target was supposed to be junk.

"Say that again," I told her. "The part about what your hands actually do."

"They keep the brace indexed. They don't supply the change. I change my own momentum with my whole body coupled to the object, and the object comes with me."

"So if the brace were not improvised. If somebody built a frame around you and attached that frame to a load."

"Then the frame comes too, as long as the total mass stays on the right side of whatever limit I haven't finished finding."

"A payload frame."

"Sure."

"A pressure hull."

There was a short pause on her end.

"That's the idea," she said. "Not another satellite. A ship. People, cargo, life support, whatever we can keep under the limit and accelerate in small enough increments that nobody aboard gets hurt."

I heard myself ask the next question before I'd finished deciding to ask it. It came out with none of the composure I generally prided myself on maintaining across four decades of a career built on not sounding surprised on an open line.

"Wait, you can do what?"

"Not yet," she said, which was exactly the answer that made me believe the rest. "Right now I can move dead hardware. I need a safe brace, a way to keep every kick survivable, and a real upper mass limit. But if those problems have engineering answers, then yes. I think I can move a ship."

I cleared my throat, the long way, the way that usually bought me two extra seconds to arrange a response that sounded like I'd expected the answer. It didn't work this time. I didn't have a response arranged. I had thirty years of orbital mechanics telling me what spacecraft propulsion cost, and a young woman telling me the expensive part might now be a chair and enough patience.

I caught myself clucking my tongue against the roof of my mouth, an old habit from four decades of listening to briefings I didn't yet know how to feel about, and made myself stop.

"How far?" I asked.

"For the ship? I don't know yet."

"For you."

"That's different."

"I understand that. How far have you gone?"

"I've already been to Mars," she said, like it was nothing, like it was a weekend trip she'd mentioned once and assumed I remembered. "Seen the Melas Chasma dunes. Brought back samples. I can go back whenever I like."

I have given testimony to Congress. I have briefed three-star generals on threat assessments that would keep most civilians up at night. I have never, in my professional life, heard myself say what I said next, and I said it with absolutely no dignity whatsoever.

"Wait, you have actual samples? WE WANT SOME!"

She laughed, a real laugh, and for a second the whole conversation felt almost normal again, two people talking, not a man restructuring his entire understanding of what was operationally possible in low Earth orbit while on the phone. Then her voice came back level, and pulled the conversation back to where it actually needed to go.

"But that's just me," she said. "One person, going somewhere I've already stood. That's not the interesting part, not anymore. We need to move things that aren't me: people, cargo, real mass. Things that have to get there under their own momentum, because they can't jump there themselves the way I can."

I cleared my throat again, and this time it did what it was supposed to do, gave me the two seconds I needed to say something that sounded like a general and not like a man who'd just been handed the answer to a problem humanity had been chemically brute-forcing since 1957.

"What do you need?" I said. "Whatever it is. We'll build every bit of it."

I meant every word. I spent the next hour after that call staring at the same spreadsheet, except now it no longer looked like a catalog report. It looked like the first line of a mission ledger, and I was already doing the quiet math on how fast I could get the right people into a room without anybody outside that room understanding why it mattered yet.

I picked up the phone again. This time I called Truman.

"This had better be good," Truman said, by way of answering. "I'm between two meetings and a bathroom break I've been putting off since lunch."

"It's good. It's also going to sound insane, and I need you to hear the whole thing before you decide whether to believe me."

"You've never once called me sounding like this. Go ahead."

I told him. The velocity changes. The PAM-D. The tank. The catalog tests we had both already treated as debris work, and the simple fact that nothing in the numbers required the next load to be debris. Then the proposed frame, the survivable increments, and Mars. Truman didn't interrupt once, which told me more than anything he could have said, because Truman interrupted everything, as a matter of professional habit, the same way I cleared my throat.

When I finished, there was a silence on his end long enough that I checked the connection.

"Say the last part again," he said finally. "The part about moving things that aren't her."

"People, cargo, real mass. Her exact words. She's already solved personal transport. This is about everything else."

"Sterling." His voice had gone very quiet, very careful, the voice I imagined he used on people right before he told them something that would change the rest of their careers. "Do you understand what you're describing to me?"

"I've had weeks with the test numbers and about forty minutes with what they imply. I'm not sure I fully understand it yet. I understand enough to know it's real, and I understand enough to know we can't sit on it."

"No," he agreed. "We can't." I heard him exhale, long, the sound of a man recalibrating an entire career's worth of assumptions in real time. "I've spent thirty years watching this country throw obscene amounts of money and worse amounts of grief at the simple problem of getting mass from one place to another against a gravity well. If what you're telling me is real, that problem just stopped being the problem."

"That's what I keep arriving at too."

"Who else knows?"

"Just us. And her, obviously. And whoever she's told at home, which I haven't asked."

"Keep it that way for now. Not because I want to hide it. Because I want the first room this goes into to be a room full of people who can actually do something useful with it, not a room full of people who panic first and think second." A pause. "Can you get her to Houston? Or do I need to come to her?"

"I think," I said, "given everything, it's probably going to be a lot of both, for a long time."

"Good," Truman said, decision settling into his voice, the sound of a man who'd just found the thing he'd apparently been waiting his whole career to be handed. "Set it up. Whatever she needs. What you already told her was right. We'll build every bit of it."
