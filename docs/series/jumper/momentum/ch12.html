Chapter 12 of 49

Holcomb: Before we ask what she can do for us, we decide what we owe her.

The first briefing reached me at 9:10 on a Tuesday morning in a folder thick enough to stop a small-caliber round and still somehow missing the only answer I wanted.

"Start over," I told Truman. "No equations. No career-protecting language. Tell me what is true."

We were in the Oval, not the Situation Room, because I wanted daylight for this one and because I wanted to watch the faces around me without a wall of screens doing half the thinking for us. Truman sat nearest the desk. Sterling was beside him with the posture of a man who had been awake for two days and intended to call that a scheduling choice. Around them: Halloran, my National Security Advisor; the Chief of Staff; the Secretary of Defense; the Chairman of the Joint Chiefs; the Director of National Intelligence; and Special Agent Baines, who ran protective-intelligence coordination for the Secret Service and had already marked six pages of the folder with yellow tabs.

Truman started again.

"Cent Rice can alter her own momentum without reaction mass. When she braces her entire body to a load, she can bring that load with her. NASA has instrumented the effect, repeated it, found a dangerous upper bound, and engineered around that bound with a full-body fixture and incremental kicks. She has independently visited Mars and returned. We have not flown the crewed ship. We have not even finished qualifying the complete system. What we have is a real capability, a validated method, and a partner who can walk away whenever she chooses."

"Partner," I said.

"Yes, sir."

"Not asset."

The DNI shifted once in his chair. Nobody else moved.

"Not asset," Truman said again.

Sterling walked me through the evidence. The tank. The catalog. The dead upper stage moved without fuel. The chair. The adapter failure and the second design that held. He did not oversell any of it. Every time the story approached the edge of what they'd actually proven, he stopped there and named the edge, which made me trust the rest more than a polished certainty would have.

"How much of that did Ms. Rice do for the first time in orbit?" I asked.

"Since NASA began engineering the system? Nothing we could reproduce safely on the ground," Truman said. "Every step is rehearsed. Every device we build is physically tested and simulated, as close to perfect as we can get it before we ask her to trust it in space. The chair simulator takes her real pushes and measures her precision and timing. The adapter stand applies those measured loads to the flight hardware. We test the warnings, the stops, and the failures, not only the clean run. Orbit is acceptance under conditions the ground cannot reproduce. It is not where we begin learning how the machine works."

"The first collar still failed."

"Yes, sir. It passed its ground envelope. The qualification article did not reproduce the degraded outer layers of a stage that had spent decades in orbit. The collar slipped after eleven kicks. The sensors caught the changing load, Ms. Rice stopped instead of trying to save the test, and nobody was hurt. They brought it home, added shear sensing and a load-carrying saddle, and qualified the new design before flying it." Truman held my eyes. "As close to perfect as we can get does not mean claiming perfection. It means a failure becomes data instead of a casualty."

"We've had eleven thousand people available to work that exact problem this whole time, sir," Truman said.

"Consequences," I said.

The Chairman answered first. "Strategic lift changes. Orbital logistics changes. Eventually deterrence changes, though not tomorrow and not cleanly. If she can move a civilian ship, she can move other mass. Every government with a competent intelligence service will reach that conclusion whether we announce it or not."

"Scientific," Truman said. "Mars stops being a generational project and becomes an engineering project. Same for targets beyond it, within the limits we still haven't found."

"Economic," the Chief of Staff added. "Anything that depends on launch cost gets rewritten. Not erased, because hardware still has to reach orbit conventionally in manageable pieces, but rewritten."

"Threat," I said, looking at Baines.

She opened to her first yellow tab. "Right now Ms. Rice has public visibility, a known business, a known station, and family members whose identities are not difficult to establish. Once the capability is understood, we should assume foreign intelligence interest, commercial coercion, kidnapping attempts, technical surveillance, insider collection, and ordinary unstable-person attention amplified by publicity. The threat is not hypothetical. The only uncertainty is timing."

"What are we doing about it?"

"At present? NASA badge controls and the family's own habits."

"That's not an answer."

"No, sir. It isn't." She met my eyes without blinking. "My recommendation is a twenty-four-hour protective-intelligence cell, advance screening for every facility she enters, a small rotating physical detail, secure transport available whether she uses it or not, compartmented medical and family-location records, and direct reporting to my office on any insider collection pattern. The detail would protect, not confine. She retains freedom of movement."

The DNI gave a small, humorless breath. "Freedom of movement is a complicated phrase when the protectee can disappear between one camera frame and the next."

"Then the detail learns humility," Baines said. "We do not solve a protection problem by pretending we can physically contain the person being protected."

That was the first completely useful sentence anybody said all morning.

"And the family?" I asked.

"Covered if they consent. Monitored threat environment whether they consent or not. We can watch a threat without putting an agent in their kitchen."

Halloran leaned forward. "We need to settle the policy before she enters the room. If we improvise in front of her, every disagreement will sound like the government deciding whether it owns her."

"It doesn't," I said.

The DNI looked at me. "Mr. President, no one is proposing ownership."

"Someone will. Maybe not in this room and maybe not using that word. Someone will call it continuity of government, national necessity, emergency authority, or a duty to prevent adversary access. So let's decide now, while nobody is frightened enough to dress coercion up as prudence."

I stood and went to the windows. It was a gray Washington morning, the lawn bright in the flat way winter lawns are bright, every bare branch visible.

"Here's the decision," I said. "We protect her. We resource the work. We ask for partnership and make partnership worth choosing. We do not direct her body, her ability, or her family. We do not hide ownership language in a contract and call it boilerplate. We do not collect medical information she has not seen. We do not turn physical protection into surveillance of the protectee. And we build an actual process for her to say no after she has said yes to the program."

"That leaves us with no leverage if her interests diverge from ours," the DNI said.

"Correct."

"Sir..."

"Our leverage is that we behave like people she has reason to keep working with. If you think that's inadequate, improve our behavior."

The Chairman covered a cough that might have been a laugh.

The DNI did not.

"Due diligence still requires a plan for the version where partnership fails," he said.

"Then draft one," I said. "Quietly, with Counsel attached and no operational authority hidden inside it. It covers continuity of judgment if her cooperation ends. It does not create permission to coerce her, track her family, or act without a separate presidential finding. The point is to keep frightened people from improvising later, not to give ambitious people a menu now."

I looked around the room, one face at a time.

"That file exists to constrain this government. Anyone who treats it as authorization will be acting against my order. Put that sentence on the cover."

The DNI nodded. So did Devereaux, still taking notes along the wall.

I looked at Truman. "Is she prepared to tell us no in person?"

"More prepared than anyone you've met."

"Good. Bring her in. I want the conditions from her, not a cleaned-up version drafted by people who think plain speech needs legal counsel."

As the room began to move, Baines stayed seated.

"One warning, sir. Her father will hear 'protective detail' as 'government personnel close enough to seize my daughter.' His history makes that a rational response, even if the conclusion is wrong."

"Then we'll explain it to him."

"He may not wait to be invited."

"General Truman has spent the last hour explaining that this family doesn't wait for transportation. We'll keep the afternoon open."

It was meant as a joke. Nobody laughed.

Before they left, I made them all say the decision back, one at a time. Not because I thought repetition made men honest. Because it made later dishonesty harder to disguise as misunderstanding.

Protect, not possess.

Partner, not direct.

Ask, and accept the answer.

Everyone in the room said it back.
