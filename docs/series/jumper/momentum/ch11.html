Chapter 11 of 49

Davy: You don't get to tell me what I can and can't do.

The decommissioned hotel in Los Angeles was quiet in November only by comparison with what it had been built for. The old ballroom still smelled faintly of dust and floor wax. The emptied guest floors carried every closing door farther than it deserved. I'd taken to walking the service corridors and roof most evenings, checking cameras, stairwells, and the locked vehicle entrance, less because anything was actually wrong with them than because the route gave me somewhere to put a worry that had been building for three weeks and hadn't found a proper sentence yet.

I found the sentence that evening coming down the back stairs, watching Cent through the wired-glass kitchen door before she knew I was there: hunched over a tablet at the old banquet table, scrolling through something with NASA's seal in the corner, entirely absorbed, entirely comfortable, the way you get comfortable with a thing only after you've stopped noticing how strange it actually is.

I waited until Millie had put the kettle on before I said anything, because some conversations go better with something to do with your hands, and I'd learned that the hard way, more than once, over more than one kitchen table.

"I want to talk about NASA," I said.

Cent didn't look up from the tablet, still reading whatever engineering update Osei's team had sent that day. "What about them."

"All of it. How deep we've let them in. Three weeks ago it was a debris catalog. Now there's a whiteboard in Houston with your name on a chair design and a general who calls you at home and a man who runs mission crews for the actual government asking what you need built. I want to talk about how far that goes before somebody up that chain decides they'd rather own this than borrow it."

I know what it looks like when an institution decides it owns you instead of trusting you, because I lived it. We ran from exactly that kind of decision made by exactly that kind of person, in a building with exactly that kind of good intentions painted on the outside of it. I still remembered, some nights more than others, exactly what it felt like to realize a file existed on you that you'd never get to read, written by people who'd never met you, deciding things about your life that you'd have no say in at all. Nobody in this family needed the history lesson. They'd all lived versions of it themselves, one way or another.

"They're not the NSA, Davy," Millie said, not sharp, just level, pouring water over tea leaves like the conversation hadn't raised her pulse at all, though I knew her well enough to see that it had. "Sterling's spent two years earning trust the slow way. Truman's spent three weeks building actual hardware instead of asking actual questions. If this were an ownership play, it would look different. It would look like paperwork before help, not the other way around."

"It always looks like help at the start. That's the whole design of it."

"That's the whole design of some of it," Millie said. "Not all of it. You're allowed to have learned the lesson without applying it to everyone who was never in the room that taught it to you."

Cent set the tablet down, finally, and I watched her decide to actually be in this conversation instead of letting it happen around her.

"I hear both of you," she said. "I do. Dad, I know exactly what you're afraid of, and I'm not going to pretend it's not a real thing that happens to real people, because it happened to you, and it almost happened to me, more than once, before I was old enough to understand what almost meant. But I'm not eight. I'm not the girl you had to keep off the grid because a piece of paper with my name on it would've been a leash. I've got a public name. A public station. A public relationship with a general who calls me Cent instead of Space Girl because he decided, on his own, somewhere along the way, that I'd earned that. I already can't go dark the way we did after Daarkon. That door's been closed for two years, and it wasn't NASA that closed it. It was every public choice I made afterward, on purpose, with my eyes open."

"That doesn't mean this particular door is safe just because the last one already closed."

"No," she agreed. "It doesn't. But it means the choice in front of me isn't 'trust NASA or stay invisible.' That second option's already gone. The actual choice is 'trust NASA, or don't, and either way I'm still the most visible person on the planet who can do what I do.' I'd rather spend that visibility building something real with people who've earned it than spend it hiding from a threat that isn't actually the shape you're picturing."

I wanted to argue with that. I worried at the impulse, because I'd spent most of my adult life being right about dangers like this and I didn't know how to stop being braced for them just because my daughter had grown up believing, reasonably, that the danger had changed shape since my day.

"Tell me what it would actually look like," I said, "if I'm wrong about all of it and you're right. Walk me through it, the way you'd walk an engineer through a spec."

Cent thought about that seriously, which I appreciated more than she probably knew. "It looks like Sterling and Truman getting exactly what they're asking for right now: my time, my ability, my say in how it gets used. It doesn't look like them getting my name on anything I haven't read, or a badge that says I work for them instead of with them, or a version of me that shows up because I'm told to instead of because I decided to. The day any of that changes, I walk. I've walked away from bigger things than a NASA contract."

"And if it changes quietly? The way these things usually do?"

"Then that's exactly what having people watching for it is for," Millie said, before Cent could answer. "That's not a reason to say no to all of it. That's a reason to keep paying attention, which we were always going to do anyway."

"You're grown, and the thing that scared me happened before you existed," I said finally. "I know that. I know you're not her, the girl in the cave, the girl I used to have to hide. I just." I stopped, and made myself say the actual thing instead of the shape of it. "I'm scared of losing you to an institution the way I almost lost myself to one. That's not really about NASA. That's about me."

"I know it is," Cent said, gentler than I deserved. "And I love you for it. But you don't get to tell me what I can and can't do because of something that happened to you before I existed. You get to worry. You get to ask hard questions, same as Mom just did. You don't get veto."

Joe had been quiet the whole time, sitting by the window, still in the way of a man who'd learned, the expensive way, exactly where the line was between raising a concern and trying to own somebody else's decision. He spoke now, careful.

"That's a lesson I had to learn the hard way," he said. "I confused being scared for you with having the right to decide for you, and it almost cost me everything. Your dad's allowed to be afraid. He just can't make the fear your orders. Neither can I."

"I'd feel better if there were some actual boundary drawn somewhere," he said. "Not us pulling back, but you deciding in advance what you're not willing to hand over, so it's your line and not something that gets discovered after the fact because nobody drew it first."

"Give me an example," Cent said. "Concretely. What would you draw the line at?"

Joe thought about it, genuinely, the way he thought about everything technical, working it through before committing to an answer. "Your family's location, obviously, though that's already been off the table since before any of this. Full access to your medical data without you reviewing it first. Any contract language that talks about you in terms of ownership rather than partnership, even in the boring legal boilerplate nobody reads. And a standing right to say no to any specific mission element, even after you've said yes to the program in general."

"That's a good list."

"It's a start," Millie said. "We'll build the rest of it as we go, same as we've built everything else in this family. Nobody expects you to have the whole thing solved at a kitchen table on a Tuesday night."

Cent's shoulders came down half an inch. She'd braced for something with more weight behind it, and this wasn't that.

"That's fair," she said. "That I can do."

Millie set four cups of tea across the old banquet table, the same steady ritual she'd carried through twenty years and more than one kitchen.

"For the record," she said, "I still think it's the right call. Not because I'm not scared too. Because I've watched what Sterling's actually done with two years of chances to betray us, and he hasn't taken a single one of them, and at some point that has to count for something, or none of the last two years of your life have meant anything at all."

I picked up the tea and didn't drink it right away, just held it, warm, the way I'd been holding a version of this fear for longer than my daughter had been alive.

"All right," I said. "I'm still scared. I'm going along with it anyway, because it's not actually my call to veto, and because some part of me has to eventually believe the world's gotten a little bit safer since the last time it tried to eat my family whole." I looked at Cent, at the tablet with the chair design still glowing on it. "Just. Draw the line Joe's talking about. And let me be the one who's still scared, even after you've decided. I've earned that much."

"You have," Cent said. "Always."

We drank the tea. Nobody solved anything else that night, and nothing needed solving further: the actual decision had already been made, the way it always was in this family, by the person it belonged to, with everyone else's fear heard and folded in without being allowed to become a veto. I'd been on both sides of that arrangement in my life, the one deciding and the one afraid for somebody else's decision, and I still didn't know which side was harder. I only knew which one I was on tonight.

Cent called Sterling and Truman the next morning with Joe's list expanded to a full page.

She put it on the old banquet table between them on the video screen and read every condition out loud.

"Family location remains compartmented," she began.

"Already is," Sterling said. "We can put the compartment owner and audit schedule in writing."

"Medical data does not move outside flight medicine until I review what is moving and why."

Truman frowned. "Emergency telemetry still goes to mission control in real time."

"During an active flight, yes. It does not become a research archive everybody gets to browse afterward."

"Agreed."

"No contract language describing me, my ability, or my availability as property, inventory, an assigned asset, or anything else a lawyer thinks is close enough not to count."

Sterling made the throat-clearing sound. "The government will use the word asset somewhere."

"Then the asset is the program, the chair, the data, or the hardware. Not me."

"That distinction can be written."

"I can refuse any mission element after accepting the program. No clause that turns one yes into every yes that follows."

"You owe us an operational reason if the refusal affects crew safety," Truman said. "You do not owe us a reason if it does not."

"Fair."

"Threats involving my family get disclosed to us. Not six months later because somebody decided the investigation mattered more than our ability to protect ourselves."

That one took longer. Sterling wanted a judicial-delay exception for active investigations. Cent wanted the delay measured in hours, not months. They settled on immediate disclosure of the protective facts, with sources and methods withheld only as long as a court specifically allowed.

"And I say no to protective custody," Cent finished. "Protection can watch for threats. It cannot become a back door to control where I go."

Truman studied her before answering.

"Those are serious conditions."

"Yes."

"Good. I was afraid somebody was going to ask for the Lincoln Bedroom at the White House."

Joe laughed before he could stop himself. Even Sterling made a sound suspiciously close to one.

"We'll take this to Counsel," Truman said. "Plain language, no hidden trade in the definitions section. Anything they say is impossible comes back to this table instead of disappearing into a draft."

"And the answer is still yes?" Cent asked.

"The answer was yes before the list," Sterling said. "The list is how we keep it yes when more people enter the room."

That was the point where I stopped listening for the trick in their voices. Not forever. I wasn't built for forever. But for that morning, they sounded like two men being handed demands and feeling relieved to know exactly what partnership would cost.

I walked the roof again before bed, out of habit more than need, and stood a long while beside the dead neon frame looking down into the hotel's lit courtyard, at my daughter's shadow moving behind the old restaurant glass, tablet back in her hands, already reading the next update. Millie found me there eventually, wrapped in the same heavy coat she always wore for these late checks, and didn't say anything at first, just stood beside me looking at the same lit windows.

"You did good in there," she said finally. "Asking for the spec instead of just saying no. That's growth, from the man I married."

"I learned it from watching her negotiate with contractors. Turns out asking for details works better than digging in."

"Turns out a lot of things work better than digging in." She took my arm, and we stood there a while longer in the cold, watching the old hotel hold its light against the city dark. I let myself believe the world had gotten a little safer since the last time it tried to take everything from us.
