Chapter 30 of 49

Cent POV: Gimbal not verified.

Six weeks passed between the shuttle evaluation and our first integrated carrier run. Formal assignment, medical baselines, systems classes, and ordinary procedure came first. The primary simulator occupied the next high bay. It had been built around the carrier ground article I'd watched take shape. It reproduced the whole crew compartment: the flight stations, every couch, the actual displays and warning logic, and a qualification copy of my chair on the same two-axis gimbaled bearing the flight ship would use. Its primary axis carried the full one-hundred-eighty-degree reversal; its orthogonal axis supplied limited out-of-plane trim. Together they pointed the commanded vector in pitch and yaw. The cabin sat on a six-axis motion base, but the motion base wasn't expected to absorb what I did. My chair's load yoke ran through it into an enlarged descendant of the hydraulic reaction frame NASA had built for my original remediation chair, sunk into the reinforced foundation. Accumulators took the impulse when I pushed. Load cells measured its magnitude, direction, and timing. The simulator converted that real measured impulse into the velocity change the carrier would have made, and the motion base gave everyone else the corresponding acceleration cue before washing itself back to center.

That let the simulator test me instead of merely testing equipment around me. The two gimbal axes established the commanded direction, but I still controlled how much change I delivered and exactly when I delivered it. Each green came with a target impulse and an allowed timing window. Push early, late, light, hard, or off the chair's neutral brace, and the navigation displays showed the resulting error instead of supplying the answer the run expected.

Our first integrated run began at departure, carried the scheduler through a changing set of structural modes, then jumped ahead to insertion. I rotated the primary axis toward the reverse vector while the trim axis found the commanded out-of-plane offset. The primary lock seated. The trim lock's witness switch was four hundredths of a second late.

The scheduler released the interval while the gimbal vote still disagreed. Yellow went dark. Green stayed off.

I didn't move.

"Gimbal not verified," Childs said.

"Hold momentum," I ordered.

Osei stopped the run. The load-path interlock had prevented a bad kick, but the display had just told Cent that the sequence was ready while another part of the system was still telling us it wasn't.

"Yellow cannot mean two different things," she said. "Sequence wait stays independent. Bearing disagreement gets amber. Green gets nothing until both are clean."

The software team changed the indication before we ran again. After that, the yellow SEQUENCE WAIT light remained on until the scheduler released the next interval. The amber CAUTION light reported sensor or gimbal disagreement. Red and the cabin alarm meant abort. Green required the interval, both commanded angles, both mechanical locks, independent position votes, and the load path to agree at the same time.

We trained the whole boost and insertion procedure there for months. The simulator shifted cargo, moved a crew member in her restraints, drifted an encoder on either axis, skipped a scheduled interval, and changed the navigation solution after I had already rotated. Sometimes the right response was a new pair of angles. Sometimes it was to wait. Sometimes it was to stop the sequence, recenter both axes on a safe load path, and let the crew fly the vehicle while mission control worked the problem. The early runs showed more scatter in my impulse than the paper model had allowed. Osei widened the correction model instead of hiding the data, then tightened the training gates as I learned to put repeated kicks inside the requested band without chasing a small miss into a larger one. I didn't merely sit in the chair for the integrated runs. I pushed, the reaction frame took it, and the crew flew the result I actually produced.
