Chapter 4 of 49

Cent: I know I don't have any right to ask, but...

Three weeks of stacking tests had turned into a rhythm by the time Sterling called on a Thursday: mornings on the catalog, afternoons on whatever business Apex Orbital actually needed to survive as a company, evenings arguing gently with Joe about whether the greenhouse's second lettuce bed was ambition or hubris. I was elbow-deep in an invoice dispute with a client who didn't want to pay for a job I'd done cleanly and quickly, which felt like the least dramatic problem I'd had in a month, when the comm chimed with Sterling's priority tone.

I knew before he got past his name that it wasn't catalog business. The throat-clear came immediately after it, the meaningful one, the one that meant he was about to say something smaller than what he was actually feeling.

"Sterling." A throat-clear, the meaningful one. "Cent. You have a minute."

"I have a minute."

"Grebenchekov had a bad morning at Johnson. Centrifuge requal, routine vascular imaging on account of his history, and the old repair did not like the pressure spike. The Dacron graft held. There is no leak and no new aneurysm. He's stable. He's also," another throat-clear, "the most famous cosmonaut on Earth right now and furious that flight medicine suspended the run."

"He's still under NASA care?"

"He has been since you got him clear of the Russians. Protective status, American medical team, the whole arrangement. Nobody is handing him back. That's why this landed on my desk instead of disappearing into Star City." He paused. "He asked for you. His vascular team also thinks you may be the one person he'll hear when you tell him stopping a test is not the same as surrendering the rest of his life. I know I don't have any right to ask, but... could you go look at him? Not officially. Just you."

I let the request settle for a second, because it was such a strange thing to hear out of him. Sterling didn't ask like that. Sterling told me things needed doing and trusted me to decide whether to do them, or occasionally, rarely, ordered them like he had the standing to, which he mostly didn't, and we both knew it. This was neither. This was a man handing me something and admitting up front he had no right to.

"Yeah," I said. "I'll go."

"I'll have somebody meet you at the flight medicine clinic. Dr. Nolan. She runs his team and won't ask you a single question you don't want answered."

"You've already arranged this."

"I arranged the possibility of it. Whether you actually went was always going to be your call." A beat. "I'm glad you said yes."

Johnson in November was warm and bright in the aggressively controlled way medical buildings are, every corridor polished, every window shut against weather the ventilation system had decided did not exist. I arrived in a service stairwell three floors below flight medicine and walked the rest of the way like someone who belonged there, which, loosely, I did by now. Dr. Nolan found me before I'd gone twenty feet, a tired, kind-faced woman with Grebenchekov's chart folded against her chest, and walked me the rest of the way without asking a single question, exactly as advertised.

"He'll be glad to see you," Nolan said at the door. "He's been telling everyone who will listen that he's fine. Nobody believes him. He may tell you the truth."

Grebenchekov looked smaller in a hospital bed than he had floating free in a suit while the aneurysm tried to kill him, which didn't make sense until I remembered gravity does that to everybody, pins them down to their actual size. He had lines in both arms and a monitor doing quiet, patient work beside him, and when he saw me his face did something complicated: glad, and embarrassed, and searching for the joke that would let him not have to be either one out loud.

"They called the girl who deorbits garbage," he said, in English, for my benefit. "I did not think I qualified."

"You're heavier than the garbage. Bigger vote of confidence."

That got a real laugh out of him, short, and it cost him something; I watched it cost him something, a small wince he tried to fold into the laugh and didn't quite manage. I pulled a chair over instead of standing across the bed from him like a doctor would, and Nolan, with the tact of a woman who'd clearly done this exact quiet exit before, found something urgent to attend to down the hall.

"How bad," I said, once we were alone.

"Bad enough that they will not clear me for another centrifuge run for six months. Maybe longer." He looked at the ceiling, not at me. "Bad enough that a man starts doing arithmetic about how many more chances he actually has left, in a life that already used most of its chances up on one very good decision I do not regret."

"The decision where I took you off that station without making you survive reentry."

"The decision where I trusted a teenager I had known for eleven minutes with the rest of my life. I have never regretted it. I am telling you this so that whatever comes next, you already know where I stand before you ask me anything."

I hadn't come with anything to ask him. Sterling hadn't given me anything to ask him. Grebenchekov had apparently decided to answer anyway.

"Nothing to ask yet," I said. "Just wanted eyes on you. You look like a man who's going to be fine and hates every minute of being told to rest."

"You have accurately diagnosed me. You could stop practicing medicine and go into diagnosis instead."

"I'll stick to garbage."

He was quiet a moment, working something over, and when he spoke again his voice had lost some of its performance. "Can I ask you something, since you have come all this way and are too polite to leave immediately after."

"Go ahead."

"Do you think about it? What we did. The choice you made when there was no safe choice left."

"I know you'd have died without it," I said. There wasn't an honest version that made the fact gentler, and he deserved the honest version. "The aneurysm was already leaking. The surgeons put the graft in afterward. I know that in my head. I still replay carrying you down because I made the choice before anyone could promise the jump itself wouldn't make things worse. I don't regret choosing the life you still have. I just don't get to decide whether the risk was worth it to you."

"It was worth it," he said, without hesitation, the performance gone entirely now. "I would do it again tomorrow, knowing everything I know now. The aneurysm broke. You did not. You delivered me without reentry forces to surgeons who replaced the damaged section with a graft. I want you to hear me say that plainly, because I suspect you carry responsibility for the part that was already killing me, and I would like you to put that particular weight down."

I hadn't expected that, and it took me a second to find something to say back that wasn't just gratitude dressed up as a joke. "Thank you. I needed to hear that more than I knew."

"I know you did. It is why I said it."

I stayed another twenty minutes after that, mostly quiet, mostly just present, the way I figured Sterling would've wanted somebody to be present for him if their positions were reversed. Nolan walked me back down through the stairwell afterward. At the door she stopped me.

"He speaks of you often," Nolan said. "Not as a hero. As a fact he has decided to be grateful for, the way a man is grateful for weather that happened to be good on an important day. I thought you should know that, since he will never say it to you directly in those words."

When I jumped home I called Sterling first, before I even took my coat off.

"He's stable. Scared and putting a brave face on it, which, frankly, tracks. Tell Nolan her patient is exactly as stubborn as his chart says."

"She already knows. I suspect that's in twelve-point type on the first page." A pause, and then, quieter: "Thank you. That wasn't mine to ask, and you did it anyway."

"You said please. Practically unheard of from you."

"Don't get used to it."

I hung up and stood there a minute in my own kitchen, thinking about Grebenchekov's face doing that complicated thing, glad and embarrassed at once, and about the weight he'd told me to put down. I wasn't sure I knew how. It felt lighter anyway.
