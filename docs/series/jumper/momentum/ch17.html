Chapter 17 of 49

Cent POV: It was... unsettling.

The craft came together over the next two months the way anything does when NASA actually decides to move fast: a pressure hull first, tested and retested before I ever touched it, then life support, then instrumentation, then the dedicated copy of my chair on its swivel bearing, installed as close to the nominal loaded center of mass as the hull allowed. I watched most of it happen the way you watch a house get built when you're not the contractor: showing up, checking pieces, jumping home again, the actual labor mostly somebody else's to do.

What needed doing that nobody else could do was pick a target.

"Something long-duration," Sterling said, over a call that had become almost routine by then, two or three a week, more friend than officer at this point even if neither of us had said so out loud. "Well-characterized orbit, enough mass to be a meaningful shakedown, tracked continuously so there's no ambiguity about where it actually is when you go looking for it."

"You've got a candidate."

"I've got several. There's one that stands out on the numbers alone." A pause, and I heard him pull up whatever file he was reading from. "Sixty-five kilograms, tracked continuously since it entered a stable decaying orbit a little over two years ago, characterized better than almost anything else in the catalog because of how closely it's been watched. Technically classified as debris, though the file's got a person's name attached instead of a part number, which I've always found a strange thing for a catalog to allow."

I didn't need him to say the name. I felt it arrive before the sentence finished, a cold drop through my chest that had nothing to do with the room's actual temperature.

"Cent?" He caught it immediately, the way he caught everything, and his voice shifted the way it did over comm when something wasn't reading right. "You went quiet."

"I'm here."

"You're not, actually. What did I just say?"

I hadn't told him. In two years of calls, in everything Hunt had shared with the family, in every debrief anybody had run on Daarkon and Gilead and the whole ugly shape of what had happened, Sterling had never once heard the specific thing that happened to Hyacinth Pope at the very end of it, because there had never been a reason to tell him, and because it wasn't the kind of thing you brought up on a routine catalog call.

"The object you're describing," I said. "It's not debris. It's a person. It's Hyacinth."

The line went quiet in a way I'd learned meant he was recalculating something, fast, from a completely different set of assumptions than the ones he'd started with.

"I didn't know that," he said, careful now, all the routine gone out of his voice. "I pulled it because the numbers were good. I didn't cross-reference the name against anything: it's flagged debris, it's always been flagged debris, nobody ever told me otherwise. Cent, I'm sorry. I'll pull another candidate."

"No. It's fine. It's a good candidate, actually, on the numbers." I made myself say it plainly, the way I said everything, because the alternative was letting it get bigger in the silence than it needed to be. "I'm the one who put her there."

I told him, then, the whole of it, because there was no version of not telling him that made any sense once we'd gotten this far: the twin I'd opened on purpose, the interrogation room, Gilead's people and what they'd been about to do, and the accidental part in the middle of it, when Hyacinth crossed the connection through me, through my own body, before I understood exactly where the opening would take her.

"She passed through me," I said. "It was... unsettling. That's the whole word I've ever had for it. I said it once, to my parents, right after, and then I put it away and didn't take it back out again, because there wasn't anything more useful to say about it than that."

Sterling let the silence stretch. When he spoke, it wasn't the general talking. It was just him.

"I've asked you to do something with her remains without knowing what she was to you," he said. "I'd like to un-ask it, if there's any part of you that needs that."

"There isn't. She's a good candidate. Use her." I surprised myself saying it, but it was true the second it came out: there was something almost right about it, using Hyacinth's own tracked, careful, endlessly-observed orbit as the proving ground for the thing that would take people to Mars, instead of leaving her up there as nothing but a data point nobody looked at twice. "I think I'd rather it be her than some anonymous chunk of an old rocket. At least this way it means something."

"All right," he said. "Then it's her." Another pause, and then, quieter: "I've spent two years talking to you on the phone, and I don't think I ever once asked what any of it actually cost you to live through. I should have. I'm asking now."

"It cost plenty," I said. "I've mostly stopped adding it up. But thank you for asking instead of just assuming I was fine because I sound fine."

"You always sound fine. I've started to suspect that's not the same thing as being fine."

"No," I said. "It generally isn't."

He came out to the station himself two weeks later: his own idea, his own initiative, offered the way you offer something you're a little afraid will be refused. Mom was the one who said yes, over Dad's objection, the same argument we'd already had once about NASA in general, run again in miniature over one specific man setting foot on our family's own hardware for the first time. I jumped him up myself, hand on his shoulder.

He went rigid the second the station's interior resolved around us. His knees bent for gravity that wasn't there. One hand grabbed my forearm hard enough to hurt while the other reached toward a wall that had become a floor only because his body desperately wanted one. His face drained, and his eyes made three fast, useless corrections between me, the curved inner ring, and Earth moving beneath the observation port.

I pulled an emesis bag from the pocket beside my suit seal, snapped it open, and put it into his free hand.

"Hold this."

He looked at the bag, then at me.

"That's not a prediction or an insult," I said. "It's first-visit equipment."

"Don't fight for down," I told him. "There isn't one. Pick a surface and let your eyes keep it."

He swallowed, once, jaw clenched, and forced his grip on my arm to loosen finger by finger.

He made it through one more breath before the bag stopped being precautionary. I kept one hand on his shoulder while he was sick and the other hooked through one of the guy ropes we'd placed through the cabin specifically with Sam in mind, so neither of us drifted. When he finished, I sealed the bag and handed him a fresh one from the same pocket.

"I'm sorry," he said.

"Don't be. Almost everybody needs the first one."

"For the next few minutes, no sudden movements. Don't tilt your head and don't turn it separately from your shoulders. We generally think of the viewport as 'down,' so pick one surface and keep your eyes on it. If you need to look somewhere else, move your whole body as one piece. Treat your body like it's mounted on a swivel. Use the ropes, rotate slowly, let your head come with you."

"It worked for Sam. You're probably thirty years younger than she was, so give it a minute."

"Samantha Harrison," he said.

"You watch her show?"

"I have seen enough of it to know she made this look considerably easier."

"Didn't I see your name on that list?"

His expression became very careful. "I sent a letter. Whether it qualified as a proposal is between Mrs. Harrison and me."

"I knew the description," he said. "That was not the same as knowing this."

"It never is."

It took him another minute before he trusted the guy ropes, and another after that before he looked through the viewport without immediately looking away.

I pulled a tissue from my pocket and held it out. "Tissue?"

He took it without looking away.

"Forty-three phone calls," he said, once he'd found his voice again, the fresh bag still closed in one hand. "I never once accounted for what zero-g feels like."

"Nobody does from a description," I said. "Knowing there's no down and having your inner ear discover it are two different things."

"No," he agreed. "I don't imagine it does." He looked at me the way he'd sounded on the phone for months without my ever quite naming it: warm, unguarded, none of the throat-clearing left in it. "Thank you for finally letting me see it. And for telling me about her. Both of those were yours to keep private, and you didn't."

"You've earned a few things you didn't have to earn," I said. "That was one of them."

"Now," he said, "introduce me to your grandmother."
