Chapter 42 of 49

Cent POV: Space Girl's already done the boring version of this. This one's yours, all of yours.

There was an argument, gentle and short, about who should go out the airlock first, and I ended it before it really got started.

"Not me," I said. "I've done this. Stood right out there, alone, a year ago, bag of sand and a long walk to nowhere. Space Girl's already done the boring version of this particular moment. This one's yours, all of yours. I didn't build a ship and cross a hundred and eighty million kilometers with six other people just so I could take the same walk twice."

"Somebody has to be first," Iverson said, not arguing, just noting the shape of the problem.

"Then let it be somebody it means something new to." I looked around the cramped cabin at six faces that had each earned this in a different way: Grebenchekov, who'd trusted a girl he'd known eleven minutes with the rest of his life, twice over; Iverson and Childs, who'd landed a broken ship on nothing but skill once already and were about to do the opposite of broken, on purpose, on another world; Ochoa, carrying Nasser's promises as carefully as her own equipment; Reyes, quiet and steady through every medical alarm; Truman, who'd spent a career sending other people and had finally come himself. "Any of you. All of you. Just not me first. I've had my turn."

Grebenchekov went first, in the end, mostly because nobody argued hard enough to stop him and because I think, secretly, everyone agreed it should be him: the man whose life had already been tested twice by this whole strange chain of events, stepping out onto ground his own government had never been able to send him to.

I watched it through the same window I'd once looked out at empty dunes alone, and this time the dunes weren't empty. Grebenchekov's boots left prints in red dust that had never held a footprint before, and behind him came Iverson, then Childs, then Ochoa moving faster than dignity strictly allowed toward the nearest outcrop Nasser had made her promise to examine, then Reyes, then Truman, unhurried for once, taking it in like someone who intended to remember every second of it clearly.

I went out last, and stood beside all six of them on ground I'd technically stood on before and had never once felt the way I felt now, surrounded, witnessed, the loneliest thing I'd ever done turned into the least lonely thing I'd ever be part of.

"Well," Childs said, into the thin, cold air, looking back at the capsule, at the dust settling around seven sets of prints instead of one. "That's one small step, and six more right behind it."

Nobody laughed, exactly, but something loosened in all of us at once, the specific, shared relief of people who'd trained for years toward a single moment and found it, when it actually arrived, simpler and quieter and more entirely real than any of the training had prepared them to expect.

Ochoa was already on her knees at the nearest outcrop, running a gloved hand over a striated band of rock with the reverence most people reserve for things behind museum glass. "Layered sediment. Actual layered sediment, in place, not a fragment somebody bagged and shipped home. Priya, if you're hearing this later, sector seven was worth the walk. I could cry, and I'm not going to, because my faceplate doesn't have anywhere for the tears to go."

"Take the win anyway," Reyes said, crouching beside her, more interested in Ochoa's vitals than the rock, though her eyes kept drifting to it too.

Joe's voice came through eleven minutes later, the light delay long enough to feel like a held breath before an answer. "I heard all of that. Ochoa, half of mission control just went quiet in a way that means everyone's more moved than they want to admit on an open channel."

"Tell them I said thank you for building us a ship instead of a museum tour," Ochoa said, not looking up.

I looked back at Earth, a bright point low on the horizon, farther away than I'd ever stood from it in my life and somehow, standing there with six people who trusted me and each other completely, not lonely at all.

"Okay," I said, mostly to myself. "Now the actual work starts."
