Chapter 8 of 49

Cent: We've got eleven thousand people at NASA. Let us help.

The room NASA put together to fix my chair, as I came to call it even after everyone else had stopped, had a structural engineer, a biomechanics specialist, a G-force physiologist on loan from the flight surgeon's office, and Cory, who kept trying to introduce himself as "the suit guy" like that undersold what he'd been doing for me for two years. Four specialists, a whiteboard, and me, sitting on an actual chair in an actual conference room in Houston, describing a problem none of them had ever had to solve because nobody had ever needed to before.

"Walk us through the failure again," the structural engineer said. Her name was Osei, and she'd clearly already decided this was the most interesting problem of her career; she wasn't going to be rushed through it.

"I don't push with my hands. I brace my body against the load, change my own momentum, and bring the load with me. My hands only keep the contact from sliding. On the satellite bus I had two bad brace points, so the reaction came back through my arms and shoulders before the rest of my body could share it. Past a certain force, the geometry is the bottleneck, not me."

"So the fix isn't making you stronger," said the biomechanics specialist, a soft-spoken man named Delgado who'd spent the first ten minutes of the meeting just watching me move my own shoulder experimentally without saying anything at all. "It's spreading the load so no single joint takes the whole hit."

"That's the whole idea, yeah."

"May I?" Delgado came around the table and, with my permission, walked me through a series of simple motions, arms braced this way, that way, watching where the strain actually traveled. "Your body's already doing something clever without any training for it: bracing through the whole skeleton rather than just the arms, the way a good lifter braces a deadlift. The problem isn't your technique. It's that there's nothing external to brace against except your own joints. Give the load somewhere else to go and you solve most of this without touching your physiology at all."

Osei was already sketching before he'd finished the sentence: a seat braced across the back, hips, thighs, and shoulders, with two short index grips mounted where my hands naturally closed when the rest of me was locked into the structure.

"The handles are indexes, not levers," she said, more to the whiteboard than to any of us. "They tell your hands where the neutral brace is and give you something to close around while the harness and seat carry the actual load. You sit down, your whole body is already coupled to the frame, every time, and the reaction spreads through the seat structure instead of bottlenecking in your arms."

"That solves my point-load problem." I sat up straighter, hearing it. "That's the exact thing I've been fighting for a month. Every test article's a fresh puzzle for how to brace. This fixes my geometry once."

"It also lets us instrument the seat directly," said the physiologist, a brisk, precise woman named Chu who hadn't said much yet but clearly missed nothing. "Real-time load data on you, every push, not just after the fact. We stop reconstructing what happened from bruises and start actually watching it happen."

Cory had been quiet, which for Cory was its own kind of loud, and when he finally spoke it was the other half of the problem, the half that had actually killed the satellite-bus scenario on paper. "Spreading your own load solves your half. It does nothing for the payload's half. You can survive a harder push now. That doesn't mean whatever you're pushing can."

"So don't push harder," I said. "Push smaller, more often."

The room went quiet the way it does when four smart people all reach the same idea within about two seconds of each other.

"Discrete increments," Osei said slowly. "Instead of one change at full effort, a lot of small changes, spaced out. Each one small enough that nothing, payload or you, ever sees a G-spike past what's survivable. You just do it more times."

"How many times, for a given delta-v?"

"Depends on the mass and the target velocity." Chu was already flipping through a small notebook of her own. "But if each kick's small enough, the number doesn't matter, engineering-wise. It's just time."

"I have time," I said. "I don't have a spare shoulder."

That got a laugh, the real kind, and it was the first time the room had felt less like a briefing and more like people actually building something together. We spent the next two hours working out spacing: close enough together that a mission profile didn't take forever, far enough apart that I could self-correct my own drift between each one before the next, which was its own separate skill I'd have to actually practice, not just theorize about. Delgado wanted five seconds, worried about cumulative fatigue on the joints even at reduced load. Chu wanted three, worried more about total mission time than fatigue she was confident the braced seat would already solve. Osei sat between them, sketching load curves for both, until the argument stopped being an argument and became two people converging on the same number from opposite directions.

"Three," Chu said finally. "Provisionally. We test it before either of us gets attached to being right."

"I have time," I said again, "and now apparently I have a research team. This is already better than a month ago."

Truman came in near the end of it, coat still on, having clearly come straight from somewhere else, and stood at the back listening for a few minutes before he said anything.

"How's it looking?"

"Like we solved her half of it," Osei said, flat and certain, meaning it completely. "Braced seat, distributed load, incremental delivery instead of single-shot force. She won't need to improvise her own body position again, and neither the payload nor she will ever see a lethal spike, assuming the numbers hold."

Truman looked at the whiteboard a long moment, at four different specialties' worth of handwriting crowded onto it, at me sitting in a borrowed chair describing a problem that hadn't existed as a category of engineering problem three weeks ago. His jaw unclenched, just slightly.

"This is what I keep trying to tell people," he said, mostly to me, though the room heard it too. "We've got eleven thousand people at NASA, many of them smarter than me and you combined. You come to us with a problem nobody's ever had, and inside three weeks four of them build you a chair for it. Let us help. That's the whole pitch. That's all I ever want anybody to hear when I say it."

"I'm hearing it," I said. "Loud and clear."

"Good." He clapped Osei on the shoulder on his way past, an old, easy gesture that told me this wasn't the first whiteboard he'd walked into like this. "Build her the chair. Then build the load side of it. The satellite bus is not the upper end of this."

Sitting there with four strangers who'd stopped being strangers somewhere in the last two hours, I realized I wasn't going to be improvising braces alone anymore.

✳

Cory walked me out to the parking lot afterward, in no hurry, hands in his pockets, wearing the particular quiet of a man deciding how to say something.

"You know this changes things for me too," he said eventually. "Two years I've been the only outside expert on 'the Cent effect.' Now I'm one voice in a room of six, and next month it'll probably be a room of twenty."

"Does that bother you?"

"Not as much as I expected it to." He looked up at the flat Houston sky, gray with the particular haze of a city that never quite got a clear night. "I think I spent two years quietly proud of being the only one who understood you, in the ways that mattered for the suits. Watching Osei sketch that chair in about ninety seconds was humbling. It was also the right thing to have happen, and I'd rather be humbled and useful than solitary and slow."

"You're still useful. You've been in every room since this started."

"I intend to stay in every room. I'm just no longer the only chair in it." He bumped my good shoulder, gently, mindful of the other one. "Go home, Cent. Ice the shoulder that's actually still bruised. Let the six of us build you something to sit in before you go finding any more walls."

"Deal."

I jumped home that evening lighter than I'd felt in weeks, not because the problem was solved, exactly, but because it had stopped being only mine to solve. Joe found me in the kitchen, staring at nothing in particular, and asked what I was thinking about.

"Eleven thousand people," I said. "Apparently that's how many NASA's got. I only needed six of them today and it still felt like more help than I've had in my whole life put together."

"Get used to it," he said. "I have a feeling that number's only going to grow on you."
