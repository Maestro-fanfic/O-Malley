Chapter 23 of 49

Iverson POV: That isn't anywhere near our ground track.

Truman did not offer Childs or me Mars when we arrived at Johnson. He took us and Timmins to the Shuttle Mission Simulator.

The simulation director was waiting at the instructor station with Endeavour's landing file already loaded: entry energy, communications dropout, corrupted guidance and beacon returns, ground track, weather, control inputs, every recorded system response down to the last useful fraction of a second. The forward displays showed the same reassuring guidance we had seen that day. The visual system held Los Angeles outside, waiting for us to notice it.

Truman stood behind the instructor console with Cent beside him and spoke to the simulation director without lowering his voice for our benefit. Cent knew our names and knew Endeavour had landed in Los Angeles. She had never seen the cockpit record or the complete descent.

"Feed everything from that landing into the shuttle simulator. Every failure, every bad indication, every second of flight data. I want to see if you were just lucky that day, or if you really are as good as the stories tell."

"We know where the river is now," Childs said.

"I know. This run doesn't test whether you can be surprised by the same answer twice. It tests whether the answer still works when I can watch every decision that produced it. After that, we change the parts you couldn't have known."

"I'm game," Childs said.

I nodded.

✳

I took the left seat. Childs took the right. The simulator gave us Endeavour at entry interface.

Some language was going to come back exactly the same. There were only so many ways to call a heading, arm the landing gear, report a configuration, or ask for vehicle status. Those words were built into procedure and drilled into us by years of simulator training because procedure had to remain usable when everything around it stopped being familiar. The judgment between the calls was the part Truman was testing.

We were carrying the same excess energy.

"Timmins, give me additional drag," Childs said.

"Coming in."

The motion base rolled and began feeding the entry loads through our seats. At four hundred thousand feet the attitude was correct, the guidance solution was green, and the simulated Houston loop confirmed a good entry configuration.

Then communications dropped.

"Starting internal comm reset," Timmins said.

Houston vanished behind static. The circuit came back. An alarm sounded with it.

The vehicle remained controllable. Hydraulics read good. The control surfaces still felt live. Guidance held the needles centered and reported us on course. Both landing beacons agreed. The simulator had reproduced the most dangerous part correctly: nearly everything on the panels told us there was no navigation failure.

I looked through the windshield.

"That isn't our ground track. That isn't anywhere near our ground track."

Childs checked the predicted ground track. Guidance and both beacons gave her the same answer they had given the first time. The terrain under us gave us Los Angeles.

Static cleared on the loop.

"Endeavour, Houston. Disregard onboard guidance and landing beacons. Both are corrupt. Ground tracking shows you one hundred twenty-nine miles off course."

"Confirm present position," I said.

"Los Angeles."

Downtown stood ahead of us at fifteen thousand feet. We had roughly two minutes of glide left. The simulator gave us no engine, no reserve altitude, and no extra answer just because everyone in the building knew how the real landing had ended.

"Options," I said.

The simulated ground controllers began searching. A freeway appeared on the candidate list and disappeared again when the traffic model showed it packed from barrier to barrier.

Childs pulled the local survey grid over the bad guidance display. Airports remained beyond our reachable footprint. Surface streets were interrupted. The concrete flood-control channel ran almost straight through the city.

"I have an alternate," Childs said.

I glanced across. "Show me."

Childs gave Houston the coordinates and rebuilt the maximum lift-to-drag solution. The numbers came up within a few seconds of the original flight values.

"Endeavour, Houston. River solution is marginal but possible. Turn right to heading one-seven-five. Visual in five seconds."

I rolled. The motion base put the turn into our bodies. The Los Angeles River came into view, broad enough to accept the orbiter and crossed by the same sequence of bridges.

"Those spans are closer than our wingspan wants," I said.

"We can make the channel. No lateral margin."

"Build the line."

Childs put the course on my display and began calling it.

"High and fast. Two-ninety feet, three-twenty knots. Two-seventy, three-oh-five."

I held the center.

"Arm the gear."

"Gear armed," Timmins said.

"Gear down."

The simulator gave us the drag and vibration of the mains entering the airflow. The first bridge filled the windshield and passed overhead.

"Two-forty feet, two-ninety."

"Brace," I said. "We're going to hit hard."

The main gear struck concrete. The motion base hit hard enough to drive the harnesses into our shoulders. I lowered the nose.

"Speed brake."

"Full," Timmins said.

The walls accelerated past us. We were still too fast.

"Chute."

The next bridge was already ahead. "Negative chute. It'll catch the span and take the tail."

I steered around the center support while the gear still carried enough weight to answer me. The right wing passed the simulated concrete with the same lack of margin.

"Next bridge," Childs said. "We're too high."

"Gear up."

Timmins's hands paused for less than a second. "We lose steering."

"There won't be anything left to steer if we leave it down," I said. "Bring it up."

Timmins moved the handle. The landing gear folded under load. Endeavour dropped onto her belly. The simulator filled the cabin with metal-on-concrete vibration while the vehicle passed beneath the bridge.

"Harnesses locked," I said. "We're committed."

There was nothing left to fly. The orbiter slid down the channel, shedding simulated tile and speed. The controls went soft as the wings stopped carrying useful air. One more bridge passed overhead.

The scaffold appeared beneath the next span.

Endeavour stopped directly below it.

The motion base gave us the last deceleration and went still.

Nobody in the cockpit moved until I let out one breath.

"Status."

Timmins ran the systems. Childs checked the crew. The simulator marked the orbiter destroyed beyond repair and everyone aboard alive.

When we finally unstrapped, my legs took a moment to remember the floor. Childs had to open and close one hand before it stopped trembling from the control calls she'd been gripping through the landing. Neither of us tried to conceal it from flight medicine.

Truman looked at the traces for perhaps five seconds.

"Again."

"That was the record," Truman said. "Now we change it. De-orbit performance. Which sensors lie and which ones tell the truth. When communications fail. Where you come down and what the ground gives you when you get there. This is really gonna put you through the wringer."

"Still game," Childs said.

I nodded.

"Tomorrow," Truman said. "You're done for today. Flight medicine clears all three of you before every run, and nobody flies two of these in one day. I want judgment under pressure, not exhaustion pretending to be judgment."
