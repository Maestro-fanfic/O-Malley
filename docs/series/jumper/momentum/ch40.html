Chapter 40 of 49

Holcomb POV: He used my own order as a targeting brief.

The second time Millicent Rice's name brought everyone into the Situation Room, nobody needed the capability explained.

They needed the damage.

Sterling stood at the end of the table in yesterday's shirt. Agent Baines sat beside him with two folders and the expression of someone who had taken a professional failure personally without letting the feeling make her sloppy. Halloran, the Chief of Staff, SecDef, DNI, Attorney General, White House Counsel. David Rice sat against the wall beside Joseph Trujeque, who looked less frightened by the armed men he'd seen than by the fact he'd crossed twenty feet without moving through the space between.

One chair was conspicuously empty.

"Give me the sequence," I said.

Sterling did.

Devereaux had used his position on the NSC staff to map access authorities around the program. He had parceled questions across contractors so no single inquiry looked like collection. He had waited until the carrier was in coast and the outbound feed carried enough delay to make Millicent a witness instead of a responder. Then he'd tried to revoke Sterling's credentials, replace the mission-control chain with personnel answerable through his office, and put two armed men inside the building under color of a signed administrative order.

"Signed by whom," the Attorney General asked.

"Deputy authority routed through three offices," Baines said. "Each person thought they were approving a narrow continuity measure. Devereaux assembled the pieces into something none of them had authorized whole."

Baines opened her first folder. "Hunt caught the badge mismatch. Sterling caught the timing. Mr. Rice removed both armed men before either could fire. The posted physical team was too far from the inner corridor and arrived after the confrontation. That gap is ours."

"I want the footage."

Baines put the corridor feed on the main display. David flickered and the first armed man vanished. He flickered again and the second man was gone too. Hunt had barely started reaching inside his jacket. Then Joseph appeared on one knee and checked David with both hands.

David said, "Not a hostile."

Joe answered, "Right," and vanished.

"And Joe Trujeque transported himself from the console to the corridor," the DNI said.

Joe flinched at the formal phrasing.

"I saw a weapon pointed toward Davy," he said. "I needed to be there. Then I was."

No one asked Joe to demonstrate. I made sure of that with a look before curiosity could become procedure.

"The ship?" I asked.

"Safe," Sterling said. "Outbound feed exposed the crew to the incident nearly four minutes after it occurred. We reported everyone accounted for. Full operational report followed. Cent saw Joe arrive."

"And Devereaux."

The room changed.

"Mrs. Rice appeared in the Oval while Devereaux was in federal custody," I said. "She shot him once. The Secret Service disarmed her immediately. She did not resist."

The Attorney General's voice stayed level. "I will need every fact, including the facts no one in this room enjoys."

"You'll have them. I am a fact witness. I will give a complete statement and recuse myself entirely from this matter." I looked at White House Counsel. "No disappeared evidence. No political conclusion written before an investigation. That applies to Devereaux and to Mrs. Rice."

David looked at me, hard.

"Millie surrendered to federal agents before this meeting," he said. "Counsel is with her. She is not asking you to make it vanish."

"She gets the same presumption, the same right to counsel, and the same complete record you would demand for anyone else," David continued. "No punishment written in this room."

"She'll get all of that and more. MY Chief of Staff will authorize counsel at the highest level."

"It also means," I continued, "that no one will pretend the man was an innocent staffer attacked without context while in federal custody. He targeted a mission in flight, sent armed personnel into mission control, and used the communications delay separating her daughter from Earth as part of the plan. The investigation gets all of it."

Halloran put both hands flat on the table. "He sat in this room."

"Twice," Baines said.

"He heard the protection order."

"Yes."

"He helped draft the implementation."

"Yes."

The Chief of Staff's anger finally broke through his usual control. "He used the order as a targeting brief. Every protection measure told him what mattered. Every access rule told him what had to be replaced."

Baines opened a recovered draft from Devereaux's private file. "This was the next step. Once his replacement chain held mission control, he intended to declare a continuity emergency, put Cent's family and ground contacts under security review, and make her future mission access conditional on compliance. He wasn't collecting options. He was building a command structure around her."

"And the contingency?" the DNI asked.

Silence went around the table.

The private contingency I had authorized after the first meeting, the due-diligence file for a hypothetical loss of Millicent's cooperation, had not ordered any of this. It had still given Devereaux a vocabulary and an institutional shape he could bend.

"Suspend it," I said.

"Sir, destroying planning because one man abused adjacent access..."

"I said suspend, not destroy. We review who can read it, what assumptions it makes, and whether the existence of a coercive branch invites people to confuse imagination with authority. Until then, nobody touches it."

The DNI nodded once. He didn't like it. He understood it.

Baines slid over her second folder.

"Immediate changes. Two-person authorization for any program access revocation. Independent callback to mission control on every order issued during a crewed flight. Armed-entry credentials validated against a live roster held by a separate office. Insider-threat reports go simultaneously to Secret Service, NASA security, and the representative Ms. Rice selected, so no one office can bury a pattern. The family is notified of any collection attempt within twenty-four hours unless a federal judge signs a delay."

"Physical."

"Perimeter detail expands while the ship is in flight. No entry into the hotel without consent. Mission-control corridors get continuous posted coverage, not response-only coverage. Sterling retains a sealed secondary command channel outside the building. And we assign a protective-intelligence officer whose only job is to look for timing: who is waiting for Cent to be distant, asleep, injured, or committed elsewhere."

David spoke for the first time.

"That's what I told you in the Oval. Protection becomes possession the minute somebody decides our vulnerability is an opportunity."

"You were right."

The words cost me nothing. They changed the room anyway.

"Do you know how often a President says that in this office?" I asked him.

"Not enough."

"Correct."

Joe had been staring at his hands. "What happens when Cent comes home?"

Sterling answered. "You tell her about the jump. Her mother tells her the rest, when her mother chooses. Operationally, Cent gets the facts she needs to fly. Personally, we do not make a delayed channel the place she learns every detail of what her family did."

"She'll know we're holding something back."

"Yes."

"She'll hate it."

"Probably."

Joe looked at David. David nodded once, not agreement so much as recognition of a bad choice that was still better than the alternatives.

I looked around the table, at the anger no one was trying to hide now.

"This is the standing direction. We do not answer Devereaux by tightening control over the Rice family. We answer him by tightening control over ourselves. Every authority gets an owner. Every owner gets review. Every secret order leaves a second trail. Protect, not possess, was not a slogan he was entitled to reinterpret. It remains policy."

No murmur this time. One by one, people answered.

When the meeting ended, Baines stayed behind.

"Mr. President, Mrs. Rice may never trust this government after today."

"She may be right not to."

"That complicates protection."

"Then protect her without demanding gratitude. We are not owed the feeling."

Outside the room, staff were already beginning the work. Inside it, Devereaux's empty chair remained where it was until everyone else had left, a useful reminder that the gravest threat to the decision had not broken through a door.

He had been invited in.
