Chapter 18 of 49

Cent POV: I know what my limits are. I found out the hard way. Same as you did.

Grebenchekov volunteered before anyone finished asking him.

Truman had gone to Johnson's protected medical wing himself to ask in person, which told me, when I heard about it afterward, exactly how seriously he took the asking; a phone call would have been easier for everyone, and he'd chosen the harder, more respectful version anyway.

"You want a man to sit in a chair while a girl who saved his life pushes him toward a dead woman's orbit," Grebenchekov said, when Truman finally worked up to the actual question, careful, giving him every opportunity to say no. "I have been waiting for you to ask since the moment I heard you were building the test craft."

"Your vascular team may not agree with your enthusiasm," Truman told him, flat, laying the actual risk out plainly rather than letting the moment's warmth paper over it. "I need you to hear the real numbers before you say yes again."

"I have heard the real numbers from three different doctors in two languages. I am aware of my own repair, General. The graft is stable. I would like to use the life it preserved on something worth using it on."

His vascular surgeons and flight physicians cleared him with conditions: continuous monitoring, abort criteria written in blood-pressure numbers nobody wanted to see triggered, and a promise from me, extracted in the same breakfast conversation where he volunteered, that I would call it off the instant anything looked wrong, whether he agreed or not.

"I know what my limits are," he told me, when I brought that condition to him directly instead of letting it come through NASA. "I found out the hard way, once, on a station where nothing broke except an aneurysm inside me, with a girl I'd known eleven minutes. I trust you to find them again if I can't find them myself in time."

Joe came to Houston for it, uninvited by anyone official and unwilling to be talked out of it, and stood at the back of the mission control room Truman had stood up for the flight. He didn't have a role there, not officially, not anything anyone had given him a badge for. He just wasn't going to be anywhere else while I flew a stranger's life through open space on nothing but my own two hands. Truman noticed him within the first ten minutes and didn't ask him to leave.

I flew the boost myself, seated across from Grebenchekov at the fixture, both of us braced, both of us wired with instrumentation from three different agencies now instead of just NASA's. The theory was simple. The mission was not: get a person, not cargo, up to real velocity using discrete kicks, hold formation the whole way, and bring him back down again clean, proving the crewed configuration, the timing, and the whole incremental method actually worked on something that could talk back to me if I got it wrong.

The first dozen kicks went exactly like the simulations. Three-second spacing, small clean pushes, my own drift corrected between each one, Grebenchekov's face settling from tense to something almost bored by kick six. He'd stopped bracing for disaster. He trusted the process now.

Kick thirteen went wrong.

I felt it before the telemetry did: a wrongness in the change itself, like stepping through a door that was supposed to be locked and finding it already open. The craft's actual center of mass had moved. Grebenchekov had shifted in his restraints, minor, unconscious, the kind of thing a body does without meaning anything by it, and a loose coolant line I hadn't accounted for had shifted half a kilogram of fluid along with him. Together they moved the load path off the point we'd calculated thirty seconds earlier.

The craft didn't just accelerate. It tumbled, slow at first and then faster, a slight cant becoming a real one, and an alarm somewhere in the cabin started up, sharp and insistent, and Grebenchekov's voice came through clipped and controlled in a way that told me exactly how much control it was costing him to sound that way.

"Cent."

"I've got it."

I didn't have it yet. I had a tumbling, occupied spacecraft with a cosmonaut's repaired abdominal aorta strapped inside it, and maybe two more kicks' worth of margin before the lateral load drove his blood pressure past the vascular team's abort line. In that half-second, I did not actually know I could fix it.

I fixed it anyway. Jumped in tighter, re-read the craft's real center of mass off the tumble itself instead of the outdated calculation, and put a corrective kick exactly where it actually needed to go instead of where the plan said it should go. It caught. The tumble slowed, steadied, stopped, and the alarm cut off into a silence that felt louder than the alarm had.

"Status," I said, my own voice not as steady as I wanted it.

"Elevated heart rate. Blood pressure under the abort line. Graft monitor stable." Grebenchekov, and underneath the calm I heard exactly how much control it cost him. "You may resume, when you are ready. I am not going anywhere."

I wasn't ready, not for another full minute, sitting there with my hands still on the grips, feeling my own pulse doing something it hadn't done since the tank. I wasn't afraid for myself. Nobody in that cabin doubted I could fly it. What scared me was specific and personal and had a face and a Dacron graft below his kidneys protecting the place an aneurysm had nearly opened all the way.

"Talk to me," I said, once I'd found my own voice again. "Really talk to me. Not the brave version."

"Really," he said, "my pressure is already falling and the graft readings are stable because you caught it early. I trust the correction. That does not mean the thing that caused it is allowed to remain."

"I thought it, for about a second."

"I know you did. I heard it in your voice." A small sound that might have been a laugh, tired. "That is the correct amount to have thought it. A person who feels no fear at all is not paying attention. Now call the abort. I did not come up here to help NASA pretend a correction is the same as fixing a cause."

Truman called it before I could. We returned to Kristen at low increment, Grebenchekov under continuous monitoring, the loose coolant line locked down only well enough to get us home.

The investigation took eleven days.

The fluid shift was not one problem. It was three: a retaining clip rated for vibration but not repeated lateral impulses; a center-of-mass model that assumed secured consumables stayed secured; and a control procedure that recalculated between mission phases instead of before every kick. Truman would not clear another occupied flight until all three were gone.

Osei replaced the clip with a double-captured line guide and then replaced every similar clip on the craft whether it had moved or not. The software team turned the static center-of-mass solution into a live estimate fed by seat sensors, tank levels, and the yoke's own load cells. We ran the repaired craft unoccupied, shifting ballast on purpose between kicks to make the model chase a moving center. It lagged on the first test by four tenths of a second, enough to matter. They rewrote it. On the second test a sensor disagreement lit the amber warning before the next kick. I held. We forced the mismatch across the abort threshold and the cabin alarm joined it. Neither signal prevented a jump; they told me exactly how bad an idea the jump had become. On the third test, the ballast moved, the estimate moved with it, and the chair bearing adjusted two degrees before my hands ever closed around the brace.

Only then did Truman put Grebenchekov back in the seat.

"You understand," Truman told both of us, "that success today does not erase the abort. The abort is why success today deserves to count."

We finished the flight on the second attempt. Kick after kick ran clean, the dynamic model re-checking the real center of mass before every cycle instead of trusting the plan's older version of it, and we brought the craft home the same way, decelerating burst by burst, until it hung steady relative to Hyacinth's tracked orbit with sixty-five careful kilograms drifting nearby, exactly where the catalog had always said she'd be.

Later, once he was cleared and resting and the telemetry had been picked apart by four separate specialists who all agreed the correction had been, in Osei's words, "textbook, once you account for how far off textbook the actual problem was," Grebenchekov asked me the thing I'd been braced for since the moment his heart rate spiked on that recording.

"Would you ask me to fly again? For the real mission, not a test."

I hesitated, and he saw me hesitate, and didn't let it stand.

"I know what my limits are," he said again, quieter this time, without an audience to perform steadiness for. "I found out the hard way, on my own, same as you did, that afternoon with the satellite bus everybody still whispers about. You did not stop testing yourself because you found a wall. You found where it was, and you built a chair, and you kept going smarter instead of not at all. Let me do the same."

"It's your repaired aorta."

"It is my body, and my choice what to do after the surgeons and you gave it back to me. You do not get to make that choice smaller than it is, out of love for me. I would not let you."

I thought about Dad, at that kitchen table, being told the same thing by his own daughter, and understood, all at once, how much of what I'd said to him that night I now owed right back to a cosmonaut with a damaged aorta and a smile that didn't reach all the way to worried.

"Okay," I said. "Then yes. When the real mission's crewed, I want you on it."

"Good," he said. "I was going to volunteer either way. This is simply more polite."

Joe found me afterward, in the corridor outside the medical bay, and didn't say anything at all. He just held on, hard enough that I felt it in the same shoulder I'd already half-wrecked once that year.

"I heard the alarm," he said finally, into my hair. "I heard your voice do the thing it does when you're scared and trying not to sound like it. I have never in my life wanted so badly to be useful and had absolutely no idea how."

"You were useful. You were there."

"That's not the same as useful."

"It's exactly the same as useful," I said. "Ask Truman. He noticed you standing there the whole time and didn't say a word about it, which from him is basically a job offer."

He laughed, unsteady, still not letting go. "I don't have anything to offer a room like that. I'm not an engineer on this. I'm not a pilot."

"You're the person who stands in the room and doesn't look away," I said. "That's rarer than the engineering. Truman noticed. So did I."
