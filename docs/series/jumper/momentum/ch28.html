Chapter 28 of 49

Cent POV: The localizer is lying.

Three days later, Dad and I joined the fifth review from the beginning. The run mixed failures instead of giving them to the crew one at a time. Iverson and Childs had stopped spending the opening minutes waiting for the simulator to hurt them. They checked the machine, checked each other, and went to work.

The de-orbit burn ended late. One inertial unit began drifting during entry. Communications returned for twelve seconds, disappeared for thirty, then came back carrying half a transmission. Over rising terrain, two radar altimeters disagreed by six hundred feet.

The microwave landing system captured a localizer and painted a runway on Childs's display.

"Field acquired," Timmins said.

Childs checked it against the two inertial units that still agreed.

"No," Childs said. "The localizer is lying."

"Signal is valid," Timmins said.

"Signal can be valid and belong to the wrong airport. That runway is outside our footprint," Childs said.

Iverson left the false steering bar centered on his display but did not follow it. "What reaches?"

The terrain map showed a private runway first. Too far. A state highway turned across our path but ran downhill into a town. Beyond both was a broad dry floodplain, nearly level, no structures, long enough if its surface held.

Houston came back through static.

"Endeavour, ground radar places you six miles south of indicated track. Repeat, six miles south."

That agreed with the two inertial units Childs trusted.

"Floodplain is reachable," Childs said. "Runway isn't. Surface confidence moderate. No approach obstacles."

"Build it."

The bad localizer kept commanding a right turn. Childs blanked it from her working display. The radar altimeters continued disagreeing, so she used inertial descent, terrain elevation, and visual closure.

"One thousand feet above surface," Childs said. "Descent high by four hundred feet per minute."

Iverson corrected.

"Five hundred," Childs said. "Surface looks firm on the east half. Standing water west."

He moved east.

The mains touched dry earth. Dust erased the rear cameras. Iverson held the nose up until speed fell, then lowered it gently. The floodplain carried us without collapsing.

When the platform stopped, Iverson looked tired, not battered. Childs was already building the discrepancy list. The runs were not getting easier. They were getting harder to shake.

In the review, Truman put the valid localizer signal beside the ground track that proved it belonged to the wrong airport.

"Nothing failed there," I said.

"Correct," Childs said. "It told the truth about the wrong place."

That bothered me more than a broken sensor. A red light announced a problem. This had been a clean green answer that would have killed them if they had obeyed it without asking what the answer described.

Truman scheduled the last shuttle evaluation for the following week.
