Chapter 14 of 49

Davy: Protection is not a polite word in this family.

Cent made it through the conditions first.

Family location compartmented. Medical records reviewed. No ownership language. Right of refusal. Threat disclosure. I listened from the far end of the old hotel kitchen and felt, against my will, a little of the pressure behind my ribs ease. It was a good list. Better than the one I'd have trusted myself to write in the mood I was in.

Then she got to the protective detail.

"A what?"

I was on my feet before the chair finished scraping backward. The words hit the old kitchen hard enough to come back from the tile.

Cent had the sense not to tell me to calm down. "Secret Service. Small team, rotating. Protective intelligence all the time, physical coverage when I'm at NASA or anywhere public. They want coverage on you and Mom too, if you consent."

"No. Absolutely not. You do not put armed government agents on my daughter and call it a favor."

"That's not what I said."

"It's what they always call it before the vocabulary gets honest!" I drove a hand down against the table. Cups jumped. "Protection. Oversight. Temporary restriction. Every damned cage ever built by a government started with somebody choosing a word that didn't sound like bars."

Millie watched my face change from across the table. She didn't interrupt yet. She knew the difference between me being angry and me needing the anger to finish arranging itself into words.

"They do not get to put a team on your six. They do not get to learn every place you go, every person you see, every room in this building, and call the file protection. They do not get close enough to grab you and ask us to be grateful that their guns are pointed outward!"

"Dad." Cent didn't raise her voice. She didn't need to.

"Take me to him. Now."

"To who."

"The President! He made the decision. He can hear what I think of it without you sanding the edges off first."

Cent stared at me.

"You said I get to ask hard questions. I'm asking them where the answer lives."

Millie finally spoke. "He's not going alone."

That was how the three of us arrived at a screened visitor entrance on the east side of the White House less than ten minutes later. We jumped separately to a service alley two blocks away and walked the rest. Appearing without warning inside the White House was possible. It was also a stupid way to begin an argument about armed agents overreacting.

The first officer reached for his radio.

"Rice family," Cent said. "The President told me to call if the protection terms needed work."

The officer looked at me, looked at Millie, looked back at Cent, and made a decision I respected: he called instead of pretending the situation fit a normal visitor procedure.

We were waved through three checkpoints in under four minutes. People in the corridors knew Cent on sight. More than one face began with recognition and ended with a silent question at the expression I wasn't bothering to hide. Nobody tried to slow us down after the first call came back with instructions.

Truman met us outside the Oval.

"I was told the afternoon might stay interesting," he said.

"Then you were warned."

"Davy—"

"Open the door, General."

He opened the door.

The President stood when we entered, not for ceremony, I thought, but because he preferred meeting anger on his feet. Agent Baines was already there with a thin folder. Two members of the Presidential detail held the wall near the inner door.

"You do not put armed federal agents on my daughter and call it protection!"

I was three strides into the room and still getting louder. Both agents moved. One stepped into my path with an open hand while the other came off the wall toward my right side, fast and practiced.

The President raised one hand. "Stand down."

Neither agent looked happy about it. They stopped because he was the President, not because they had stopped considering me a threat.

"No," I said, pointing at the space the first agent had tried to close. "Don't wave that away like it isn't the whole damned point. I walk into a room angry, armed men decide where I'm allowed to stand, and everybody calls it security. You want to build that around my daughter."

"Protection is not a polite word in this family."

"I know," the President said.

"No, sir, you know a briefing!" My fist hit the President's desk before I decided to put it there. Behind me, a shoe moved against carpet. The President kept his hand raised and the agents held. "I know what it looks like when someone with authority says the danger is too large to leave a person in charge of her own life. I know how quickly a watcher becomes a handler and a handler becomes a guard. You want agents close enough to protect my daughter, you're asking for agents close enough to take her."

Baines answered instead of the President. "If any agent on my detail attempts to take custody of your daughter absent an immediate physical threat, that agent is outside the mission and outside my orders."

"Orders change."

"Then the detail leader carries written authorities Ms. Rice can inspect, and any change gets disclosed to her and the representative she named."

"Orders get classified."

"Not these."

"People lie."

"Yes," Baines said.

The agreement in her voice stopped me for half a second.

"That's why the design can't depend on trust alone," she continued. "No agent enters your residence or Kristen Station without a specific invitation. No continuous location beacon on Ms. Rice. No attempt to follow a jump. Public-site coverage stays at the perimeter unless a threat moves inward. Protective intelligence watches people moving toward your family, not the family itself. Raw family-location data stays in a compartment Ms. Rice can audit. She chooses a civilian representative for every quarterly review."

"Quarterly."

"Monthly for the first six months."

"And when she says she wants the detail gone?"

"Physical coverage withdraws unless there is a specific, articulable, immediate threat. Threat monitoring continues because we do not need to surveil her to notice someone buying weapons and asking for her schedule."

Millie moved up beside me. Her anger ran colder than mine and generally saw further.

"What about us?" she asked. "Not whether you'll put agents in the hotel. What happens when someone decides we're leverage?"

Baines opened the folder. "Panic channels independent of the local network. Exterior cameras you control, with an alert mirror we see only when your system trips it. Advance warning on identified surveillance. Safe locations offered, never ordered. Training for any family member who wants it. And a standing instruction that a threat against you is treated as a threat against Ms. Rice's continued voluntary partnership, not as a reason to direct her behavior."

"That last part came from me," the President said. "I wanted it written so nobody later decides the easiest way to protect your daughter is to give her family instructions."

I looked at him. "You understand she'll leave if you try."

"Yes."

"You understand she can."

"General Sterling spent an hour making that point."

"You understand that if one of your people confuses protection with possession, there may not be a second meeting."

"Yes."

He gave each answer without bristling, which helped. Not enough, but some.

Cent had been quiet, letting me spend the fear in the form I'd chosen. She spoke now.

"This is my call."

"I know."

"Do you?"

The question hurt because it was fair.

"Yes," I said. "And that's a lesson I keep having to relearn because the stakes keep changing costumes. I am not here to veto the detail. I'm here to make sure the version you consented to is the version that actually shows up."

The President looked at Baines. "Can we put that sentence at the top of the order?"

"Not in those words."

"Use better ones."

She made a note.

We stayed another hour. Not a briefing: a negotiation. I asked ugly questions. Millie asked practical ones. Cent cut off both sides whenever we started discussing her like she wasn't in the room. Truman mostly listened, offering a NASA facility detail only where it actually fit. By the end, the physical team had become smaller, the perimeter rules clearer, the review schedule faster, and the distinction between watching Cent and watching for threats against Cent written three different ways in case one of them failed.

At the door, the President stopped me.

"Agent Baines predicted this meeting, Mr. Rice."

"Did she."

"She said you would hear 'detail' and think 'custody.'"

"She was right."

"She also said that was rational."

I looked past him at Baines, who had gone back to her folder without any sign that she cared whether I thanked her.

"Good hire," I said.

"One of my best."

The President turned to Baines. "One more standing order. If Ms. Rice or anyone in her immediate family comes to any White House gate asking for help or asking to see me, they get screened, brought inside, and your office and my Chief of Staff are notified immediately. They do not get told to make an appointment. They do not wait on the sidewalk while three agencies decide whose problem they are. Effective now."

"Yes, sir," Baines said.

When we were done, we walked back through the same controlled corridors to the screened entrance where we'd arrived. We could have jumped from the Oval. None of us did. It wasn't respect for the man behind the desk; I was still too angry for that. It was respect for the room, and for what the room was supposed to mean even when the person sitting in it got something wrong.

At the east entrance, the officer on duty logged us out. Millie jumped first. I followed her home. Cent arrived in the hotel kitchen a breath after I did.

Back in the hotel kitchen, Millie put the kettle on.

"Feel better?" she asked.

"No."

"Trust it more?"

I considered the actual question.

"A little."

Cent leaned against the old banquet table, tired in a way no orbital test had made her look.

"That's enough for tonight," she said.

It wasn't approval. It wasn't surrender. It was the thing this family had always done when fear and freedom wanted the same space: draw the line where everyone could see it, then keep watching the people who promised not to cross.
