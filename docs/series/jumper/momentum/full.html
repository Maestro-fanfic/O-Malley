Momentum

Chapter 1: Cent: It worked once. That's not the same as knowing it works.

Tuesday mornings on Kristen Station meant net inspections, and net inspections meant floating along sixty meters of truss with a flashlight and a checklist, looking for failures that were never supposed to happen. I'd done it every Tuesday for four months. I was, if anything, getting bored with it, which should have told me something.

"Net one, clear," I said into the comm, working my way hand over hand along the rail. "Net two." I stopped. Looked again. "Net two, clear. Same as last week."

"Copy," Sterling said, from wherever Sterling actually was these days, an office in Colorado Springs I'd never seen and had stopped trying to picture. "Cargo manifest still says water tank, aft cradle three, four hundred kilos nominal."

"Manifest's right. It's sitting pretty."

"Good. COLA's got two objects transiting your altitude band in the next six hours, nothing close, just wanted eyes confirmed before I signed off on the report."

"Eyes confirmed. Tell your board to relax."

"Tell my board yourself sometime. I'd enjoy watching that conversation."

I finished the rest of the inspection the way I finished every Tuesday inspection, which was to say thoroughly and with the specific, low-grade satisfaction of a job that never had anything wrong with it. Kristen Station wasn't finished, wasn't going to be finished for a long while yet, but it was solid, it was ours, and every Tuesday it held together exactly the way it was supposed to.

I was three-quarters of the way back to the hub, thinking about lunch, when the truss jumped under my boots.

Not a big jump. A jolt, quick and hard, more felt than heard, the way anything mechanical announces itself out here where sound has nowhere to travel and vibration does all the talking. I froze, both hands on the rail, and looked back the way I'd come.

Net two was gone.

Not gone-gone. Failed. I could see the shape of it even from sixty meters off, torn webbing trailing off the end of the truss like a flag nobody had ever meant to fly, and behind the webbing, tumbling slow and stupid and completely unbothered by the fact that it wasn't supposed to be able to do this, four hundred kilos of water tank.

"Cent." Sterling's voice again, faster this time, flat in the particular way he got when he was working hard at not sounding alarmed. "Net two just failed. You have a visual?"

"I have a visual." I did the math without meaning to, the way you do math on a falling glass before you've consciously decided to reach for it. Tumbling slow, end over end, drifting off the truss at maybe half a meter a second. Half a meter a second doesn't sound like much. On the ground, it isn't much; the ground eats that energy for you, you stub a toe, you move on with your day. Out here there's no ground to eat anything. There's no air to drag against it, no friction to bleed it off, nothing at all standing between four hundred kilos of momentum and whatever happened to be in its way when it finally arrived somewhere.

The something, in about ninety seconds at that drift rate, was going to be the solar array Joe had spent six hours aligning that morning.

"I see it," I said, and jumped.

I put myself twenty meters out, matched to the tank's tumble, close enough to read the manufacturer's stencil peeling off the side. Slow spin, slight wobble, torn webbing whipping around on every rotation like a rope someone had let go of mid-throw. No way to grab it clean. Get an arm in the wrong place when that webbing came around and I'd lose the arm, or close enough to it that the distinction wouldn't matter much. I'd done that kind of math before, on a different piece of gear, a different afternoon. I wasn't doing it again.

What I actually needed to be was a wall. Something for the tank to push against that could push back without losing a fight to a strip of nylon webbing.

"Talk to me," Sterling said.

"Thinking."

Here's the thing I knew for a fact, the thing Cory had made me demonstrate for him twice in his office with a stepladder and a very worried expression on his face the whole time: I could change my own velocity in a jump. Not carry it. Not cancel it to match a new frame, the way any jumper could. Actually add some, or take some away, out of nothing, the way I'd added twenty feet a second straight up once and never used my legs to do it. Nobody had ever explained where the energy came from. Cory had tried, for about a week, checking numbers against numbers that refused to balance, and then given up and started calling it "the Cent effect" specifically to annoy me. I'd stopped chasing the why a long time before that. You use what works. You don't have to understand a hammer to drive a nail.

I'd done the other thing before, too. The actual push, not just the velocity change. Exactly once. The PAM-D, a year and a half back: a dead Delta third stage, two thousand kilos of tumbling junk nobody on Earth would ever miss, four hundred kilometers up with an entire orbit's worth of time to decide whether I actually wanted to try it. I remembered thinking it through out loud, to nobody, in my own head, the way you talk yourself into cold water: I could push one of those on Earth, easily, if it were in neutral. In micro-G, if I had something to push against, I could easily move it. Wouldn't know if I didn't try. And it had worked. Clean, first try, the perigee dropping four hundred klicks in about the time it takes to say so, gone into the mesosphere and gone for good, and me left hanging there afterward feeling like I'd gotten away with something.

That had been on purpose. Borrowed time, an empty sky, and nothing riding on being wrong except my own pride.

This was ninety seconds and Joe's array on the other end of the tumble.

I jumped again, in close this time, close enough that my forearms and shoulder were already set against the tank's shell before I'd finished arriving, no impact, no collision, just suddenly there with my body in contact and the tumble already trying to wrench me sideways with it. My hands steadied the contact; they weren't what moved four hundred kilos. I tightened through my whole body the way I had against the PAM-D, changed my own momentum, and made the tank come with me. Not an arm push. A jump delivered while braced to something else.

The tank stopped tumbling.

Not slowed. Stopped, dead, the way a spinning coin stops when you slap your palm down flat on top of it, except this was four hundred kilos of water and steel and the "palm" was something that didn't have a name I could give Sterling if he asked, which he was about to. I felt the reaction of it come back through my hands into my shoulders, a solid shove, real and physical and entirely too easy for the mass involved.

I hung there a second with my hands still on the shell, breathing harder than the effort should have cost me.

"Cent?" Sterling, careful now, the way he got when the silence on his end had run a beat too long.

"I'm okay. It's not tumbling anymore."

"How."

"Working on that part."

I did it again then, on purpose, body aligned down the truss toward the empty docking port instead of toward Joe's array. I changed my own velocity and kept the contact long enough to bring the tank along for the ride. Felt the moment we separated, its new momentum continuing without me, something that outmassed me eight times over moving exactly where I'd put it and nowhere else, because there was nothing out here to argue with me about it. No air to drag against it. No ground to eat the energy sideways. No gravity trying to bend the path into something curved and complicated. I'd told it where to go, and it went.

On the ground, I could push a truck if I really put my back into it and had good footing and a run at it, and the truck would move about as far as my legs could make it move before friction and the tires and Newton's third law all voted me down. Up here I didn't have legs in it at all. I had my own changed momentum, contact, and intention, and apparently that was still the whole recipe. Same thing I'd done with the PAM-D. Just a smaller truck this time, and about eighteen months less runway to be sure it would work.

"It's headed for the empty port," I said. "Low and slow. Somebody want to catch it before it decides to come back this way?"

Joe's voice, from over by the array, breathless in a way that had nothing to do with exertion: "On it. Cent, what did you just do?"

"Told the tank where to go. It listened. Story for later."

I watched it drift away, tumble-free, obedient, going exactly where I'd sent it at exactly the speed I'd given it, and thought about all the things around the station heavier than me that I'd never once let myself count as things I could just do, in the moment, without an orbit's worth of runway first. A section of the inner ring that needed nudging into alignment without burning fuel we didn't have to spare. Debris on a bad vector with nobody's math fast enough to plot an intercept in time. A person, someday, moving too fast in the wrong direction, with nothing between them and a very long fall that technically wasn't falling at all.

I'd known, since the PAM-D, that it wasn't a fluke.

I had not known, until about forty seconds ago, that it would still be there when I didn't have time to talk myself into it first.

Joe caught the tank at the docking port with both hands and a grunt I heard clean over the comm, and by the time I jumped back to the hub he'd already lashed it down twice over with a fresh net, the kind of overkill that meant he was still running on adrenaline and hadn't found anywhere else to put it yet.

"You want to tell me what that was," he said, not turning around, still checking the lashing a third time.

"I pushed it."

"You pushed it." He did turn around then. Joe had a good face for worry, unfortunately, all the tells right there on the surface where I could read them: a tightness around the eyes, a stillness that wasn't calm so much as held. "Cent, that thing outmassed you by a factor of eight."

"I know what it outmassed me by. I did the math on the way over." I caught the edge of the truss and let myself drift to a stop next to him, close enough that our shoulders touched, which I'd learned over the last year was the fastest way to talk him down off whatever ledge his imagination had climbed. "It worked. I'm fine. The array's fine. The tank's fine. Everybody's fine."

"This time."

"This time," I agreed, because there wasn't a version of that sentence I could argue with honestly. "I've done it before, Joe. Once. The PAM-D, a year and a half ago."

"You never told me you could do that."

"I never told anybody I could do that, except Cory, and only because he made me demonstrate the velocity thing in his office until I got tired of arguing about it." I looked at the tank, lashed down twice over, going nowhere. "I didn't know I could still do it under pressure until about four minutes ago. That's the actual new information here. Not that I can push things. That I can push things when I don't have an orbit's worth of time to decide whether I trust myself."

Joe was quiet for a second, working the last of the adrenaline out of his hands, opening and closing them against nothing. "Are you going to tell Sterling that part? The pressure part?"

"He's going to ask. He always asks the actual question eventually."

"He's good at his job."

"He's annoyingly good at his job." I bumped my shoulder against his again, gentler this time. "Come on. I promised him a full report and my lunch is getting later by the minute."

Sterling's full report, when I finally sat down for it, took about ten minutes and covered everything except the one thing I knew he actually wanted, which he saved, predictably, for last.

"So to be clear," he said, in the voice he used when he already had the shape of the answer and just wanted me to say it out loud for the record. "This is the same thing you did to the PAM-D."

"Same mechanism. Different circumstances."

"Different how."

"The PAM-D I thought about for the better part of an orbit before I tried it. Empty sky, dead junk, nothing on the line but my own pride if it didn't work. Today I had about ninety seconds and Joe's array on the other end of a four-hundred-kilo mistake. I didn't have time to talk myself into it. I just did it."

"And it worked."

"And it worked."

Another pause, and I could picture him exactly, sitting at whatever desk he sat at in Colorado Springs, running the numbers behind his own eyes the way he always did before he let himself say anything he might have to walk back later.

"I want that written up properly," he said finally. "Not for me. For the file. If this is repeatable under time pressure and not just under ideal conditions, that's a different category of fact than the one I've had on record since the PAM-D."

"It's not going in any file that leaves this station."

"I didn't say it was leaving the station. I said I wanted it written down somewhere that isn't just your own head, in case you're ever not around to ask." A beat. "That wasn't meant to be as grim as it just sounded."

"It's fine. You're not wrong." I thought about it a second longer than I meant to, the tank drifting harmless at the empty port, Joe's hands still faintly unsteady an hour later. "I'll write it up. Tonight. You'll have it by morning."

"Good. And Cent?"

"Yeah?"

"Nice work today. I mean that plainly, not as a preamble to a question."

"I know you do," I said, and meant it. "Thanks."

I called home that evening, the way I did most evenings, mostly out of habit and partly because the secure line into the old hotel still made a family call feel like an event instead of a formality.

"You sound tired," Mom said, before I'd said much of anything.

"Had an interesting day."

"Define interesting."

I told her. All of it, the net failure and the tumble and the ninety seconds and the push, the whole thing laid out plain the way I'd have told it to Sterling if Sterling had been the kind of person you told things to for comfort instead of for the record. Mom listened without interrupting, which was its own kind of tell; she interrupted plenty when she thought I was underselling a risk, and she wasn't interrupting now.

"You're saying this like it's a footnote," she said, once I'd finished. "The pushing-things-fast part. That's not a footnote, Cent, that's a new fact about how you work."

"I know it's not a footnote."

"Does your father know yet?"

"Not yet. You're first."

"He's going to want to talk about it the way he talks about everything that changes the shape of the danger you're in." Which was true, and which I'd been putting off precisely because it was true; Dad's questions always came from the right place and still managed to feel, some days, like being cross-examined by someone who loved you. "I'll tell him gently. Mostly gently."

"Thanks."

"How's Joe?"

"Shaken. He'll be fine by tomorrow. He's better at recovering from being scared for me than he used to be." I thought about his hands, opening and closing against nothing. "He's gotten a lot better, actually, over the last year. He used to spiral. Now he just gets quiet and checks the lashings three times."

"That's growth," Mom said, her smile surviving even the uplink's flat, compressed audio. "Get some sleep. Write your report for Sterling. And Cent, whatever this new thing turns out to mean, take it slow finding out. You don't have to solve all of it in one Tuesday."

"I wasn't planning to."

"You never plan to. It just tends to happen that way around you."

I laughed, because she wasn't wrong about that either, and said goodnight, and stayed for a while afterward in the quiet of my own room with the lights of Earth sliding by underneath, thinking about a tank that had stopped dead the instant I told it to, and a general in Colorado who wanted it written down somewhere that wasn't just my own head, and a boyfriend down the corridor who'd needed three passes at the same lashing before his hands stopped shaking.

I'd known, since the PAM-D, that it wasn't a fluke. Now Joe's shaking hands were part of the data too. I liked that considerably less than the part about the tank.

Chapter 2: Cent: This is free. No fuel. No reaction mass.

I wrote the report Sterling wanted that same night, the way I'd promised, and spent most of the next morning reading it back to myself over coffee and deciding I didn't like how much of it was guesswork dressed up as confidence. It worked. I don't fully know why it worked, or whether it will work the same way twice, or what the actual limits are. That wasn't a report. That was a shrug with better grammar.

So I called Sterling before he'd finished his own coffee, by the sound of it.

"I want to try something," I told him. "On purpose this time. Not another tank."

There was a pause on the line, the kind he used when he was deciding whether to ask a question he already knew the answer to. "Define 'try something.'"

"The tank. What I did to the tank. I want to do it again, small, controlled, where nobody's array is on the other end of it if I get it wrong."

"You want a test article."

"I want a lot of test articles. You've got a whole catalog of junk you're already tracking so it doesn't put a hole in something that matters. I want to borrow some of it."

Another pause, shorter. "You're not asking my permission. You're telling me what you're doing so I don't have a coronary when my board lights up with something maneuvering that shouldn't be."

"Pretty much."

"...Send me a window. I'll pull you three candidates, ascending mass order, nothing with an active mission dependency. And Cent, no cover story on this one. If anybody above me asks what Apex Orbital's doing out there, I'm telling them the truth."

"That's all I wanted anyway."

"You realize what you're actually asking me to do, don't you? I'm handing you live tracking data on real orbital debris so you can go push it around by hand, off any established protocol, with no insurance company on Earth that would touch this if I described it to them honestly."

"You could describe it dishonestly."

"I have integrity issues with that plan." He paused long enough to decide to say the rest anyway. "I'm doing it because whatever you did to that tank yesterday, on a ninety-second clock, with real stakes, is either the most useful thing that's happened to my catalog in a decade, or a fluke I need to understand well enough to rule out. Either way I need data, not a war story."

"You'll get data. Give me the window."

The window came through an hour later: three candidates, coordinates and tracking data attached, no fanfare, exactly the businesslike handoff I'd asked for.

The first one was small on purpose. A dead CubeSat, eleven kilos, tumbling lazy over the Pacific, no webbing to catch me across the wrist this time, just aluminum and a stripped solar panel that caught the sun in slow, patient flashes as it turned. I jumped to it slow, took my time lining up, folded my forearms across the frame with my hands only keeping the contact from slipping, and this time I didn't just survive what came next. I watched for it.

The change went out clean, a small flat shove delivered by my whole braced body, no drama, and I felt the reaction arrive a half-second later through my arms, shoulder, and ribs. It would've spun me if I'd let it. I hadn't. I killed it with a follow-up jump before it ever built into anything, the same way I'd cancel a bad fall: jump in place, kill the velocity, land like I'd never had it.

"Talk to me," Sterling said.

"It's not luck. There's a real kickback, every time, and it's not something I have to think hard about correcting. It's like catching a step I already knew was there." I watched the CubeSat drift off, unhurried, exactly where I'd told it to go, spinning gently in a way it hadn't been spinning thirty seconds before. "One push, one measurable shove back at me. Every time."

"Every time is a strong claim after one data point."

"Give me the second one, then."

The second was bigger: a Delta-K upper stage, dead weight north of six hundred kilos, close enough in class to the PAM-D that some part of me relaxed just recognizing the shape of it, the same kind of tank-and-nozzle silhouette, the same rust-orange thermal blanket gone brittle and peeling after however many years up here. I set my shoulder and forearms across the nozzle housing, hands locking the position rather than supplying the force, and changed us the same way I had the CubeSat, no more effort behind it, no less.

The kickback nearly took my arm out of the socket.

Not injury. Not even close to what it would take to actually hurt me. But there was no mistaking that six hundred kilos pushed back harder than eleven did, and pushed back a lot harder, not a little. I hung there a second, working my shoulder, filing that away without chasing it any further yet. There'd be a place where the number stopped being interesting and started being a limit. I hadn't found it. I wasn't looking for it today.

"You okay?" Sterling, fast.

"Fine. Just... bigger pushes back harder. Which, yes, I know, that's the whole idea of mass, I'm not being profound. I mean it's not free. Whatever this is, it costs something on my end too, and the cost scales with what I'm pushing."

"Noted." A beat. "Third candidate's a Cosmos fragment, small, similar mass to your first. Repeat run, if you want it."

I did. Two quick pushes on the same object, thirty seconds apart, both clean, both correctable, and somewhere in the gap between them, not a new test, not a separate thought, just the same breath I was already taking, it landed.

I wasn't burning anything to do this.

No propellant. No reaction mass thrown the other way to buy my half of the equation. Every satellite Apex Orbital had ever nudged into a better orbit, every dead thing Sterling's catalog had ever needed moved off a collision course, every gram of fuel anybody had ever budgeted for doing that kind of work from the ground: all of it existed because moving mass in space normally costs mass. You throw something one way to go the other way. That was the whole tyranny of the rocket equation, the thing that made every mission planner's life miserable, the reason a satellite's usable life ended the day its station-keeping fuel ran dry, not the day its electronics failed.

I didn't have that problem. I had never had that problem. I'd just never stood still long enough to notice I didn't have it.

"Sterling."

"Still here."

"This is free. Whatever I just did to that fragment, it didn't cost anything except me, personally, and a bruised shoulder. No fuel. No reaction mass. Every dead thing in your catalog that's ever needed a nudge: I don't need to bring anything with me to do it. I just need to be there."

The line was quiet long enough that I checked the connection was still up.

"I heard you the first time," he said finally. "I'm doing the math on what that means for the catalog and I don't like how fast the number's moving."

"Yeah."

"Same time tomorrow. Bring a bigger shoulder."

I laughed, actually laughed, hanging there over the Pacific with a dead fragment of somebody else's satellite drifting off exactly where I'd sent it, and thought that it was a strange thing to be tired and thrilled about in exactly the same breath.

I found Joe in the greenhouse when I got back, which was where he retreated to most evenings lately, tending a row of lettuce that had no business growing as well as it did on a space station and doing it anyway out of pure stubbornness.

"You've got that look," he said, not turning around.

"What look?"

"The one where you've found a new thing to think about and you're going to think about it whether anybody wants you to or not." He pinched a dead leaf off one of the plants and dropped it into the recycler slot. "How'd the testing go?"

"Better than expected. Worse on my shoulder than expected." I flexed it experimentally and regretted the honesty of the gesture when it actually hurt. "Three test articles, three clean pushes, no falloff, no accidents. Sterling's running the numbers on what it means for his catalog."

"And what does it mean?"

I told him the same way I'd told Sterling, except slower, because Joe didn't need the businesslike version; he needed the version where I let the meaning of it show instead of just reporting the result. No fuel. No reaction mass. Every dead thing that needed moving, movable, for the cost of showing up and a sore shoulder.

Joe set down his trowel and finally looked at me properly. "That's not a small thing, Cent."

"I know it's not a small thing."

"I mean it's not small the way yesterday's tank was small. Yesterday was you saving a satellite dish. This is you telling me you've got a free version of the thing every space program on Earth has spent seventy years being poor about."

"I hadn't put it that way in my head yet, but yeah. That tracks."

He was quiet for a moment, turning a lettuce leaf over in his fingers without really looking at it. "Are you going to tell your parents the scale of this? Not the tank-saving part. The part where you've apparently been sitting on a solution to a problem that's shaped the entire history of spaceflight."

"Eventually. Once I understand it better than 'it worked three times and my shoulder hurts.'" I came and stood next to him, close enough that our shoulders touched the way they had after the tank. "I'm not hiding it. I'm just not ready to hand it to anybody bigger than Sterling until I know what it actually is."

"Fair." He went back to the lettuce, and then, without looking up: "I liked the version of you from yesterday. The one who found out something new about herself under pressure and didn't fall apart."

"I liked that version too. I'm hoping to keep being her."

"You will be." He said it simply, like a fact rather than a hope, which was one of the things I'd come to rely on about Joe without ever quite telling him so. "Come on. Dinner. You can tell me about the Delta-K over food instead of over a lettuce row."

I let him steer me toward the galley, my shoulder aching in a way that felt, absurdly, like the good kind of tired, and thought that whatever this turned into, I was glad to have somebody in the room who noticed the shape of the new thing before I'd even finished explaining it to myself.

Chapter 3: Cent: Two kicks. Same object, different frames, no falloff between them.

Apex Orbital ran on ordinary Tuesdays too, same as Kristen Station did, and most of them looked nothing like a tank breaking loose or a general handing over live tracking data on debris nobody else was allowed to touch. Most of them looked like invoices.

"You're billing this as a standard mitigation pass," Dad said, over the business line, going through the week's paperwork the way he did every Monday morning with the particular thoroughness of a man who'd spent years making sure nothing about this family left a trail that could be used against it later. "Same rate as the Iridium job in March?"

"Same rate. It's the same kind of work. Coordinates, jump, push, confirm. I don't charge more just because I understand it better than I used to."

"You could. Nobody else on Earth can do it at all."

"Nobody else on Earth is billing for it either, Dad. Let's not get greedy about a monopoly built on something I still don't fully understand myself."

He laughed, the short, dry sound that meant he agreed but wasn't going to say so outright. "Fair. What's on today's docket?"

"Fengyun fragment. Fast one, in and out before dinner. Close pass with an Iridium replacement, needs the miss distance opened up."

"Routine, then."

"Routine," I agreed, and believed it completely, which tells you something about how little I understood, that morning, about what "routine" was about to stop meaning.

The Fengyun fragment was supposed to be routine.

Sterling's catalog had it flagged three weeks out from the close pass, the kind of job Apex Orbital did four or five times a year now and billed for like anybody else: relayed coordinates, a jump to visual range, a push to open the miss distance up past anybody's nerves. I'd done a dozen of these. I wasn't thinking about the CubeSat or the Delta-K or any of it. I was thinking about getting it done before dinner, and maybe about whether Joe would want to trade the greenhouse duty for cooking tonight, since his lettuce experiment had produced actual, edible lettuce for the first time and he'd want an excuse to show it off.

I jumped to it, matched its tumble, got a hand flat against the housing, and pushed the way I'd push anything: same effort I'd have used on the Cosmos fragment, calibrated by feel more than math. The kickback came back through my palm right on schedule. I killed my own drift with a follow-up jump, same as always, and checked the numbers Sterling read me off the ground track.

Not enough.

"That opens the miss distance to about four hundred meters," Sterling said. "COLA wants six."

"It's not going to get there on one push."

"Can you get more out of it?"

I could've asked what he meant. I could've assumed he meant push harder. Some instinct that had nothing to do with caution and everything to do with efficiency told me harder wasn't actually the question worth asking. Instead I just did the obvious thing, the thing that didn't feel like a decision at all: I let the fragment finish tumbling away from me, jumped after it, same object, new position, a new little sliver of its post-push drift, then got my hand back against the housing, and pushed it again.

The second shove landed the same as the first. Clean. No falloff, no argument from the fragment about already having been pushed once, no sense that it had spent something on the first push that it now owed me interest on. Sterling read the new numbers back a minute later.

"That's six-twenty. You're clear."

"Copy. Heading back."

I jumped home, ate dinner, admired Joe's lettuce with the appropriate amount of ceremony, told him about the array alignment holding steady and not much else, because there wasn't much else to tell. It had been an ordinary Tuesday's worth of work. It wasn't until I was lying in my bunk that night, staring at the same ceiling panel I always stared at, that I actually turned the afternoon over and looked at what I'd done.

Two kicks. Same object, different frames, no falloff between them.

I sat up.

It wasn't that the first push had somehow not been enough and I'd made up the difference by trying harder. It was that there wasn't a difference to make up in any special way at all: the second push worked exactly like the first, full value, no discount for already having been kicked once. Nothing about the fragment's history mattered to what I could do to it now. I could've stopped there and called it four hundred meters and gone home unsatisfied, or I could chase it down again and again and again, a hundred small kicks strung end to end with nothing capping the sum of them except how many times I was willing to jump and how much shoulder I had left at the end of it.

That wasn't about me going fast. I'd already solved going fast, for myself, alone: I'd stood on Mars a year ago and come home the same afternoon, and going fast for one person who can already put herself anywhere she's been was never the hard part.

This was about everything that wasn't me. A tank. A section of station structure. A hundred meters of dumb tumbling junk with nobody aboard to ask if it minded being nudged. Things that had to get somewhere the ordinary way, riding fuel and thrust and the tyranny of the rocket equation the whole way there, except now maybe they didn't, not if I was willing to keep showing up and doing it again.

I lay back down. The ceiling panel hadn't moved. Everything else kind of had.

"You're thinking loud," Joe said from the other side of the bunk, not quite asleep either.

"Just doing math."

"Anything I need to worry about?"

I thought about the fragment, obedient, six hundred and twenty meters clear of anything it might have hit, having taken two kicks from me like it was nothing, like it would've taken twenty.

"No," I said. "I think I need to worry about it. Go back to sleep."

He didn't answer, which meant he had. I lay there a while longer, doing the math anyway.

I ran the idea past Sterling two days later, on a call that started as routine catalog business and didn't stay that way for long.

"I want to try something with the Fengyun fragment," I said. "Not the moving-it part. The repeating-it part."

Ochoa drew the first vial from Dad's loop with hands that shook hard enough for Reyes to steady her wrist.

We ran the analysis inside the capsule, all seven of us crowded around Ochoa's instruments while she worked through sample after sample with the meticulous, disbelieving joy of a scientist getting exactly what she'd hoped for and still not quite believing it.

"Liquid water," she said, finally, looking up at all of us with an expression I don't think I'll ever forget. "Actual, confirmed liquid water from Europa's subsurface ocean, isolated in Dad's loop. Trace minerals compatible with the water-rock interaction models, though one sample does not prove direct mantle contact. This is real. This isn't a projection or a model anymore. This is a sample."

"What does it mean," Iverson asked, "for the actual point of all this? Not the science. The bigger idea."

I thought about the answer before I gave it, because it deserved to be given plainly, the same way every other big moment on this project had earned a plain answer instead of a grand one.

"It means the method works all the way out here, under conditions nobody's ever tested it against before," I said. "It also means the twin is not a hose we can throttle. It's always person-shaped. For samples, we match the pressure. For bulk transfer, Dad's next receiver has to survive the gusher: a reinforced surge chamber feeding the reservoir, no evacuated space in front of the opening and no person anywhere near the first water hammer."

Truman looked at the clock trace from the successful test. "Thirty seconds."

"That's the useful window," I said. "Long enough for a full transfer, short enough that I know I can hold the twin cleanly. With the flow we just measured and a receiver built to accept it, even the largest reservoir in the current design catalog fills in about half a minute. No intermediate tank for me to carry. I twin the water directly from the source chamber to the reservoir that needs it."

"That is a sentence people on Earth are going to need time with," Iverson said.

"They can have time. The engineering gets more tests."

"That's not nothing," Grebenchekov said, quiet, echoing a phrase I'd heard once before, months back, from a flight surgeon who'd never wanted her own name remembered for it.

"No," I agreed, looking at the small vial sitting inside Ochoa's analyzer, the surviving fraction of an experiment that had begun by blowing a pressure vessel across the ice. "It's not nothing at all."

Chapter 48: Holcomb POV: Thirty seconds is not a small unit of history.

The first video from Europa made half the Situation Room duck.

We knew the pressure vessel had already failed. We knew everyone was safe behind the second ice ridge. We had Sterling's written summary in front of us. None of that stopped the body's first opinion when the forward cap crossed the screen fast enough to exist in only one frame and a white eruption swallowed the camera.

"Jesus," the Chief of Staff said.

David Rice, seated at the far end of the table because the replacement receiver was his design, watched the plume clear without blinking.

"Empty can," he said. "Ocean under pressure. It was always going to lose."

"You could have mentioned 'explodes like artillery' in the heading," Halloran said.

"I did. Somebody changed it to 'catastrophic receiver failure.'"

Every eye went to the briefing staff along the wall. One of them made a note I suspected would restore the original wording.

The briefing staff queued the successful test next. For the crew it had happened four days after the explosion, once David's replacement chamber reached Europa. The new assembly filled the screen: flooded, pre-pressurized, accumulator at mid-stroke. Millicent opened the twin somewhere inside it. The piston jumped, found equilibrium, and held.

Five seconds.

Ten.

Thirty.

Connection closed. Chamber intact.

Ochoa drew the sample. The preliminary chemistry populated beside the video, numbers most of us could not interpret and one plain line all of us could:

CONFIRMED SUBSURFACE OCEAN WATER.

Nobody spoke for a while.

The Chairman finally broke the silence. "A person-shaped opening, held thirty seconds, at the measured flow rate."

"Fills the largest reservoir NASA put in the use-case study," David said. "If the receiver survives the first hit."

"Cities," the Chief of Staff said.

"Disaster zones," Halloran added.

"Military logistics," the Chairman said, because it was his job not to leave the implication politely unspoken.

"And every bad actor who can threaten the family," Baines said, because it was hers.

The room's awe lasted less than a minute before responsibility caught it.

"No operational deployment," I said. "Not off one surviving chamber."

David looked at me. "Cent will say the same."

"Good. Then nobody has to talk her out of saving the world badly."

I ordered three parallel programs. Science would validate the sample without contaminating or consuming the only material we had. Engineering would repeat the pressure-balanced transfer and build the full-scale surge receiver around the fixed person-shaped opening, with an unmanned destruction envelope for every early test. Humanitarian planners would identify real reservoir designs and real delivery sites without promising a drop of water to anyone.

Baines got a fourth program nobody announced: every name, company, and government entity that changed behavior after the result became known, watched for the difference between scientific interest and movement toward the Rice family.

"The result stays compartmented until the crew is home," I said. "Not because we own it. Because they are still inside Jupiter's radiation environment and cannot participate in the consequences from there."

The Attorney General glanced at David. "Family condition requires threat disclosure."

"Joe and Millie get the protective-intelligence summary," Baines said. "Cent gets the operational threat picture through Sterling. We do not bury a specific threat. We also do not send her every speculative cable while she's climbing off Europa."

David nodded. It was not deference. It was confirmation that the terms from our first ugly meeting were still functioning under pressure.

Before he left, he stopped beside my chair.

"Thirty seconds," he said. "People are going to hear that and think easy."

"I heard it and thought expensive."

"Good."

"You know how many private citizens have said 'good' to a President in that tone in this room?"

"More than none?"

"Now, yes."

He almost smiled.

After the room emptied, I replayed the two tests once more: the cylinder becoming shrapnel, the piston striking and holding, failure and success separated by a family argument, four days of fabrication, and the refusal to pretend the first answer had been close enough.

Thirty seconds was not a small unit of history.

It was also not permission to skip the next test.

Chapter 49: Cent POV: It worked once. Now we know it works everywhere we've bothered to try it.

We stayed at Europa another five days after the water sample, mostly for Ochoa's sake and partly because nobody wanted to be the one who said it was time to go home when there was still this much left to learn.

The trip back felt different than any other leg of this whole strange year. Not shorter, exactly; the numbers were what they were, seventeen days of coast bracketed by the same careful boosts at either end. Truman still called the full poll before each one. Familiarity did not turn anybody's go into a permanent condition. The trip just felt different, in the way a walk home always feels different from the same walk out, familiar in a way nothing about the outbound trip had been.

I called Sterling once we'd cleared Jupiter's own radiation belt again, timing it to land during one of his ordinary mornings back on Earth, the way I'd once called him about a tank breaking loose near a solar array, a lifetime and an entire solar system ago.

"You did it," he said, once I'd walked him through the sample data Ochoa had already transmitted ahead of us. "I've had that sentence prepared for a week and it still doesn't feel adequate."

"We did it. All of us. You included, since none of this exists without a general who decided a debris catalog was worth handing over to a teenager who wanted to test something."

"I remember that phone call," Sterling said, the particular warmth that had built between us carrying even across the growing distance. "I remember thinking you were about to tell me you'd broken something. I did not expect, on that call, to end up here."

"Neither did I."

"What do you want to do with it? The proof, I mean. Not the science. The bigger question."

I thought about that the way I'd thought about every big question this project had ever handed me: honestly, out loud, without reaching for something grander than what was actually true. "I want to keep doing exactly what we've been doing. Small steps, proven carefully, one at a time. There's a whole humanitarian case to be made for what this technique could eventually do, moving real water to real people who need it, and I want to get there. I'm not going to pretend we're ready for the full scale of that tomorrow. I'm going to keep doing this the way we've done everything else. Properly."

"That's the answer I was hoping for," Sterling said. "It's also the reason this entire program has worked as well as it has. You've never once reached for more than you'd actually tested."

The legal answer about Mom arrived before we did. She had spent eleven days in federal custody, then the rest of the investigation under release with counsel and a standing order to appear. Devereaux's office camera, Hunt's testimony, and the weapon recovered beside his desk established that he had drawn on her after she confronted him. A federal grand jury declined to indict. The President issued no pardon and the Attorney General announced no special immunity. The process reached an answer instead of having one handed down from the Situation Room. That did not make what my mother had chosen simple. It meant she would be standing at Johnson when I came home, free because the evidence had been heard rather than because anyone had made it disappear.

We did not land the carrier on Earth. We decelerated into orbit, brought it alongside Kristen, and put it back on the aft tether where the whole voyage had begun. Then I jumped the crew down in pairs to the secured receiving hangar at Johnson. Joe was there, and Dad, and Mom, and for one long moment before anyone said a single word, we all just stood there, looking at each other: seven people back from farther than anyone in history had ever traveled, and three people who'd waited for me the whole time, each in a different way.

"You're heavier than the last time I hugged you," Dad said, into my hair, laughing despite himself. "I assume that's the suit, and not some new trick you haven't mentioned yet."

"It's the suit, Dad."

"Good. I don't think I have room for one more new trick this month."

Once the debriefings were done and the crew had scattered back to their own lives and families, I stood alone on Kristen Station's observation deck, watching Earth turn beneath me the way I had a thousand times before. For the first time in a year, there was nothing on the schedule after sleep.

A tank had broken loose. I'd pushed it, without knowing yet whether the trick would hold under real pressure, and it had. A satellite bus had nearly broken my shoulder, teaching me where the wall actually was. A general had believed me before he'd fully understood me. A President had chosen protection over control, and meant it, mostly, though I understood now that meaning something in a room full of people didn't guarantee everyone in that room meant it back. A man who should have been trustworthy by every measure that mattered hadn't been, and my mother had made sure he never would be again, in a way I was still learning how to carry. A crew had trusted me with their lives, more than once, and I'd trusted them with mine right back. Mars had stopped being a place only I could reach alone. Europa had stopped being a rumor of water under ice and become, instead, a sample sitting in a lab on Earth, real, testable, proof instead of hope.

It worked once. That's not the same as knowing it works. I'd thought that, a lifetime ago, about a single frightened momentum change against a tumbling tank. I understood it differently now, standing here, having shifted, braked, and twinned my way across more of the solar system than anyone before me ever had. It had worked once. Now we knew it worked everywhere we'd bothered to actually try it, carefully, on purpose, one honest test at a time.

Sterling called again that evening, later than his usual hour, and I heard something in his voice before he even said a word, the particular quality of a man who'd worried a question long enough that he'd finally decided to ask it.

"Can I put something to you," he said, "that I don't want you to answer tonight?"

"Go ahead."

"We've proven this works on everything the solar system's had to offer. Mars. Europa. Every class of debris in my old catalog. Osei has already sketched the ugly first version of what comes next: an empty shell launched and assembled at Kristen, light enough for you to boost, filled with shielding water only after the acceleration phase, then vented before deceleration so the mass comes back off before you have to stop it. I have started, against my own better judgment, wondering what the actual ceiling is. Not the G-force ceiling. The real one. How far does this go, if we ever decide to find out?"

I looked out at the stars past Earth's own curve, at all that dark, patient distance between here and anywhere else, and felt something stir in me that I recognized, distantly, as the same feeling I'd had the very first time Cory demonstrated the velocity trick in his own cluttered office, years before any of this: not fear, and not quite readiness either. Just the particular, quiet curiosity of a person who'd never once found a wall she didn't eventually want to know the true shape of.

"Not tonight," I said. "But ask me again sometime. I have a feeling I already know what I'm going to say."

"I had a feeling you might," Sterling said, smiling all the way from Earth. "Get some rest, Cent. You've earned it. Whatever comes next can wait until morning."

I stayed at the window a while longer after we hung up, watching the Earth turn, the same patient, ordinary motion it had always made, indifferent to how far any of us had traveled or how far any of us might go next. Somewhere out past all of it, past Mars, past Jupiter, past even Europa's quiet, ancient ocean, there was more dark, more distance, more of whatever this whole strange gift of mine was actually for.

It could wait until morning. For now, I let myself just watch the Earth turn, and be, for one more quiet night, exactly as far as I'd already come, and no further.

## Spot the Moment This Stopped Being a Test

The rules are simple: every time somebody insists they are only testing one small thing, put a marker on the next permanent piece of space infrastructure that appears because of it.

Possible starting points include the water tank, the first adapter, the gimbaled chair, the Mars vehicle, and the Europa chamber.

Anyone who chooses Europa is eliminated for failing to recognize scope creep at interplanetary distances.

Anyone who chooses the chair must explain why eleven thousand people were needed for something that was supposedly not a program yet.

Anyone who chooses the water tank is technically closest, but still wrong.

The correct answer is the instant Cent pushed back.

There is no prize. The prize money has been reassigned to the next validation campaign Cent is pretending not to plan.
