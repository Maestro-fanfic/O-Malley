Chapter 16 of 49

Cent POV: Space Girl's going to have some new tricks. That's all I can say right now.

Nobody had asked me if I wanted a press conference, which was probably the right call, because I'd have said no, and Truman had learned somewhere along the way that this particular no wasn't one he could afford to honor.

"You're already public," he told me, the week before, in the same plain, unsoftened voice he used for everything. "Space Girl's a known quantity. A private contractor doing debris mitigation for the Air Force is a known quantity. What we're building now is not a known quantity, and if we don't control how the first version of this story gets told, somebody else will control it for us, and they won't have your interests anywhere near the front of their mind."

"So we tell people what, exactly? 'I can move things without fuel and NASA's building me a spaceship'?"

"We tell them exactly as much as is true and exactly as little as gets the job done. You're partnering with NASA on advanced propulsion research. That's not a lie. It's just not the whole shape of the truth yet, and it doesn't need to be, not on day one."

Baines's people could not escort me to Houston in any useful sense, so they built the plan around the way I actually traveled. I jumped into a preparation room they had swept and locked, called the detail leader on the phone mounted beside the door, and waited while he confirmed that the service corridor was clear. The press entered through the front after their credentials and equipment had been checked. Two agents held the auditorium doors, another watched the loading entrance, and nobody tried to stand close enough to me to pretend they could stop a jump.

The press conference itself happened in a NASA auditorium in Houston, more cameras in the room than I'd ever stood in front of in my life, Truman beside me at a podium doing most of the actual talking while I sat at a table answering the handful of questions aimed specifically at me.

"Ms. Rice," one reporter said, a young woman from a wire service whose badge I didn't catch, "can you describe, in general terms, what this partnership actually involves?"

"In general terms, I can move mass in orbit more efficiently than any propulsion system currently in use. NASA's helping me understand the limits of that ability and build the infrastructure to apply it at a larger scale than I can manage alone." I'd rehearsed that sentence with Truman a dozen times until it came out sounding like something I actually believed instead of something I'd memorized, which, by the day of, it mostly was.

"Is this related to your work clearing orbital debris for Air Force Space Command?"

"It grew directly out of that work, yes."

"Can you give us any specifics about how the propulsion itself functions?"

"Not today," I said, which was the one line Truman had insisted I deliver word for word, no improvising. "We're still characterizing it properly. I'd rather tell you something true and complete later than something incomplete and misleading now."

A reporter near the back, older, more skeptical, the kind of voice that had clearly sat through a hundred of these and believed maybe three of them: "Space Girl's already famous for doing things nobody can explain. Is this just more of the same, dressed up in a NASA logo?"

I took a second before answering, because it deserved an honest answer instead of a rehearsed one. "Space Girl's a name Samantha and the girls gave me before anybody understood what I could actually do. The press borrowed it later and spent two years trying to explain me instead of asking me directly. This is the opposite of that. This is me and NASA actually doing the careful work of understanding something new, in the open, instead of leaving it to speculation. I'd think that's worth taking seriously, even if the source is unusual."

Somebody in the second row laughed, short and surprised, and even the skeptical reporter's mouth twitched at one corner before he wrote something down. Truman, beside me, didn't say anything, but I caught the small, satisfied stillness of a man who'd gotten exactly the answer he'd hoped for without having to feed it to me first.

The clip that actually went viral, out of the whole hour, wasn't anything Truman had planned for. It was a moment near the end, when a reporter asked, half-joking, whether this meant Space Girl was getting an upgrade.

"Space Girl's going to have some new tricks," I said, before I'd fully decided to say it, some old reflex from a hundred smaller interviews taking over. "That's all I can say right now."

Five minutes later the red camera lights went dark and Truman led me out through the side corridor. We were crossing the open end of the lobby when a man slipped under the rope separating a cluster of NASA employees from the press exit and came straight at me. He was calling my name, fast and urgent, one hand held out and the other buried inside his jacket.

The detail moved before I had time to decide whether he frightened me. One agent stepped into his path and turned him into the wall without slamming him into it. A second put herself between me and his concealed hand. The third took Truman by the elbow and moved him two steps back, which he accepted with considerably less surprise than I did.

"I just need her to see it," the man said. His hidden hand came out under the first agent's control holding a folded packet of paper. "I've done the equations. She needs to see them."

"Hands open," the agent said. "Both hands where I can see them."

The whole thing took perhaps eight seconds. The man had no weapon, only a borrowed credential, a sheaf of handwritten calculations, and the absolute conviction that access to me was something he had earned by wanting it badly enough. The agents removed him through the loading corridor. Nobody asked my permission while he was still moving toward me.

One of them sealed the packet in a clear evidence sleeve. Truman saw me watching.

"Security clears it, then it goes to technical intake," he said.

"You're going to have someone read it?"

"Breaking a security line doesn't make him right. It doesn't make his equations wrong either." Truman nodded toward the packet. "NASA has eleven thousand people. Somewhere in there is somebody qualified to check his work without giving him access to you."

The detail leader listened to his earpiece, then looked at me. "He's contained. No weapon. The preparation room is still secure."

"Good."

I went back to the preparation room, called Joe to tell him about it before the incident could reach him some other way, and jumped home.

It ran on every evening broadcast that night, that one line, clipped out of context and replayed with increasingly dramatic music under it, and by morning half the internet had decided I'd meant something between a superhero sequel and a national security threat, depending on which corner of the internet you asked.

Mom called before I'd even had coffee. "You're trending," she said, in the tone of a woman who'd clearly already read several hundred comments she probably shouldn't have. "Some people think you're joking. Some people think you've just declared war on somebody. One very confident man on a morning show has decided you're getting cybernetic implants."

"I'm getting a chair. An extremely well-engineered chair."

"I know that. The internet does not know that." I heard the smile under it despite the words. "Your father watched the clip four times and each time got slightly less scared and slightly more proud. That's about as good a review as you're going to get out of him this month."

Sterling's take, when he called an hour later, was characteristically drier. "I've fielded eleven calls this morning from people who outrank me, all asking some version of 'what does Space Girl actually mean by new tricks,' and I have told all eleven of them, truthfully, that I don't have clearance to answer that, which has bought us exactly one week before somebody above my pay grade demands an actual briefing."

"One week's better than none."

"One week's better than none," he agreed. "Use it well. And Cent: that answer you gave, about the source being unusual but worth taking seriously anyway? That was the best thing said in that entire hour, and I include my own boss's remarks in that assessment."

"High praise, coming from you."

"Don't get used to it," he said, his smile carrying even through the flat comm audio.

Truman called three days later with the packet's disposition.

"The equations hold," he said. "He reconstructed the discrete nature of your pushes from public tracking data on objects you've moved. Then he modeled what happens when a long flexible hull takes the same impulse at an absolutely regular interval."

"I knew enough engineering by then to hear the problem before he named it. "Resonance."

"Exactly. The test craft is short and stiff enough that three seconds never lines up with anything important. The carrier won't be. Osei's team ran his model against the preliminary hull and reproduced the coupling. They're adding live structural modes to the chair model and letting the interval vary inside the medically cleared window instead of striking the ship on a metronome. The rig gets a separate yellow SEQUENCE WAIT light controlled by that scheduler. It stays independent of the amber sensor caution and the red-and-audible abort chain. Yellow means the next kick isn't due yet, not that the hardware is unsafe. The green GO light requires both systems: the scheduler has to release the interval and the sensor vote has to be clean before it comes on."

"So he was right."

"He was right. Three independent teams say so." Truman paused. "His submission history was less impressive. He sent versions of that packet through three public NASA addresses before the press conference. Public Affairs sent him to technology transfer. Technology transfer sent him to procurement. Procurement told him there was no open solicitation and closed the correspondence. Nobody with the right background ever saw it."

"That doesn't excuse charging at me."

"No. Fixing our procedure doesn't pardon his conduct." Truman's voice stayed flat. "It does mean that by Friday we'll have a screened technical-submission channel with a rotating review panel behind it. Security decides whether a package is safe. Engineers decide whether the work is useful. Neither decision substitutes for the other."

I thought about a single sentence clipped out of an hour of careful, rehearsed truth, spreading faster and further than anything Truman and I had actually planned to say. The public story of this program would never be entirely mine to control, no matter how carefully I chose my words. If we expected people to take the work seriously, we needed somewhere serious for them to put what they had to say—not direct access to me, but a door that actually led to someone qualified to answer.
