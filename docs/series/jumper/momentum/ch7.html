Chapter 7 of 49

Cent: Cent! Are you okay?

NASA moved fast once Sterling made the calls, and I mean actually fast, not government fast. Inside a week I had a name and a badge and a standing invitation to a facility in Houston I'd never once needed to visit before, and inside two weeks I had accelerometers taped to my wrists, my chest, and the small of my back, proper instrumentation instead of me just reporting what I'd felt after the fact.

The first time I actually walked into a NASA building for this, rather than talking to Sterling or Truman over comm from orbit, it was a converted lab space with more cable runs taped to the floor than I'd ever seen in one room, and a dozen people who all looked at me like I was either a fraud or a miracle and hadn't yet decided which. Cory flew out for it too, delighted in a way that made me nervous, because Cory delighted usually meant somebody was about to learn something the hard way.

"You understand," he said, walking me through the wiring harness for the third time because he genuinely enjoyed explaining things twice as much as he enjoyed being right the first time, "that every single one of these sensors exists because I've spent two years watching you shrug off things that should have hurt you, and none of us actually know your baseline. We're about to find out."

"Find out how bad, or find out that I'm fine?"

"Statistically, in my experience with you specifically? Find out how bad."

He wasn't wrong, though it took the test articles themselves getting wired too, before the actual answer showed up.

It was my idea to push harder. NASA didn't ask me to do it. We'd established the relationship: bigger object, bigger kickback, no cap I'd found yet. I got impatient with the pace of finding it gently.

"Let's just find the wall," I told the room, over comm, from twenty meters off a fresh test article, a dead weather satellite bus north of a metric ton. "Full effort. Let's see what full effort actually does."

Nobody told me not to, because nobody knew what I was about to try.

I spread my body across two improvised points the way I'd been doing for a month, hands locking the brace in place, and instead of the calibrated change I'd been metering out, enough to move it, not more, I put everything into it, the actual ceiling of whatever it is I do, no holding back.

The satellite bus went where I sent it, hard and fast and exactly on the vector I'd aimed, and for about a tenth of a second that felt like victory.

Then the kickback arrived, and it did not arrive gently.

It felt like getting hit by a car. Not metaphorically: I've been hit by a car, once, years back, testing a suit prototype that failed worse than the car did, and this had the same quality of violence to it, total and immediate and entirely too fast for me to do anything graceful about. My own follow-up correction landed late and sloppy, and I came out of it with my shoulder wrenched hard enough that I felt it in my teeth and my wrist bent somewhere it wasn't built to bend, and I actually cried out, which I don't do, which scared everybody listening a lot more than the injury itself probably warranted.

"Cent!" That was Truman, and I didn't know his voice yet, not really, we'd spoken maybe twice, but there was no missing what was in it. Not procedure. Not a checklist. A man who'd forgotten, for one full second, that he was supposed to sound like NASA. "Cent! Are you okay?"

"...Yeah." I got the word out through my teeth. "Yeah. Give me a second."

I jumped home before I answered anything else, because home was where the good ice was, and because I needed thirty seconds where nobody could hear me not be fine before I went back and told them I was fine. When I came back on the line my wrist was already going stiff and my shoulder felt like somebody had taken it apart and put it back together slightly wrong.

"Talk to me," Sterling said, and I recognized the voice under the voice, the same flat control from the tank, except this time I understood what it cost him to sound like that.

"I found the wall," I said. "It hurts. A lot. I don't think anything's broken, but I'm not doing that again today."

The telemetry told the rest of it better than I could. Cory read it back to me two hours later, once the numbers were processed, and his usual delight had gone somewhere quiet and serious.

"The payload data alone should scare you," he said. "That satellite bus, on its end of things, pulled a G-load that would've killed a person outright if there'd been one aboard. Not injured. Killed. And your own biometrics..." he stopped, actually stopped, which Cory almost never did. "Your own accelerometer data says your body took a peak load that, if you were carrying a passenger who wasn't you, who didn't have whatever you've got that lets you shrug this off, would've broken bones. Maybe worse."

"So there's a ceiling."

"There's two ceilings. What a payload survives, and what you survive delivering it. And near as I can tell from one data point, you found both of them in the same afternoon, which, Cent, I need you to not do again, because I don't actually know how close to the second one you got."

I spent that night with my arm in a sling, which felt faintly ridiculous given nothing was actually broken, thinking about the satellite bus arriving exactly where I'd sent it, dead on vector, exactly as advertised, and how a person riding along with it would've been dead on arrival right along with it. It hadn't felt like failure in the moment. It had felt like success with a bill attached I hadn't seen coming.

Joe found me on the sofa with the sling and a bag of ice balanced against my shoulder. He didn't say anything at first, just looked at the sling the way he'd looked at the lashings after the tank, checking it three times without needing to.

"I wasn't there this time," he said finally.

"You couldn't have been. You were in the greenhouse. It's fine, Joe."

"I know it's fine. I'm still annoyed I wasn't there." He sat down carefully on my good side and took my free hand instead of my hurt one, which I noticed and loved him a little more for noticing himself. "Tell me the actual number. Not the version you gave Sterling."

"The actual number is I found out today that going full effort on the wrong object could kill somebody who isn't me, and it nearly did something serious to my own shoulder finding that out. That's the actual number."

He was quiet a while, turning my hand over in his, and when he spoke again his voice had gone careful in a way that told me he'd been thinking about something bigger than tonight. "This is going to keep happening, isn't it. You finding walls the hard way."

"Some of them, probably. I'm trying to find them on purpose from here on, not by accident. Tonight wasn't supposed to happen this way."

"I know. I just want it on the record that I don't love this part of the process."

"Noted," I said, and meant it the way Sterling meant it when he said it to me, which made him laugh despite himself, and some of the tightness went out of his shoulders along with it.

Truman called me directly that evening, which he hadn't done before: everything up to that point had run through Sterling.

"I wanted to hear your voice," he said, plain, no dressing it up. "Make sure it still worked right."

"It works. Bruised, not broken."

"Good." A pause, and I heard him decide to say the next part anyway. "I've sent people up before. Watched from the ground while they went, and there's a particular kind of helpless that comes with hearing somebody cry out over a comm line and not being able to do one single thing about it faster than the laws of physics allow. I didn't expect to feel it again quite that hard for someone who wasn't, technically, one of mine yet."

"I'm not one of yours."

"No," he agreed. "Not yet. But I'd like to fix that, if you're willing, because whatever just happened up there, we're not letting it happen again the same way. We're building you something better to do this with."

"What does 'something better' actually look like? You're not just going to hand me a bigger ice pack."

"No. I'm going to hand you an engineer, or six of them, and let them argue about it in front of you until they arrive at something that makes today's mistake structurally impossible to repeat." A dry note crept into his voice, the first I'd heard from him. "It'll be less dramatic than it sounds. Mostly whiteboards."

"I can do whiteboards."

"Good. I'll set it up." A beat, softer. "Get some rest tonight, Cent. That's not a NASA instruction. That's just me asking."

I thought about the wall I'd just found, and the number Cory couldn't quite say out loud, and the two things, payload and pilot, cargo and Cent, that both needed protecting from the exact same problem.

"Yeah," I said. "Let's build something better."
