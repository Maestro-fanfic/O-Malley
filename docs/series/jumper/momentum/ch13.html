Chapter 13 of 49

Cent: We protect this girl at all costs.

Truman called at six in the morning, which told me before he said a word that NASA was no longer the largest room involved.

"I need you in Washington," he said, no preamble. "Today, if you can manage it. The President has heard the technical and threat briefings. He wants the conditions from you, in person, before anything gets written into policy."

"Summoned by who."

"The President wants a meeting. In person. This morning." A pause, and I heard, underneath the flatness, something that might have been nerves, the first I'd ever heard from him. "The room has already agreed on protection over control. I believe they meant it. Today is where we find out whether they can turn that decision into terms you can actually live with."

"You understand it fine."

"I understand the engineering. I also understand that good intentions change shape when lawyers and security people start writing them down. That's the part that has me up at six in the morning instead of asleep like a reasonable person."

I jumped to Washington an hour later, to a nondescript building three blocks from the White House where Truman met me himself, tie already slightly crooked, the first time I'd ever seen him look less than perfectly composed.

"Rules," he said, walking fast, me matching his pace. "You answer direct questions honestly and completely. You don't volunteer anything nobody asked. If somebody asks something you don't want to answer, you say so plainly instead of dodging, because dodging in that room reads as hiding something, and honesty reads as exactly what it is."

"I can do honest."

"I know you can. That's why I brought you instead of leaving you home and describing you badly." He glanced at me, something almost like an apology in it. "I'm sorry to throw you into this with an hour's notice. The prebrief ran late, and the President wants your voice in the record before the bureaucracy gets a day to harden around its own wording."

The room they walked me into wasn't the Oval Office, which surprised me until Truman explained, low, that a matter this size went straight to the Situation Room, where the walls themselves were built to keep a conversation from leaving it. Around the table: the President, a compact, weathered man whose face I recognized from a thousand news photos and who looked, in person, both smaller and more solid than any of them had suggested. The Chief of Staff, sharp-eyed, already reading something on a tablet. The National Security Advisor, a tall woman named Halloran whose stillness reminded me, oddly, of Sterling's own particular flavor of controlled calm. The Secretary of Defense. The Director of National Intelligence, a heavyset man who watched me the entire time I crossed the room and didn't stop watching once I sat down. And behind them, along the wall, half a dozen senior staff, aides and note-takers, faces I didn't have names for and mostly wouldn't get.

"Ms. Rice," the President said, without standing, without much ceremony at all. "I've had four separate briefings on you in the last eighteen hours, and every single one of them contradicted the last one on at least one important point. So we're going to skip the polite version. Tell me, in plain English, what you can actually do, and then tell me what General Truman here wants to do about it, and then I'm going to ask you both a lot of very direct questions."

"I can move mass without fuel," I said. "In space, in microgravity, I brace against an object and push it, and it moves, and I don't throw anything the other direction to make that happen. I don't know where the energy comes from. Nobody does. It's been true since before I ever told anyone about it, and it's gotten stronger, or maybe just more deliberate, the more I've used it on purpose instead of by accident."

"And you've been to Mars."

"I've been to Mars. Alone, a year ago. Since then NASA and I have moved real mass, found a failure limit, built a chair and load yoke around it, broken an adapter in orbit, fixed it, and repeated the test successfully. The crewed ship hasn't flown. I'm not claiming ground we haven't earned. I'm saying the part we have tested is real."

"Do you know how many times I've heard someone in this room say, 'That's as far as the data goes'?"

"No, sir."

"Zero."

The President's jaw worked once, a small, contained motion, and when he spoke again his voice had an edge to it that hadn't been there a moment before. "I've spent twenty-four hours listening to people call this an anomaly. Enough of that horseshit. A young woman has already visited another planet, NASA has built a flight program around how she did it, and I found out three steps down the road. That ends today. Whatever this is, whatever it becomes, I want to know about it as it happens, not after."

"That's fair," Truman said, before I could. "That's on me. I made the call to build the technical foundation before I brought this upstairs, because I judged, correctly or not, that a half-formed program would waste your time worse than a late briefing would. I'll take the consequences of that judgment."

"You will," the President said, flat, "but not today, because today I need you useful more than I need you punished, and there'll be time to sort out the org chart afterward." He looked back at me. "What do you want out of this, Ms. Rice? Not what NASA wants. What you want. And I don't mean the mission. I mean the terms."

I hadn't expected the second half of the question, and I took a second before answering it, because it deserved a real answer instead of a rehearsed one.

"My family's location remains compartmented. Medical data doesn't leave flight medicine until I've reviewed what leaves and why. No ownership language, including the boilerplate version that somebody insists doesn't mean ownership. I can refuse any mission element without withdrawing from the whole program. Public disclosure gets discussed with me before my name or ability is used to sell a decision. My family gets told when a threat touches them. And nobody turns protection into custody."

The President glanced toward a woman at the wall I hadn't met yet. "Agent Baines, problems?"

"One clarification," she said. "We need authority to act against an immediate threat without waiting for permission from the person being threatened. That does not create authority to direct Ms. Rice's movements after the threat is contained."

"I can live with that if 'immediate' means immediate and somebody writes down who reviews it afterward."

"My office, the White House Counsel, and a representative you choose," Baines said.

"Joe," I said. "Or my parents. Whoever is available."

"Accepted."

The President pointed at Truman. "Put every condition into the agreement in language a normal person can read."

Truman nodded once. "Yes, sir."

"There are two NASA conditions in return," the President said. "You don't conceal a known safety limit from the people building around it, and when mission control calls an abort for a technical reason, you abort first and argue afterward."

"That's fair."

"Then we're negotiating instead of requisitioning. Good."

"I want to keep doing what I've been doing," I said, "at a bigger scale, with people who've earned the right to help me do it. I don't want a badge that says I work for anybody instead of with them. I've got a family who spent most of my childhood keeping me off any government's books at all, for reasons that were good reasons at the time, and I'm not interested in becoming the thing they were protecting me from."

"Somebody in this room," the DNI said, quiet, the first thing he'd said, "is going to ask whether an asset with this capability can simply be directed, given sufficient legal framework, regardless of her personal preferences on the matter."

The room went very still.

"I'll answer that," Halloran said, before the President could, before anyone could turn it into a real proposal instead of a hypothetical hanging in the air. "The legal framework you're describing would require compelling a private citizen's cooperation through some form of coercion, which is not only ugly, it's also operationally stupid. Everything this capability has produced so far, every test, every proof, every ounce of goodwill NASA's built with this family, exists because she chose to build it, in partnership, with people she trusts. Coerce her, and the very first thing you lose is the willingness that makes any of this work at all. You don't get a functional asset out of that equation. You get a hostage who does the bare legal minimum and looks for the nearest door."

"She's not wrong," I said, into the silence that followed. "I want to be extremely clear about this, since I don't think I'll get a more direct chance to say it. If this room decides the right move is to own me instead of partner with me, I will still exist. I will still be able to do everything I can do right now. I will simply stop doing it for any of you, and you will have spent an enormous amount of institutional effort turning a willing partner into someone with every reason to disappear."

The DNI's expression didn't change, which told me he'd expected that answer and had asked the question mostly to get it on the record rather than because he believed in it. The President looked at me a long moment, then at Truman, then around the table at the rest of them.

"Here's where I landed before you walked in," he said, in the voice of a man who'd made harder calls than this one and knew exactly how to make them stick. "This meeting is not deciding whether to protect you or control you. We already made that decision. We protect you at all costs. Not own you. Not direct you. Protect you, support you, give you every resource this government can put behind you, and let you keep choosing to work with us because working with us is actually good for you, not because you have no other option. The terms we just discussed are how we make the decision enforceable when good intentions get tired. If anyone in this room, or anyone under any of you, so much as looks at coercion as a live option going forward, I want to hear about it directly, because that person will not be working for me anymore. Is that understood."

A murmur of assent went around the table, general and immediate. I watched it happen and understood, even then, that a murmur of assent from a dozen people meant nothing about what any one of them actually believed, only what they were willing to say out loud in this particular room, in front of this particular man.

"General Truman," the President said. "You've got backing, budget, and my personal attention on this from here forward. Don't waste any of the three. Ms. Rice." He looked at me directly, and something in his weathered face shifted, just slightly, into something almost gentle. "Thank you for your candor today."
