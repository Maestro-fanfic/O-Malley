Chapter 27 of 49

Holcomb POV: Not enough runway. Still the best ground we've got.

I watched the fourth run from the Situation Room with Halloran, the Chairman, the Secretary of Defense, and two NASA flight specialists whose names I wrote down because I was getting tired of rooms where only the generals were memorable.

It was not the first one I had seen. Truman had sent over the complete Los Angeles reconstruction after the crew finished it. I watched the orbiter touch down, pull its own landing gear back up to clear a bridge, and stop under a scaffold with three living people inside.

"Seeing what those three went through that day, they deserve the Medal of Honor. All three of them."

Halloran wrote it down. NASA could supply the paperwork and the formal title. I had supplied the decision.

The secure feed gave us the simulator cockpit, the forward visual, and a telemetry page dense enough that one of the NASA specialists translated quietly whenever a number mattered. Truman sat behind the instructor console in Houston. He did not brief the crew that the White House was watching.

After a recovery day, he gave them farmland. Iverson and Childs climbed in with fading harness bruises and none of the guarded movement they had shown after the first run.

There was no runway, highway, dry lake, or floodplain inside the final footprint. Houston marked two fields among power lines, drainage ditches, fences, and narrow roads.

The first was longer, but wires crossed its approach and its far end fell toward a drainage channel. The second cost another turn and almost a mile of usable length. Its approach was clear, and the ditches ran along its edges instead of across the rollout.

"Second field," Iverson said. "Cleaner approach."

"Can we stop?" Childs asked.

"If the soil holds."

"Build it."

Childs turned for the shorter field. A final power line appeared below the nose, lower than the terrain model had placed it.

"Lines ahead," Iverson said. "Twenty feet below projected path."

"Visual."

She held the glide. The wheels cleared the wires. Timmins lowered the gear, and Childs put the mains between the ditches.

The left gear began sinking. Endeavour slewed twenty degrees. Childs kept the nose between the drainage cuts and let the soft ground take the speed. They stopped with the left wing over one ditch and the right main gear still upright.

The simulator marked the vehicle a total loss and the crew alive.

Nobody in the Situation Room spoke until the debrief began.

Truman put the three crew members at a table with the landing geometry on the screen behind them. He started with Iverson.

"You rejected the longer field."

"Yes, sir."

"Why use it?"

"Because the power lines could take off a wing before touchdown, and the drainage channel could take the gear after it. Length we couldn't safely reach or use wasn't margin."

He looked at Childs. "You accepted shorter ground with unknown bearing strength."

"The approach was clear, the soil looked firmer, and the ditches ran beside the rollout instead of through it," Childs said. "It was the only surface that gave us a complete landing."

"Timmins. Why no chute when the left gear started digging in?"

"By then the drag was asymmetric," Timmins said. "A canopy loading the tail could have increased the yaw and collapsed the right gear. Childs was already using the soft ground to take the speed."

I pressed the talk control on the table. The Houston room went still when my light appeared.

"One question," I said. "At any point did one of you make a choice to preserve the vehicle at additional risk to the crew?"

"No, sir," Iverson said.

Childs and Timmins answered with him.

"Good. General, continue."

The review lasted longer than the run. Every decision went back onto the timeline. Every rejected option stayed visible beside the chosen one. Nobody called the result perfect. They called it survivable and showed their work.

The post-run medical checks found nothing worse than harness bruising. Their heart rates came down faster than they had after Los Angeles, and both were on their feet before the physician entered the cabin. Truman still waited three days before the next evaluation.
