Chapter 22 of 49

Childs POV: The guidance said we were on course. Los Angeles said otherwise.

Endeavour was already committed to reentry when the instruments began lying.

Commander Iverson had the left seat. I had navigation and the right-hand flight instruments, with Timmins at the systems station behind us. We came toward entry interface carrying more energy than I wanted, and I had Timmins give me additional drag until the numbers settled. By four hundred thousand feet our attitude was correct, the guidance solution was green, and Houston confirmed a good entry configuration.

Then communications dropped out.

Timmins started the internal communications set. Houston disappeared behind entry static, then returned.

An alarm sounded almost as the circuit cleared.

The orbiter still flew. That made the failure harder to recognize. There was no loss of hydraulics, no control-surface fault, no warning that the vehicle itself was in trouble. The guidance needles stayed centered. The flight computers still reported us on energy and on course. The landing beacons agreed with them.

Iverson looked through the windshield, then back at the display.

"We're off course," he said. He looked outside again, longer this time. "We're way off course."

He had flown it enough times to know the sight picture without needing a label. I checked the predicted ground track, the approach geometry, and the beacon returns. The instruments agreed with each other. The terrain outside did not.

Communications came back in a wash of static.

"Endeavour, Houston. Disregard onboard guidance and landing beacons. Both are corrupt. Ground tracking shows you one hundred twenty-nine miles off course."

"Confirm present position," Iverson said.

The answer arrived half a beat later.

"Los Angeles."

Downtown was already visible ahead of us. Guidance and the beacons had both given us the same false answer, and now we were at fifteen thousand feet with roughly two minutes of glide remaining and no engine to buy back a single foot of altitude. The orbiter was flyable. The systems that were supposed to tell us where to fly it were not.

Iverson kept his hands quiet on the controls. "Options."

Houston began searching. Someone suggested clearing a freeway. The answer came back almost immediately: rush hour, bumper to bumper, no possible way to empty enough pavement before we reached it.

I pulled the local survey grid over the failed guidance display and looked for length rather than labels. Airports required turns we could not afford. Streets were interrupted. The flood-control channel ran almost straight through the city.

"I have an alternate," I said.

Iverson glanced across once. "Show me."

I gave Houston the coordinates while I worked the maximum lift-to-drag numbers by hand. The ground computers were still plotting when the Los Angeles River appeared on my map.

"Endeavour, Houston. The river solution is marginal but possible. Turn right to heading one-seven-five. You should have visual in five seconds."

Iverson rolled us onto the heading. The concrete channel came into view, broad enough for the orbiter and crossed by bridge after bridge.

"Those spans are closer than our wingspan wants," he said.

"We can make the channel if you bring us straight in. No lateral margin."

"Build the line."

I put the course on his display and started calling it.

"High and fast. Two-ninety feet, three-twenty knots. Two-seventy, three-oh-five."

Iverson held the center of the channel.

"Arm the gear."

Timmins armed it.

"Gear down."

The mains came into the airflow. The first bridge rushed toward us and then passed overhead with less room than any of us wanted to measure.

"Two-forty feet, two-ninety."

"This is going to be hard," Iverson said. There was no drama in it. He was telling the cabin what their bodies were about to learn.

The wheels struck concrete. Iverson lowered the nose.

"Speed brake."

"Full."

The river walls blurred past. We were still far too fast.

"Chute."

I saw the next bridge and stopped Timmins before he deployed it. "Negative. It will catch the span and take the tail with it."

Iverson banked us around a center support with the landing gear still carrying enough weight to steer. The right wing cleared by what looked like inches.

"Next bridge," I said. "We're too high."

"Gear up."

Timmins hesitated. "We lose steering."

"If that gear hits the span, we lose the whole vehicle! Retract it now!"

"Right." Timmins flipped the switch.

The wheels folded as far as the mechanism could pull them under load. Endeavour dropped onto her belly, metal and tile grinding against concrete, momentum carrying us under the bridge while the vertical stabilizer missed the underside by less than it had any right to.

"Harnesses locked," Iverson said. "We're committed."

After that there was no flying left to do. The orbiter skidded down the river channel on its belly, shedding tile, throwing water and concrete dust to both walls. The controls stopped answering because there was no longer air under the wings enough to matter.

We passed one more bridge and began, finally, to slow.

A maintenance worker stood on a scaffold beneath the next span with a grinder in his hands and his face shield down. He felt the vibration, stopped his work, and looked along the channel.

Endeavour slid to a halt directly below him.

For one second he only stared at the orbiter's nose where no orbiter could possibly be. Then the grinder kicked against the rail in his hand and he recoiled so hard the scaffold shook.

Nobody in our cabin moved.

Iverson let out one breath. "Status."

Timmins checked the systems. I checked the crew. Nobody was badly hurt. Endeavour would never fly again, but everyone aboard her had landed.

The flight review lasted six weeks.

They reconstructed the approach second by second: our high-energy entry and the added drag; the communications dropout and restoration; the geomagnetic instability that left both guidance and the landing beacons giving the wrong answer; Iverson recognizing the wrong sight picture despite instruments that still showed us on course; Houston's failed search for conventional pavement; my river coordinates; the hand-calculated lift-to-drag margin; every bridge clearance; the decision not to deploy the chute; and the decision to retract the gear before the last span.

The review board asked Iverson whether he had considered refusing my river solution.

"Of course," he said. "Considering an option is part of accepting it. Major Childs gave me the only geometry that reached survivable ground. Once I verified that, refusing it would have been choosing downtown Los Angeles instead."

They asked me whether I had exceeded my authority by substituting my own calculation for the guidance system and the ground computers.

"The guidance system was wrong," I said. "The ground was still calculating. We had two minutes. I gave the commander an answer and the evidence behind it. He made the decision."

Timmins's testimony was shorter.

"They were both doing their jobs. That's why the rest of us are alive."

The final finding assigned no crew fault. Endeavour's flight controls, hydraulics, and entry configuration had remained functional until the emergency landing; the pre-landing failure was navigational, a consequence of the same geomagnetic instability producing impossible events elsewhere. The board credited Iverson with trusting the view through the glass over a complete set of reassuring instruments, identifying the navigation failure, and flying an unrehearsed approach through an obstructed channel. It credited me with identifying the Los Angeles River and producing a workable landing solution before the ground system could finish one. It credited Timmins with restoring communications and executing configuration changes fast enough to make both decisions matter.

Iverson found me outside the review room after the finding came down.

"You understand why people are going to remember the river as yours."

"You flew it."

"We both did our jobs," he said. "Remember that version. It's the accurate one."
