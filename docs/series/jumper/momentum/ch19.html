Chapter 19 of 49

Cent POV: Nobody famous. Nobody irreplaceable. Just data.

After Grebenchekov, the next flight was almost boring, and everybody involved agreed that was exactly the point.

The test craft lived at Kristen between flights, secured to the station's aft cradle with a mechanical tether and hard power connection. It had reached orbit the same way the later carrier would: conventional boosters lifting the pressure-hull sections and fixture separately, assembly at the station, then checkout against Kristen's own stand-alone life support before we ever released it. Nothing that large had come out of Earth's gravity well on my ability, and every test plan said so in the first paragraph.

"No cosmonauts with repaired aneurysms," Truman said, laying it out at a briefing that lasted all of fifteen minutes because there wasn't much left to argue about. "No mission profile, no crew selection, no politics. Just a flight surgeon, a longer duration, and real physiological data on what repeated boosts actually do to an ordinary body over time, so the next set of decisions gets made off numbers instead of guesses."

The flight surgeon was a quiet, unflappable woman named Dr. Alvarez who'd spent a career on parabolic flights and centrifuge runs and had, by her own admission, "seen every way a human body complains about acceleration except this exact one," which she seemed to find more interesting than alarming. She rode the fixture's second seat through six daily free-flight blocks, kicks spaced out over long stretches instead of compressed into one dramatic afternoon, sensors on both of us reading heart rate, blood pressure, and whatever else six days of data-gathering could reasonably ask a body to hand over. We returned to Kristen's hard power connection each night, recharged both battery cassettes, and did not pretend a short-duration test article had the electrical endurance of the carrier we had not built yet.

Day one was mostly baseline: resting numbers, a dozen gentle test kicks spread across twelve hours, Alvarez narrating her own readings into a recorder with the flat, unhurried cadence of someone who'd made a career of careful, boring work and had long since stopped needing it to be exciting to find it worthwhile.

Day two she started pushing the interval, testing whether five seconds between kicks read differently in the body than three did. "Three's fine," she reported that evening, rubbing at a spot on her sternum where the sensor patch had started to itch. "Five's fine too. The body doesn't seem to care much past 'not immediate,' which is good news for anybody designing a mission profile who'd rather not argue about half a second one way or the other."

Day three she asked me questions that had nothing to do with the mission at all, mostly to pass the time, and I found myself telling her things I hadn't told the engineers: what it had felt like the first time I added velocity in Cory's office, what Mars smelled like through a suit's stand-alone life-support loop, which was almost nothing, a faint hot-metal note from the suit itself that never fully went away. She listened the way good doctors listen, half for content and half for what your voice was doing while you said it.

"You're remarkably boring to monitor," she told me, on day four, not unkindly. "Your body treats this like exercise. Elevated heart rate during the push, recovery within ninety seconds, no lasting strain markers even after this many repetitions."

"Is that good?"

"It's excellent. It's also almost certainly specific to you, which is the whole reason we're doing this: so nobody assumes your numbers are what everyone else's numbers will look like." She glanced at her own readout. "Mine recover slower. Nothing concerning. Just slower. Which is useful information in itself."

Nothing dramatic happened on the flight, which was exactly what the test required. Six days, dozens of kicks, no tumbles, no alarms, no shoulder that needed a sling afterward: just a clean, patient accumulation of the boring data mission designers needed, the difference between guessing at what a body could take and knowing it.

"Nobody famous," Alvarez said, on the last day, watching Earth turn beneath us with the same unhurried interest she'd given the readouts all week. "Nobody irreplaceable. Just data, collected properly, so that the people who do end up famous for this get to fly on numbers instead of nerve."

"That's not nothing."

"It's not," she agreed. "It's the part nobody writes the movie about. I like it better that way."

We came home on schedule and put the craft back on Kristen's aft tether before anyone called the run complete. The numbers went into the mission-design file. Nobody outside the program heard Dr. Alvarez's name, which she told me, cheerfully, on the way out, was exactly how she liked it.
