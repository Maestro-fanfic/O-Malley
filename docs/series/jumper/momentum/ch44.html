Chapter 44 of 49

Cent POV: That's not a planet anymore.

Seventeen days of coast, once the boost itself was done, turned out to be its own kind of education in patience.

Ochoa spent the stretch cataloging every Mars sample against the mineralogical database Nasser had built before launch, muttering happily to herself about formations they'd need a second trip to properly understand. Iverson and Childs ran a rotating series of maintenance checks that had less to do with actual necessity and more to do with the particular restlessness of pilots with nothing urgent to fly. Truman tried chess with Grebenchekov, lost faster than Reyes had, and accused the board of being insufficiently transparent. Every thirty-six hours I took one depleted cassette to Kristen and brought one charged cassette back. The expanded banks meant no single delayed exchange threatened the ship; they did not eliminate the rotations. Between them, I spent most of the coast the way I'd spent the Mars transit, present rather than active, though this time the passivity felt earned rather than strange, a rhythm I'd actually learned to live inside instead of merely endure.

It was on day eleven, drifting near the observation port with Childs, that Jupiter first became more than a bright point in the black.

"That's not a planet anymore," Childs said, quiet, watching the banded, churning disc grow visibly larger even across the short span of a single conversation. "That's a whole different category of thing."

"It's got more mass than every other planet in the solar system combined," I said, half to myself, half to her. "And a radiation environment that would cook a person alive in hours if we got the timing wrong."

"You say that so calmly."

"I've learned to say most things calmly. It doesn't mean I'm not paying attention." I watched the storms churn across that vast, banded face, colors no photograph had ever quite captured honestly, and felt something I hadn't expected to feel this far from home: not fear, exactly, but a very clear, very focused respect for exactly how much bigger this next step actually was than anything that had come before it. "Petrov's right. This is a harder problem. I intend to solve it the same way I've solved every other one. Carefully. On purpose. One kick at a time."

"I believe you," Childs said. "I've watched you do it enough times now to stop being surprised by it."

Ahead of us, Jupiter kept growing, patient and enormous, utterly indifferent to how small it made everything else feel by comparison.
