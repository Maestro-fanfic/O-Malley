Chapter 45 of 49

Cent POV: The sky itself is trying to kill us here. That's new.

"Jupiter's magnetosphere isn't like anything you dealt with at Mars," Petrov told us, three days out from orbital insertion, in the voice of a man who needed everyone in the room to actually absorb what he was saying rather than just nod along. "Trapped radiation belts, far more intense than Earth's own Van Allen belts, fed continuously by Io's volcanic activity. Get the timing or the shielding wrong and you're not looking at a survivable mistake. You're looking at a lethal dose within hours, not the gentler risk profile we managed with the storm shelter on the way to Mars."

"So we don't get the timing wrong," I said.

"No," Petrov agreed. "We do not. Here is the plan, and I want everyone to understand every piece of it, not just the parts that concern their own job."

The plan had been closed by mission control before the White House ever approved the extension: Petrov's radiation team, Osei's vehicle group, Iverson and Childs on navigation, Reyes on exposure limits, every console signing the part it owned. It threaded the carrier through Jupiter's inner system on a trajectory that spent the absolute minimum time inside the worst of the radiation belts, using the storm shelter's already-proven shielding for the brief, unavoidable passage through the roughest patch, then out again toward Europa's still-severe orbital environment. It was nothing like Earth's gentle magnetic cocoon, but the exposure profile left a survivable margin for a limited stay if the timing held.

"Real numbers," Reyes said, flight surgeon's voice, the one that cut through any comfortable vagueness. "What's our actual exposure, worst case?"

"Worst case, if the timing holds within the margins we've planned for: elevated, measurable, within limits NASA's own long-duration exposure standards would still call acceptable, though not comfortable. If the timing slips more than the margin allows: considerably worse, and I am not going to sugarcoat that number for anyone in this room."

"Then the timing doesn't slip," Iverson said, flat, the same voice he'd apparently used flying a broken shuttle home years before any of us had known him. "Give me the numbers. I'll hold them."

We ran the insertion three days later, the whole crew strapped in, instruments logging every second of exposure in real time the way Alvarez's original validation flight had once logged every kick. Truman called the complete go/no-go poll before the first deceleration kick. I flew the burst myself, kicks precise, the nominal two-second cadence flexing around the carrier's live structural modes through the actual insertion despite Jupiter's own gravity trying to complicate every correction I made, the largest gravitational field any of us had ever operated inside.

"Radiation reading climbing," Reyes read off, watching her own instruments with the specific stillness of someone choosing not to let her voice reflect what the numbers were actually doing. "Within predicted parameters. Still climbing."

"Copy," I said, holding my focus on the kicks themselves, on the ship's actual trajectory rather than the numbers I couldn't do anything about directly except by flying the plan exactly as designed.

The worst of it lasted eleven minutes, by the ship's own clock, eleven minutes that felt considerably longer to everyone aboard, watching a dosimeter climb toward a number none of us wanted to actually reach, trusting a plan built by people who weren't in the room to actually hold against a physical reality that didn't care how good the plan was if the timing went even slightly wrong.

"Clear of the belt," Reyes said, reading the ship's own instruments. "Radiation falling. We're through the worst of it."

Nobody cheered, exactly. Grebenchekov let out a long breath he'd clearly been holding for most of those eleven minutes. Ochoa, of all people, was the one who actually laughed, short and startled, the specific laugh of someone who'd just realized how frightened she'd actually been only once the fear had somewhere safe to go.

"The sky itself is trying to kill us here," she said, to nobody in particular. "That's new. Mars just sat there being cold and empty. This place is actively hostile."

"Welcome to the outer solar system," Childs said, dry, already running her own post-insertion checklist like nothing dramatic had happened at all, which was, I was starting to understand, exactly how pilots like her processed exactly this kind of fear: by immediately going back to being useful.

Europa itself, once we'd cleared Jupiter's worst radiation and settled into an approach orbit, resolved slowly out of the dark: smooth, pale, cracked across its surface with long, dark lines like something that had been broken and healed imperfectly, over and over, for longer than any of us could easily picture. No visible atmosphere to speak of. No color beyond a pale, icy white shot through with faint ochre streaks along the largest fractures.

"That's ice," Ochoa said, pressed against the observation port, professional hunger already overriding whatever fear the radiation belt had left in her. "An entire moon's worth of ice, and underneath it, roughly twice the water in all Earth's oceans combined. We're looking at one of the largest accessible bodies of liquid water in the solar system, and it's hiding under a shell of ice we can't see all the way through from here."

"Can we get through it?" I asked, though I already half-knew the answer, since it was the whole reason we'd come this far.

"Not by drilling," Ochoa said. "Not conventionally. The best premission estimates put most of that shell around fifteen to thirty kilometers, and the more recent microwave result averaged close to twenty-nine in the region it measured. I don't intend to spend the rest of my life trying to bore through it with equipment built for a much smaller job." She looked at me directly, something sharp and hopeful in her expression. "But you don't need to drill through it at all, do you. You just need to reach somewhere on the other side and open the connection."

"That's the idea," I said, looking past her at the pale, cracked, impossibly patient face of the moon below us, holding an entire ocean's worth of secret underneath a shell nothing on Earth had ever properly touched. "Let's go find out if the idea actually works."
