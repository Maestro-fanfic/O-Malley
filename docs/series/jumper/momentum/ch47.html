Chapter 47 of 49

Cent POV: There was a Cent-shaped hole, and on the other side of it, an entire ocean.

We built the test the same deliberate way we'd built everything else on this project: small first, controlled, nothing risked that didn't have to be risked to actually learn something.

"Talk me through what you're actually planning," Reyes said, the night before, the two of us going over the setup one more time in the ship's cramped common area while the others slept. "I want to understand the physics before I sign off on you doing this."

"The twin's not the push-mechanic. It's older, established since before any of this. I open a person-shaped connection between two places I remember or can see. The probe gives me the second one: a live look at an actual place, not coordinates. And the opening is always person-shaped. I don't get to make it a pinhole because a pinhole would be convenient. Whatever's on one side becomes reachable from the other while the connection holds. I've done it before, once with something a lot heavier than water, and once since, filling Kristen's outer shell from an underground reservoir." I pointed through the lander's window at the test receiver anchored two hundred meters from the fracture, a person-sized pressure cylinder Osei's team had built around the fixed geometry of the twin. "The risk isn't the mechanism. It's the pressure differential. Twenty or thirty kilometers of ice means tens of megapascals at the ocean interface even before we argue about salinity or local structure. Open a person-shaped twin into vacuum and it won't trickle. It'll come a gusher."

"And the evacuated cylinder controls that?"

"It limits the total volume only if I close the twin almost immediately. The receiver is behind a berm, we're behind another one, and every instrument is remote. If our estimate is wrong, the cylinder fails and nothing living is close enough to care. That's the whole point of doing the first test small."

We ran it the next morning at the fracture site Ochoa had identified. The evacuated receiver sat on a remote stand behind an ice ridge, valves closed, cameras and pressure sensors live. We watched from the capsule.

I watched the probe's live image: black water, white particles, the edge of its own light fading into an ocean no human eye had ever seen before. I held that real place in view and opened the connection inside the receiver.

There was a Cent-shaped hole, and on the other side of it, an entire ocean.

The first pressure trace crossed the top of the graph before the software could draw a second point.

The cylinder exploded.

Not split. Not leaked. The forward cap left the stand as a flat silver blur and buried itself in an ice ridge fifty meters away. The body peeled open along its longitudinal seam. A white plume of Europan water flashed into vapor and ice crystals in the vacuum, expanding through the person-shaped connection faster than any valve in our design could have reacted. I closed the twin on reflex. It had been open less than half a second.

For several seconds the cameras showed nothing but glittering ice.

Then the plume cleared enough to reveal the stand. Most of it was gone.

"Well," Truman said into the silence. "It did, in fact, come a gusher."

Ochoa stared at the pressure graph frozen at its upper limit. "The sensor rated to sixty megapascals."

"Then we learned the pressure is higher than the sensor or the transient is," Reyes said. "Either way, we do not repeat that geometry."

Nobody tried to call the failure a partial success because a few frozen droplets had landed in the collection tray. The test had answered the question we'd actually asked. An evacuated receiver did not control a person-shaped twin. The pressure differential controlled the receiver, briefly and destructively.

We transmitted everything home, then I stepped through a twin into the mission room rather than waiting most of an hour for the failure telemetry and another for the answer. Dad called before Osei did.

"Stop trying to catch an ocean in an empty can," he said.

"That conclusion has achieved broad support."

"You need pressure on your side before you open it. Water against water. No gas volume to compress, no vacuum asking the whole ocean to arrive at once."

"We don't know the exact source pressure."

"You don't need exact for the first balanced test. You need close enough that your structure sees the mismatch instead of the entire head under the ice. Build a water-filled chamber, pre-pressurize it in steps, put a piston accumulator behind it, and let the piston show you which way the error runs. The twin stays person-shaped. The receiver is shaped around the twin."

Osei came onto the call halfway through and stopped arguing with him after six minutes, which was how I knew the idea was good. I returned to Europa with the redesign settled while the slower telemetry exchange was still crossing interplanetary space.

The redesign used hardware we'd staged at Kristen for a future bulk-water demonstration: a person-sized cylindrical throat leading into a fully flooded pressure chamber, backed by a hydraulic accumulator with enough piston travel to absorb a bad estimate without leaving a gas pocket in front of the twin. Every wetted part was sterilized. There was no intermediate bucket for me to fill and carry. I would connect Europa's ocean directly to Dad's chamber. After the twin closed, isolation valves would trap the received water and Ochoa could draw a small sample from the side loop.

It took four days for Dad, Osei, and the fabrication team to build the new throat and another launch already on the manifest to carry it to Kristen.

Joe arrived before the main assembly did.

He appeared inside the landing capsule in a pressure suit with the sterile seal packs, valve cartridges, and installation tools secured flat against his body. I caught him before Europa's low gravity tipped him into Ochoa's instrument rack.

"Second time?" I asked.

"Second time on purpose." He looked through the viewport at Europa's broken ice and Jupiter above it. "I brought parts."

"You could have sent the parts with me."

"I know."

That was the whole explanation, and it was enough. He stayed through the receiving-chamber briefing, helped Childs inventory every seal, and spent ten quiet minutes at the viewport with me. Then I took him home to Kristen, checked the completed throat and accumulator with him and Osei, and brought the full assembly back to Europa as a separate load.

The second test did not look heroic. That was one reason I trusted it.

We filled the chamber with sterile water from the carrier. Pressurized it to the low end of Ochoa's estimate. Locked it behind the same ice ridge that had saved us the first time. The accumulator piston sat at mid-travel so it could move either direction.

I opened the person-shaped twin.

The piston slammed twenty centimeters, stopped, then crept another three.

The chamber held.

Pressure equalized across the connection. There was no explosion, no flash plume, no cap leaving for parts unknown. Just two bodies of water touching through a Cent-shaped opening where twenty-some kilometers of ice should have made touching impossible.

We held it for five seconds. Then ten.

At thirty seconds I closed the twin.

Every gauge stayed inside its rated band.

"Receiver secure," Childs said from the local panel. "Isolation valves closed. Sample side is yours."

Ochoa drew the first vial from Dad's loop with hands that shook hard enough for Reyes to steady her wrist.

We ran the analysis inside the capsule, all seven of us crowded around Ochoa's instruments while she worked through sample after sample with the meticulous, disbelieving joy of a scientist getting exactly what she'd hoped for and still not quite believing it.

"Liquid water," she said, finally, looking up at all of us with an expression I don't think I'll ever forget. "Actual, confirmed liquid water from Europa's subsurface ocean, isolated in Dad's loop. Trace minerals compatible with the water-rock interaction models, though one sample does not prove direct mantle contact. This is real. This isn't a projection or a model anymore. This is a sample."

"What does it mean," Iverson asked, "for the actual point of all this? Not the science. The bigger idea."

I thought about the answer before I gave it, because it deserved to be given plainly, the same way every other big moment on this project had earned a plain answer instead of a grand one.

"It means the method works all the way out here, under conditions nobody's ever tested it against before," I said. "It also means the twin is not a hose we can throttle. It's always person-shaped. For samples, we match the pressure. For bulk transfer, Dad's next receiver has to survive the gusher: a reinforced surge chamber feeding the reservoir, no evacuated space in front of the opening and no person anywhere near the first water hammer."

Truman looked at the clock trace from the successful test. "Thirty seconds."

"That's the useful window," I said. "Long enough for a full transfer, short enough that I know I can hold the twin cleanly. With the flow we just measured and a receiver built to accept it, even the largest reservoir in the current design catalog fills in about half a minute. No intermediate tank for me to carry. I twin the water directly from the source chamber to the reservoir that needs it."

"That is a sentence people on Earth are going to need time with," Iverson said.

"They can have time. The engineering gets more tests."

"That's not nothing," Grebenchekov said, quiet, echoing a phrase I'd heard once before, months back, from a flight surgeon who'd never wanted her own name remembered for it.

"No," I agreed, looking at the small vial sitting inside Ochoa's analyzer, the surviving fraction of an experiment that had begun by blowing a pressure vessel across the ice. "It's not nothing at all."
