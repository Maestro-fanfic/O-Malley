Chapter 1 of 49

Cent: It worked once. That's not the same as knowing it works.

Tuesday mornings on Kristen Station meant net inspections, and net inspections meant floating along sixty meters of truss with a flashlight and a checklist, looking for failures that were never supposed to happen. I'd done it every Tuesday for four months. I was, if anything, getting bored with it, which should have told me something.

"Net one, clear," I said into the comm, working my way hand over hand along the rail. "Net two." I stopped. Looked again. "Net two, clear. Same as last week."

"Copy," Sterling said, from wherever Sterling actually was these days, an office in Colorado Springs I'd never seen and had stopped trying to picture. "Cargo manifest still says water tank, aft cradle three, four hundred kilos nominal."

"Manifest's right. It's sitting pretty."

"Good. COLA's got two objects transiting your altitude band in the next six hours, nothing close, just wanted eyes confirmed before I signed off on the report."

"Eyes confirmed. Tell your board to relax."

"Tell my board yourself sometime. I'd enjoy watching that conversation."

I finished the rest of the inspection the way I finished every Tuesday inspection, which was to say thoroughly and with the specific, low-grade satisfaction of a job that never had anything wrong with it. Kristen Station wasn't finished, wasn't going to be finished for a long while yet, but it was solid, it was ours, and every Tuesday it held together exactly the way it was supposed to.

I was three-quarters of the way back to the hub, thinking about lunch, when the truss jumped under my boots.

Not a big jump. A jolt, quick and hard, more felt than heard, the way anything mechanical announces itself out here where sound has nowhere to travel and vibration does all the talking. I froze, both hands on the rail, and looked back the way I'd come.

Net two was gone.

Not gone-gone. Failed. I could see the shape of it even from sixty meters off, torn webbing trailing off the end of the truss like a flag nobody had ever meant to fly, and behind the webbing, tumbling slow and stupid and completely unbothered by the fact that it wasn't supposed to be able to do this, four hundred kilos of water tank.

"Cent." Sterling's voice again, faster this time, flat in the particular way he got when he was working hard at not sounding alarmed. "Net two just failed. You have a visual?"

"I have a visual." I did the math without meaning to, the way you do math on a falling glass before you've consciously decided to reach for it. Tumbling slow, end over end, drifting off the truss at maybe half a meter a second. Half a meter a second doesn't sound like much. On the ground, it isn't much; the ground eats that energy for you, you stub a toe, you move on with your day. Out here there's no ground to eat anything. There's no air to drag against it, no friction to bleed it off, nothing at all standing between four hundred kilos of momentum and whatever happened to be in its way when it finally arrived somewhere.

The something, in about ninety seconds at that drift rate, was going to be the solar array Joe had spent six hours aligning that morning.

"I see it," I said, and jumped.

I put myself twenty meters out, matched to the tank's tumble, close enough to read the manufacturer's stencil peeling off the side. Slow spin, slight wobble, torn webbing whipping around on every rotation like a rope someone had let go of mid-throw. No way to grab it clean. Get an arm in the wrong place when that webbing came around and I'd lose the arm, or close enough to it that the distinction wouldn't matter much. I'd done that kind of math before, on a different piece of gear, a different afternoon. I wasn't doing it again.

What I actually needed to be was a wall. Something for the tank to push against that could push back without losing a fight to a strip of nylon webbing.

"Talk to me," Sterling said.

"Thinking."

Here's the thing I knew for a fact, the thing Cory had made me demonstrate for him twice in his office with a stepladder and a very worried expression on his face the whole time: I could change my own velocity in a jump. Not carry it. Not cancel it to match a new frame, the way any jumper could. Actually add some, or take some away, out of nothing, the way I'd added twenty feet a second straight up once and never used my legs to do it. Nobody had ever explained where the energy came from. Cory had tried, for about a week, checking numbers against numbers that refused to balance, and then given up and started calling it "the Cent effect" specifically to annoy me. I'd stopped chasing the why a long time before that. You use what works. You don't have to understand a hammer to drive a nail.

I'd done the other thing before, too. The actual push, not just the velocity change. Exactly once. The PAM-D, a year and a half back: a dead Delta third stage, two thousand kilos of tumbling junk nobody on Earth would ever miss, four hundred kilometers up with an entire orbit's worth of time to decide whether I actually wanted to try it. I remembered thinking it through out loud, to nobody, in my own head, the way you talk yourself into cold water: I could push one of those on Earth, easily, if it were in neutral. In micro-G, if I had something to push against, I could easily move it. Wouldn't know if I didn't try. And it had worked. Clean, first try, the perigee dropping four hundred klicks in about the time it takes to say so, gone into the mesosphere and gone for good, and me left hanging there afterward feeling like I'd gotten away with something.

That had been on purpose. Borrowed time, an empty sky, and nothing riding on being wrong except my own pride.

This was ninety seconds and Joe's array on the other end of the tumble.

I jumped again, in close this time, close enough that my forearms and shoulder were already set against the tank's shell before I'd finished arriving, no impact, no collision, just suddenly there with my body in contact and the tumble already trying to wrench me sideways with it. My hands steadied the contact; they weren't what moved four hundred kilos. I tightened through my whole body the way I had against the PAM-D, changed my own momentum, and made the tank come with me. Not an arm push. A jump delivered while braced to something else.

The tank stopped tumbling.

Not slowed. Stopped, dead, the way a spinning coin stops when you slap your palm down flat on top of it, except this was four hundred kilos of water and steel and the "palm" was something that didn't have a name I could give Sterling if he asked, which he was about to. I felt the reaction of it come back through my hands into my shoulders, a solid shove, real and physical and entirely too easy for the mass involved.

I hung there a second with my hands still on the shell, breathing harder than the effort should have cost me.

"Cent?" Sterling, careful now, the way he got when the silence on his end had run a beat too long.

"I'm okay. It's not tumbling anymore."

"How."

"Working on that part."

I did it again then, on purpose, body aligned down the truss toward the empty docking port instead of toward Joe's array. I changed my own velocity and kept the contact long enough to bring the tank along for the ride. Felt the moment we separated, its new momentum continuing without me, something that outmassed me eight times over moving exactly where I'd put it and nowhere else, because there was nothing out here to argue with me about it. No air to drag against it. No ground to eat the energy sideways. No gravity trying to bend the path into something curved and complicated. I'd told it where to go, and it went.

On the ground, I could push a truck if I really put my back into it and had good footing and a run at it, and the truck would move about as far as my legs could make it move before friction and the tires and Newton's third law all voted me down. Up here I didn't have legs in it at all. I had my own changed momentum, contact, and intention, and apparently that was still the whole recipe. Same thing I'd done with the PAM-D. Just a smaller truck this time, and about eighteen months less runway to be sure it would work.

"It's headed for the empty port," I said. "Low and slow. Somebody want to catch it before it decides to come back this way?"

Joe's voice, from over by the array, breathless in a way that had nothing to do with exertion: "On it. Cent, what did you just do?"

"Told the tank where to go. It listened. Story for later."

I watched it drift away, tumble-free, obedient, going exactly where I'd sent it at exactly the speed I'd given it, and thought about all the things around the station heavier than me that I'd never once let myself count as things I could just do, in the moment, without an orbit's worth of runway first. A section of the inner ring that needed nudging into alignment without burning fuel we didn't have to spare. Debris on a bad vector with nobody's math fast enough to plot an intercept in time. A person, someday, moving too fast in the wrong direction, with nothing between them and a very long fall that technically wasn't falling at all.

I'd known, since the PAM-D, that it wasn't a fluke.

I had not known, until about forty seconds ago, that it would still be there when I didn't have time to talk myself into it first.

Joe caught the tank at the docking port with both hands and a grunt I heard clean over the comm, and by the time I jumped back to the hub he'd already lashed it down twice over with a fresh net, the kind of overkill that meant he was still running on adrenaline and hadn't found anywhere else to put it yet.

"You want to tell me what that was," he said, not turning around, still checking the lashing a third time.

"I pushed it."

"You pushed it." He did turn around then. Joe had a good face for worry, unfortunately, all the tells right there on the surface where I could read them: a tightness around the eyes, a stillness that wasn't calm so much as held. "Cent, that thing outmassed you by a factor of eight."

"I know what it outmassed me by. I did the math on the way over." I caught the edge of the truss and let myself drift to a stop next to him, close enough that our shoulders touched, which I'd learned over the last year was the fastest way to talk him down off whatever ledge his imagination had climbed. "It worked. I'm fine. The array's fine. The tank's fine. Everybody's fine."

"This time."

"This time," I agreed, because there wasn't a version of that sentence I could argue with honestly. "I've done it before, Joe. Once. The PAM-D, a year and a half ago."

"You never told me you could do that."

"I never told anybody I could do that, except Cory, and only because he made me demonstrate the velocity thing in his office until I got tired of arguing about it." I looked at the tank, lashed down twice over, going nowhere. "I didn't know I could still do it under pressure until about four minutes ago. That's the actual new information here. Not that I can push things. That I can push things when I don't have an orbit's worth of time to decide whether I trust myself."

Joe was quiet for a second, working the last of the adrenaline out of his hands, opening and closing them against nothing. "Are you going to tell Sterling that part? The pressure part?"

"He's going to ask. He always asks the actual question eventually."

"He's good at his job."

"He's annoyingly good at his job." I bumped my shoulder against his again, gentler this time. "Come on. I promised him a full report and my lunch is getting later by the minute."

Sterling's full report, when I finally sat down for it, took about ten minutes and covered everything except the one thing I knew he actually wanted, which he saved, predictably, for last.

"So to be clear," he said, in the voice he used when he already had the shape of the answer and just wanted me to say it out loud for the record. "This is the same thing you did to the PAM-D."

"Same mechanism. Different circumstances."

"Different how."

"The PAM-D I thought about for the better part of an orbit before I tried it. Empty sky, dead junk, nothing on the line but my own pride if it didn't work. Today I had about ninety seconds and Joe's array on the other end of a four-hundred-kilo mistake. I didn't have time to talk myself into it. I just did it."

"And it worked."

"And it worked."

Another pause, and I could picture him exactly, sitting at whatever desk he sat at in Colorado Springs, running the numbers behind his own eyes the way he always did before he let himself say anything he might have to walk back later.

"I want that written up properly," he said finally. "Not for me. For the file. If this is repeatable under time pressure and not just under ideal conditions, that's a different category of fact than the one I've had on record since the PAM-D."

"It's not going in any file that leaves this station."

"I didn't say it was leaving the station. I said I wanted it written down somewhere that isn't just your own head, in case you're ever not around to ask." A beat. "That wasn't meant to be as grim as it just sounded."

"It's fine. You're not wrong." I thought about it a second longer than I meant to, the tank drifting harmless at the empty port, Joe's hands still faintly unsteady an hour later. "I'll write it up. Tonight. You'll have it by morning."

"Good. And Cent?"

"Yeah?"

"Nice work today. I mean that plainly, not as a preamble to a question."

"I know you do," I said, and meant it. "Thanks."

I called home that evening, the way I did most evenings, mostly out of habit and partly because the secure line into the old hotel still made a family call feel like an event instead of a formality.

"You sound tired," Mom said, before I'd said much of anything.

"Had an interesting day."

"Define interesting."

I told her. All of it, the net failure and the tumble and the ninety seconds and the push, the whole thing laid out plain the way I'd have told it to Sterling if Sterling had been the kind of person you told things to for comfort instead of for the record. Mom listened without interrupting, which was its own kind of tell; she interrupted plenty when she thought I was underselling a risk, and she wasn't interrupting now.

"You're saying this like it's a footnote," she said, once I'd finished. "The pushing-things-fast part. That's not a footnote, Cent, that's a new fact about how you work."

"I know it's not a footnote."

"Does your father know yet?"

"Not yet. You're first."

"He's going to want to talk about it the way he talks about everything that changes the shape of the danger you're in." Which was true, and which I'd been putting off precisely because it was true; Dad's questions always came from the right place and still managed to feel, some days, like being cross-examined by someone who loved you. "I'll tell him gently. Mostly gently."

"Thanks."

"How's Joe?"

"Shaken. He'll be fine by tomorrow. He's better at recovering from being scared for me than he used to be." I thought about his hands, opening and closing against nothing. "He's gotten a lot better, actually, over the last year. He used to spiral. Now he just gets quiet and checks the lashings three times."

"That's growth," Mom said, her smile surviving even the uplink's flat, compressed audio. "Get some sleep. Write your report for Sterling. And Cent, whatever this new thing turns out to mean, take it slow finding out. You don't have to solve all of it in one Tuesday."

"I wasn't planning to."

"You never plan to. It just tends to happen that way around you."

I laughed, because she wasn't wrong about that either, and said goodnight, and stayed for a while afterward in the quiet of my own room with the lights of Earth sliding by underneath, thinking about a tank that had stopped dead the instant I told it to, and a general in Colorado who wanted it written down somewhere that wasn't just my own head, and a boyfriend down the corridor who'd needed three passes at the same lashing before his hands stopped shaking.

I'd known, since the PAM-D, that it wasn't a fluke. Now Joe's shaking hands were part of the data too. I liked that considerably less than the part about the tank.
