Chapter 41 of 49

Cent POV: For once, working the way it was planned.

The rest of the coast passed quieter than anything that had come before it, which felt strange after everything, but nobody aboard complained about quiet by then.

The full operational account reached us as a recorded packet during final approach, answers still separated from questions by the same delay that had shaped the attack. Sterling delivered it plain and complete: the two men, Hunt and Dad in the hallway, the printed revocation letter, and the access chain Devereaux had tried to replace. He stopped before the part that belonged to my mother.

Her recording followed.

"I told them to wait until you were home," Mom said from the screen. "That was me trying to protect you from a fact you had the right to decide when to hear. I changed my mind. You are about to land on Mars carrying six other people, and I will not make you do that while everyone on Earth knows something about your family that you don't."

She told me what she had done. She also told me she had surrendered, that she had counsel, and that nobody in the White House had promised her an outcome.

"You do not have to understand it now," she finished. "You do not have to forgive it on my schedule. Fly the landing. Come home when the mission is done. Then ask me anything."

I sent back the only answer I had. "I love you. I don't know what else yet."

Her reply could not arrive before we began insertion. That was the physical fact of the distance, and this time nobody pretended otherwise.

I took the whole mess into the one corner of the ship that counted as privacy and worried it raw. I tried anger first, then gratitude, then the useless wish that nobody had needed to be brave on my behalf at all. I didn't cry, exactly, though my throat hurt with not doing it and something under my ribs stayed drawn tight for hours. I had no idea how to carry what my mother had chosen. My father had stood in a hallway and been exactly as brave as I'd always known he could be when it mattered. Joe had crossed space for the first time because he saw a gun aimed at someone he loved. None of it had cost any of them their lives. That was the one fact I could hold without cutting myself on the rest.

The remaining power rotations happened on schedule. I carried each charged cassette from Kristen by itself, waited for the crew to transfer the live bus and release the depleted one, then took that one home for recharge. After the first exchange it became exactly what Osei had intended: repetitive, verified, and not worth turning into an emergency.

Then there was Mars, close enough to fill the window, and eight days' worth of anticipation collapsing into work.

"Orbital insertion in six hours," Iverson said, running through the same checklist he'd have run for any ordinary mission, because a checklist doesn't care that the propulsion behind it has never existed before. "Deceleration burst, same as departure, mirrored through the chair gimbal."

Osei and Sterling had spent the coast turning that sentence into a time-tagged guidance solution. A full one-hundred-eighty-degree rotation on the primary axis would oppose the cruise vector. The actual insertion needed smaller out-of-plane offsets that changed as Mars gravity bent our approach. Mission control uploaded target vectors at full navigation precision; onboard guidance translated them into paired gimbal commands displayed to thousandths of a degree. Iverson and Childs checked the solution against onboard navigation; Truman read the abort gates. Absolute encoders on both axes resolved finer than the display, and opposed mechanical locks held each commanded vector. The displays did not confirm the next one until two independent systems agreed on both axes; disagreement meant an amber warning, and crossing an abort gate meant the alarm.

Before the gimbal moved, Truman called the same complete poll: guidance, navigation, vehicle, medical, payload, momentum, command. Every station answered for the insertion in front of us. Nothing carried forward from the departure poll.

We had run the insertion in the carrier simulator until paired-angle changes, sequence holds, and a new navigation solution were ordinary procedure. Mars made the numbers real. It didn't change who checked them or what each of us did next.

I settled into the fixture again, closed my hands around the brace grips, and felt something return that had been missing since the coast began: purpose, useful again, the passenger stretch over. Primary to one-eighty. Trim zero. Both locks. The yellow WAIT light went dark; both position votes agreed; green. Kick. Correct my own drift. Primary one-seventy-nine point six-zero-three. Trim zero point zero-eight-four starboard. Confirm. Yellow held a fraction longer while a bending mode passed. Then it went dark, both axes stayed clean, and green came up. Kick. The carrier bled velocity the same patient way it had built it, burst after burst, until Mars stopped being a red disc in a window and became a horizon, close, real, curved the way a planet is curved when you're finally near enough to see it as a place instead of an idea.

"Orbit achieved," Iverson said, and his voice cracked just slightly at the edges, the first time I'd heard it do that the whole trip. "We are in Mars orbit. I have wanted to say that sentence since I was nine years old."

The carrier stayed in Mars orbit. It was far too massive for me to lower through the gravity well and then raise again, and nobody had forgotten that limit. We transferred into the lightweight landing capsule with only surface consumables, suits, science gear, and a second chair built specifically for the lander. It used the same full-body supports, index grips, and two-axis principle as the carrier fixture, but the compact crossed-trunnion gimbal had wider travel and faster slew. Locking drives and absolute encoders still had to seat and agree before the light could turn green. That gave Iverson and Childs a way to point each change in velocity against whatever the atmosphere was actually doing instead of whatever the forecast had promised. Ochoa checked Nasser's geology list twice before sealing the hatch.

Landing should have been the hard part. Every account of every Mars mission anybody had ever planned on paper called the descent "seven minutes of terror": heat shield, parachute sequence, retro-rockets, a hundred things that had to work in the right order in an atmosphere too thin to trust and too thick to ignore. We killed our orbital velocity above the atmosphere and came in slowly enough not to need that sequence. Slowly did not mean straight. The first density layer arrived higher than forecast and put a measurable eastward drift into the capsule. We had practiced that class of failure in the lander simulator until the response was automatic. Onboard navigation recomputed the desired vector; Childs verified it against the independent solution, Iverson held the capsule attitude, and the gimbal tipped my chair three hundred twelve thousandths of a degree west and forty-seven thousandths up. The locks seated. Yellow held until both the position encoders and the load-path sensors agreed. Green. I kicked, and the crossrange number came back toward zero.

I killed the rest of the capsule's velocity in discrete kicks, patient, every one inside the limit we'd proved. I'd tried doing it the other way once. One full-effort push had nearly killed me and would have killed anyone riding the load outright. The gimbal trimmed the vector between kicks as the atmosphere changed its mind. The ground rose to meet us instead of rushing up in a panic.

"Altitude two thousand meters, descent rate nominal," Childs read off, and I heard, underneath the professional calm, real wonder. "This is the most boring landing sequence I have ever been part of, and I mean that as the highest compliment I know how to give."

"For once," I said, working the last few kicks, feeling the capsule settle instead of fall, "working the way it was planned."

"Sometimes the plan does survive enemy contact," Truman said from the couch behind Iverson. "Nobody tell the planners. They'll become unbearable."

We touched down soft, dust rising slow in the thin air and settling slower, the whole capsule quiet in a way that felt earned rather than accidental. Nobody spoke for a long moment. Then Ochoa, from the science station, said the only thing that needed saying.

"We're here."

"We're here," I agreed, and felt the words land somewhere different than they ever had before, standing on these same dunes alone, a bag of sand and a long walk back to an empty ship. This time there were six other people breathing the recycled air beside me, and every one of them had earned the right to say it too.
