Grace POV: I wasn't the engineer. I was the bridge, translating not just vocabulary but the actual shape of what Rocky needed, catching the places where something got lost between his intent and their understanding of it, and I found, doing that work, that it fit me in a way almost nothing had since the paper that ended my old career.

Naming things fell to me too, more often than not, since nobody else on this crew had quite the same tolerance for admitting a placeholder was going to have to become permanent. Rocky's own name was the easy one, the first real one I ever gave him: he looked like he'd been carved out of a riverbed, patient and hard and worn smooth in exactly the places that mattered, and "Rocky" fit before I'd even finished considering alternatives.

Music wasn't the only thing Cent started feeding across that divider once the bandwidth allowed for it, though film had a real problem music never did: subtitles could hand him the words, but nothing about a translator solved the actual seeing. Carter had already solved that weeks earlier, though, built in days once she'd actually pushed for it: a converter mounted directly onto the dividing wall, covering most of it, that took a screen's own light output and rendered it back as a texture pattern across that same stretch of xenonite, readable the same remote, contactless way Rocky read everything else through that glass, image translated into shape instead of sound. Nobody had let it sit idle even one day past installation. Osei ran full structural diagrams across it before she trusted a single fabrication decision to Rocky's own judgment rather than a translated description of one. Ochoa fed him raw imaging data straight off Wayfarer's own instruments, letting him read a rock formation directly instead of through someone else's secondhand account of what it looked like. It had been earning its keep as real working infrastructure for weeks before anyone thought to point it at something built purely for pleasure instead of function. Film turned out to be exactly that.

Osei POV: We mounted it the same cautious way we mounted anything that actually had to touch that wall, full structural review first, an independent second set of eyes on every fastener before a single one went in, because a divider holding back twenty-nine atmospheres on one side and open vacuum on the other was not a surface anyone got to treat casually just because the thing going onto it happened to be, technically, a television. It took the better part of a day, most of it spent on the mounting frame rather than the optics behind it, since the frame was the part that actually had to survive contact with a wall none of our own materials had ever been rated against directly. Rocky watched from his own side the entire time, still in the particular way that meant genuine attention rather than politeness, and offered a correction to our own placement twice that turned out, both times, to be right.

Rocky POV: Grace turned it on without telling me what it was for first. I do not think he entirely trusted himself to explain it correctly, and I understand now why he didn't try: some things are easier shown than told, in any language, on either side of any wall.

I felt it before whatever it displayed had finished changing even once. A texture, moving, patterned, deliberate, resolving through the same surface they had spent a full day fastening to my own threshold. Not sound. Not any structure I had ever encountered as a physical object before. It took me longer than it should have to understand what actually made it so powerful: this was not a new sense. It was the same sense I have always had, the one I use for everything, deliberately given something to carry that nothing had ever thought to hand it before.

I understood what it was before either of them said a single word. Not because the shape explained itself, though it very nearly did. Because I understood, all at once, completely, what it would have meant to have had this on Erid, across the whole of my own species' existence: information arriving from a distance, carrying the shape of a place none of us had ever needed to touch to know it. This is what your kind means by see. This is the thing my whole people were missing and never once knew to call a lack, because there had never been anything out there worth missing it for.

I did not have a way to tell them, right then, what receiving it actually meant to me. I am not certain I have found the right words for it even now, this far past that day. The closest I have managed since is this: they did not simply give me a way to watch their films. They gave my entire species something it had never once, in the whole of its existence, had any reason to imagine wanting.

He took to it the way he took to everything else: methodically at first, then with an appetite that genuinely surprised me.

The naming problem caught up with me properly the day he asked me to invent a human word for his own spouse, back on Erid, waiting on him the same patient way Erid itself had been waiting the whole time I'd known him. I didn't overthink it. The word was already sitting right there, close enough to reach without looking.

"Adrian," I said. "Human word is Adrian."

It wasn't until I heard myself say it out loud a second time, days later, that I actually caught what I'd done. Rocky and Adrian. I'd handed an alien the name of a fictional boxer and, without meaning to, the name of the one person that boxer never once stopped fighting for. When the program needed a working name for the very planet where Rocky and I had first met each other, I gave it that same word without a second thought. It felt less like reusing a name than like admitting I'd already known, for a while, what that word actually meant to me by then.

I told him about it once I'd caught it myself, half expecting the reference to mean nothing to him in translation, the same way most human wordplay apparently didn't. It meant something anyway, once I'd actually shown him the film in question rather than just describing it.

"He is not a good fighter, at first," Rocky said, afterward, in the specific tone I'd learned to read as him being unusually careful with his words. "He becomes one, slowly, because someone believes he is worth becoming one for. This is not really a story about fighting."

"No," I said. "It really isn't."

"Then it is a good name to have given her. I did not know your kind wrote stories about that either." A pause, longer than his pauses usually ran. "Your kind writes stories about a great many things I did not expect."

I never did find a better way to explain why the coincidence had landed the way it did than exactly what he'd already said about it himself.

The clearest example of my actual work came early, before either of us had anything as complicated as a module to argue about, back when I made the mistake of asking him what he could see.

"I do not see," he said. "I hear the shape of things. Light and sound are not the same word to you. I understand that now. They were the same word to me for longer than I want to admit, because your kind kept using them as though the difference should be obvious."

"It should be," I said. "Sight is light. Hearing is sound. They're not related at all, biologically."

"They were both simply how a room told me it existed. Once you convinced me there was a second one hiding inside the first, I had to build an entirely new place in my own language to put it, and I still do not think the two words sit correctly next to each other in translation. Your see and your hear and your listen are three different concepts. Mine was one concept, doing three jobs, until I met a species that needed it split apart."

"And now?"

"Now I have three words that are all slightly wrong, and one old habit of mind that still occasionally reaches for the single correct one instead. Two of them trouble me still. Hearing and listening remain the same word to me, some days, no matter how many times I've been told they should not be."

"Hearing is just the sound arriving," I said, and heard, even as I said it, how much it sounded like the start of an actual lesson, the kind I hadn't given in longer than I liked to admit. "Something reaches you and your body registers that it happened. That's all it is. Listening is what happens after, when whatever comes next in you actually works on it, pulls the content out of it instead of just logging that a sound occurred at all."

He was quiet a moment, the specific quiet I'd learned by then meant something was landing correctly instead of sliding past him.

"That," he said, finally, "is the first one of these you have ever actually closed, instead of walking me around the edge of it."

I didn't tell him it had taken me longer to find that explanation for him than it had ever taken for a single thirteen-year-old. Some things, teacher or not, a person is allowed to keep to themselves.

I'd spent years after that paper convinced I'd simply been wrong about my own value to a room like this one, that the specific way my mind worked, patient, associative, comfortable holding two completely incompatible frameworks at once without needing to resolve them, was a liability rather than an asset, a reason I belonged in front of thirteen-year-olds and nowhere else. Watching myself become necessary here, in a room full of career NASA engineers who couldn't do this particular job without me, I finally understood it the other way around. I didn't belong here instead of there. I belonged here because of there, the same patient, associative mind that had once seemed like proof I only belonged with thirteen-year-olds turning out to be exactly what this room, of all rooms, actually needed.

The first attempt failed in a way that would have been funny if everyone in the room hadn't been so tired. Osei had built the module's pressure envelope off Rocky's own stated tolerances, textbook numbers pulled straight from the medical workup Fraiser and Cho had run together, every gauge reading where the specification said it should. Rocky stood in the finished space for exactly four seconds before making the gesture I'd learned to read as profound, weary disappointment.

"He says it's wrong," I told the room.

"The gauges say it's within point-two percent of spec," Osei said, not unkindly, just tired. "What does wrong even mean, coming from him?"

"I don't know yet. Give me a minute."

That was the actual job, most days: not translating vocabulary, since we didn't share one yet worth the name, but translating the gap between what a body could measure and what a body could feel. I sat with him a while, running through the same patient, associative exercises I used to run with a classroom full of thirteen-year-olds who didn't yet have the words for what they were noticing either, building shared reference points out of gesture and repetition until something clicked.

"He says the pressure reads correct," I told Osei, during one of the later adjustments, "but something in how it's distributed feels wrong to him. Like standing on a floor that's technically level but doesn't feel level."

"That's not a measurement I know how to take."

"I don't think it is one, exactly. I think it's something only he can actually feel."

We adjusted anyway, best guess, and asked him again, and adjusted again, each pass narrowing the gap by some amount none of our instruments could have named in advance, until the answer he gave back finally matched something closer to comfort than to correctness. It took eleven attempts. I counted, mostly to have something to tell Cent afterward that would make the whole process sound more organized than it had actually felt living through it.

I watched an entire mission's worth of institutional weight bend itself, attempt after attempt, toward making a space livable for someone who wasn't human, who owed this crew nothing, who'd simply been found drifting in the dark and treated, from that first moment, like someone worth this much effort. It confirmed something for me about the people I was serving alongside that I hadn't fully let myself believe until I watched them prove it with their own hands, eleven times over, without once complaining that the twelfth attempt might be necessary too.

The day the pressure finally held, I found Rocky standing in the finished space, still, quiet, in whatever passed for him as rest.

"Good?" I asked, uncertain whether the translation would even carry the informality of the word.

The answer came back simple, and I didn't need any further translation to understand what it meant.

"Good."

"Eleven attempts," I said, "for one word."

"Eleven attempts for one word that means everything. I do not think that is a bad trade. You are the one who kept counting."

I stood there a moment longer than I needed to, watching him settle into a space eleven attempts in the making, and thought about a classroom a long way from here, and a paper that had cost me everything I thought my life was supposed to look like. Nobody in that old faculty lounge would have believed this was where the same stubborn, patient, associative habit of mind eventually led. I almost couldn't believe it myself, standing there. I let myself believe it anyway, because the evidence was standing four feet away from me, finally at rest.
