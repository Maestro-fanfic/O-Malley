Chapter 48 of 89

McGarrity POV: I'd run election nights out of a war room for other people my entire career. This was the first one where the machinery was running for me instead of through me, and I didn't much care for how that felt.

Holcomb had invited me to watch it with him, in the Situation Room, not my own campaign headquarters, a quiet gesture that told me what I needed to know about where I actually stood with him after all these years. I sat at the central table while staffers murmured into secure lines around us, advisors leaning over chairs trading precinct numbers, the same institutional static I'd run myself for most of my career, low light and late-night stillness pressing in on all of it.

I tapped my pen against my knuckles, an old habit I'd never managed to break, and rolled my shoulders against a tension that had been sitting there since the polls closed.

The feed ran split, the way these things always did now. On the right, ordinary broadcast footage, a small town's mayor and a knot of residents standing on cracked ground, looking up at a municipal water tower that read, even compressed through a news feed, as unmistakably empty. On the left, Davy's own helmet camera, a tighter, stranger register, HUD readouts where the news side ran a chyron. I watched my own attention move between the two halves the way anyone reads a split screen, except I already knew, in a way that audience didn't yet, exactly what the left side was about to do to the right.

Davy stepped into the primary distribution conduit at one of NASA's own holding tanks, water already pressure-regulated, already tamed, waiting. The instant his body occupied that space, the twin opened, straight through to the tower's own internal riser pipe, no visible strain on the feed, no drama in it at all. Both ends of this one sat on Earth, under engineered hydraulics somebody had already spent months taming properly. It read, watching it, as mechanical. A heavy, deliberate siphon opening. Not a rupture.

Reservoir camera, interior, wide fisheye, timestamp running in the corner nobody watching live ever actually looked at. A man in a depth-rated suit, waist-deep, motionless for four-tenths of a second longer than the frame before it, and then simply not there. The water behind him did not ripple outward the way displaced water should. It went still all at once, the way a held breath goes still.

Water tower camera, interior riser pipe, infrared only, no ambient light this far inside a sealed structure. Empty pipe, dry corrosion staining visible on the walls, and then, in the same four-tenths of a second the first feed lost him, full. Water occupying space it had not occupied one frame earlier, rising, no point of entry either camera's own optics could actually resolve.

Davy POV: The tank went quiet around my knees the way it always did right before, water settling into that specific stillness I'd learned to read as ready rather than calm. I found the riser's own signature in my head, the exact shape of a pipe I'd never once physically stood in, memorized off a schematic and a single practice run months back, and let go.

There's no transition to describe, whatever the feeds end up making of it. Tank, then pipe. My own boots on a different floor, water already moving through the space my body had just finished occupying, the job already three-quarters done before my own head finished registering I'd actually moved. I held the link anyway, felt the exact texture of a system moving water at a rate that would have terrified me the first year I ever tried anything like this, and held it steady until pressure on both ends finally agreed with each other and let me go.

I don't get to see the tower fill. I never do. I get the moment before, and the moment after, and I made my peace, a long time ago, with everything in between being something other people get to watch instead of me.

McGarrity POV: Back on the right side, the field lines hummed audibly under the load, water flooding the town's own grid under hydraulics behaving exactly as engineered, no improvisation anywhere in it. Davy held the twin open, continuous, an invisible pipeline instead of a discrete event. I watched the room's reaction instead of the tower's own gauge for a moment: the Secretary of State leaning back, gripping the table's edge; the Defense Secretary muttering something I didn't quite catch and didn't need to; an advisor's hand flying up to cover her own mouth.

Holcomb just watched. His shoulders dropped, some weight I hadn't realized he was still carrying finally easing loose.

"We actually did it," he said, quiet, almost disbelieving, the words meant for no one else in the room, the exact tone of a man who'd spent years working toward this exact moment without ever quite letting himself believe it would arrive.

Then something in him gave. A short, surprised laugh broke loose before he could stop it, and he dropped back into his chair like his legs had gone out from under him, one hand coming up over his mouth, as if he could still catch the sound and pull it back in before anyone else in the room heard it.

"Sorry," he said, once he'd found his breath again, not sounding sorry at all. "My granddaughter used to make that exact sound. Some movie she watched a hundred times, girl practically falls out of her chair over it." He shook his head, still half laughing. "Took me until right now to understand why."

For a moment none of it had anything to do with the election at all.

It was only after that settled that my own eye drifted to the secondary displays, where the precinct maps had started moving in earnest, real contested ground from earlier in the night giving way, state by state, to a margin wider and more comfortable than anyone in the room had predicted going in. A landslide, bigger even than Holcomb's own blowout of a debut win, decisive, unambiguous, nothing left in it for anyone to argue with.

The room broke into a roar around us.

I understand now, in a way none of us said out loud in that room, exactly what that split screen actually did to the numbers coming in behind it. Nobody let a humanitarian miracle land in the middle of election night by accident. Somebody on his own staff had scheduled that test window with exquisite care, and everyone who mattered enough to know it kept the knowing to themselves, then and for years afterward. But it landed live, undeniable, precisely when the whole country was already watching the other screen, and I have never once been able to find the actual line between the man who delivered that water and the margin that came in an hour later. Holcomb never claimed the connection out loud, not that night, not after. He didn't need to. He was still watching Davy's own shoulders ease on the feed, relief on his face that had everything to do with the mission succeeding, and, I have no doubt at all, something quieter underneath it too, already counting the same numbers I was. He earned me that landslide deliberately, and never once said so where anyone could quote him on it.

Holcomb turned to me before anyone else had fully registered what the numbers meant. He didn't say anything. He just gave me a firm, heavy nod, private, the two of us alone inside all that noise, and I knew what he'd just handed me without either of us needing a single word for it.

The mission. The mandate. Everything that came with both.

I thought, sitting there with the room still roaring around me, about every war room I'd ever run for somebody else, every victory I'd helped build and then quietly handed off to the person whose name was actually on the ballot. This one had my own name on it, finally, after a career spent standing one careful step behind the chair instead of in it. I found I didn't feel the triumph I'd always assumed I'd feel, imagining this exact moment on the nights it seemed impossibly far away. I felt something heavier instead, and steadier, and sitting there, I knew the heavier feeling was probably the more honest one, and the one I was going to need to actually do the job well.

Bartlett POV: I was in that same room that night, off to one side with McGarrity's own skeleton campaign staff, close enough to watch it happen without ever being mistaken for someone who belonged at the center of it.

The sitting Chief of Staff introduced himself to me sometime after the numbers had settled and the roar had died down into something closer to exhausted celebration, offering his hand with the specific weary graciousness of a man watching someone else's future arrive while his own quietly wound down.

"You're the one who's been running his campaigns since the school board days," he said. Not a question.

"Something like that."

"He's going to need you in a real office soon, not just a war room." No bitterness in it at all, which surprised me more than the observation itself did. "Get some sleep first, if you can manage it. I never could, the one time it was my own name on the ballot."

I didn't know yet that he meant that literally, that this decent, tired man had run for something himself once, long before he became anyone's Chief of Staff, and lost. I found that out later, the way I found out most things about him: secondhand, offered without complaint, long after it might have changed how I treated him.

I caught it happening across the room before the handshake, before the introduction: Holcomb's composure cracking clean open, that short surprised laugh, one hand flying up over his mouth like he could still stuff the sound back in.

Something surfaced first, half-formed, faster than actual thought: two girls, shrieking with glee, superimposed for one disorienting second directly over the room in front of me, the Situation Room's own hard edges bleeding through some carpeted floor a world and a decade away. Gone before I could even ask myself where it had come from. Then it landed, whole. I knew exactly what that sound was before I'd finished consciously placing it. Some movie Chloe had made me sit through more times than either of us could count, some girl practically sliding out of her own chair with a joy too big to stay inside her body. I'd watched my own daughter do exactly what Holcomb had just done, hand over her mouth and everything, more times than I could count on one hand.

The memory hit before I could stop it, and some undignified sound of my own escaped right along with his, laughing at a joke nobody else in that room had any way of understanding, over an election that had nothing to do with either of us.
