Chapter 84 of 89

Cent POV: We closed the expedition once the outpost certified, rather than staying indefinitely just because we could. Samples and records went home through me, the same courier route I'd run a thousand times by then, full quarantine procedure intact on every single trip.

Wayfarer couldn't stay behind at Proxima b. No propellant of her own, no way to function as a stationed asset anywhere I couldn't already reach directly myself, and working through it, I knew where she actually belonged instead.

Erid. I'd made a promise, and keeping it meant a detour before anyone's own return home.

I expected it to be a small trip. Me, Rocky, Grace, maybe one or two others.

The whole crew wanted to come.

"Sheer curiosity," Iverson said, when I asked him why. "How many people get the chance to see a second inhabited system in one lifetime? None of us are missing this."

So all of us went, the entire sixteen, curiosity winning out over any argument for staying behind. Watching them all pile in for the detour, I recognized the same impulse that had put most of them on this mission in the first place.

The crossing itself took the better part of two years, ship time, kick and coast the same disciplined way we'd covered every other stretch of this whole program, except this time nobody was counting down toward a mission objective. We were just going somewhere, together, because a promise mattered more than a checklist did, and the whole crew treated those two years less like a transit log and more like the first real vacation any of us had taken since the program started. Iverson taught half the crew a card game none of us had ever heard of and refused, on principle, to explain its actual rules until we'd lost enough hands to deserve knowing them. Grebenchekov ran a standing bet on Rocky's opinion of every new record Cent brought out, and lost more of it than he ever admitted to out loud. Grace finally had the time he'd never quite found before to read Rocky whole books instead of songs, a chapter at a stretch whenever both of them had an evening to spare, and came out the other side of those two years with a running catalogue of which human narrative structures repeated themselves and which ones apparently only worked once.

Rocky spent most of that stretch quieter than I'd ever seen him, not withdrawn exactly, just turned inward in a way I recognized because I'd done the same thing myself once, counting down toward something too large to talk about casually. Grace kept mostly to himself too, more than usual, and I noticed but didn't push, the same way I'd learned not to push at anything he clearly needed to work through on his own first.

NASA had the whole two years to solve the suit problem properly, and solved it exactly the way this program solved everything else: calm, methodical, try and fail and try again, the same discipline that had built not-water and the gimbal and every other piece of hardware nobody had ever gotten right on the first attempt. A dedicated twenty-nine-atmosphere suit, engineered for Eridian conditions specifically rather than adapted from anything built for Earth or Proxima b, went through four separate pressure-hull failures on unmanned test rigs before the fifth design actually held. I ferried crew back to Earth a few at a time for fittings as the design matured, never more than three or four people gone from Wayfarer at once, until everyone had a suit built and tested specifically for their own body, which, by the way, was everyone, sitting ready in a locker long before any of us needed it.

Osei spent the last stretch of the approach with a tablet in one hand, running comparative structural analysis against every alien-built thing she'd ever gotten real data on, muttering to herself about load paths and material science she'd never once expected an entire planet's architecture to hand her all at once. Fraiser stood near the medical bay's own viewport instead of the main one, quieter than usual, already turning over what an actual clinical exam of an entire Eridian population might someday look like, if Grace's whole strange embassy ever needed one. Harding just watched the atmospheric readouts scroll past, hungry for it, a man who'd spent a career reading incomplete weather off a planet he already knew, finally handed a whole new sky nobody on Earth had ever modeled at all.

I stood beside Rocky at the observation port as Erid resolved out of the dark, a world unlike anything our own solar system had ever shown us, its own colors, its own light, its own atmosphere wrapped around it like something patient and old. I glanced at him and found something in his posture I hadn't seen since before the shielding failure, a kind of stillness that read, even across every difference between us, as unmistakably like coming home.

"Good?" I asked, the same word Grace had used, because I'd picked it up from him without meaning to.

The translation came back simple, the same way it always did with him.

"Good."

Rocky POV: I had heard my own world from outside itself exactly once before this. The first time, my whole crew stood together at instruments none of us fully trusted yet, newly built, newly launched, hearing Erid fall away beneath a ship none of us would ever bring home again. There had been no time to actually listen to it properly, not really, not with the object already waiting and every hour spent on a world instead of on work we did not have the hours to spare. I did not know, hearing it then, in whatever fraction of attention I could actually spare for it, that I was hearing something none of the rest of them would ever hear a second time. I did not know, either, that I would be the only one of us who ever did.

This time was different in every way that mattered. Not launched away from something, with an object still waiting and every wasted hour a cost someone would eventually have to answer for. Arriving back toward it instead, aboard a vessel built by hands that were not my own, in the company of a species that had found me half-dead in the dark and decided, for reasons I still do not fully understand and no longer feel any real need to question, that I was worth the keeping. Nothing was waiting on the other side of this arrival that required haste. For the first time in my life, I was permitted to simply listen to it, for as long as listening actually took, and I discovered, standing there, that I did not know how to do that quickly even once I no longer had any reason not to. My own crew heard their own world fall silent behind them, alone, uncertain, believing they would return one day to tell their own people what they had found. I was hearing mine resolve out of that same silence instead, uncertain in an entirely different direction, wondering only whether the people I was bringing home to it would still recognize the man who had left.

Cent stood beside me the whole time, silent, understanding, correctly, that this was not a moment that needed narration. Grace stood a little apart, giving me the same distance he had learned to give me for anything that actually mattered, which I had come to understand, by then, was its own kind of gift.

I do not have a word yet, in either of our languages, for what it is to be the only survivor of a launch and also the only one who gets to hear the return. I have had a very long time to think about it since. I still do not have one. Some things, I have decided, are not made worse by remaining unnamed.

Wayfarer settled into orbit, and getting down to the actual surface turned out to be its own new problem before I ever got as far as worrying about jump-site logistics. Twenty-nine atmospheres meant a completely different descent than anything Threshold had ever been certified for: thicker air braking us far harder, far earlier, than Proxima b's own thin, unforgiving atmosphere ever had, a heating curve that looked nothing like the one Iverson and Childs had spent years perfecting, drag numbers they ended up re-deriving from scratch rather than trust to a profile built for a world with a fraction of this one's own pressure.

"It's not harder," Childs told me, running the new numbers a third time before she'd sign off on any of them. "It's not easier either. It's just a completely different sky, and I'm not flying anybody into it on Proxima math."

Harding spent that same stretch buried in atmospheric data of his own, decades of confident weather-reading meeting a sky that ran on rules he'd never once had to account for. "Twenty-nine atmospheres of mostly ammonia doesn't behave like anything I've ever personally called weather," he told me, somewhere in the middle of it, "but it still behaves like something. Give me a few more days and I'll have opinions worth trusting." He did, eventually, hand Childs and Iverson a real profile of expected turbulence bands and pressure layering, built the same patient way he built every read of every sky he'd ever actually understood, one stubborn data point at a time.

We took the shuttle down once all three of them had actually finished, first trip to the surface with just Rocky and Grace aboard. The descent itself was rough almost from the first moment we hit the outer edge of that impossible atmosphere, the shuttle bucking harder than even Harding's own profile had fully prepared us for, ammonia haze streaking the outer ports a deep orange-brown, heat building on the hull in the same uneven, patchy way he'd warned us to expect and that none of us had quite believed until we felt it firsthand.

"This is certainly living up to your name," I told Rocky, teeth gritted through another hard jolt, strapped in beside me and entirely unbothered by any of it.

"Explain," he said, and I decided, mid-shudder, that now was not the moment.

We came through anyway, rough the whole way down, and set down on open, unmarked ground a careful distance out from the nearest cluster of Eridian structures, hard enough to rattle every strap in the cabin but clean enough that nothing on the hull ever came close to failing. Rough. Real. But through, same as it always seemed to be out here.

Setting foot on actual Eridian ground, boots first, in the suit NASA had spent two years building for exactly this pressure and nothing else, I still felt the weight of the place land on me the second I cleared the hatch: heat that fogged my own faceplate before I'd taken three full steps, an atmosphere so dense it moved around me more like water than like air, and the deep, almost unfathomable dark of a place that had never once seen light, my own helmet lamps carving out a small, largely failing exception against darkness this world had never once needed to make on its own behalf. The suit did exactly what two years of failed test rigs had promised it would, and not one gram more than that.

It was enough. A verified site, walked, real, mine to jump back to whenever I actually needed to. Standing there, boots on ground that had never once had a human footprint on it before mine, I felt something click into place that I hadn't fully planned for: once I had that, reachable directly, Wayfarer never needed to move under her own power again. She could simply stay in orbit above it. Lab. Relay. Depot. A permanent foothold, here instead of at Proxima b, exactly the working asset the program had always wanted, just in a different sky than anyone had originally planned, watching over a friendship none of us could have predicted when this whole mission first left the ground.

I called Truman before I called anyone else, standing back aboard Wayfarer with Erid still turning slow beneath us.

"She's not coming home," I told him. "Not in the way anyone planned for. She's staying here instead."

He was quiet a moment, and waiting through it, I knew he was doing the same math I'd already done myself: budgets, precedent, every institutional argument a program this size could throw at a decision this size.

"Good," he said finally. "That's a better use of her than anything we originally drew up for her anyway. Log it. I'll handle the rest from this end."

"That's it? No committee?"

"There'll be a committee. There's always a committee. But you already made the actual decision the second you saw what she meant to him, and no committee on Earth is going to argue you out of that one, so I'm not going to waste either of our time pretending otherwise."

I stood there a long while after the call ended, watching a world turn beneath a ship that had just quietly become its own permanent piece of history, and thought about how strange it was that the biggest institutional decision of the whole detour had taken less than a minute to actually make.
