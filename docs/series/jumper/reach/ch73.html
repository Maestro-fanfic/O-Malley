McGarrity POV: I'd delivered a great many pieces of news to the American public by then, going all the way back to my first days as Chief of Staff standing quietly at the edge of someone else's briefings. Nothing had prepared me to stand at my own podium, as President, and share it with someone who wasn't human.

We verified everything first, same discipline the program had always run on, the same discipline Bartlett had reminded me of more than once since he'd taken the job I'd once held myself. What we could actually state as fact, and nothing beyond it, before a single word went out to the public.

Bartlett found me in the small room off the briefing hall ten minutes before we went live, going over the same three lines of my own remarks I'd already gone over a dozen times that morning, less because they needed the polish and more because my hands needed something to do that wasn't shaking.

"You don't have to say anything beyond the opening," he said. "Truman's got the technical framing. Cent's got the personal weight. This is a formality, not a performance."

"Everything about this job has been a formality that turned out to matter enormously the second I actually said it out loud." I set the cards down. "I keep thinking about the fact that whatever I say in the next fifteen minutes is going to be the first thing history remembers a human president ever said to another species. That's not a formality. That's the whole weight of everyone who's ever held this office, standing behind me at once."

"Then say something true and let the weight take care of itself. It generally does, with you."

I nodded, and didn't fully believe him, and walked out to the podium anyway, because believing him had never actually been the requirement. Doing the job regardless of the noise in my own chest was.

Truman spoke. Grace spoke. Cent spoke, brief and plain the way she always was, the same directness I remembered from the very first time I'd watched her speak in front of a camera, years and years before, when I was still someone else's staff and this whole program was still just an idea with a number attached to it. And then Rocky, joining a presidential address for the first time in the history of this country, or any country, his own voice carrying first across the broadcast, alien, unmistakably not human, several notes landing at once, layered, chorded, something closer to overlapping music than anything I'd have called speech on my own.

The synthesized translation caught up a beat behind it, and I felt that gap the same way, I suspect, everyone watching felt it: the vertiginous space between hearing something alien and understanding, half a second later, what it actually meant.

"We are grateful," the translation said, flat and mechanical, carrying almost none of the weight the original sound had carried. "We did not expect to find you here."

I stood there at my own podium and felt the whole scale of it land on me at once, bigger than anything the address format had ever carried before, bigger than the night my own landslide confirmed itself on a split screen while an ocean's worth of water fell calibrated and gentle over a drought-stricken camp on the far side of the planet, Holcomb still in the room with me when the numbers came in. I'd thought that night was the biggest thing I'd ever witness in this job. Standing there now, I knew how wrong I'd been.

Intelligent contact had occurred. Peaceful. Ongoing communication, actually working, both directions. That was the whole of what we said, and it was more than enough.

Bartlett found me again afterward, in the same small room, both of us quiet for a moment before either of us trusted our own voice to say anything useful.

"You did say something true," he said finally.

"I don't even remember most of what I said."

"That's usually how you can tell it was true. The rehearsed parts, you remember. The real ones just happen, and you're stuck afterward trying to reconstruct them the same way everyone else is." He almost smiled. "For what it's worth, Mr. President, I don't think anyone who held this job before you ever had a better first line for this particular occasion."

I sat down, finally, the adrenaline draining out of me all at once the way it always did once the actual moment had passed and left only its aftermath behind. Somewhere out past the edge of everything I could see or reach, a species that wasn't ours had just heard a human voice speak to it, honestly, and answered back. I didn't know yet what that was going to mean for the rest of my presidency, or the rest of the species, or the rest of anything. Sitting there, I could tell nobody in the room with me knew that either, and that we were all, together, about to find out.

Carter POV: I ran the first spectrometer scan on the resin casing myself, mostly out of idle curiosity while everyone else crowded around the star map, and got a result I read three times before I trusted it enough to say out loud.

Xenon. The whole surface, edge to edge, nothing else.

"That's not possible," I said, to nobody in particular, then again, louder, once Ochoa actually looked up. "Xenon is a noble gas. It doesn't form bonds. It doesn't do this. You cannot build a load-bearing anything out of an element that refuses to react with literally anything else on the periodic table. That is not how chemistry is allowed to work."

"And yet," Ochoa said, nodding at the casing still sitting on the table, entirely solid, entirely real, entirely unbothered by what I'd just said about it. "Xenon's whole claim to nobility was never reacting with anything. So what does that make this stuff? Ignoble?"

I ran the scan four more times, on four different samples, in case the first result was contamination, or a calibration fault, or some kind of elaborate joke at my expense. It wasn't. Whatever process had gone into making that material, it had apparently solved a problem in noble-gas chemistry our own periodic table said flatly could not be solved, and it had done it, as far as I could tell, on purpose, as a packaging material, without leaving behind a single note about how.

We started calling it xenonite within the hour, mostly because somebody had to call it something, and none of us had a better idea what else an impossible element was supposed to be named.

Osei put a proper hardness test on a sample before anyone let her near it with anything sharper than a fingernail, and came away more frustrated than satisfied. A diamond-tipped scriber, rated for tool steel, left nothing behind but a faint smear of its own diamond. The mill wouldn't cut it. The bench vise couldn't crack it under torque that had already bent the vise's own jaw first. "I have never once in my career failed to leave a mark on something I was actively trying to damage," she said, setting the ruined scriber down with the particular gentleness of someone handling a small personal defeat. "This is going to be a very annoying material to work with."

We flagged that sample for the return manifest before the day was even out, first in line for NASA's own labs once we actually had a way to get it there, whatever heavier equipment Earth-side metallurgy could throw at it that we simply had no room to carry out here. Nobody on Wayfarer expected a different result. Everybody wanted to be wrong about that anyway.

It also explained something that should have bothered us from the very first hour: an entire alien hull built out of flat, unreinforced panels, the worst possible geometry for holding back pressure, doing exactly that anyway, twenty-nine atmospheres of ammonia contained behind a wall no thicker than a finger. Nobody builds a pressure vessel that way unless the material itself has simply made the usual rules irrelevant.

Ochoa POV: Rocky's own composition read to me less like biology and more like geology the first time I got real data on it, and the closer I looked, the less that first impression ever actually let go. His carapace turned out to be built from oxidized minerals, not tissue in any sense I'd have recognized. His skeleton was a honeycombed metallic alloy instead of bone. What should have been blood ran as liquid mercury through him, and even his own nerves carried their signal as inorganic silicate light-pulses instead of the electrochemical kind ours do. The actual living part of him, cell for cell, amounted to only a few kilograms total, tucked in around a brain that sat protected dead center of that carapace, everything else built to serve and shield that one small, precious core the same way a planet's own crust serves and shields whatever's still molten at its middle.

Even the way he cooled himself read more like an engine than an animal. Ammonia moved through him the way steam moves through a turbine, carrying heat out to a radiator built into the crown of his own carapace, five slits venting it back into open air without a single molecule of that air ever actually touching his own bloodstream. I spent an entire evening cross-referencing his tissue density against basalt samples I'd pulled off Mars myself, half convinced I'd made some elementary error, before I finally accepted that the error wasn't mine. He really was built more like a planet than like an animal, and I'd simply never had cause to look at a living thing that way before.

Nasser POV: I worked it alongside her, the geophysics of him, an entire living creature that made more sense as a planet's own chemistry given shape and motion than as an animal in any sense either of us had trained for. It was the strangest, most thrilling data set of my career. I told Ochoa, more than once, that Rocky had personally ruined every future rock sample for me, because nothing I'd ever collect again was going to be capable of asking me a question back.

Cho POV: My own turn came separately, the direct biological study neither of theirs had actually been. Life detection, contamination protocols, everything I'd trained for pointed now at a living, thinking creature standing three meters away instead of a hypothetical. I don't think I stopped smiling for most of that first week.

Fraiser POV: I went in with nothing but curiosity, no clinical agenda at all, and I think Rocky understood that immediately, the way patients sometimes understand which visitor actually wants to know them and which one wants to study them.

I was also the one who asked, gently, once we had enough shared vocabulary to risk it, what had happened to his crew.

The translation that came back was simple and plain, no drama attached to it, and somehow harder to hear for exactly that reason. They had grown sick, all of them, with no visible cause he had ever been able to name. He had not. He did not have a word in his own language for whatever had actually done it, because his own people had never once needed one.

He gave me one more piece of it, unprompted, in the same flat register he gave everything else that actually cost him something. "I watch. They no wake up." I didn't understand at first, and he explained it the patient way he explained everything technical, even this: his own people sleep in total paralysis, mind included, unable to be roused for any reason. Period. That absolute helplessness is exactly what makes his whole species so vulnerable, and so dependent on having someone else nearby who hasn't gone under at the same time, which is why watched and not-watched is the actual line his species draws between safe and not. He had been watching. That was the whole of it, and also the whole of what he actually had. He had done the one thing his own culture asked of him, exactly the way he was supposed to, and it had not been enough. He did not know why. He had never known why. Watched, and not woken anyway, was the entire shape of an answer he had been carrying around unfinished for however many years it had actually been.

I didn't push further. Some griefs don't need excavating, just acknowledging, and I made sure he understood I'd heard him.

Childs POV: My own scene came once he started showing us where he was from.

The object he sent across that time was bigger than anything he'd built us yet, a whole cluster of embedded points scattered through a clear resin matrix, each one set at its own careful depth, no two on the same plane. I didn't need more than a glance to know I wasn't looking at decoration. I was looking at a map, three-dimensional, to scale, of every star system within whatever range his own instruments could resolve, ours among them, marked with the same small incised symbol he'd used for his own home the very first time he'd tried to tell us anything at all.

I found our own system before anyone else in the room even started looking. I'd know that kind of configuration blind, in the dark, with both hands tied behind my back, the point I already knew better than any other star in the sky besides the sun I'd grown up under. Then I hunted for the second mark, the one I already suspected was coming, and found it embedded deep in the far corner of the resin, farther from us than I'd let myself guess out loud beforehand: something like three light-years, give or take, close enough to reach in a handful of years at our own cruise speed, once anyone actually decided to go. Not close. Not impossibly far either, not on the scale we'd already proven ourselves capable of crossing. The kind of distance that would have sounded insurmountable to me four years earlier and now just looked like the next item on a list I hadn't known existed yet.

Reading a sky and trusting it to tell me exactly where I actually was had been my whole career, long before any of this. I sat with that disbelief a long while, holding a map of a star system I'd never expected to see confirmed as real, home to someone standing right in front of me, and did the only thing that felt adequate to the moment: I asked him to walk me through every other marked point in the resin, one at a time, and he did, patiently, as though he'd been waiting the whole conversation for someone to finally ask.
