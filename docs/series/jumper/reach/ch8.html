Chapter 8 of 89

Harding: Six days after the wedge came through Coyote County, the seven of us were still finding pieces of Dorothy V scattered in trees a quarter mile from where we'd lost her, and none of us had said much of anything worth remembering out loud yet.

Dorothy V was the whole apparatus: the steel drum and its hundreds of small sensor pods, the release rig, the mast of anemometers and wind vanes bolted around the frame reading the approach in real time, the beacon, the antenna, all of it strapped down in the bed of my own truck, brand new three weeks earlier, replacing the one the tornado took from me the last time any of this actually worked. Jo and I had designed her together, years back, before Wichita and everything that came after it pulled me away from actually building anything myself, and Jo's own team had built the physical units off that same design, one after another, exactly because they'd known from the start how many of them the work was probably going to cost. This was the fifth one off that line, built the same week as the rest and held in reserve until now. Jo drove, same as she always had, and I rode the release rig myself in the bed behind her, both of us running the same two-person sequence neither of us had apparently ever actually forgotten how to work together, and this was the first time she'd actually gone all the way through one clean, every pod transmitting, every reading intact, right up to the moment we ran out of time getting clear. I got the release fully cycled and we bailed, both of us clear of the cab before the wedge ever actually reached the truck itself, and it still wasn't distance enough. The wedge crossed our position directly, took the truck bed and the drum both, and scattered the wreckage across three different fence lines. We'd wanted this exact data for years. Getting it cost me a second truck in as many weeks, and cost Preacher most of the feeling in his left hand where a piece of the drum's own housing had caught him on the way past, and none of us were in any real hurry to celebrate yet.

We were in what was left of Meg's place, the pole barn, the one structure on the whole property that had come through the other tornado standing, gear spread out across two workbenches doing double duty now as the only flat surfaces she had left. Sorting salvage into piles that made sense to nobody but us, watching the six of them work in the kind of silence that only comes after everybody's nearly died together, I already knew I was looking at something that had started ending, even before anybody said so out loud. Everybody in the gang had called her Aunt Meg since long before I ever joined it, an honorary title she'd earned the old-fashioned way, by feeding and housing and occasionally bailing out every single one of us at one point or another, and by never once asking to be paid back for any of it.

Meg: I'm sixty-one years old, and I have chased more storms than I can accurately count anymore, and I saw the one I actually wanted to see six days ago, so I am done, plainly, so nobody spends the next year wondering if I meant it.

I lost the house days before that, and I want it on the record that the storm that took it and the wedge that just took Dorothy V aren't the same event in my own accounting, whatever the timing looks like from the outside. One made me finally admit I was tired. The other made me admit I still had one more good look left in me before I let myself be. Both things are true. I've stopped expecting anybody to find that tidy.

I taught Bill everything his own father didn't get the chance to finish teaching him, and I did it because he was worth teaching, not because I owed anybody's memory a debt. He surpassed me a long time ago in the actual reading of a storm, which I've never once resented, whatever he might tell you. What I taught him was patience. The reading, he built entirely himself, out of grief and stubbornness and more nights on a truck hood than any sane person's spine should have to forgive.

I'm not sad, watching this end. I chased the big one for thirty years and I finally got a clean look at her, and I walked away standing upright, which is more than a great many people in my line of work ever get to say. That's not a tragedy. That's the whole rare, lucky shape of a life well spent doing something worth doing.

Jo: I watched that wedge take the only thing I ever built that worked exactly the way I'd drawn it, twice now, in one lifetime, and it doesn't get any easier the second time, whatever anybody tells you about growing a thicker skin for it.

We designed her together, back before either of us had any business designing anything, back when we were both still trying to prove something to a field that had already decided neither of us was worth listening to. He moved on to Wichita not long after, chasing a career instead of a storm for a while, and it was my own team that actually got her built, real, unit by unit, on my own time, off a design neither of us had ever quite let go of even while we weren't speaking. Losing this one doesn't undo everything the rest of them already proved. I know that. I still stood in that field longer than I needed to, watching the last piece of her come down out of a tree, before I could make myself go help sort the rest.

I'm not the one deciding anything tonight. Meg's earned her ending. Laurence's about to earn his beginning. I'm just the one who's sat through enough of these nights to know this gang doesn't hold its shape forever, and that whatever comes next for the rest of us is going to ask something real of every single person in this barn, whether we're ready for it yet or not.

Laurence: I built the relay rig that actually caught all of it, this exact one, still in one piece in the truck bed we didn't lose, and I am not, generally, an emotional person about hardware, but I will admit I sat with the recording a solid five minutes before I could make myself start logging it properly.

It worked. That's the part I keep coming back to. Six years of relays that half-worked, mostly-worked, worked right up until the exact moment it mattered and then didn't, six years of building toward a day I genuinely didn't know I'd ever actually see, and the one time everything actually rode on it, mine caught every single pod's signal clean, start to finish, zero dropout, the first time any of us had ever actually gotten that. Bill and Jo designed Dorothy together, years before I ever showed up to this gang, and Jo's team built her real, every piece, the same week as the rest of the line, years after that, and I've spent six years just trying to build something worthy of actually hearing her properly. I've got an offer sitting in my inbox from an actual aerospace instrumentation lab, the kind of place that would've laughed me out of the building three years ago, and I think I'm finally going to answer it, because I built something that worked when it counted, and I want to find out what I can build next when somebody hands me an actual budget instead of a mother's garage and a credit card.

I'm going to miss this more than I'm letting myself admit right now. I'm also, if I'm honest, more excited than I've been in years. Both of those are true at once, and I've stopped expecting that to stop being confusing.

Mara: I drove every mile of every chase this gang ever ran, and I'll keep driving after the rest of you scatter, because I don't actually know how to stop, and I stopped needing anybody else in this barn to understand why, a good while back.

I'm not built for the version of this life that ends with a garage full of salvage and a decision to walk away clean. I watched Meg get her clean ending and I was happy for her, and watching it, I knew it isn't going to be mine. There's always another season. Another cell building somewhere past the horizon that nobody's read right yet. I don't know how to want anything else, and I've stopped apologizing to myself for that.

I'll miss all of you specifically. I won't miss this particular life, because I'm not actually leaving it. Somebody has to keep driving, and it was always going to be me.

Preacher: I've got two fingers on my left hand that don't answer the phone anymore, so to speak, and I'll say plainly that I consider that a fair price, all things considered, for the best look at a wedge any of us ever got in six years of trying.

I'm the one who doesn't get a clean ending or a bright new beginning, and that settled into me easier than I expected it to. I'm going home to my wife, who has waited eleven years for me to say a sentence like the one I'm about to say, and I'm going to say it: I'm done chasing anything I can't feel with both hands anymore. Not because the storm took something from me. Because it gave me what I came for, and a man's allowed to recognize when a debt's been paid in full and walk home instead of asking for more.

I'll tell this story for the rest of my life, missing fingers and all, and I intend to tell it well.

Dusty: I'm twenty-three years old and I got launched about eleven feet by a gust that peeled a tin roof off a grain silo like it was wrapping paper, and the second thing I felt, right after landing, was pure, uncomplicated joy, because I am, by every clinical measure Reyes would probably apply to me if she ever got the chance, not entirely right in the head about any of this, and that's just who I turned out to be, plain as anything.

Six years chasing with this gang and I still can't wait for the next one. Meg's earned her rest and Preacher's earned his and I understand both of those completely and want no part of either one. Wake me up the second something's building. I'll have my boots on before anybody's finished saying the word tornado.

Rabbit: I called the wedge's exact heading off a hand-drawn line on a paper map eleven seconds before the truck radio confirmed the same number, and that map was rolled, not folded, same as every map I've touched since the fight I had with Laurence about it three days before we ever left for Coyote County. He still thinks I overreacted about Wichita. I don't. A crease through the wrong county costs you real seconds when it actually matters, and on the one night it actually mattered, that exact habit is the reason Mara had us moving before the number ever came through official.

I don't chase for the rush. I know that's not the popular thing to admit around this particular table. I chase because somebody in every truck has to be the one still counting exits while everyone else is watching the sky, and I decided a long time ago I was going to be good at the job nobody else wanted. Tonight it almost wasn't enough, and I keep circling back to that word, almost, because almost is the only reason I'm sitting in this barn instead of standing in a field explaining to somebody's family why the map wasn't fast enough this time.

I don't know what I do once this gang scatters the way it's about to. I've never once chased for the storm itself, if I'm honest with myself about it. I chased for exactly this: everybody left, crowded around whatever table somebody could still find the next morning, alive enough to argue about something that doesn't matter at all. I'll take that over a clean look at the big one any day of the week, and I don't care who at this table thinks less of me for saying so.

Truman: I saw the house before I saw anyone in it, what was left of it, a chimney and half a staircase standing alone in a field the tornado had otherwise scraped clean, somebody's whole kitchen still hanging in the tree line a quarter mile out like the storm hadn't finished deciding whether to give any of it back.

I'd read the county's own preliminary damage assessment on the drive down, numbers on a page, the kind of report I'd read a hundred times before about a hundred other places. It hadn't prepared me for the actual smell of it, wet insulation and cut lumber, or for the sound underneath the sound of my own tires on the gravel: nothing. No power tools yet, no crews, just wind moving through a structure that used to keep wind out.

What I hadn't expected, coming around the last bend in that drive, was the table. Somebody had dragged sawhorses and a sheet of plywood out from wherever they'd survived and set up breakfast in the middle of it, eight people crowded around a griddle running off a propane tank, laughing about something I couldn't hear yet from that distance. I sat in the car a full minute before I made myself get out, trying to understand what I was actually looking at. Not denial. Not shock wearing a strange face. Just people who'd already decided, faster than I ever could have, that the only sane answer to a morning like that one was breakfast, together, out loud, before anybody let themselves feel the rest of it.

I got out of the car eventually. It took me longer than I'd have admitted to anyone standing there watching.

Harding: I didn't have my own piece ready to say out loud that night, still turning over what Meg's ending and Laurence's beginning and Mara's staying all meant sitting in the same barn together, and I still didn't have it ready the next morning, when Aunt Meg put out a breakfast big enough to feed twice our number off a two-burner camp stove and a folding table dragged out of the barn, same as she'd apparently decided to keep doing the morning after any chase that had come close enough to actually scare her, house or no house.

Eggs, biscuits, a whole skillet of sausage gravy, the good coffee she only broke out for occasions she considered worth marking properly, and all eight of us crowded around that folding table passing dishes and not talking much about the wedge at all, which was its own kind of talking about it. That was when the car pulled up outside, government plates, entirely too clean for the mud every one of our own trucks had tracked across what was left of that yard for a week straight.

Aunt Meg clocked it coming up the drive before any of the rest of us did. "Well," she said, not looking up from the burner, "somebody's lost."

Nobody was lost. The man who got out introduced himself as Truman, NASA, standing there in a suit that had no business anywhere near a working farm, let alone one still missing its own house, and got about four words into explaining himself before Aunt Meg cut him off entirely.

"You can talk once you've eaten something," she said, already dragging a second folding chair over next to Laurence. "Nobody negotiates anything serious around me on an empty stomach. Sit."

He looked, for one honest second, like a man who'd planned out how this conversation was going to go and had not planned for this particular obstacle. Then he sat, and didn't argue again, which told me something about him before he'd said a single useful word about why he'd actually come.

He ate like he meant it, too, which told me something else. Two biscuits, a full plate of the gravy, and he was three bites into the second biscuit before Dusty decided he'd been quiet long enough.

Dusty passed him the biscuits before anybody had properly introduced him to the table. "Pass the gravy, Extreme," he said, over Truman's head, like the man in the government suit sitting between them wasn't even there.

Truman looked up from a biscuit he hadn't been offered yet. "Extreme?"

"Because he is," Dusty said, like that settled the whole question on its own.

"Man is the single most reckless human being I have personally ever chased weather with," Preacher said, "and I have chased weather with some genuinely unwell people."

"Second most," I said. "There's a name in front of mine on that particular list, and everybody at this table knows exactly whose."

"Oh, here we go," Mara said, not looking up from her coffee.

Jo pushed back from the table, coffee still in hand, before the story had even properly started. "I've heard four different versions of this exact story in the six days I've been back around this table," she said, "and I'm not sitting through a fifth before I've had actual water today." She was already moving toward the tank rigged up behind what was left of the barn's back wall before she added, without turning around, "Don't let him talk anybody down from the honest version. I mean it."

"I always tell the honest version," I said, to her back.

"You have never once told the honest version in your life," she said, not breaking stride, and it landed sharper than the joke it was pretending to be.

"So we get this one near Daleton, right?" Dusty said, undeterred, "and we've got a wedge on the ground, and we are way too close, closer than any of us should've admitted to being at the time, and out of absolutely nowhere this shitty-looking green Valiant comes fishtailing around the bend and about takes off what was left of Mara's front end."

Rabbit set his fork down. "Oh, God," he said, to no one, already knowing exactly where this was going.

"There wasn't a front end left to take off by that point in the season," Mara said.

Beltzer pointed his own fork at me, delighted, like a man presenting evidence. "He's naked."

"Butt naked," Rabbit said, upgrading his own testimony for the record.

"Naked," Beltzer agreed.

"NOT naked," I said. "I was NOT naked!"

Beltzer leaned toward Truman, not quite quietly enough to actually count as an aside. "He was without apparel."

"Half naked," I said.

"Naked," Dusty said, final word on the subject, and the whole table laughed.

"And he's got a bottle in his hand," Dusty said, riding straight over the objection, "a full bottle, and to this day I do not know where a man wearing absolutely nothing was also keeping room to carry a bottle, and he walks straight past every one of us, straight at the wedge, calm as church, and holds it up like he's fixing to make a toast."

"I was making a barometric point," I said.

"You were not making a barometric point," Dusty said. "You looked that thing dead in whatever a tornado's got for an eye, and you said, 'you look thirsty,' and you drank a good third of it yourself first, and then you pitched the rest straight in, and it never came back down."

"Caught it clean," Preacher said. "Sucked it right up."

"It's a magic trick," Dusty said, "and it's the only one you've got, and it's why we call you what we call you, and no review board in this country is ever going to talk you out of it, so stop trying."

"For what it's worth," I said, "there was another Bill. An evil Bill. And I killed him."

Aunt Meg came back with a fresh plate she hadn't strictly needed to make and set it down in front of Truman without comment. "That man they're describing doesn't live anywhere near me," she said. "That one only shows up when something's actually about to try and kill him. You got the quiet one today. Count yourself lucky."

"I'd still like to hear more about the bottle," Truman said, and he meant it.

"Later," Meg said, in a tone that ended the matter, and refilled Truman's coffee a second time without being asked.

It was a solid fifteen minutes before she finally nodded at him like a woman granting a permit.

"All right," she said. "Now you can talk business."

"I read your data," he said, to me specifically, like he already knew exactly who at that table he needed to be talking to. "The pressure signature you pulled out of the wedge, six days ago. Most of it shouldn't have been readable at all, by any standard model. You read it anyway."

"That's the whole job," I said. "Nothing about it's supposed to be readable. You learn to read it anyway or you don't do the job at all."

"Your own team's file called you the human barometer," he said, not quite a question. "I wanted to know if that was a nickname or an actual working method before I drove out here."

"Little of both," I said. "Ask me again once you've watched it happen instead of just read about it."

I got up from the table, still holding my coffee, and walked out toward what was left of the foundation with him following, curious despite himself. Bent down, took a handful of the yard's own dirt, loose and dry from a week without rain, and let it go through my fingers slow instead of all at once, watching the way it caught, drifted, hung a beat longer on one side than the other before it settled.

"Front's building faster than anybody's forecast has it," I said, straightening back up. "Eighteen, twenty hours out. Nothing on your phone's weather app is going to agree with me on that for at least another six."

He didn't say anything for a second, looking at the dirt still settling around my boots like it might explain itself further if he waited long enough.

"I'll want that in writing eventually," he said. "For now, I'll just remember you said it. I've got a problem that works the same way, as it happens. Different sky. Same actual problem, underneath it. I'd like to talk to you about it, if you've got the time."

I looked around that table once, at Meg already at peace with an ending, at Laurence halfway into a beginning he'd earned honestly, at Mara who was never going to stop no matter what any of the rest of us did next, at Dusty already half done with a second plate and utterly unbothered by any of it. Looking at all four of them at once, I finally had an answer to the question none of us had said out loud yet.

"I've got the time," I said.

"Good," Aunt Meg said, refilling his coffee a second time without being asked. "Then you can finish that plate while he tells you about it. I didn't cook all this so it'd go cold over business."

The front I'd read off that handful of dirt arrived almost exactly on schedule, eighteen hours later, right as Truman and I were still working through the actual shape of what he was offering. Mara was already moving before the first outflow boundary showed up on anybody's radar, same as always, truck loaded, gear checked, that particular restless energy she carried into every chase she'd ever run.

"You coming?" she asked, at the door, and hearing the question, I realized it was the first time in eleven years anybody in that gang had needed to actually ask it instead of just assuming the answer.

"Not this one," I said.

Nobody made a thing of it, exactly, which somehow made it land harder than if they had. Laurence stopped halfway through loading a case of spare sensors and just looked at me a second, working something out silently before he nodded once and kept loading. Aunt Meg didn't say a word, just came over and put her hand flat against my cheek for a moment, the same gesture she'd have given a kid heading off to his first day of school, and went back to clearing the table like that settled it, because for her it did.

Mara paused longest, keys already in hand. "Read it right, though? The front."

"Read it right."

"Then it'll still be there when you catch the next one," she said, which wasn't quite forgiveness and wasn't quite permission either, just Mara's own particular way of saying she understood, and drove off without waiting on anything more from me.

I stood at the edge of Aunt Meg's ruined yard and watched three trucks pull out down the same muddy road Truman's sedan had come in on, chasing a storm I'd called, for the first time in longer than I could remember, from the ground instead of from the middle of it. Watching them go, I knew how much of my old life I'd just quietly agreed to trade for whatever came next.
