Chapter 47 of 89

Osei / Halstead / Davy: Two sides of the same glass, and the man in the middle of it, watching the same fifteen seconds and understanding almost nothing the same way.

Osei POV: I ran the heating coils zone by zone, warming Davy's depth garment against the brine he'd be dropping into, flat confirmatory checks, no drama in any of it. Davy centered over the retention throat, stepped down, and twinned to the abyss the way he'd done it a dozen times before.

Containment throat camera, interior, wide-angle, mounted low against the curve of the wall. A man in a depth-rated suit standing at center frame, motionless, and then, between one recorded frame and the next, water instead, filling the same volume his body had occupied a frame earlier, a sphere with an edge the lens could actually resolve as an edge, not a splash, not a pour. The frame rate does not slow down for what it cannot otherwise explain.

Davy POV: The abyss doesn't announce itself, not even after however many times I've gone looking for it by now. One second I'm standing in a fabrication bay listening to Osei run through her own checklist in my ear, and the next the cold finds every seam in my suit at once, the water black and total and older than anything I have any real language for, pressing in from every direction like it's been waiting specifically for me to show up.

I don't stay long. I never do. Long enough to find the shape of it, hold the volume steady in my own head, and let go before the cold gets anywhere close to costing me something I can't get back. I surfaced back in the fabrication bay still half expecting to feel wet, and never once actually was.

Halstead POV: I'd spent eighteen years as chief engineer for a regional water authority, and not one day of that career had prepared me for a briefing packet that used the word teleportation without a hint of irony anywhere in it. I stood back from the observation glass with my binder held against my chest like it could stop something. My town's entire twenty-four-hour grid demand was about to clear this one room in fifteen seconds, and I'd run that arithmetic four times already that morning without it getting any easier to hold in my head.

Osei POV: The link triggered clean on my console. Position lock. Endpoint reassignment. Routine data, nothing more dramatic than a line item clearing green.

Halstead POV: On my side of the glass, a perfect, impossible sphere of dark alien water simply existed inside the containment throat, there without ever having arrived, and I felt my whole understanding of how the physical world worked take a step sideways it never quite recovered from.

Osei POV: The slam hit structure-borne, exactly as modeled. I watched the hydraulic accumulator pistons, each one scaled like a utility pole, drive into their nitrogen cushions and bleed the oscillation down through clean, predictable decay cycles until the whole system settled onto a flat plateau. Twenty-five thousand gallons a second, steady, unchanging.

Halstead POV: The shockwave came up through the concrete slab and into my heels before I heard it, and my binder actually buzzed against my ribs from the vibration. I flinched. I'm not ashamed of that. I'd have judged any engineer who didn't.

Osei POV: The roar held steady for minutes, no degradation, nothing the model hadn't already told us to expect.

Halstead POV: It kept going. And going. And somewhere around the two-minute mark I looked over and found two NASA technicians eating pastries by the console rows, utterly unbothered, and understood that my own panic had officially outlasted its welcome. Nothing was going to change. Nothing was going wrong.

I signed the tie-in clearance. Not because I felt confident. Because the evidence had simply worn me down past the point of arguing with it, and I've learned, over a long career, to recognize when that's actually the correct response.

Osei walked me out afterward, offered me a coffee I badly needed, and asked, conversationally, like she hadn't just upended eighteen years of my professional understanding of the physical world, whether I had any questions before I drove home.

"One," I said. "Does it get easier. Watching this."

"No," she said, without hesitating even slightly, which I appreciated more than a comforting lie would have earned from her. "You just get better at standing next to it while it happens."

I drove home with the binder still clutched against my chest out of habit, long after there was anything left in that room to actually be afraid of, and thought, somewhere past the county line, that I was going to need a considerably bigger binder for whatever came next.
