Donna (POV): I've fielded a lot of strange phone calls working in this building. That one still ranks near the top.

The phone on my desk rang, the outside line, not the switchboard extension, which by itself was odd enough that I picked it up expecting bad news before I'd even finished reaching for it.

"Donna."

"This is Donna."

"It's Cent. I need your help. I want to play a prank on Leo and your boss."

I actually laughed out loud, glancing toward Leo's open door out of pure reflex, like he might already sense a plot against him forming three offices away.

"Okay. I'm listening."

She explained. It took her about ninety seconds, and by the end of it I understood exactly two things: it was going to work, and there was no version of this I could pull off without backup.

"I need to get CJ in on this," I said, already reaching for the other line before Cent had even finished agreeing.

CJ picked up on the second ring, the way she always did when she was mid-walk somewhere and using the phone as an excuse not to stop moving. I gave her the same rundown, start to finish, and she didn't interrupt once, which from CJ was its own kind of alarming. She let me get all the way to the end before she said anything at all.

Then she said it loud enough that I heard it twice: once through my own receiver, and once, half a second later, bleeding straight through the wall from Josh's office next door, because apparently he'd left his own door open.

"Cent, NO!"

"Cent, YES," I said back, not bothering to lower my voice, since whatever was left of the secret was already dead.

Josh's door banged open instead of staying shut, which was the wrong instinct and he'd have known it if he'd stopped to think for even a second. "Donna, what the heck's going on out here?!"

"We're planning something for Leo."

Josh's door started swinging shut on reflex, the same fast retreat he always managed whenever trouble so much as glanced in his direction. It stopped halfway. Swung back open just as fast.

"Wait." He was already halfway out of his office, tie still crooked from whatever he'd been doing before any of this started. "Leo?"

"Leo," I confirmed.

"I'm in." No hesitation at all, none of the reflexive caution from thirty seconds earlier, like the word Leo had simply overridden every survival instinct he owned. "Whatever it is. I don't even need to know yet. I'm in."

"You should probably know first," I said, and put Cent back on speaker so nobody had to relay it secondhand a third time. CJ leaned in over my desk. Josh planted both hands flat on the edge of it like the extra six inches of proximity would help him hear better.

"I've made a 'frame' of my own," Cent said, and I could hear the grin in it even through a phone line. "It's only about the size of a portrait."

"Okay," I said, when she didn't immediately keep going. "And?"

"Do you know what happens if I hold that frame up flush with a solid object?"

Nobody answered right away. Josh got there first, slow, like he could already feel the shape of it even without the details yet. "I'm going to go ahead and assume that's rhetorical."

"It is," Cent said, entirely too pleased with herself. "It doesn't care what's in the way. Doesn't know the difference between open air and a locked drawer or the back of a wall. It just connects what's on one side to what's on the other, and whatever's sitting there when it does gets treated exactly the same either way."

"Look at your phone," she said.

My phone buzzed on the desk before I'd even finished parsing the sentence. A photo, Cent holding her own little frame up in front of her, grinning at whoever'd taken the picture. The side facing the camera wasn't even trying to look real: flat, cartoonish brickwork, thick black outlines and exaggerated shading straight off a Saturday morning rerun, and tucked into the bottom corner, small enough that I almost missed it on the first pass, a tiny painted Road Runner mid-stride, beak open, dust cloud and all.

Nobody needed the cartoon explained to them. CJ said it out loud anyway, flat, disbelieving, delighted despite herself.

"She built the coyote's tunnel."

CJ took considerably longer to come around than either of us did, running through every objection a press secretary is contractually obligated to think of before anyone else gets the chance: security, the dignity of the office, whether "practical joke targeting the President of the United States" was a phrase that could survive contact with an actual congressional inquiry. Josh countered every single one of them with some version of "yeah, but it's Leo," which, infuriatingly, kept working.

"Fine." CJ, hands up, the universal surrender gesture of someone who already knew exactly how this was going to end and had simply run out of energy to keep pretending otherwise. "But we do this on an actual quiet day. I am not staging this during anything that could remotely be described as a news cycle."

Quiet days were, by this White House's own standards, roughly as common as a lunar eclipse, but between the three of us and a scheduling favor Josh still apparently had banked from two administrations ago, we found one three weeks out: nothing on the book past a routine senior staff check-in, both Leo and Bartlett actually in the building at the same time with nowhere else they needed to be.

I texted Cent a single word. Set.

CJ, for the record, did not get her quiet day. None of us knew that yet, standing around my desk congratulating ourselves on a plan with no possible downside. I've since learned that's usually the exact moment a plan starts arranging its own downside behind your back.

Bartlett (POV): You reach a certain point in this job where "I hear you have something to show us" stops sounding like an invitation and starts sounding like the only warning you're going to get.

It was supposed to be a quiet morning, senior staff check-in, nothing on the schedule behind it worth rushing through. Leo was parked in his usual chair, coffee in hand, radiating the calm of a man who had personally verified there was nothing on fire anywhere in the building. Josh had shown up early, which should have told me something all by itself, and had spent the last ten minutes being suspiciously agreeable about absolutely everything on the agenda.

"I hear from Josh," I said, when Cent came in, "that you had something you wanted to show us."

"Yeah." She was carrying a flat rectangular package under one arm, wrapped in plain brown paper like something picked up from a framer's shop, and set it down against the front of the Resolute desk with the unhurried care of someone who'd clearly rehearsed this part. She peeled the paper away in one motion.

Underneath it was a frame. An actual picture frame, thin metal edges around a stretch of plain canvas, and the side facing Leo and me showed absolutely nothing at all. No painting. No photograph. Just blank fabric, taut and unremarkable, giving away exactly nothing about why any of us were supposed to care.

"It's a frame," Cent said. "One of mine. Same trick as the big ones out at Centauri, just small enough to carry under one arm."

Leo, already leaning forward in his chair with the particular wariness of a man who'd learned the hard way that this family's small tricks tended to have large consequences. "Should I be worried?"

"Depends how you feel about your own office wall." She turned it slightly, angling the blank canvas toward the two of us without quite showing us anything yet. "Do you know what happens if I hold this flush against a solid object?"

"I feel like you're about to tell us regardless of the answer," I said.

"It doesn't care what's in the way. Doesn't know the difference between open air and the back of a locked drawer or a load-bearing wall. It just connects one side to the other, and whatever's sitting there when it does gets treated exactly the same either way."

Leo looked at me. I looked at Leo. Neither one of us, I think, fully believed her yet, and I suspect that was entirely the point.

"So what," Leo said slowly, "does that actually mean, in practice, for my office wall specifically?"

"It means I found out how to turn the frame off." She said it plainly, like she wasn't in the middle of detonating something. "Not just open or closed like the big ones. I found how to flip it the other way entirely, from something that things can pass straight through to something completely impervious. Like a brick wall."

That landed slower than I expected it to, and I watched Leo get there half a beat behind me. Two different states, and nothing about looking at it would ever tell you which one you were currently standing in front of.

"So it can be a hole in the world," I said, working it through out loud, "or it can be a wall. And you can switch between the two."

"Whenever I want." Cent's grin hadn't moved an inch. "As fast as I want."

I opened my mouth to ask her to demonstrate, and Leo was up out of his chair before I got the first word out, some old instinct in him apparently deciding that whatever happened next, it wasn't going to happen to me first.

"Show me."

Cent didn't argue. She stepped out into the middle of the room and twinned it right there, standing, holding the frame out in front of her at arm's length. Whatever was on the other side was dark, featureless, giving away exactly nothing about which of her two states it currently was.

Leo reached out and put one finger against the canvas.

It didn't go anywhere. It just stopped, the way a finger stops against any ordinary wall, and I watched him register that a full second before his face caught up to it. He tried again with his whole hand this time, flat, real pressure behind it, and got the same answer: a plain, unremarkable surface that gave him nothing at all, solid clear through, exactly as convincing as the real thing because at that particular moment, as far as his hand could tell, it was the real thing.

"Maybe this is something the military could use?" Josh, from somewhere near the door, sounding entirely too pleased with himself for a man who'd spent the last three weeks pretending this whole thing terrified him.

"Good idea." I was already turning toward the door myself. "Mrs. Landingham!"

The door opened before the sound of it had even finished bouncing off the walls.

"Get me General Gray."

"I think I just saw him a minute ago." She didn't so much as blink, which told me everything I needed to know about how much of this she'd already been read in on, and how little of the actual content she'd actually been given. Gray, it turned out later, had spent that entire morning parked in an office two doors down, told in advance that something was happening today worth sticking around for, and told absolutely nothing else beyond that.

"Well, find him."

She was already gone before I finished the sentence, the door easing shut behind her with the same unhurried competence she'd brought to this job since long before I'd understood what half of it actually required.

Gray came in without knocking, which told me exactly how much Mrs. Landingham had briefed him on the way, and stopped a few feet inside the door to take the room in with one unhurried sweep. Josh, practically vibrating. Cent, standing off to one side with an expression that gave away everything and nothing at once. And Leo, dead center, one hand still pressed flat against a blank stretch of canvas like a man checking a load-bearing wall for a stud.

Gray didn't ask what he was looking at. He just came to a stop, hands loosely clasped behind his back, and waited, the same unbothered stillness I imagine had carried him through thirty years of mornings that started exactly like this one, with no warning at all.

"Cent had something to show us," I said. "We figured if you were in the building, it might interest the military."

That was the whole explanation I offered him. I watched him wait a beat for the rest of it, realize none was coming, and decide, evidently, that thirty years in this business had taught him better than to ask for more than he was given.

Gray crossed the room and stepped up beside Leo without a word, studying the canvas the way I imagine he studied anything that had shown up on his desk without a proper briefing attached. Then he reached out and tried to put his hand straight through it, same as Leo had.

Same result. Flat stop, no give at all, like the air itself had suddenly decided to have opinions about where his hand was allowed to go. He didn't pull back the way Leo had. He pressed in, slow and deliberate, testing it the way you'd test a weld, fingers spreading out to feel the edges, the texture, the faint give of taut fabric under real pressure that nonetheless refused to let anything actual through it.

I watched him work it out, or most of it. He clearly understood what he was looking at, in the sense that a man who's spent his career around things designed to do exactly one impossible thing recognizes another one when his hand runs into it. What he plainly didn't have yet, and what was written all over his face while he kept pressing at it anyway, was how.

"Okay, young lady." He finally stepped back, arms folding, giving her the same flat, evaluating look I imagine he'd once given every piece of hardware that had ever crossed his desk without an obvious explanation attached. "You've got my attention. How?"

Cent grinned and pointed over her shoulder, out the window behind her, at nothing in particular that I could immediately identify. "Take a look at that."

Every head in the room turned, mine included, some reflex too old and too automatic to override in time.

Out on the Portico, flush against the outside of my own building, stood Cent. Holding the exact same frame, at the exact same height, pressed flat to the exact same stretch of wall we were all standing in front of from the inside.

I actually looked back over my shoulder, at the Cent still standing in my office, and then back out the window at the Cent standing on my lawn, and gave up entirely on saying anything intelligent for a solid three seconds.

By the time I got my bearings back and looked toward the frame itself again, it had already changed hands, spun end over end so fast I only caught the tail of the motion. Cent, the one actually in the room with me, was holding it exactly as before, same stance, same easy grip.

Except it wasn't blank anymore. The side facing us now was flat, cartoonish brickwork, thick black outlines, exaggerated shading straight off a Saturday morning rerun, and tucked into the bottom corner, small enough that I nearly missed it, a tiny painted Road Runner mid-stride, beak open, dust cloud and all.

Every head in the room turned back at once, and I watched it land on each of them in turn, a full second of dead silence before anybody's face caught up to what they were actually looking at. Leo's jaw actually dropped, no other word for it. Gray just stared, arms still folded, like he was waiting for the joke to reveal some deeper tactical purpose he hadn't found yet.

I didn't manage half that much composure. I was laughing before I'd even finished putting it together, too hard and too suddenly to stay on my feet, and I had to sit myself down on the corner of my own desk before I did something undignified like fall over in front of a three-star general. "I knew there was something off about how agreeable Josh had been all day!" I got the words out somewhere in the middle of it, pointing at him without much accuracy, the pieces of the morning finally rearranging themselves into a shape that actually made sense.

Leo had found his voice again, if you could call it that, a string of half-formed sentences that kept collapsing under their own weight before they reached an actual verb. "You... she... that's not... how did you even..."

Gray didn't say anything at all. He just stood there, arms still folded, staring at Cent with an expression I couldn't fully read yet, somewhere between disbelief and the grudging respect of a man watching someone else pull off the single most audacious operation he'd personally ever witnessed. On his own Commander in Chief, no less, inside the Oval Office itself, with the full cooperation of half the senior staff and apparently a general's worth of advance warning nobody had thought to give him.

"Young lady," he finally said, quiet, almost reverent. "That may be the single best prank I have ever seen pulled in thirty years of federal service."

That flat, funeral-dry delivery was what finally broke the whole room. Josh went down first, actually down, folding straight onto the carpet in a heap and staying there, laughing too hard to bother getting back up. Donna, who I hadn't even noticed slip in behind Gray, was doubled over next to him, laughing too hard herself to be any use at all pulling him back to his feet.

And in the doorway, of all people, was Mrs. Landingham. Composed, unshakeable, imperturbable Mrs. Landingham, laughing as hard as any of them, one hand braced flat against the doorframe, the only thing keeping her upright.

Nobody in that room thought, even once, to ask whether anyone outside had a clean, unobstructed view of a second Cent standing on the Portico holding a small frame flush against the White House wall. Somebody did. A friend of CJ's own, somewhere in the press corps, had a camera parked on that exact stretch of lawn for reasons nobody ever did manage to pin down afterward, some unrelated favor or standing arrangement CJ herself had no idea existed until long after the tape was already out in the world.

Nobody thought to ask about the room's own cameras either, which was, in hindsight, a considerably bigger oversight. "Cameras running, doors open" had meant exactly that since long before that particular Tuesday, a standing policy nobody had ever thought to suspend just because the thing happening in front of them was small and personal instead of official. Somebody on the inside, somebody who never once needed to know a single thing about a frame or a prank or a cartoon coyote, only needed to know how to pull a file off the White House's own recording system, did exactly that, same as they'd have pulled any other file anyone asked them for. This family didn't do things quietly, on principle, and it turned out the building they'd finally moved their business into didn't either, not by policy, not for anyone, not even by accident.

And somebody, somewhere, took both of those files, the Portico shot and the Oval Office feed, and cut them together, perfectly synced, split screen from the very first frame: an empty stretch of Portico on one side, Leo and me mid-conversation on the other, both of us still entirely unaware anything was about to happen to either version of that morning. The outdoor reveal landed frame for frame with Leo's own jaw actually dropping on the inside. That part I've never once managed to get anybody to take credit for. I have my suspicions.

CJ had asked, in plain language, for a day with no possible news cycle attached to it, from the one person in that building whose job it was to actually know where every camera pointed.

She did not get one.

Cent (POV): I was three states away, halfway through explaining the frame's tolerance specs to somebody who didn't actually need to know them, when I felt my own phone start buzzing against my hip, once, twice, three times in quick succession, before I'd even finished the sentence I was on. Notifications didn't usually stack up like that unless something had already gone wrong somewhere, and some old, well-worn instinct in me was already bracing for the wrong kind of news before I'd even looked down to check which kind it actually was.

Then it started ringing outright, cutting straight through whatever the notifications had been building toward.

"Cent, it's Donna." Her voice had a pitch to it I hadn't heard from her before, not panic exactly, something closer to a woman watching a wave she'd personally helped build finally reach the shore. "I think you might wanna get here right now. The whole thing just went live."

I didn't ask which whole thing. There was only one it could be.

I jumped.

The West Wing hallway outside Leo's office had a version of chaos in it I recognized instantly, exactly the flavor this family's own history seemed to specialize in: too many people moving too fast in too many directions, everybody's phone in their hand, everybody's face lit by whatever was currently playing on it. Donna found me before I'd even finished orienting myself.

"It's everywhere," she said, already turning her own screen toward me. "Every network. Every feed. Somebody synced the Portico shot to the room footage and it's already past ten million views and climbing, and nobody in that building has any idea who actually did it, and CJ is currently trying to figure out if there's a professional way to say 'no comment' about a video that's already answered every question anyone could possibly ask."

On the screen, small and tinny and utterly unmistakable, was my own face, grinning, holding a frame painted like a Looney Tunes tunnel, while General Gray delivered his verdict with the flat, funeral-dry timing of a man who had no idea he was about to become the most quoted person on the internet by lunchtime.

"Well," I said, watching myself watch myself. "That escalated."

"That's the word CJ used too, except with a lot more syllables in front of it."

Joe (POV): I found out the same way half the country did, which was, I reflected, exactly the kind of sentence I'd have laughed at if anyone had handed it to me as a hypothetical two years ago.

Sterling was the one who noticed first, in his own way, before any of us had a screen in front of us to explain why. He looked up from his own console a beat too early, head tilted slightly, the same old instinct that had carried him through thirty years of rooms that were about to tell him something before they actually did. He didn't say anything. He just watched the door for a second longer than the moment strictly called for.

My own phone buzzed first, then Truman's, then Millie's, in a ragged little cascade across the ready room, and I had the clip open before I'd even fully processed why my hands were already moving. Millie's whole face did something I hadn't seen it do in months when I turned my screen toward her, somewhere between genuine alarm and the helpless delight of a woman who already suspected exactly which of her family members was responsible before she'd even pressed play.

"Oh, she didn't," Millie said, phone held out at arm's length like it might bite her.

"She absolutely did." I was already grinning, unashamed, tilting my own screen toward Truman before he'd finished fumbling his own phone out of his jacket. "Ten million views and it's not even lunch yet. It's still climbing while we're standing here watching it climb."

"That can't be real." Truman took the phone from Millie outright, both hands on it now, holding it the careful, slightly reverent way people hold things they don't quite believe are actually happening. He watched about fifteen seconds of it in total silence, no reaction at all, and then laughed, short and startled, the kind of laugh that gets away from a man before he's fully decided whether to let it. "Is that General Gray."

"That is General Gray." Sterling had crossed the room by then himself, close enough to see over Truman's shoulder, arms folded, his own reaction landing a solid four seconds behind everyone else's, the way it always seemed to whenever this family did something new to him. On the little screen, small and grainy and utterly unmistakable, two Cents held the same painted frame up on either side of a White House wall, a cartoon Road Runner mid-stride in the corner, Leo's own jaw dropping in perfect, split-screen sync with a hole opening in that wall four hundred feet away. Gray delivered his line right on cue, flat and funereal, and the comment section underneath was already scrolling too fast to read a single one of them.

"This deserves better than a phone screen," I said, already moving, already commandeering the big wall display we normally reserved for actual telemetry. I had the same clip pulled up and thrown across twelve feet of monitor before anyone thought to object, Gray's own face suddenly the size of a small building. "There. Now it's an event."

"You don't sound upset," Millie said, glancing at Sterling sideways, still not quite looking away from her own screen.

"I'm not upset. I gave up being upset about this family's own particular brand of chaos somewhere around the second year I worked with you." Sterling kept watching anyway, unable to look away from it any more than the rest of us could. "What I'm doing right now is the math. Specifically, how many separate congressional briefings I am personally about to sit through, explaining a genuinely significant piece of interstellar logistics hardware to a room full of very serious people who first heard about it from a cartoon roadrunner."

"Worth it," I said, still grinning, not even pretending to feel bad about it.

"Oh, absolutely worth it." Sterling didn't try to sound like he disagreed, because he didn't. "I just want it on the record, for whoever's writing the after-action report on this one, that I saw this coming the exact moment my own people signed off on letting that girl build a portable version of anything. I should have flagged it as a risk. I filed it as a delight instead. That one's on me."

Millie was still laughing, quiet now, wiping at one eye with the back of her hand. "You could have said no."

"I could have." Sterling watched the number on the screen climb another few thousand, right in front of us, real time. "I notice nobody in this family has ever once made that particular option look appealing."

"Somebody should call Cent," Truman said, still watching his own screen, still grinning despite himself.

"I've got it." I was already dialing before anyone could argue about who should be the one to do it, phone to my ear, grinning at the rest of them while it rang. "She's about to get the single most chaotic phone call of her entire week, and I intend to enjoy every second of delivering it."

It rang twice.

"This is Cent," she said, and I could already hear, even in three syllables, that she had absolutely no idea what was coming.

"You should sit down for this," I said, and didn't even try to keep the grin out of my voice.

Cent (POV): CJ herself came around the corner at that exact moment, phone in one hand, a printout in the other, moving with the exact velocity of a press secretary who had already skipped past every intermediate stage of processing and gone straight to solving. She didn't slow down for either of us.

Toby caught up to the whole group at the threshold, still visibly assembling himself, tie half knotted, looking like a man who'd been informed of the day's actual events approximately four seconds ago and was still waiting for the rest of the briefing to catch him up. "You missed a lot more than a war," Sam told him, not unkindly, already past him into the room.

Bartlett was behind the Resolute desk with a folder open in front of him when the whole crowd of us came in behind Leo, and looked up a beat slower than any of us, still half inside whatever the folder had been telling him a second earlier.

Leo leaned in first, and I watched his whole face do something I can only describe as a fish freshly landed on a dock, mouth opening and closing twice with nothing coming out either time. "Is that... is that us. Is that this office."

"That's this office," CJ confirmed, arms crossed, watching not the screen but the two of them watching it, the weary professional patience of a woman who'd already seen this clip enough times to have it memorized.

That was the exact moment Toby's head appeared to come clean off his own neck.

"Ten million views." He wasn't pacing yet, but I could see the pacing arriving, some tight coiled thing in him a half-second from actually letting go. "Ten million people have watched the Oval Office get turned into a prop. There is footage, actual footage, of a general putting his hand through a solid wall on national television, and nobody, not one single person in this building, thought to call me before it went out?"

"Nobody called anybody, Toby," CJ said, entirely unbothered. "That's rather the point."

"That is not comforting." He was fully pacing now, hands doing half the work his mouth couldn't keep up with, the same tight furious line I'd watched him wear down into a rug on at least one previous occasion. "There is a process. There has always been a process. The process exists specifically so that ten million people do not find out what the Commander in Chief did with his Tuesday before I do!"

Bartlett came around the desk to look for himself, and the whole crowd of us stood there together, crammed shoulder to shoulder around one small screen, watching the same footage unfold that I'd lived through in person only the day before, seeing it for the first time the way the rest of the world had.

The blank canvas came up first, and Leo actually pointed, sharp, like a man identifying a suspect. "That's the exact moment I asked if I should be worried."

"You should have been," CJ said, dry, not looking away from the screen herself now.

The frame flipped states, my own recorded voice explaining impervious and permeable, and I watched Bartlett's mouth move very slightly, silently repeating his own line back at himself a half second before it played, the small vanity of a man discovering he'd been genuinely quotable and hadn't realized it in the moment.

Then the Portico came up, the second Cent standing there holding her own frame against the outside wall, and Leo made a sound I wouldn't have been able to spell if someone had asked me to. "There's two of you. There are two of you standing right there and I watched this happen and I still don't understand how there's two of you."

"Neither do I," I offered, entirely unhelpfully.

The reveal landed, cartoon brickwork and all, and Bartlett's own face on-screen went from confusion to that same unguarded, delighted collapse I'd watched happen live, except this time I got to watch it happen twice, once on the screen and once, a half-beat behind it, on the actual man standing next to me, his real face catching up to his recorded one like an echo finally arriving.

Gray delivered his verdict, flat and funereal, and Leo's on-screen jaw dropped in perfect time with his actual jaw dropping again, right now, watching himself do it.

"I look completely unhinged," Leo said, with something close to wonder.

"You look delighted," CJ corrected. "There's a difference, and about four hundred million people currently disagree with your read on it."

"Every major outlet's already running it," she went on, sliding the printout across as well now, for good measure. "I need a posture in the next twenty minutes or somebody else writes one for us, and I promise you, Sir, we will not like theirs."

Bartlett didn't look up from the phone right away. "Do we actually need to deny anything."

"No, Sir. Everyone in that video is smiling. Including you."

"Then I don't see the problem." He finally looked up, and he was grinning, openly, the same unguarded delight he'd worn the night before, watching himself get outmaneuvered by his own senior staff, if anything a little wider now that the whole country had gotten to watch it too. "Write me a statement that says exactly what everyone already watched happen. The President had a good afternoon. So did a three-star general. The Chief of Staff had an... interesting afternoon." A glance at Leo, not unkind. "No further comment necessary, because the tape already made the only comment worth making."

My own phone buzzed in my pocket right then, Joe's name on the screen, and I almost let it go to voicemail before I remembered where I was standing and who I was standing next to.

"Give me one second," I said, and answered it right there in the middle of the Oval Office, on speaker, because at this point in the day privacy felt like a fight not worth having. "This is Cent."

"You should sit down for this," Joe said, before I'd even finished saying it, and I could hear Sterling somewhere behind him, dry and resigned, and underneath that, closer to the phone, my own mother's voice.

A pause, longer than it needed to be. "Of course you are."

"Young lady!" Millie's voice, sudden and sharp enough that half the room actually looked at the phone. A beat, just long enough for me to brace for exactly the kind of lecture I deserved. "How could you..." another beat, worse than the first, "...have done this without us?!"

The whole room went, all at once, Leo and CJ and Bartlett and Mrs. Landingham together, drawn in from the doorway by the noise same as she always seemed to be, the relief of people who'd been braced for something actually serious finally getting to let go of it. Something about Millie's own outrage landed hardest on Mrs. Landingham specifically, a mother herself hearing that exact register of maternal fury from four states away and recognizing every last note of it, and she laughed too hard for a second to keep her own footing, one hand missing the doorframe entirely, and actually went, knees first, straight to the carpet.

Bartlett moved before he'd finished deciding to, closing the distance in two long strides and getting a hand under her arm before she'd even fully landed, still laughing himself, somehow managing both at once. "I've got you. I've got you, easy."

"I am fine," she informed him, with as much dignity as a woman could manage from the floor of the Oval Office, already laughing too hard to actually get up on her own. "I simply misjudged a doorframe."

"You misjudged a doorframe because Millie Rice is currently chewing her own daughter out for not inviting her to a felony." Bartlett got her up anyway, steady, careful, not letting go until he was sure she actually had her feet under her. "None of us are fine right now. I've stopped pretending otherwise."

Millie didn't slow down for any of it.

"Your father's been reenacting the Portico bit for Truman for ten straight minutes," she went on, right over the laughter, entirely undeterred, "and none of us got to actually be there. None of us. I found out from a phone screen same as the rest of the country, and I am your mother."

I laughed, mostly from relief myself now, watching the rest of the room fail completely to compose itself. "That's what you're mad about."

"That is absolutely what I'm mad about."

"You couldn't even give me a five-minute head start before calling, Joe?" I glanced at Bartlett, who was watching the phone with open curiosity now, and decided the two conversations happening on either side of me had earned the right to actually meet.

"Five minutes was never on the table. You know exactly how this family operates." I could hear Joe's own grin in it even through the line. "For what it's worth, we put it up on the big screen out here. Full twelve feet of General Gray. Figured you'd want to know it's officially an event now, not just a clip."

Bartlett actually laughed at that, a real one, and leaned toward the phone without waiting to be handed anything. "Sterling. This is your Commander in Chief. I'd like it on record that I have never once regretted anything this family has done to my own press operation, and today is not about to become the exception."

Whatever Sterling said back got lost under Millie, still going, something about how next time this family pulled off the single greatest prank in presidential history, she expected an invitation, and CJ, still writing, muttering under her breath about needing an actual White House switchboard extension for calls that started this way from now on.

The room was just starting to settle, everyone finding their own breath back one at a time, when Bartlett glanced up, caught sight of something on the ceiling I hadn't even thought to look for, and grinned.

"You know," he said, to nobody in particular, and everybody at once, "this is gonna land on the news too, right?" He didn't even try to keep the wink out of it.

The room went again, all at once, harder than the first time, Leo folding straight back down into his own chair, Mrs. Landingham laughing hard enough into Bartlett's own shoulder that he had to brace himself against the desk to keep both of them upright.

I stood there in the middle of it, phone held out like an offering, laughing right along with all of them, and thought, not for the first time that week, that this family had never once in its whole history managed to do anything by half measures.
