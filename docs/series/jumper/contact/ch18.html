Chapter 18 of 53

Priya Desai: I'd caught exactly one fabrication defect in the last eleven years by looking at a printout, and four hundred by trusting the itch at the back of my neck that showed up before the printout ever did. The itch was back now, staring at a joint-seal reading on Bay 6 that was technically inside tolerance and somehow still wrong.

I'd learned a long time ago not to argue with the itch, even when I couldn't yet explain it to anybody else, even when the number in front of me swore up and down it had nothing to apologize for. Eleven years of quality assurance taught you that tolerance bands were a floor, not a truth, a line some engineer had drawn years ago based on conditions that didn't always match the conditions actually in front of you. The number on Bay 6 was inside that line. It was also, somehow, wrong, and I'd rather spend twenty minutes proving myself paranoid than spend twenty seconds not checking and finding out the hard way, four months and forty light-years from anybody who could fix it.

I pulled the batch record. Traced it back through six hands to one signature, one line item, one number: a thermal expansion coefficient, hand-converted instead of pulled from the reference table, off by a factor that wouldn't matter for eleven years and would matter enormously for the one week the joint sat at Object-adjacent temperature swings nobody on Earth had ever built for. Whoever had done the conversion hadn't been sloppy, exactly. They'd been fast, and confident, and the two of those together were a more dangerous combination than actual carelessness, because carelessness usually left a mess somebody else noticed. Confidence left a clean-looking number that nobody thought to question.

I found the Eridian in the materials bay before my radio call even finished ringing.

"I need to show you something."

"I already know." She hadn't turned around yet, both hands still moving through an unrelated task, but the hands had gone careful in a way hands didn't usually go careful, like they were trying to prove something to somebody. "I felt it wrong. I converted the number myself instead of checking the table because I was certain, and certain is supposed to mean something, and it didn't, and now Bay 6 has a joint in it that I made wrong with my own hands and my own certainty, and I don't..." The hands finally stopped. "I don't know what to say to that. I have never not known what to say to something before."

"Hey." I didn't reach for her, wasn't sure yet if that read the same across species, settled for standing close enough to matter. "You know how many defects I've personally caught with my own two eyes in eleven years doing this job?"

"...No."

"One. Everything else was a printout, or someone else's second look, or dumb luck timed right. That's not a confession, that's the job. Nobody's eyes are the whole system. Nobody's certainty is the whole system. That's why there's a Bay 6 log with your name on it and a Bay 6 log with mine, and why both of us get to be wrong sometimes as long as one of us catches it before it flies."

"I don't want to be the one whose certainty everyone quietly stops trusting."

"You want to know what I'd actually stop trusting?" I crossed my arms. "Somebody who never rechecks, because they're so sure being right the first time makes them right forever. You caught yourself feeling it wrong before I even called. That's not the mistake talking. That's the same thing that got you four hundred and eighteen good panels in a row, just aimed at your own work for once instead of somebody else's."

She turned around at that, finally, and I got a real look at her face for the first time since I'd walked in, whatever passed for an Eridian's version of stricken sitting there plain enough that I didn't need years of cross-species fluency to read it. "Panel four hundred nineteen came off the line true this morning. I remember thinking, while I set it in the rack, that I finally understood what it meant to trust my own hands completely. Four hours later I converted a number I should have checked, because I trusted the same hands the same amount, and the trust was correct four hundred and eighteen times and wrong once, and I don't know yet how to hold both of those facts at once."

"Welcome to eleven years of my actual job description." I meant it lighter than it probably landed. "You don't hold both facts at once. You hold the second one long enough to fix it, and then you let the first one keep being true, because it still is. Four hundred and eighteen good panels didn't stop being good panels because panel four hundred nineteen had a bad number in the paperwork around it."

She was quiet for a moment, hands finally still all the way. "The joint. It has to come back out."

"It has to come back out."

"Then I will take it out, and I will use the table this time, and I will feel foolish doing it, and I will do it anyway." A pause, something that might have been the Eridian equivalent of a wry look, hard to read yet on a face I hadn't spent enough years around. "Humans have a phrase. 'Measure twice.'"

"Cut once, yeah."

"I only measured once. I will not make that mistake in that particular direction again." Already reaching for the table, for the actual reference numbers this time, her hands steady again, doing instead of spiraling. "Thank you for not making this take longer than it needed to."

"That's the job too," I said, and meant it.

I stayed long enough to watch her pull the bad joint clean, no wasted motion, hands running the reference table this time instead of her own gut, and logged the correction myself before I left, name right there next to hers on the Bay 6 record, both of us on the hook, both of us fine with that. It was, I thought, walking back out into the corridor, the actual difference between a good crew and a merely competent one: not that nobody made mistakes, but that mistakes got caught by somebody who cared enough to notice, and fixed by somebody who cared enough to feel bad about it for exactly as long as it took to make it right and not one second longer.

Yeth (POV): The board went green across three subsystems at once, and my hands were moving before my brain had finished catching up to why.

Power routing, life support loop, comms backbone: three things that had spent four months being built in separate bays by separate hands, tested in isolation, never once required to actually speak to each other, and now the integration rig had them talking, actually talking, power drawing correctly through the loop while comms held steady through the same busbar that was simultaneously feeding air scrubbers a room away. Nobody had built a system. Everybody had built a part. This, right here, on this readout, was the first moment any of it stopped being parts.

"Whoa, hey..." Marcus, the integration engineer three consoles over, half rose out of his chair. "Yeth, you good?"

I was not in a position to answer that verbally yet. My hands had things to do first.

"Is that a seizure. Do I need to call somebody."

"No." I got the word out between motions, both hands still going, fingers spread and shaking at the wrists, the one gesture that had exactly one purpose and no efficient way to explain it to somebody who'd never needed it. "It worked. Marcus. It worked. Not the piece. Not my piece, not your piece. The whole thing. Green on power, green on air, green on comms, all at once, all real, all listening to each other for the first time in the entire history of this project."

"I mean, yeah, that's the milestone, that's what today was supposed to..."

"You don't understand." I finally stopped, hands settling, and looked at him with the same intensity that always seemed to unnerve humans right before it charmed them. "You build a piece for months. You know your piece. You trust your piece. But you never actually know if your piece is going to agree to be part of something bigger, not until the moment it either does or doesn't, and there is no feeling in your body that prepares you for the moment it does. My hands don't have a smaller way to say this. This is the whole way."

Marcus looked at the readout again, then back at me, something loosening in his own shoulders that had been locked up since before the test even started. "Four months," he said, mostly to himself. "Four months and every one of my status reports said 'nominal.' Nominal doesn't cover this."

"No. Nominal is a very small word for a very large feeling." I finally let my hands go still, and became aware, the way you become aware of a room only after you've stopped moving through it, that half the integration bay had turned to look at whatever had just happened at our console. I didn't mind. Some feelings deserved witnesses. "Do you understand now why the panels never bored me? Every single one of them was headed here. Every single one of them was this moment, still folded up small, waiting for four hundred more just like it to arrive and finally agree to become something together."

"I don't think I've ever thought about my own status reports that hard in my life."

"You should. 'Nominal' is doing an enormous amount of work for one small word." I reached out and tapped the readout, gentle, almost reverent, the same care I'd have given a finished panel. "This is the actual shape of the thing we're all building. Not the metal. The moment the metal agrees to listen to other metal it's never met before. Humans built the parts. Humans and Eridians both built the parts. But nobody, not one person alone, could have built this specific green light. It required all of us being wrong about nothing, all at the same time, which has never once happened by accident in the history of anything complicated."

"No pressure," Marcus said, dry, but I caught the grin underneath it.

"Enormous pressure. I have never once pretended otherwise." I glanced at him sideways. "You held up your piece of it. Comms didn't blink once, the whole run."

"Yeah, well." He rubbed the back of his neck, human enough a gesture that I'd started cataloguing it as its own small, useful piece of data: this is what humans do instead of admitting they're pleased. "Four months of nominal reports had to be good for something."

He glanced around, saw nobody else close enough to witness whatever he was about to attempt, and lifted both hands, fingers spread, giving them an experimental shake that looked exactly as uncoordinated as four decades of not being built for it would predict.

I watched him with the frank, delighted attention of someone watching a large animal attempt something small and precise. "That is terrible."

"Yeah, I felt that happening in real time."

"Do it again. Faster."

He did it again, faster, worse, and I laughed, the sound sharp and unfamiliar in a room built for status callouts and torque specs, and for one full minute the board sat green and nobody looked at it, because for one full minute the thing it represented mattered more than the number confirming it did. Somewhere behind us, I heard somebody else's readout chirp its own green light, a different subsystem, a different pair of hands, and understood, watching Marcus fail spectacularly at a gesture that had never once been built for his own body, that this was going to keep happening, quietly, all over this scaffold, for as long as it took every separate piece to finally agree to become one thing.

Rossi (POV): Built like something that used to be broad-shouldered, and never remembered how to be anything else. That was my first thought looking at him, and it turned out to be the least surprising thing about him.

"Talk me through it again." The voice behind me sounded like it had been dragged over gravel for sixty years and never fully recovered, and when I turned around the man it belonged to looked like he hadn't fully recovered from much of anything either: Commander Earl Voss, retired, seventy-four years old, old and grizzled and crusty in a way that made you believe every word of his own service record just from looking at him, skin gone the color and texture of something that had spent six decades getting rained on and salted and left out to cure anyway. A permanent squint from decades of Newport News dry-dock glare, still in a Navy ballcap so faded the eagle on it had gone nearly blank. If you'd told me the man tasted like saltwater and barnacles, I'd have believed you without asking a single follow-up question.

"Junction serves four adjoining modules. Standard NASA doctrine, redundant life support feeds, but the routing's clean, single path per module, easy to trace, easy to maintain..."

"Easy to lose all four modules if one thing goes wrong in the wrong spot." Voss didn't move to look closer at the drawing. Didn't need to. He'd already seen it from four feet away, the way I imagined he'd learned to read a hull from across a dry dock before his knees stopped letting him climb down into one.

"Sure, in theory, but we design out the failure modes before they..."

"Son, I am seventy-four years old and every joint below my waist sounds like a bag of gravel when I get out of a chair. You know why?" He didn't wait for an answer. "Because I spent thirty-one years designing ships that got hit anyway, no matter how good the paper said the design was, and every single time, something happened that wasn't on the paper. I've got the knees to prove it. You don't design out the failure mode. You can't. You design for what happens after it, because after it is the only part that's ever actually up to you."

I looked at my own drawing like it had personally let me down. "NASA missions run three, seven, twelve people. You eliminate the failure mode because there's no spare capacity to route around it anyway."

"A station that moves like a ship needs redundancies behind its redundancies. Exactly because it's the one thing nobody expects that causes the failure." Voss shifted his weight, gravel voice settling into something almost patient. "Right. And you're not building a mission anymore, kid, you're building a ship, and this ship's own paperwork says crew of five thousand eventual, which means one day this junction isn't routing air to four science modules, it's routing air to four neighborhoods. You ever design a neighborhood?"

"...No."

"I have. Sort of." A beat, something in his face going quiet. "This project's bigger than any aircraft carrier I ever worked on. And I worked the big ones." He lowered himself into the chair by the drafting table with a groan, the sound of a man who'd made peace with his own decline decades ago. "A carrier's a floating city that has to keep breathing after something blows a hole in it, and you don't get to evacuate a city, so every system on that ship exists twice, minimum, on two different paths, through two different compartments, so whatever takes out path A never had a chance of touching path B in the first place. That's not redundancy for redundancy's sake. That's just what happens when you accept, going in, that something's eventually going to go wrong somewhere you didn't expect. I've watched it go wrong. More than once. I'm not young enough anymore to pretend it won't happen again just because we're doing it in the black this time instead of the water."

"You've actually seen it. The redundancy working."

"Nineteen eighty-eight." He said the year like it still cost him something, forty-some years later. "Fire in an engine room, wrong compartment, wrong time of night, and the paperwork said it shouldn't have been able to touch the systems it touched, and it touched them anyway, because paperwork's never once fought a real fire. Lost the primary feed to two full sections. Would've lost the men in both of them too, except some engineer thirty years before me had run a second path through a different compartment on principle, no specific threat in mind, just the same doctrine I'm handing you right now. That second path is the only reason I'm sitting in this chair instead of you reading about me in somebody's after-action report." He tapped the drawing once, hard, punctuation instead of decoration. "I've spent every year since trying to be that engineer for somebody else's ship. You're the somebody else this time. Congratulations."

I stared at the junction for a long moment. "You're talking about doubling the routing mass."

"I'm talking about doubling the routing mass on a junction that's currently a single point of failure for a quarter of your station." Voss pulled a fresh sheet toward himself, sketched slow, his hand not quite as steady as it must have been forty years ago but the lines coming out right anyway, two parallel paths instead of one, splitting off through separate compartments before rejoining downstream. "This is what a hundred years of putting steel in harm's way teaches you that four years of putting checklists in orbit doesn't. Not because NASA's wrong. Because NASA's never had to keep five thousand people alive after something already went wrong."

"That's a genuinely terrifying way to think about it."

"It's the only way that's kept a crew alive every time I've watched it get tested for real." He slid the sketch across, and his hand, setting it down, shook just slightly, the kind of tremor I'd stopped noticing meant anything after the first hour. "Run the mass numbers. Tell me what it costs. I already know it's worth it, but you're gonna need the numbers to convince the people who write the checks, and that part, that part I'll leave to you. I only know how to keep the ship breathing. Never did figure out how to keep a budget breathing, thirty-one years and I still can't do it."

I almost laughed, mostly from relief. "I can do the budget part."

"Good. Then between the two of us, this thing actually survives long enough to matter." He shifted in the chair, wincing at whatever that particular motion cost his hip, and caught me watching him do it. "Don't look at me like that, son. I signed up for this posting with both eyes open. Somebody told me a family of jumpers was building a permanent structure in deep space for a crew of five thousand and needed somebody who understood what happens after the paper's wrong, and I thought about staying retired for exactly as long as it took to say yes. My knees can complain about it on their own time."

"Most guys your age would've stayed retired."

"Most guys my age never got asked. That's the whole difference, and it's not a small one." He was already looking past me at the next drawing in the stack, an old man's doctrine, older than the space program itself, quietly rewriting a piece of it in real time, one bad knee and one steady hand at a time. "Show me the water recycling loop next. I guarantee you it's got the same problem."

It did. Single path, same clean elegant routing that looked perfect on paper and would fail exactly the same way the air junction would have, and Voss found it in under a minute this time, faster than the first one, like his eye was already learning the exact shape of this ship's blind spots. By the time we'd worked through four more systems the same way, I'd stopped bothering to defend the original drawings before he found what was wrong with them, and started just handing them over and waiting, because that, it turned out, was the actually efficient way to work with a man who'd spent thirty-one years learning to see exactly what I hadn't yet learned to look for.
