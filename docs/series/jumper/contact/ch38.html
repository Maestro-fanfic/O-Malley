Chapter 38 of 53

Millie (POV): I have spent twenty years learning to read a room before it finishes telling me what's wrong with it. Nothing in that whole career prepared me to read this one.

Fraiser walked me through it slowly, deliberately, the same careful pacing she'd have used delivering a diagnosis she needed a patient to actually hear instead of just survive hearing. Daniel's citation. The border nobody else would have thought twice about. The classification index. The marker, the same one sitting underneath Earth's own entry and Erid's, now sitting underneath a third.

Theirs.

I made her say it twice, not because I doubted her, but because some sentences need saying twice before the part of you that actually has to live with them finishes catching up to the part that already understood the words.

"The Builders," I said. "They're not just the ones who found this. They're not just the ones who nearly died from it and built a warning out of the wreckage." I heard my own voice come out steadier than I felt, which was, I supposed, its own kind of training paying off at exactly the moment it needed to. "They're descended from it too. Same root. Same as us."

"Same as us," Fraiser confirmed, quiet.

I stood there a long moment, in an archive built by people who'd been gone longer than either of my own species had names for time, and felt something shift underneath everything I thought I already understood about my own family.

I thought about Cent.

Fifteen years old, buried under two cubic yards of Yukon snow with an avalanche still settling somewhere above her, gone from the middle of an open slope to the reading nook under her own bed without ever deciding to go anywhere at all. I remembered how careful we'd all been with it, afterward, how private, how rare we'd let ourselves believe it was, an inheritance so strange it felt like it belonged only to us, a gift or a curse depending on the week, something to guard and manage and eventually, once it mattered enough, hand over to people who could keep it safe.

Cent was never a strange exception to something. Cent was closer to the surface of something that touched everyone.

Every human standing on this station. Every Eridian standing beside them. The Builders who'd built the very archive I was standing inside of, who'd nearly died of the same thing that gave my own daughter her whole life's shape, who'd left a message behind precisely because they understood, better than anyone, what it costs to carry something this size alone.

Phage wasn't a rare gift threaded through one bloodline on one small planet. It was the root everyone standing in this archive had grown out of, whether anyone had ever known to call it that or not.

"I need to be the one who carries this home," I said, once I trusted my own voice again.

Fraiser didn't argue. "I thought you'd say that."

"This isn't a briefing document. This isn't something that goes through a channel, gets summarized, lands on somebody's desk between two other memos." I was already moving, already building the shape of what I'd need to actually make this land the way it deserved to land. "This is family news. In the widest sense that word has ever had to stretch to cover in the history of anyone using it. It doesn't get to travel secondhand."

We spent the next hour building the briefing together, and lost, over the course of that hour, exactly one real argument: Fraiser wanted more of the technical scaffolding included, the actual verification chain, three methods cross-checked, so that whoever received it wouldn't have to simply take our word for something this size. I wanted it lighter, faster, closer to the bone, worried that too much math standing between the news and the people hearing it would let them hide inside the math instead of actually feeling the weight of what it meant.

She won. She was right to win. I understood, even arguing against her, that a claim this size needed its own proof walking in the door behind it, not just my own conviction that it was true.

I packed the case anyway, once the argument was over, hands steadier doing something physical than they'd been through any of the conversation that came before it. Fraiser watched me from the doorway, not saying anything else, the silence of someone who'd already said everything useful and knew better than to crowd the rest of it.

"You don't have to leave tonight," she said finally, gently, the closest thing to an objection she'd raise.

"I know." I closed the case. "I'm leaving tonight anyway. But there's one stop I need to make first."

I still felt it, walking out toward that stop before the jump point, the whole shape of the day sitting heavier in my chest than I'd let myself admit standing in the archive. Not fear, not exactly. Something closer to the vertigo of watching a wall you'd built your whole understanding of your family around quietly stop being a wall at all, and become, instead, a window into something far larger than the room you thought you'd been standing in the whole time.

Somewhere back home, Davy was about to find out his daughter's strangest, most private inheritance had never actually been hers alone to carry.

I didn't know yet how to feel about that. I only knew it was true, and that true, in this family, had always been worth carrying, whatever it turned out to cost getting there. But I wasn't ready to carry it home yet, not without asking the one person on this station who might actually be able to tell me whether it went deeper than a shared root in the ground.
