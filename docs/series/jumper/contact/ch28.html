Chapter 28 of 53

Daniel (POV): I'd stopped expecting anyone to knock on this door about that paper years ago. I'd been wrong before. I was about to be wrong again, more thoroughly than I'd ever been wrong in my life.

Nobody had cited the paper in four years. I knew, because I still checked, some stubborn, humiliating part of me refusing to fully let go of the possibility that somewhere out there, somebody would finally look at the evidence honestly instead of laughing it out of the room the way every serious journal in my field had.

Deliberate mathematical patterning in half a dozen ancient sites, precise astronomical constants encoded too exactly to be coincidence, evidence, I'd argued, of contact, or at minimum of knowledge that shouldn't have existed yet in the timeline anyone credentialed was willing to defend. I hadn't proven it. I'd known, going in, that proving it was going to be nearly impossible with the tools available to me. I hadn't expected the backlash to end my career quite as thoroughly as it had.

So when the knock came, I answered it already braced for a delivery, or a solicitor, or absolutely anything except what actually stood on the other side of my own door: a woman I didn't recognize, and beside her, a man I did, from a face that had been in the news more than once over the last several years for reasons that had nothing to do with archaeology.

The woman was holding my own paper. The actual printed copy, annotated in a handwriting I'd never seen, margins full of notes that looked less like mockery and more like genuine engagement.

"Dr. Jackson," she said. "My name's Cent Rice. This is Grace."

I knew who Grace was. Everyone did, by now, the vindicated schoolteacher whose own impossible theory had turned out to be real, whose own public disgrace had turned, eventually, into something the whole world had been forced to take seriously.

"If this is a joke," I said, "it's not funny."

"It's not a joke," Grace said, and I recognized something in his voice immediately, uncomfortably, the same register of a man who'd once stood exactly where I was standing now. "I read your paper three times before I believed what I was reading. Then I read it a fourth time to make sure I wasn't just seeing what I wanted to see."

"And what exactly did you see?"

"The same thing you saw. Deliberate patterning. Constants too precise for coincidence." He held my own gaze steady, no pity in it, which was somehow worse than pity would have been, because it meant he actually understood. "I know what it costs, being the person who says the thing everyone else has already decided is impossible. I know because I was that person too, once, before this same family showed up at my own door and proved I'd been right the entire time."

I didn't say anything for a moment. I'd had this conversation in my head a hundred times, imagined it a hundred different ways, and none of them had prepared me for how much it hurt to actually hear someone say the thing I'd spent four years being told I'd only imagined.

"You're going to tell me it's real," I said, flat, guarded, because if I let myself hope before I had proof, I didn't think I'd survive being wrong about it a second time.

"I'm going to tell you it's real," Cent said, "and then I'm going to ask you to come see it yourself, because I don't think either of us is going to convince you with words alone. Some things you have to stand in front of."

"And if I say no?"

"Then we thank you for your time, and we go find someone else who's willing to look." Grace said it without any edge to it at all, plain fact rather than pressure. "But I don't think you're going to say no. I think you've spent four years hoping somebody would finally knock on this exact door, and I think some part of you already knows we're not lying to you."

He wasn't wrong. I hated that he wasn't wrong.

"There's a structure," Cent said, "out past Centauri. A record device, built by something that isn't us. It's gated, encoded in exactly the way your paper predicted something like this would have to be encoded, primes, universal constants, the same math you spent your entire career arguing was too precise to be accidental." She let that sit a moment. "We need someone who can actually read it. We think that's you."

I looked at the paper in her hands, my own words, annotated by someone who'd apparently taken them seriously enough to travel here and hand them back to me instead of just calling.

"How would I even get there," I said, already knowing, somewhere underneath the question, that I was going to say yes.

"The same way we did," Cent said. "You'd have to trust something that's going to sound impossible until the exact moment it isn't anymore."

I thought about four years of empty citations, four years of checking a database that never once returned my own name in anyone else's footnotes, four years of being right and having absolutely nothing to show for it except a corrosive loneliness all its own, knowing you're right and nobody else believing you.

"Okay," I said. "Show me."
