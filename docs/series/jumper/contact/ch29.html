Chapter 29 of 53

Daniel (POV): Four years of empty citations hadn't prepared me for how it would actually feel to be right in front of witnesses.

The jump landed me somewhere I had no adequate words for yet, standing on a surface that existed nowhere in any textbook I'd ever taught from, staring at a record device that ran on exactly the mathematics I'd spent a career defending to rooms that had already decided I was wrong. I cracked the first segment inside two days, faster than I expected, faster than anyone expected, the primes and constants falling into place the way a language falls into place once you finally understand you've been reading it correctly the whole time.

Then the gate didn't open so much as answer.

No fanfare, no light show, just a low internal hum that I felt in my teeth before I consciously registered it as sound, and then something the size of a large dog unfolded itself out of a recess none of us had identified as anything but structural, made of what looked, to my own untrained eye, like solid glass. Raccoon-adjacent, Cent called it later, and she wasn't wrong, not exactly, but the resemblance came apart the longer I actually looked at it: too smooth in the wrong places, joints that didn't quite move like joints, an internal hum underneath its own voice that never fully stopped, closer to a small motor left running than to breath.

"I am The One Who Stands." The voice was calm, unhurried, and completely without the flatness I'd half expected from a recording. "I am a simulated person. I want to be clear about that word. Not biological. Still a person."

It spoke English. Not halting, not stitched together out of fragments the way I'd half braced for, but easy and unaccented, and it took me longer than it should have to stop being startled by that and start being embarrassed at myself for the startling. Of course it did. Half the crew out here still ran around with a laptop or a tablet left open on some console or other, unlocked, screen still lit, the way you do once you've spent enough months somewhere you've stopped thinking of as dangerous. It hadn't needed six years and a listening post. It had needed about ten minutes and whatever Kaylee had left running on the console two decks up.

Nobody spoke for a moment. Grace, beside me, had gone very still.

"You built something to keep watch," I said finally, mostly to myself, though the synthetic's attention swung to me immediately anyway, unnervingly precise. "That's what this is. A sentry, standing post."

"Yes."

Something clicked into place, an old, half-forgotten shape surfacing from the part of my brain that had spent a career reading precisely this kind of story before anyone had ever handed me proof one was real. "There's a myth," I said slowly. "Greek. Talos. A bronze man, built by Hephaestus, set to guard the island of Crete. Circled the coastline three times a day, every day, for as long as anyone could remember, throwing boulders at anything that got too close. Nothing could touch him. He didn't get tired. He didn't get old. As far as anyone in that story was concerned, he simply couldn't be stopped."

"But he was," Grace said quietly, already ahead of me. Grace always was.

"There was one vein of ichor running through him, sealed by a single bronze nail at his ankle. That was the whole design, one entire immortal guardian, riding on one seal, in one place, that nobody ever needed to think about until the day someone finally did. The Argonauts pulled it. He bled out standing up." I looked back at The One Who Stands, and found I couldn't quite keep the question out of my voice. "Something built like you. Something that stands guard, doesn't age, doesn't stop. Is that the actual shape of what happened to your people too? Not a slow decline. One seal. One thing nobody thought to watch closely enough, until it was already too late to matter?"

The One Who Stands considered this for longer than it had considered anything else so far.

"I do not yet know how much you are permitted to understand," it said at last, and there was something in the stillness of it that I would only fully recognize as grief much later, in hindsight, once I had the rest of the story to compare it to. "But I will say this much now, because you have earned an honest answer rather than a comforting one: yes. It is very much that shape. One thing, small enough that an entire civilization could reasonably believe it had already accounted for it. We did not account for it well enough. That is, in the end, the entire history I carry."

Nobody said anything for a long moment after that. I understood, standing there, that whatever I'd expected coming out here, whatever vindication I'd hoped four years of empty citations might eventually earn me, it had never once included a feeling like this: not triumph, not relief, but something heavier, older, the weight of being the first person handed the beginning of a story that was very clearly going to end somewhere none of us were ready for yet.
