Chapter 9 of 53

Davy (POV): I've spent most of my life being told what my own body was capable of by somebody else's instrument. This was the first time I actually believed the readout.

Sterling's people moved fast once he actually made the call. We walked out of that conference room with a whiteboard still half full of marker ink, and by the next morning there was already a simulator standing up for us at Canaveral, purpose-built, first thing, ahead of anything else in the whole program.

It looked, from the outside, like nothing in particular, a squat gray building with more cable running into it than any structure that size should reasonably need. Inside, it was something else entirely: a full physical rig, five stations arranged in a precise circle, each one wired into a biofeedback system sensitive enough to read a person's own heart rate and breath rhythm and feed it back to the other four in real time, so that all five of us could actually see, on our own private displays, exactly how far out of sync we still were with each other.

"The old method was a verbal count," the lead engineer explained, first day, walking us through the rig like a man showing off something he was genuinely proud of. "Count of three, everybody kicks. Worked fine at smaller scales. But a verbal count has variance built into it, human reaction time, however small, and at the masses you're about to start working with, small variance turns into real force landing in the wrong place at the wrong instant."

I thought about the point-load lesson. I didn't say anything.

"So instead of a count," she went on, "we're building you a physical trigger. Two stages. First one's small, barely there, you'll probably miss it the first few times: a tingle, right at the base of the skull, the instant you're actually strapped in and the rig goes live. That's not the trigger. That's just your own body's way of knowing the system's listening now instead of idle. Second stage is the real one. Something all five of you feel simultaneously, at the exact same instant, no verbal processing required. And underneath both of those, we're training your own physiology to actually match each other beforehand, heart rate, breath, so that by the time the actual trigger fires, you're not five separate people reacting to a signal. You're already close enough to one that reacting looks almost like a single decision."

It sounded, frankly, like something out of a training montage nobody would believe if you put it in a movie. It also worked, more or less immediately, in a way that unsettled me more than I expected it to.

The part that actually got under my skin came later, once the biofeedback training had mostly taken, and they added the boundary exercise.

"Move outside the defined zone once you've felt that first tingle, while the rig's actually live," the engineer said, gesturing at a marked boundary on the simulator floor, "and you'll feel the effect. It's not a leash, and I want to be honest with you about that up front: this isn't going to physically stop you if you actually decide to leave. It's fast, and it's real, but it's not strong, not compared to an actual choice. The second it actually reaches conscious thought, the moment you can put a name to what you're feeling, you've already got everything you need to walk straight through it. Most people don't even notice they overrode it, they just notice they kept walking. It's not a wall. It's barely a flinch. Important part: this only exists while you're actually keyed into an active sync operation. Power down, walk away, go live your life. It only wakes up when the job needs all five of you in the same place at the same instant, and it only exists to buy you that instant."

"That sounds," I said, before I'd fully thought it through, "like a gentler version of what I went through on Martha's Vineyard."

The room went quiet for a second. Millie's head turned toward me, sharp, and I realized what I'd just said out loud, the thing I usually didn't lead with, the old scar tissue from Conley's own governor implant, the one that had shrunk the warning phase down to nothing until the convulsions came with no notice at all, until my own jumping stopped being something I did and started being something done to me.

"It's not the same," I said quickly, mostly to Millie, because I could see her already bracing for the wrong kind of memory to come crashing back on me. "I know it's not the same. That was forced. This is..." I stopped, found the actual shape of what I meant. "This is trained. And protective. It's not somebody else's hand on the switch. It's mine, just built to catch me before I make a mistake I can't take back."

Millie didn't say anything. She just reached over and put her hand on my shoulder, brief, steady, the kind of touch that didn't need words attached to it.

We ran the exercise. The tingle came first, right where they'd said it would, faint enough that I didn't consciously feel it at all, just the small green light on the console confirming the rig had me a half second before I'd have sworn nothing had happened yet. Then I crossed the boundary line and felt the real thing arrive, quick, unmistakable, a pull with no ambiguity to it at all. I noticed it the instant it registered.

And kept walking. That was the whole trick of it, near as I could tell: the instant I could actually name what I was feeling, I'd already used the only leverage it had. It wasn't strength against strength. It was speed against speed, and conscious thought, once it actually showed up, was faster. I stopped a few steps later anyway, not because anything had made me, but because I'd proven what I'd come out here to prove and didn't see the point in belaboring it. I turned back into formation on my own two feet, still mine the whole way, scoped to exactly the window it was built for, gone the instant the rig powered back down, and this time it was protecting the exact people I'd have chosen to protect if anyone had asked me first.

I stood there afterward, back inside the boundary, breathing harder than the exercise should have earned.

"You okay?" Millie asked.

"Yeah." I meant it, mostly. "Yeah. I think I finally like the word reflexive again."

Six weeks, start to finish, before the rig called the five of us clean: matched physiology, trained trigger, boundary reflex proven out enough times that nobody in that building doubted it anymore. Six weeks felt like nothing, next to what it was actually buying us.
