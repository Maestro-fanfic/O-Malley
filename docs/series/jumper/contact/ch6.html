Chapter 6 of 53

Millie (POV): I'd rehearsed the walk in. I hadn't rehearsed the small talk first.

The doors opened on a conversation already in progress, President Bartlett and Leo McGarrity, a man I'd known personally since he'd sat in the chair Bartlett was sitting in now, mid-argument over something involving a Hill vote that sounded, from the three sentences I caught of it, exactly as tedious as every other Hill vote I'd ever half-listened to on the radio.

Bartlett looked up first. Whatever he saw in my face, briefcase in hand, unannounced, standing in his own doorway like I had every right to be there, made him stop mid-sentence without any visible effort at all.

"Shelve that, Leo," he said, not taking his eyes off me. "Bigger business just walked in."

He rose, unhurried, and crossed to meet me halfway across the room instead of making me walk the whole distance alone. "Can I get you something to drink? Coffee, water, something stronger if the hour calls for it?"

"Water's fine."

"Water it is." He gestured me toward the couch, taking the one across from it himself, Leo settling in beside him, eyes already flicking between us the way a man watches for cues a beat before his boss needs them. Bartlett told a short, easy story while somebody fetched the water, something about a garden mishap involving his own dog and a very expensive rosebush, and I found myself laughing despite everything sitting heavy in my chest, because the man had a genuine gift for making a room feel like it had all the time in the world right up until the exact moment it didn't.

Then he set his own cup down. Something changed in the room, quiet but unmistakable, a man deciding it was time.

"Let's get down to brass tacks," he said. "What can my administration do for you today?"

He caught himself almost immediately, and I watched him grin at his own accident, a man who'd just handed himself a tangent he couldn't resist chasing.

"You know," he said, turning slightly toward Leo, "nobody actually agrees where that phrase comes from. 'Brass tacks.' First recorded use, far as anyone's traced it, is an 1863 Texas newspaper, and even that's disputed as the true origin rather than just the earliest surviving citation. Three real theories, and not one of them's ever managed to fully bury the other two. Some say it's haberdashers, drapers measuring cloth against brass tacks set into a counter, fixed distances, no argument about the actual yardage once you got down to them. Others say upholsterers, stripping fabric away until you hit the actual tacks holding the frame together, the literal structural truth underneath all the decoration. And then there's the third one, less charming, Cockney rhyming slang, 'brass tacks' standing in for 'facts,' plain as that." He spread his hands slightly. "I've heard scholars argue all three with a straight face. I've never once heard anyone actually settle it."

Nobody interrupted him. I hadn't planned to, exactly, but I sat very still through the whole thing, some old instinct telling me this wasn't idle rambling so much as a man taking the room's full measure before he let anyone else speak in it, and that the correct response to that was patience, not politeness.

He finished, and let the silence after it sit for exactly as long as it needed to.

"So," he said. "Whichever version's true. What are yours?"

I set the briefcase down on the low table between us, unopened, because the numbers inside it weren't actually the thing I'd come to say first.

"We damn near killed ourselves," I said, "trying to do this on our own. Sterling decided that wasn't our call to make. That's why I'm here."

The room went very quiet.

Bartlett (POV): I'd expected numbers. I'd expected some version of a pitch, a request, a problem laid out in the careful order people usually lay problems out in when they want something from this office. I had not expected a sentence that arrived already carrying its own conclusion, delivered flat and plain by a woman who looked like she'd rehearsed everything except how much it would actually cost her to say it out loud.

Leo leaned forward beside me, some instinct of his own already engaged. "Go on."

She did. Twenty minutes, maybe less, though it felt considerably longer sitting through it: the push frames, built originally because Truman had argued orbital remediation wasn't a job for one person alone; the mass numbers climbing faster than anyone had planned for; the point-load failure, no injuries, a lesson learned the hard way about what happens when five kicks land eleven milliseconds out of true; and then the second test, the one that hadn't gone cleanly, the one where she herself, sent spinning loose off the hull with no anchor point in sight, had come within a genuinely uncertain margin of not making it home.

Sterling's name came up more than once, always with real respect, a man who'd shouldered more than his own share of the risk for longer than he should have. And beneath all of it, unspoken but entirely legible, the plain fact that this family had been quietly redefining what was physically possible for going on two years now, almost entirely without anyone in my own government fully understanding the scale of it.

I let her finish completely before I let myself react at all. That much discipline, at least, hadn't abandoned me.

"You are able to do what, again?"

It came out slower than I meant it to, each word landing with its own separate weight, because some part of my mind had understood the sentence the instant she'd said it and some other, larger part was still catching up to what that sentence actually meant, turning it over, failing to find the edges of it.

*

Leo (POV): I'd taken a sip of coffee at exactly the wrong moment, which in hindsight felt less like bad luck and more like the universe's own sense of comic timing, because there was no version of the sentence Millie Rice had just finished saying that anyone in this room was going to hear calmly, coffee or no coffee.

It came back up fast, an undignified spray I'd deny with a straight face for the rest of my natural life, and I was still coughing, eyes watering, when I caught Bartlett's own reaction out of the corner of my vision, that same stillness he got right before his mind went somewhere I couldn't follow yet.

"Sir," I managed, once my throat had mostly cleared, "I think we're going to need a bigger room."
