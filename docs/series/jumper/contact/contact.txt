Contact

Book Three of the Jumper Universe



Contact



Chapter 1: Millie (POV): Curiosity is not a plan. It just feels like one, right up until the moment it works.



The object was called 2071-KL on every manifest that mattered, and "the junker" by everyone actually out here doing something about it, and it had been drifting through a decaying orbit for the better part of three years by the time it finally reached the top of somebody's priority list. Nine hundred tons of dead upper stage, tumbling slow, no propellant left to move itself and no plan on record for anyone else to move it either, until eventually the plan became us.

Nobody had asked us to burn it up. That would've been the tidy answer, drop it into a reentry corridor and let the atmosphere finish the job, except nine hundred tons of dense old hardware doesn't reliably finish burning before it hits something, and a job that size needs a targeting precision none of us could honestly promise yet. So we aimed higher instead of lower: a push onto a trans-lunar coast, five days out, that would let the Moon have it instead. Crude, compared to a real deorbit burn. But it was the one option that couldn't miss, and inside a week it wouldn't be anybody's problem but a crater nobody would ever go looking for.

"Talk to me," Truman said, from his own spot on the hull, sixty meters around the curve from mine, close enough now that we ran these jobs together instead of him calling them in from an office in Colorado Springs the way he used to.

Davy's voice came first. Then Joe's. Then Cent's. Then Truman's own, checking in on himself same as the rest of us. Four confirms. My own voice would make five, and I gave it once everyone else had. "In position."

We'd done bigger jobs than this. We'd done harder ones. But we'd never done one exactly this shape before, five kicks aimed at the same dead mass from five different points around its hull, timed to the same spoken count we'd drilled a hundred times in the simulator at Canaveral and maybe a dozen times for real, always on smaller objects, always with somebody's hand hovering near an abort switch that had never once needed pressing.

"Trigger in three," Truman said. "Two. One."

There wasn't a sound. There never was. Just the nothing under my boots that still, after all these years, felt less like a physical sensation and more like the universe briefly forgetting to ask where I actually belonged, and then I was somewhere else on the hull, palm flat against cold metal, kicking with everything I had at the exact instant four other people I loved were doing the same thing from four other points I couldn't see.

The junker moved.

It didn't groan. It didn't shudder or protest or do any of the things nine hundred tons of dead stage should have done getting shoved into a new orbit by five sets of very determined hands. It just moved, smooth, clean, already settling onto the corrected track before I'd even finished the kick that was supposed to be the hard part.

"Confirmed," Truman said, and his voice hadn't caught up yet to whatever his eyes had just seen. "Track's clean, coasting for the Moon right on schedule. It's not our problem anymore. Good work, all of you."

Nobody said anything for a second. Davy's voice, when it came, sounded like a man checking his own math out loud because he didn't quite trust it yet.

"That was way easier than it should have been."

"We've done bigger," Cent said, though I could hear the same uncertainty underneath her own voice too.

"Not the mass," Davy said. "The mass tracks fine, we've done the mass before. I mean the whole thing. I barely felt it push back. Nine hundred tons and it felt like shoving open a door."

I ran it back in my own head, still hanging there in the empty stretch where the junker used to be, nothing under me at all now, just the actual physical memory of the kick, the resistance that should have been there and mostly hadn't been. Davy wasn't wrong. I'd felt it too, and hadn't let myself say so yet, the way you don't say a thing out loud until somebody else says it first and gives you permission to have noticed it.

"Ok, let's go home for debrief," Truman said, and there was something careful in how he said it, like a man deciding not to think too hard about something until he had four other people in a room with him to think about it too.

Nobody argued.

We didn't talk much once we were down, out of suits. That wasn't unusual, exactly, five people who'd worked together long enough that silence didn't need filling. But this silence had a different weight to it, the kind that comes right before somebody says the thing everyone's already thinking.

Truman was the one who said it, sitting around the kitchen table that had somehow ended up as this family's actual mission control more often than any real one ever had.

"We've been building toward bigger and bigger masses for two years," he said, turning his coffee mug in slow half circles without drinking from it. "Assuming every step up would cost us more. That's how it's worked every single time. More mass, more effort, more strain, right up to the edge of what five people can actually do together." He stopped turning the mug. "Today didn't feel like the edge of anything."

"Maybe we're just better at it now," Joe offered, not sounding like he believed it either.

"Maybe." Truman didn't sound like he believed it. "Or maybe the math we built two years ago was already too conservative the day we wrote it down, and we've been quietly outgrowing our own numbers every single test since, and today's just the first time the gap got big enough that all five of us actually noticed it at the same time."

Nobody had an answer for that. Davy's face went still, the way it always did right before he said something that mattered.

"So what do we do," he said. Not really a question.

Truman was quiet for a while. Long enough that I started to think he wasn't going to answer at all, that maybe the question was going to just sit there on the table with the coffee mug until somebody found a smaller one to ask instead.

"We're gonna need help figuring out how far this *reaches*," he said finally, and something about the way he leaned on that last word made it feel like it was doing more work than a single word usually does, some private echo only he was fully hearing yet.

He let it sit there a moment, the way he always let the true things sit before he moved past them.

Then, dry, the corner of his mouth finally moving: "Just so happens I know a guy who knows a guy."



Chapter 2: Truman (POV): Some conversations only happen twice in a career. I was about to have my third.



Sterling's house hadn't changed much since the last time I'd stood on this particular doorstep, which was itself long enough ago that I'd had to think for a second about which turn actually got me there. Same porch light, same slightly crooked mailbox nobody had ever bothered straightening, same general sense of a man who spent so much of his working life inside mission control that he'd deliberately built a home that looked like nothing could possibly go wrong in it.

I rang the bell with my free hand. The other one had the briefcase in it, and I was aware, standing there, of exactly how much weight it was about to carry through that door.

Sterling opened the door already smiling, the way he always did, like every visitor was good news until proven otherwise. "Hey Truman! Long time, no see!"

"Hey yourself." I took the offered hand, then the hug that came after it, because Sterling had never been a man who could be talked out of a hug once he'd decided one was owed.

Then his eyes dropped to the briefcase, and his shoulders came up half an inch, the same brace I'd seen on him before, the one he'd learned the hard way, from what it meant every time somebody from this family showed up at his actual front door instead of calling.

"Ok," he said slowly, stepping back to let me in, "what have you maniacs brought to blow my mind this time?"

"You won't believe me."

"You'd better come inside," he said, glancing past me at the quiet street like he half expected a curtain to twitch somewhere. "Don't want the neighbors getting curious."

I didn't answer right away. Waited until we were both sitting in his living room, briefcase on the coffee table between us, unopened, because there was a rhythm to this conversation and I'd learned a long time ago not to rush past it.

Sterling looked at the case. Looked at me. And whatever easy humor had been in his voice at the door was gone by the time he spoke again.

"First it was 'no, you don't know what I'm doing.'" He said it slow, like he was reading it off a list he'd kept somewhere for exactly this occasion. "Then it was 'what do you mean you can push a satellite.'" A pause, longer this time. "Now what?"

I'd known this question was coming since the porch. I still felt the actual weight of it land, three separate times now across more years than either of us probably wanted to count, the same shape of conversation every time, and every time it had ended somewhere neither of us expected to be standing at the start of it.

I opened the case.

"We moved nine hundred tons yesterday," I said, sliding the summary sheet across so he could see the numbers for himself instead of just hearing them from me. "Five kicks, synchronized, single count. Didn't even feel like the hard part of the job anymore."

Sterling picked up the sheet. Read it once. Read it again, slower, the way a man reads something when the first pass hasn't actually finished landing yet.

"Nine hundred tons."

"Nine hundred tons, and it felt, in Davy's own words, like shoving open a door." I let that sit a second before I said the rest of it. "We've already reached the point where we know nothing in orbit is big enough to test."

Sterling set the sheet down very carefully, like it might do something unexpected if he moved too fast.

"You're telling me," he said, "that a mass-scaling problem I already didn't fully believe the first time you brought it to me has gotten so far past that belief that you've run out of things in Earth orbit worth pushing."

"That's exactly what I'm telling you."

He was quiet for a moment, weighing something, the same look I'd seen on him a hundred times across three administrations now, a man deciding how much of what he already knew he was actually allowed to say out loud yet.

"I'm going to tell you something," he said finally, "and I want you to understand I'm telling you because I trust you, not because I have any idea what it means yet." He rubbed a hand across his face. "NASA's own tracking network has an open anomaly file. Two of them, actually, and until about ten minutes ago I'd have told you they were unrelated." He held up one finger. "The newer one, running months now, not years: objects breaking apart, no debris signature on record, no engine burn, nothing that fits any catalogued failure mode. Nobody's been able to explain it." A second finger. "The older one runs almost two years back. Tracked debris just... leaving. No burn plume, ever, on any of them. No gradual track change a model could have predicted, no reentry anybody logged. One orbit on Monday, coasting out toward lunar distance by Tuesday, on a trajectory nothing in that orbit should have been able to get onto on its own. We never see them again. We assume the Moon's been quietly picking up our trash for two years and just never told anybody. We chalked most of it up to tracking error for the first year. We stopped being able to chalk it up to tracking error somewhere around the fortieth case." He looked back at me, and something behind his eyes had gone very focused. "You gave me the numbers, Truman. I read them, same as I always do. But I didn't understand what I was actually reading until I saw them sitting next to two mysteries I've had open on my desk for two years with absolutely no explanation attached to either one."

Then he stopped, mid-motion, hand still half-raised from the second finger, and I watched him actually get there in real time.

"Wait." Quieter now. "There's a third thing. Different program entirely, different building, I only know about it because I sit on a review board that occasionally crosses paths with theirs. Lunar impact monitoring, people who watch the dark limb of the Moon for meteoroid flashes, been running that program since the mid-2000s. They've had an open question of their own the last couple years, flashes bigger and more frequent than any known meteoroid population should produce, nothing that fits a shower, nothing that fits background rate. Two completely different teams, two completely different anomaly files, and as far as I know not one single person in either building has ever put them on the same desk." He looked at me like the floor had just moved under him. "You're telling me you've been pushing large masses out of Earth orbit for two years. I'm telling you objects have been vanishing off our tracking for two years. And somebody else, three buildings over, has been watching things hit the Moon for two years, and none of us ever talked to each other."

Neither of us said anything for a while after that.

"I think we found your explanation," I said eventually. "All three of them."

"I think you might have." Sterling picked the summary sheet back up, and this time when he looked at it, I could see him already working, already three steps into whatever came next even though neither of us had said what that was yet. "God help us both, I think you might have."



Chapter 3: Cent (POV): Force doesn't care how much of it you've got. It only cares where you put it.



Sterling got us a target inside four days, which for a man operating through channels that normally moved at the speed of committee meetings, was its own kind of small miracle. A decommissioned upper stage, twice the mass of the junker, cleared for disposal anyway, sitting in an orbit nobody would miss if something went sideways. His exact words, delivered dry, the tone of a man who already suspected something might.

"Treat it like a real op," he'd told us, over a line that was technically secure and definitely being listened to by at least two people who weren't him. "Full telemetry, full logging. But it's still just the five of you making the call in real time out there. I haven't got the paperwork for anything more official than that yet."

We didn't need more official than that. We'd been running our own calls for two years.

The kick sequence was the same one we'd used on the junker, the same trigger, the same spoken count drilled into muscle memory until none of us had to think about it anymore. Five points around the hull, five kicks, one shared instant. Simple, by now. Or it should have been.

I felt mine land clean. Felt the mass answer the way mass was supposed to answer, that particular give that meant the push had actually taken.

Then I felt the hull come apart underneath my palm.

Not explosively. Not the way a movie would have shown it. Just a low, structural groan, metal complaining somewhere it had never been built to complain, and then a section of the old stage's own aft skirt simply peeled away from the rest of it, tumbling off on its own vector while the main body lurched sideways instead of forward, sluggish and wrong, like something that had been shoved instead of moved.

"Report," Truman's voice, tight, already halfway to alarmed. "Everybody report."

"I'm clear," I said, and heard the others say the same in overlapping bursts, Davy, Joe, Millie, everyone accounted for, nobody hurt, which was the only piece of that first thirty seconds that actually mattered.

But the object itself was a mess. Two separate pieces now instead of one, tumbling on two different tracks, neither of them anywhere close to the orbit we'd meant to put it in.

Back on the ground, in the debrief, Sterling pulled the telemetry up on a screen big enough that all five of us could stare at it together, and none of us said anything for a long moment.

"Here," he said finally, tapping a spot on the timing trace. "Millie's kick landed eleven milliseconds ahead of the other four. Not much. Not enough that any of you would've felt the difference in the moment."

"Eleven milliseconds," Millie repeated, and I could hear her doing the same math I was, turning that tiny number over, trying to make it mean something big enough to explain what had actually happened.

"Eleven milliseconds is nothing to the human nervous system." Sterling didn't sound like he was blaming her. He sounded like a man laying out a fact that needed laying out plainly, no softer than it actually was. "It's not nothing to human anatomy. And it's not nothing to a structural load. For eleven milliseconds, every bit of force from that kick was concentrated at one single point on that hull instead of distributed across the whole surface the way it would've been if all five kicks had landed together. You didn't push the object. For eleven milliseconds, you punched it."

"That's not fair," Davy started. "We drilled the exact same count..."

"I'm not saying it's your fault." Sterling's voice stayed level. "I'm saying force doesn't care how good your intentions are. It doesn't care how much total mass five people can move between them if the timing's off by even a fraction most humans can't consciously perceive. Point load is point load. Concentrate enough force on one small enough spot and something's going to give, and it's not going to give gently." He looked at each of us in turn before he said the rest of it. "Today the hull was the thing that gave. Cent's told me more than once there's two ceilings on a job like this, not one: what the payload survives, and what the jumper survives delivering it. Eleven milliseconds run the other direction, and it doesn't have to be the hull."

I looked at the two tumbling pieces on the screen, still slowly separating from each other in real time, and went cold in a way that had nothing to do with the actual temperature of the room.

"We got lucky," I said. "That thing broke apart clean. It didn't shed debris into anything populated. Nobody was standing where the pieces went."

"You got lucky," Sterling agreed. "This time."

Nobody argued with that.

"So how do we fix it," Millie said, and I could hear her already moving past the fear of it, into the part of her that always wanted the next actionable thing rather than time to sit with what had just almost happened.

Sterling didn't answer right away. When he did, it wasn't really an answer, more an admission.

"I don't know yet. That's the honest truth of it. You five are the only people alive who can even attempt what you just attempted, which means there's no existing engineering discipline built around solving this specific problem. We're going to have to build one." He looked at each of us in turn, and for the first time since he'd walked back into this family's life, his face looked less like an old friend helping out and more like a man who'd just quietly picked up more responsibility than he'd bargained for. "But we're going to build it right, with real margins and real oversight, not five people making the call by themselves in real time anymore. What happened today is exactly why."

Nobody argued with that either.

Later, once the others had gone, I stayed a while longer, watching the two pieces of dead stage keep drifting further apart from each other on the tracking display, slower now, the violence of the moment already fading into something almost peaceful to look at from far enough away.

Eleven milliseconds, I thought. That was all it had taken. Eleven milliseconds to turn a controlled push into something that could have gone very differently, if the wrong piece had gone the wrong direction, if anyone had been standing anywhere they shouldn't have been. Or the other direction entirely, if the gap had run wide enough that one of us ended up carrying more of that mass alone than a single body could survive delivering it. Cent had found that wall years ago, long before any of the rest of us were doing this, testing a dead weather satellite bus north of a metric ton with nobody's permission but her own. *Let's just find the wall,* she'd told the room. *Full effort. Let's see what full effort actually does.* She found it. Two ceilings in the same afternoon, she told us once, years later, half laughing about it the way you only laugh about the ones that didn't kill you: what a payload survives, and what you survive delivering it. A dislocated shoulder and a satellite bus pulling a G-load that would've killed a passenger outright were the actual receipts. Discrete increments were the fix, small pushes instead of one big one, and five of us kicking together instead of one of us kicking alone was just that same lesson, scaled up and handed down. Eleven milliseconds hadn't been enough to run any of us into that wall today. It hadn't needed to miss by much.

We'd learned the lesson the easy way, this time. I didn't think we'd get to count on that twice.



Chapter 4: Davy (POV): I've been scared for people I love before. I hadn't ever watched it happen this slowly.



Sterling kept covering for us longer than I think either he or we ever actually said out loud. No formal program, no line item, no paper trail that would have made this family's own backyard experiments look like anything more than what they officially weren't. Just Sterling, quietly redirecting decommissioned mass our way, quietly keeping the reports vague enough that nobody above him started asking the questions that would have shut the whole thing down.

I understood why, even then. He believed in what we were doing. He'd believed in it since Cent first pushed a satellite with her bare hands, and belief like that doesn't just evaporate because the paperwork's inconvenient.

But belief wasn't going to be enough, and I think some part of him already knew that the day he cleared us for the bigger test.

Twice the mass of the one that broke apart on us. A real target this time, purpose-selected instead of just convenient, with enough structural integrity that Sterling's engineers had actually run the numbers on it beforehand, real margins built in, real safety review, as real as anything could be while still technically living under Sterling's own personal cover instead of an actual program.

We ran the count clean. Five kicks, same trigger, same spoken rhythm we'd drilled a hundred times since the last lesson taught us exactly how badly that rhythm could go wrong.

For about four seconds, it worked.

Then Millie's voice came through the comm, and it had a shape to it I'd never heard from her before, flat and controlled, the way people go flat right before the fear underneath actually breaks through.

"I've lost my anchor point."

"Say again," Truman, already sharp.

"Kick landed. I didn't." A pause that lasted about half a second and felt like it lasted an hour. "I'm off the hull. Tumbling. I don't have a jump home from a spin like this, not without a fixed point to aim at, and I can't get my bearings fast enough to find one."

I was already moving before I'd consciously decided to, kicking off my own position, eyes scanning for her against the black, and for three or four of the longest seconds of my entire life I couldn't find her at all, just empty space where a person should have been.

Then I saw her. Tumbling, but not fighting it, arms already spreading wide the way Cent had drilled into every one of us years back: don't fight a spin, control it. Cent had learned that trick on her very first solo deployment, arms out to slow the tumble, arms pulled in tight to speed it back up when she wanted it, the same conservation of momentum that spins a figure skater faster the moment their arms come home to their chest, and she'd made every one of us practice both directions until neither one could panic us anymore. Millie wasn't panicking now. She was reading her own rotation, patient, arms out, buying herself the time to time a jump to somewhere stable instead of making it worse by rushing.

"I see her," I said. "Millie, I've got a fix on you. Don't jump yet. Let me get lined up."

"Copy." Her voice was still flat. Still controlled. I could hear underneath it exactly how much that control was costing her.

Getting lined up took eleven seconds. I counted every one of them, out loud in my own head, because counting was the only thing keeping my own hands from shaking badly enough to miss the jump myself.

"Now," I said. "Anchor's me. Jump to me."

She did. Landed hard, both of us tumbling together for a second before our combined mass found something like stability, my arms locked around her, her breath coming fast and uneven against my shoulder in a way I knew she'd never admit to later.

"I'm okay," she said, before I'd even asked. "I'm okay. I'm okay."

She said it three times. I don't think either of us believed it until the fourth or fifth time either of us actually got to say it out loud, hours later, safely back on the ground, still not quite steady.

Sterling was waiting for us when we arrived. Didn't say anything at first, just looked at each of us in turn, doing a headcount he already knew the answer to, needing to see it with his own eyes anyway.

"Nobody's hurt," he said finally, like he was confirming it for himself as much as for us.

"Nobody's hurt," Truman agreed.

Sterling nodded slowly. Then he sat down, heavy, in the nearest chair, and put his head in his hands for a long moment before he looked back up.

"This is on me," he said. "All of it. I've been covering this program with nothing behind it but my own judgment and my own signature, and today my own judgment nearly got somebody killed doing something I authorized without the actual authority to authorize it." He looked at Truman specifically, and something passed between the two of them that I didn't fully understand until years later, something that went back further than any of us currently in the room. "Not on our dime. That was always your line, Truman. Yours, and everyone who ever stood at a console during a mission that could've gone wrong and made sure it didn't cost anybody anything they didn't already know they were risking. I've spent this whole program borrowing that line without earning the right to it yet."

"Sterling..."

"I'm not finished." He stood up again, and when he spoke next, his voice had steadied, a decision already made rather than one still being weighed. "I'm done covering this personally. Not because I don't believe in it. Because believing in it isn't enough anymore, and today proved that in a way I'm not willing to risk proving again. This needs to be a real program, with real oversight, answering to people whose actual job it is to make sure nobody almost dies doing this again. And if that means going over my own head to make it happen, then that's exactly what I'm going to do." A pause, something loosening in his shoulders, a weight he'd carried alone for too long finally set down. "Not on our dime. Starting today, I mean to actually earn saying it."

Nobody argued with him. I don't think any of us wanted to.

Millie was still holding my arm, tighter than she probably realized, and I let her, because some days the only thing you can actually offer somebody is exactly that: a hand to hold onto until the shaking stops.



Chapter 5: Millie (POV): Sterling was working the front door. I decided somebody needed to try the side one.



Sterling spent three weeks doing exactly what he'd promised he would, going over his own head the proper way, memos and briefings and quiet conversations with people whose job titles I couldn't have recited if my life depended on it. I watched him do it with real admiration, and real, growing impatience, because three weeks is a long time to sit on a problem that had already nearly cost me my life once and could cost somebody else theirs while a dozen people in a dozen offices took their time deciding whose desk it actually belonged on.

I didn't tell Sterling what I was about to do. I didn't tell Truman either, not directly, though I suspect he knew something was coming the morning I put on the one blazer I still owned from before Cent's own accident changed the entire shape of my life, and didn't say where I was headed.

I brought the briefcase. Same model as Truman's own, more or less, because if there was one thing I'd learned watching this family operate for the last several years, it was that a briefcase in the right hands said everything a person needed said before they'd opened their mouth once.

Truman saw it on my way out. I watched the recognition land on his face, watched his mouth open around the start of something, my name, maybe, or a question he already half suspected the answer to. He never got the chance to finish it. I was already gone.

I landed just outside the White House's own holding area, unannounced, the way Truman had landed on Sterling's own porch unannounced, because some conversations only earn the weight they need if you show up for them in person instead of asking politely through channels that could say no without ever having to look you in the eye.

Security didn't blink. I'd half expected a confrontation, badges and raised voices and at least one very stern conversation about restricted airspace, and instead got a young agent glancing at a tablet, glancing at my face, glancing back at the tablet, and simply waving me through like I'd RSVP'd weeks ago.

"You're on the list, ma'am."

"I'm on the list."

"Unrestricted access. Photo's a few years old, but it's clearly you."

It took me an actual moment to place what he meant. Then I remembered, the old photo from my counseling practice, the one I'd forgotten I'd ever had to submit for some long-ago White House family day nobody had bothered removing me from the rolls for since.

*Man, I've gotten oooold,* I thought, and let myself have exactly one beat of quiet horror about it before I kept walking.

The corridor toward the Oval was busier than I expected, full of people moving fast, the kind of building that never actually stopped working, and I caught fragments of half a dozen conversations that weren't mine to be part of. Two staffers, a man and a woman, mid-argument about something that sounded important to both of them and completely opaque to me. A third, distracted, phone against his ear, saying something about press language that made him wince even as he said it. A woman with an armload of folders doing a full double take at me as I passed, like she was trying to place my face and failing, then giving up and hurrying on to wherever she was actually needed.

None of them stopped me. None of them needed to know who I was. I wasn't here for them.

Mrs. Landingham's desk sat just outside the Oval itself, and the woman behind it looked up at me with the same composed, unhurried attention she seemed to give everyone, not a flicker of surprise anywhere in it, not even for an unannounced walk-in.

"Mrs. Rice! How good to see you!" Genuine warmth in it, nothing performed about it at all. Then, barely a breath later, without waiting to be asked: "You'll want the President. It's urgent, and it can't wait for a scheduled appointment." Something that might, on anyone else's face, have been the start of a smile. "I know. I always know."

She picked up the phone before I'd said a single word.



Chapter 6: Millie (POV): I'd rehearsed the walk in. I hadn't rehearsed the small talk first.



The doors opened on a conversation already in progress, President Bartlett and Leo McGarrity, a man I'd known personally since he'd sat in the chair Bartlett was sitting in now, mid-argument over something involving a Hill vote that sounded, from the three sentences I caught of it, exactly as tedious as every other Hill vote I'd ever half-listened to on the radio.

Bartlett looked up first. Whatever he saw in my face, briefcase in hand, unannounced, standing in his own doorway like I had every right to be there, made him stop mid-sentence without any visible effort at all.

"Shelve that, Leo," he said, not taking his eyes off me. "Bigger business just walked in."

He rose, unhurried, and crossed to meet me halfway across the room instead of making me walk the whole distance alone. "Can I get you something to drink? Coffee, water, something stronger if the hour calls for it?"

"Water's fine."

"Water it is." He gestured me toward the couch, taking the one across from it himself, Leo settling in beside him, eyes already flicking between us the way a man watches for cues a beat before his boss needs them. Bartlett told a short, easy story while somebody fetched the water, something about a garden mishap involving his own dog and a very expensive rosebush, and I found myself laughing despite everything sitting heavy in my chest, because the man had a genuine gift for making a room feel like it had all the time in the world right up until the exact moment it didn't.

Then he set his own cup down. Something changed in the room, quiet but unmistakable, a man deciding it was time.

"Let's get down to brass tacks," he said. "What can my administration do for you today?"

He caught himself almost immediately, and I watched him grin at his own accident, a man who'd just handed himself a tangent he couldn't resist chasing.

"You know," he said, turning slightly toward Leo, "nobody actually agrees where that phrase comes from. 'Brass tacks.' First recorded use, far as anyone's traced it, is an 1863 Texas newspaper, and even that's disputed as the true origin rather than just the earliest surviving citation. Three real theories, and not one of them's ever managed to fully bury the other two. Some say it's haberdashers, drapers measuring cloth against brass tacks set into a counter, fixed distances, no argument about the actual yardage once you got down to them. Others say upholsterers, stripping fabric away until you hit the actual tacks holding the frame together, the literal structural truth underneath all the decoration. And then there's the third one, less charming, Cockney rhyming slang, 'brass tacks' standing in for 'facts,' plain as that." He spread his hands slightly. "I've heard scholars argue all three with a straight face. I've never once heard anyone actually settle it."

Nobody interrupted him. I hadn't planned to, exactly, but I sat very still through the whole thing, some old instinct telling me this wasn't idle rambling so much as a man taking the room's full measure before he let anyone else speak in it, and that the correct response to that was patience, not politeness.
