Iverson (POV): I had not, in my entire career, lost my composure. Not during the Endeavour's own hard landing in the L.A. River, not during anything before or since. I assumed, walking into the spin-up assignment, that this would be no different.

I was wrong within about four days.

The assignment itself was simple enough on paper: bring the station up to centrifugal-gravity rotation for the long coast phase ahead, the same spin-gravity solution Kristen Station had proven out years earlier, nothing that needed inventing fresh, just careful, patient execution.

"Why do I suddenly feel like a hamster?" Joe's voice came through the comm partway through the first spin-up test, the real, disorienting sensation of standing inside a slowly turning drum settling into everyone's inner ear at once.

"You know what they say about hamsters, don't you?" Millie's reply, deliberately loaded, fully aware Cent was on the same comm line.

"Mom!" Immediate. Strangled. "There are people on this channel!"

"There are people on every channel, sweetheart. That's rather the point of a channel."

"I hate this family so much right now."

"I still just want to know if there's a wheel," Joe added, delighted, not helping at all.

It was, I reflected, a fairly ordinary start to what turned out to be a considerably less ordinary week.

The Eridian assigned to work spin-up alongside me didn't walk so much as arrive in three places at once, maintaining a running commentary that never stopped for breath. Where Rocky's own energy read as delighted, hers read as combustion, barely contained, and I found myself, by day three, timing my own coffee breaks around avoiding her.

It wasn't just the talking. That, I could have survived. It was that her hands and her feet apparently answered to a different, faster clock than the rest of the ship did, so that by the time I'd finished reading one gauge, she'd already crossed the bay, rerouted a conduit, caught a dropped tool out of the air without appearing to look at it, and arrived back at my shoulder mid-sentence about something entirely unrelated to any of the three things she'd just done. I once watched her tighten a bolt, translate a joke for Joe, and reroute a coolant line in what I clocked, afterward, out of pure self-defense, at just under nine seconds.

"You ever finish a sentence before starting the next task?" I asked, somewhere around day four, mostly out of genuine curiosity, and partly because I'd started to suspect I was losing actual cognitive ground just standing near her.

"Why would I do that. Sentence isn't the task. Sentence is just noise while hands do the real work. You people are so slow about everything. It's very restful, actually. For you." She was already three meters away by the end of that sentence, doing something to a bracket I hadn't authorized yet.

I did not consider that restful. I considered it, privately, the single most exhausting five days of my career, Endeavour included, and I had genuinely believed, walking into this assignment, that nothing was ever going to beat Endeavour.

"Could you," I tried, day five, hearing my own voice come out flatter than I meant it to, "pick one task. Just one. And finish it before starting the next."

"I finish all of them. Just not one at a time. One at a time is how you people lose time between tasks. I don't lose time. I don't understand losing time. Why would anyone want to lose time." She was gone again before I finished processing the question, already halfway up a ladder I hadn't seen her approach.

By day six, I tried a different question, mostly out of genuine, slightly desperate curiosity about whether the woman actually had an off switch. "Does anything ever slow you down?"

That was the first time she actually stopped, all the way, for maybe two full seconds.

"Once. Emergency, real one, someone hurt. Everything got very quiet in my head. Just her. Just what she needed." Already moving again. "Didn't like it. Too loud, being that quiet."

I didn't have anything ready for that. When I answered, it wasn't what I expected either.

"All right. What do I call you? Because whatever I've been calling you in my head isn't fit to say out loud."

That was the first time I'd seen her hold still enough for an expression to actually land. "Something good, Commander. I trust you."

"Static. You're Static."

"Static." She tried it, liked it, said it again just to hear it. "Yes. Good. Fits." Gone again before I could confirm it stuck.

By the end of the crossing, I still hadn't slowed her down once. I'd stopped trying.

By the end of that shift I'd started talking to myself in the corridor between checks, low and rapid, just to remind my own nervous system what a normal human pace actually felt like. It did not help. I caught myself, twice, finishing my own sentences faster than I meant to, some part of me apparently deciding that if I couldn't beat her I'd at least try to keep up.

Childs found me afterward, straightening my collar in a way that fixed nothing.

"Looks like the unflappable has flapped."

"You! Shut up, you!"

"Bob. That's not even a sentence."

"I'm aware." I gave up on the collar, hands dropping to my sides in what I recognized, with real embarrassment, as actual defeat. "You've been timing your coffee breaks around a woman who talks faster than she thinks and thinks faster than anyone else on this ship moves, and you did it in about four days. I get to have one bad sentence. I get to have several. I may in fact never form a complete sentence again."

Childs didn't argue that one.

Fraiser (POV): I trained as a trauma surgeon because I wanted to be the person a body could count on in the worst thirty seconds of its entire life. It took a whole different crossing to teach me what I actually am when nobody's having the worst thirty seconds of their life at all.

Trauma work rewires you in ways that don't undo themselves just because the work stops. You learn to read a room in the half-second it takes to walk into it, triage before you've even set your bag down, keep your own hands steady while everything else in the room is doing the opposite. I'd spent a career being the calm center of other people's catastrophes, and some deep, useful, exhausting part of my own nervous system had never fully learned how to stand down from that posture, not on Reach, not really, not once in years.

Routine physicals, partway through the crossing, the same battery of checks I'd run constantly during Reach's own years-long haul, and it was the numbers that got me before anything else did. Steady sleep cycles. Normal cortisol, across the whole crew. Nobody's weight trending the wrong direction. I kept waiting, chart after chart, for the thing a trauma surgeon's own instincts are built to catch, the small tell that says a body is quietly failing before it says so out loud, and it simply wasn't there. Nobody on this crossing needed the version of me trained to catch a person mid-collapse. That version of me sat in the exam room fully suited up for a fight that never happened, chart after chart, and I didn't fully know what to do with my own hands once I noticed it.

On Reach, I'd spent years chasing the slow-motion attrition of bodies asked to endure something nobody had ever tested a human against for that long. I'd learned to read exhaustion in handwriting, in the length of a pause before a simple answer, in how many times someone said I'm fine before I stopped believing the words meant anything at all. I'd been good at that job the way a trauma surgeon is good at a job: because the alternative was somebody I loved bleeding out on a table I couldn't reach in time.

Nobody was saying I'm fine on this crossing. Mostly, I realized, going through chart after chart, because nobody had needed to lie about it.

I sat with that longer than the numbers themselves strictly required. Not only relief, though there was plenty of that. Something closer to grief, too, for how hard the last one had actually been, looking back at it now from somewhere that didn't have to be that hard anymore. I'd carried every one of those old numbers personally, back then, because there hadn't been anyone else whose job it was to carry them for me, the same way I'd carried every patient who ever came through a trauma bay under my own two hands, whether they made it or they didn't.

This time, the numbers just looked like numbers. Good ones. A trauma surgeon with nothing to triage, for the first time in longer than I could actually remember.

I let myself have that. Closed the chart. Moved on to the next name on the list, and found, for once, that I didn't need to brace myself before opening it.

Grace (POV): I'd expected this to be the boring drill. I have never been so completely, magnificently wrong about anything in my entire life.

Eridians don't rattle. Don't panic. Hyper-focus through anything, which was exactly why I'd walked into their first solo training drill expecting something clinically smooth, six bodies moving like a single efficient machine.

The alarm went off, and every single Eridian in the bay committed, instantly and completely, to their own individually optimal solution to the drill. The problem, which became evident within about two seconds, was that none of the six solutions had anything to do with each other.

One sprinted for the emergency hatch. A second had already decided the vent was faster and was halfway into it backward. A third, hyper-focused past any peripheral awareness whatsoever, walked straight through the space the first two had just vacated and got clipped by both of them at once, spinning him a full quarter turn before he even registered he'd been hit. A fourth, trying to help, ripped an equipment locker door clean off its hinges in one motion, overcorrected on the follow-through, and took out a fire extinguisher mount with the same swing. The extinguisher itself came loose, rolled the length of the bay under its own momentum, and clipped a fifth Eridian directly behind both knees, which startled her badly enough that she shoved the sixth directly into the tangle already forming at the hatch, arms windmilling the entire way down.

Within eleven seconds, four of them were physically stuck together in a configuration nobody could possibly have planned for, one arm trapped at an angle that looked, from where I stood, anatomically uncharitable. A fifth was wearing the ripped-off locker door like a shield, holding it up defensively against absolutely nothing, for reasons I never got a satisfying answer to. The loose fire extinguisher, still rolling, completed one final lazy circuit of the bay and came to rest directly against the sixth Eridian's foot, who had, against all odds, completed the drill objective perfectly and entirely alone, and looked down at it with the mild, faintly offended confusion of a woman discovering a stray cat had wandered into her kitchen.

The alarm kept going. Somewhere in the tangle, someone's boot came fully off and sailed a clean six feet before landing, with a soft, almost polite thud, directly on top of the locker door shield. I just stared.

"Is this... part of it?"

Sterling, beside me, didn't look up from his clipboard. "No."

"I thought they didn't need the practice. Hyper-focus. 'Just get things done.'"

"They don't need practice at the individual task." A small note added itself to whatever he was writing, entirely unbothered by the boot. "Nobody's ever taught them to foul up as a team before. Turns out that doesn't need talent."

The four stuck together finally worked themselves loose, one at a time, in a sequence that briefly re-tangled twice before actually resolving, the anatomically uncharitable arm coming free last with a sound none of us examined too closely. The woman wearing the locker door made no move to take it off even once the emergency had clearly, thoroughly ended, apparently having decided somewhere in the last minute that this was simply what she wore now.

By the time the tangle finally sorted itself out, one Eridian laughing too hard to currently stand upright, the drill's official time had been blown by a factor of six, the missing boot still sat on the shield exactly where it had landed, and the woman in the locker door still hadn't taken it off.

"I have never," I said, with real wonder, "seen anything so completely without dignity in my entire life."

"Wait till you see them try the docking-collar drill," Sterling said, still not looking up. "That one's worse. Somebody loses a shoe on that one too. Different shoe, usually. We've stopped trying to track a pattern."
