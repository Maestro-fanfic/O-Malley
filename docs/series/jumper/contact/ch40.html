Bartlett (POV): Thirty years in and out of rooms with armed guards standing in them had taught me to trust a certain kind of silence on the other end of a phone line more than I trusted most speeches.

Leo took the call at his own desk, and I watched whatever the guard on the other end was saying drain something out of his face in real time.

"Sir." He'd already covered the receiver with one hand. "It's Millie. She's at the gate. Whitfield says she said good morning to him twice, word for word, both times like she'd never said it the first time. He says her eyes aren't tracking right. He didn't want to just wave her through."

"Good man." I was already standing. "Don't send anyone to bring her in. I'll go myself."

"Sir, if something's actually wrong with her..."

"Then the last thing she needs is to be walked through a building by a stranger with a clipboard." I was already moving, and Leo fell in beside me without being asked to, the way he always did once he'd registered I'd already made up my mind. "Have Charlie redirect us to the Residence. Not the Oval."

"That's not the usual..."

"I'm aware it's not the usual." I said it without breaking stride. "The usual is for business. This isn't business yet. I don't know what this is yet, and until I do, I'd rather meet her somewhere with a couch instead of a desk between us."

She was exactly the shape Whitfield had described, standing very still just past the gate, a case held in both hands like it had become, at some point on the walk over, the only thing left in the world she trusted herself to hold onto. Her eyes found mine, and something in them focused, slow, the effort of a person surfacing from somewhere very far down.

"Mr. President." Her voice came out steady enough. Everything underneath it didn't.

"Millie." I didn't ask her a single question standing at that gate. Whatever this was, it didn't belong out here, in the open, in front of a guard who'd already done exactly the right thing by not waving her through. "Walk with me."

She didn't argue. That, more than anything Whitfield had said on the phone, told me how much of her was still somewhere else.

I made the tea myself, once we'd gotten her settled into the lounge, ignoring the look Leo gave me when I waved off the staff who'd normally have handled it. Some things a president does himself, not because he's better at them, but because the doing of it says something a spoken sentence can't quite manage on its own. I set the cup down in front of her, sat across from her, and let the silence sit there as long as it needed to, Leo standing quietly by the window, patient in the particular way he'd gotten very good at over thirty years of watching me handle things this way.

She wrapped both hands around the cup without drinking from it, and by the time her shoulders finally came down half an inch, I judged it was time.

"Talk to me, Millie."

"It's bigger than us." The words came out uneven, a halting run at something too large for the sentence carrying it. "Not just Earth. Not just Erid. I thought I was bringing you a warning about a crisis that already happened to somebody else, a long time ago, somewhere far away. That's not what this is." She stopped, started again, feeling her way forward instead of reciting anything rehearsed. "Everything. Every species phage's own root has ever touched. We're not standing next to a tragedy that happened somewhere off to the side of us. We're standing inside the same root system as everyone else who ever grew out of it."

Leo's own face had gone carefully neutral the way it always went when he genuinely had no frame yet for what he was hearing.

"I don't have the language for this properly," Millie said, and I watched something old and practiced flicker behind her own eyes, some private instruction arriving from somewhere she'd built a long time ago for exactly moments like this one. Her hands finally stopped shaking around the cup.

She reached for the case instead.

"Let me just show you," she said. "I think I've been trying to explain this in words for the last hour, and I don't think words are actually the right tool for a thing this size."

Leo (POV): I have sat through a great many briefings in this job. I had never once sat through one where nobody in the room interrupted a single sentence for the better part of an hour, out of nothing but plain respect for what it was visibly costing the person delivering it.

Millie talked, and I took sheets from her hand as fast as she produced them, passing each one across to the President the instant I'd finished with it, and neither of us said a word past what she needed to hear back from us to keep going.

Daniel's citation, buried in a decorative border nobody else had thought twice about. The classification index underneath it. Fraiser's own confirmation, three separate methods, cross-checked against each other and against the seeding-radius data days old already. Phage's own star-to-star migration as the actual mechanism, not a single unlucky hitchhike but a living thing doing exactly what living things do, over and over, for as long as the deep time behind it allowed. Earth and Erid, two confirmed branches off one root, not two coincidences standing next to each other. Descent, Millie said carefully, not contamination. Nothing had touched us. We had grown out of it, the same as everything else that grew.

Then the Builders. A third branch, landed on its own, given its own silence to sit in before she moved past it.

Then the part that made my own hand stop moving mid-sheet: the jump trait itself, dormant in phage's own base material, confirmed directly, not merely inferred from what it had gone on to build three separate times. Not a gift stitched into any one bloodline. Something sitting at the root of the root, waiting, long before anyone existed yet to use it.

I stepped out once, mid-briefing, for a legal pad, because some things you just handle without being told to, and came back to fill several pages without once noticing when Millie had actually stopped talking. It was the silence itself, eventually, that made me look up.

The President was very still, looking at the last sheet in his own hand.

"Now I am become Death, the destroyer of worlds," he said, quiet, not performing it. "Oppenheimer. Borrowed it from the Gita himself, watching the first one go off in the desert, because he needed something bigger than his own language to carry what he'd just done." He set the sheet down, careful, the way you set down something you don't fully trust your own hands with yet. "This only gets him half right, though. Destroyer's the easy half. This is creator too. Whatever built every single one of us, human and Eridian and Builder alike, is sitting in the same root as the thing that nearly ended all three of us separately. I don't know what a man's supposed to do with a fact that large."

"You already know," Millie said, quiet. "You're doing it right now. You're sitting with it instead of running from it."

I looked back down at my own legal pad, and only then actually saw it: her hands, finally still, empty of every sheet she'd carried into this room. Whatever the tea hadn't managed to do earlier, the telling itself had finished doing on its own.

Millie (POV): I heard it before I think he meant for me to say it out loud, a private phrase falling out of his own mouth the way it had fallen out of mine hours earlier, on my own knees in an archive four and a half light-years from this couch.

"Pull up your bootstraps," Bartlett murmured, mostly to himself, and I never did find out whether he'd caught it slipping out of me earlier without my noticing, or simply arrived at the same old trick on his own, the way two people sometimes build the identical tool out of nothing but necessity. It didn't matter which. Either way, I recognized the sound of a man finishing the process of steadying himself, and understood, watching it happen, that he was about to be useful.

He stood. Whatever softness the tea and the silence had let him wear for the last hour was gone, replaced by something I recognized from every account I'd ever read of a man doing the actual job he'd signed up for.

"Leo. Get everyone who could possibly help with something like this in a room together. Right now." Then, to me, direct, no hedging left in it at all. "Janet and Daniel will need to be there too. In person."

"That's a second Pony Express run," I said. "Not a comms link."

"I'm aware of what I'm asking." He was already moving toward the door. "This is not going to be a short meeting, Millie. I want you to understand that going in. This is the kind of thing that clears calendars instead of filling them, that runs until it's actually finished instead of until some clock on the wall says it should be."

"I wouldn't have expected anything less," I said, and meant it, and felt, for the first time since I'd stood in that archive with Fraiser's voice landing on me like a diagnosis, something that wasn't quite relief but stood close enough to it to borrow the shape.
