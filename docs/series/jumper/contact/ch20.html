Rocky (POV): Some sky worth crossing again. Not for duty, this time. Because family asked.

Erid's own sky looked different every time I came back to it, not because the sky itself ever changed but because I never came back the same person twice. I was midway through a routine fuel survey, hands full of instruments that had nothing urgent about them, when Cent's own particular knock sounded against the outer hatch, the one she'd used since before either of us had a word good enough for what we were to each other.

I didn't need to see her face to know something had shifted. I felt it the way I felt weather coming, a change in the pressure of a day before the day itself announced what kind of day it was going to be.

"Cent!" Grace was already moving toward the hatch before I'd fully processed the knock, delight arriving in him the plain, unguarded way it always did whenever family showed up unannounced. "Rocky, it's Cent!"

"I know it is Cent," I said, though I was already moving too, hands still full of instruments I hadn't thought to set down.

Cent came through the hatch already talking, the energy of someone who'd been holding something in for exactly as long as it took to get here and not one second longer.

"I need to ask you both something," she said, no preamble, the way this family had always preferred to lead with the actual thing instead of circling it politely first. "Together. Because it's not really something you'd want to hear separately."

Grace's face was already doing something complicated, hope and readiness arriving before the actual question had even landed. "Ask."

"We're going back to the Object. Building a station out there, a real one, permanent, the whole Wayfarer crew coming back for it. And I want you both there. Not as a courtesy invitation. As crew. As the two people who taught this family more about what's actually possible than anyone else alive." Cent looked between us, something raw and hopeful in her own face now too. "It's dangerous. It's real. It's going to take years out of both your lives, maybe longer. I'm not going to pretend otherwise."

Grace didn't wait for the rest of it. "Yes," he said, before Cent had even finished her sentence, before the shape of the danger had fully landed, the same instant, unguarded yes that had once, a lifetime ago now, made him the first among his own kind to trust this strange family with everything he had left to give. "Yes, yes, yes."

Something rose in my own chest that had no single word attached to it yet, joy and readiness and something older and steadier underneath both of those, the gladness of being asked rather than simply assumed. My hands, still full of instruments, finally found somewhere to go: I set them down, deliberate, and let my whole body answer the way my whole body had always answered the things that actually mattered to me.

Jazz hands, both of us, fingers spread and shaking, the intensity reserved for news too large for words alone, and underneath the hands, my feet left the ground entirely, one full jump straight up in place, the same physical vocabulary Cent had first learned to read off me years and years ago now, back when neither of us had any idea how far any of this would eventually carry us.

"Yes," I said, once my feet found ground again, once the hands finally slowed enough to let actual language back through. "Yes. Family asks. Rocky answers. Every time. This time most of all."

Cent laughed, something wet at the edges of it, and pulled us both into an embrace that held on considerably longer than any of us strictly needed it to.

"We leave once the station's ready," she said, finally, once she'd let go. "I'll come get you both myself."

"We will be ready," I said, and meant every part of it.

Grebenchekov (POV): Grounded, by age and health, ten years now since the graft that had saved my life. I sat through the centrifuge trial knowing before it started what the results were going to say. I ran it anyway, because not running it would have felt like conceding before anyone had actually made me.

The centrifuge came down off its final G-spike, and my hands were shaking before the restraints even released.

That wasn't new. My hands had shaken after every hard pull for eleven years now, ever since the graft, a small tremor my body had learned to file under normal the same way it had learned to file the faint ache along my ribs under normal, the ache that showed up whenever pressure changed too fast for tissue that had been rebuilt instead of simply healed. I'd passed worse pulls than this one. I'd passed this exact pull, four years ago, on the last mission's own qualifying round.

Dr. Reyes was already at the readout when the pod opened, and she didn't say anything right away, which told me more than any number on that screen could have.

"Say it," I said.

"Heart rate recovery's slower than baseline. Twenty percent slower than your own baseline four years ago, not some generic curve. And the graft site showed inflammation on the last two pulls that it didn't show on the first two." She turned the screen so I could see it, because she'd always done me that courtesy, treated me like a colleague reading my own data instead of a patient being managed. "You cleared the pull, Grebenchekov. Your body's telling me what clearing it cost."

"So I rest. Between pulls. I train harder for the recovery curve..."

"You've trained harder for eleven years. That's not the variable that's changed." Reyes' voice stayed level, which was somehow worse than if it had cracked. "This isn't a fitness problem I can fix you into passing. It's eleven years on a graft that was built to save your life, not to keep taking G-loads on the far side of sixty."

I sat with that a moment, in the pod, restraints already off, not moving anyway.

"I have been EVA lead on every mission since before Cent Rice could drive a car," I said, and stopped, because the sentence I'd meant to finish wasn't there yet. Started again. "I have been EVA lead. That is not a job I forgot how to do."

"I know."

"I did not forget."

"I know you didn't." Reyes crouched down to my eye level, which she didn't have to do, which I noticed anyway. "Nobody's saying you forgot how. I'm saying the body underneath the skill can't take what the mission's going to ask of it, and if I clear you anyway and something fails at the Object, four and a half light-years from the nearest hospital that's ever heard your name, that's not brave. That's just me being the reason you don't come home."

I didn't have an answer for that either. I'd had answers ready for every version of this conversation I'd imagined over the years, every version except the real one, apparently, because the real one had emptied my head of all of them.

Millie found me twenty minutes later, still sitting on the edge of the pod bay, staring at nothing in particular. Half a bottle of vodka already gone beside me, no glass, no pretense there was ever going to be a glass.

I held the bottle out to her without a word. She took it, and drank, and didn't ask where a man who'd just failed a NASA medical trial had gotten a full bottle of vodka onto a secured Canaveral facility inside twenty minutes, because some questions weren't actually questions, they were just her giving me a second to not have to talk yet.

"Reyes told me," she said finally, handing the bottle back. "I'm sorry."

"I have flown with this family since before some of this family could fly." I took a pull before I said the rest of it, quietly, to the floor more than to her. "And now I get to watch the rest of you go, from a screen, four light-years back, like some retired man at a window."

"You're not retired."

"I am grounded. Say the word you actually mean."

Millie let that sit a second before she answered, which I appreciated more than I could have said right then. "Fine. Grounded." Then, gentler, and she used my name this time, not the surname the whole program called me, the one only a handful of people had ever earned the right to use: "But you're not gone, Dima. You're not written out. You're the reason half this crew knows how to do their own jobs in the first place. That doesn't stop being true because your heart rate curve does something different than it used to."

I didn't answer for a while. When I did, my voice had found something steadier underneath it, not acceptance yet, but the shape acceptance would eventually fill. "OK." A breath. "Fine." Another. "But you come get me the very миг you arrive at the Object."

Millie's Russian wasn't good. It had never been good, a handful of phrases picked up secondhand and kept mostly for exactly this kind of moment, insults surviving longest in any borrowed language because insults were the words you actually practiced. But it came out clean, and fast, and completely certain.

"Конечно, имбецил!" Of course, you idiot.

For the first time since the centrifuge stopped, I laughed, and took the bottle back, and held it up between us.

"To the very миг," I said.

"To the very миг," Millie echoed, and we drank to it, right there on the edge of the pod bay, the closest thing to a toast either of us had the words for.

Davy (POV): Eight months since Sterling stood in that conference room and told me, plainly, that I was the only name on the roster without a second job attached. Eight months of hating that math and finding, somewhere in there, that hating it didn't actually make it wrong. I'd had eight months to get used to the shape of tomorrow. It hadn't made tonight any easier to sit through.

Millie found me on the same stretch of scaffold decking Grebenchekov had apparently claimed before me, going by the second bottle already sitting open between us before she'd even said a word.

"Still hating the math," Millie said, not a question, settling down beside me.

"Still hating the math." I turned the bottle in my hand, not drinking yet. "Thirty years, Millie. Thirty years and tomorrow I finally get to be the one standing at the window."

"You're not at a window. You're at the only door that still needs a hand on it." She took the bottle from me, drank, handed it back, the same unhurried ritual I'd watched her run with half this crew over the last eight months, patient in a way she hadn't always been, earned the hard way over thirty years same as everything else about her. "I don't love this any more than you do. I would rather have you on that hull with me tomorrow than anywhere else in this universe. But she's right, and you know she's right, or you'd still be yelling instead of sitting here quietly getting drunk with me."

"I'm allowed to do both."

"You are." A small smile, the first one either of us had managed since Cent walked out. "For the record. I intend to come home. Every single time. That's not a promise I'm making lightly, and it's not one I've ever broken yet."

"You'd better not start now." I finally drank, the burn of it doing less than I wanted it to against the actual thing sitting in my chest. "Four and a third light-years is a long way to come find out you broke it."

"Then I won't." She leaned her head against my shoulder, simple, unhurried, thirty years of exactly this same gesture behind it. "Hold the ground, Davy. I'll be back for it."

Camera (POV): No character's own eyes, deliberately. By the end, the scaffold didn't look like scaffold anymore.

Frank Delgado's welds, four hundred and some of them scattered across a hull nobody could see all of at once anymore, held pressure the first time and every time after. Yeth's board stayed green, had stayed green for weeks now, three subsystems that had once needed an entire afternoon and a fabrication engineer's shaking hands to agree to speak to each other, now just quietly speaking, the way finished things do, without anyone in the room to admire it. Priya Desai signed her last pre-launch log at 0300 and went home to sleep for the first time in eleven hours, and somewhere behind her Bay 6's joint sat exactly as true as the rest of the ship now, no different from any other seam, the mistake it used to be already forgotten by everyone except the two people who'd fixed it. Commander Voss's redundant corridor ran its full length through four modules that had never once needed the second path, not yet, not during construction, and he'd already told Rossi that not needing it yet meant nothing, that the whole design existed for a day that hadn't come. Sket's drill bay sat quiet, checklists retired, twelve reps of a scenario that would now, if it ever came, meet a body that already knew what to do before deciding to.

Rockhound stood in what used to be a mining survey and was now just a ship, running one last spectral pass on a xenonite sample nobody would ever need him to run again before departure, and didn't call anyone about it, because some numbers you check just to be the one who checked them.

None of it looked finished, exactly. It looked like the moment right before a held breath lets go: every crew quarter assigned, every system logged nominal, every checklist that could be closed, closed, and one that couldn't be, not yet, sitting open on four names, with a fifth held back on Earth, at the one post that still needed a hand on it.

Four people sat at four separate stations along the hull, strapped into the same gimbaled reaction chairs the simulator had trained them on for months, out of sight of each other, and waited for a trigger that made no sound and gave no warning.

Somewhere behind them, Centauri hung exactly where it always hung, patient, indifferent, four and a third light-years from the only home any of them had ever known before this one.

The trigger fired.
