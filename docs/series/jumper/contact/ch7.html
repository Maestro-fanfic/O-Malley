Chapter 7 of 53

Mrs. Landingham: The Oval Office door muffled most things down to a low hum on an ordinary day. This was not an ordinary day.

Forty years of doors exactly like that one had taught her the difference between a raised voice arguing a point and a raised voice that meant something had genuinely come apart on the other side, and this was the second kind. The President's own voice, pitched louder than she'd heard it since the day she'd started working for him.

"Mrs. Landingham! Get General Gray in here, pronto!"

She was already reaching for the phone before the door had finished shaking in its frame.

Gray picked up on the second ring, same as always.

"The President has asked for your presence, 'pronto.'"

"Right."

Click.

Bartlett (POV): A sentence too large for the room it's said in leaves a real quiet behind. I'd been sitting in it for twenty minutes.

Mrs. Landingham didn't ask questions. I gave her the name, and she relayed the summons exactly as given, no clarification requested, no raised eyebrow, the same unflappable competence she'd brought to every strange request I'd ever handed her across two administrations now.

General William Gray arrived inside twenty minutes, fast enough that I found myself wondering whether he'd already been somewhere in the building, on the off chance today turned into one of those days. He didn't look surprised to be summoned into the Oval with no notice and no context. He never looked surprised at anything. I'd started to suspect it wasn't a skill so much as a fact about the man, load-bearing, the way some people simply didn't startle at loud noises.

"Mr. President." A nod, economical, and I watched him take the room's temperature before he'd said a full sentence: Millie, still visibly rattled from twenty minutes ago; Leo, coffee stain still drying on his shirt in a spreading, undignified continent; me, working hard to look considerably more composed than I currently felt.

"General. Thank you for coming so fast."

"You don't usually call me in for small things, Sir."

I didn't dress it up. I'd learned, in the last half hour, that dressing things up only delayed the part where the other person actually understood.

"This young woman and four members of her family can teleport," I said. "Individually, and as a group. They've been doing it for years. Tonight I learned they can move enough mass together to build a space station."

Gray's expression didn't change. Not a flicker. He'd known about the individual capability for years, the same as most of the country had, since it had never exactly been a secret since Reach. What he hadn't seen was any of it happen in front of him. He looked at Millie the way a man looks at a data point he'd only ever read about, finally standing close enough to confirm it in person.

"Show me."

She didn't ask permission. Her eyes landed on the closest thing within reach, which happened to be my own prize collector baseball, sitting in its little display stand on the Resolute desk, and she'd already picked it up before anyone in the room had time to object.

She didn't jump it anywhere. She just gave it momentum, no windup, no visible effort at all, the ball simply leaving her open palm straight up, just enough force to kiss the ceiling with the faintest tap before gravity took it back, dropping it right back into her waiting hand as gently as it had left.

She turned back around already smiling, ready to hand it back, and found both of us staring at her with the exact same expression. The unflappable had flapped on both faces at once. Not at the physics, not really, at least not for Gray, who'd braced for something impossible the instant he'd said show me. It was the sheer nerve of it. A private citizen casually palming the President's own irreplaceable keepsake off his desk without so much as a by-your-leave, using it like a prop, handing it back like it had never once been in question that she could.

"That's..." I started, and didn't finish.

"Adequately demonstrated," Gray said, a beat too late, still visibly recalibrating. He looked back at me. "What do you need from me, Sir?"

"Joint Chiefs-level backing. This doesn't belong to NASA anymore, and it doesn't belong to any one service either."

"Understood." No hesitation, no negotiation, just a man recalculating the size of a problem and updating his own commitment to match it, the same way I got the distinct impression he'd done a dozen times before for things far stranger than this. "I'll need everything you have by morning, and I'll need it complete. I don't work off partial pictures. Learned that the hard way, more than once."

He caught himself half a second after the words were out, and turned to Millie, some small recalibration crossing his face. "Apologies. That came out as an order, and you don't work for me. Would you be willing to have it to me by morning?"

He excused himself to make calls. Once the door had closed behind him, Leo let out a breath he'd clearly been holding since somewhere around the coffee incident.

"That's the least fazed I've seen anyone react to any of this."

"He's had practice." I looked at the closed door a moment longer than I strictly needed to. "I almost envy him that."

Millie was still standing near the desk, baseball returned to its stand exactly where it had come from, watching the two of us like someone who'd just watched their own life quietly stop being small.

"So," she said. "What happens now?"

I thought about the answer for longer than the question probably deserved, because the honest one was that I genuinely didn't know yet, not the shape of it, not the scale, only that whatever came next was going to be bigger than the room we were standing in, bigger than the desk between us, bigger, very possibly, than anything my own administration had ever actually been built to hold.

"Now," I said, "we find out how big this actually gets."

I had no idea, that night, just how right I was.
