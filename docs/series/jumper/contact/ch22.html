Rat (POV): I'd been staring at the same conversion error for forty minutes, and the fourth energy drink wasn't helping any more than the first three had.

I'd made peace a long time ago with the fact that I was never going to be the guy who ran on coffee like every other engineer I'd ever worked with. Coffee tasted like punishment to me, always had, and four hundred some-odd days out from the nearest grocery store was a genuinely terrible time to discover a new opinion about caffeine delivery systems, so I'd put in my supply requisition back at Canaveral with the same seriousness most people reserved for oxygen and water: six cases, alphabetized by flavor, plus, because I'd learned my own limits the hard way on shorter missions, a separate standing order for red licorice, the good kind, not the cheap stuff that tasted like sadness. Sterling's own logistics people had actually pushed back on the mass budget for it once, gently, and I'd held my ground exactly as gently, and won.

"The problem," I said, mostly to myself, mostly for the fifth time, cracking open can number four and feeling only a mild, familiar despair about it, "is that Eridian eyes don't process motion the same way ours do. Doesn't matter how clean the source footage is. Straight playback just reads as noise to them, or worse, actively unpleasant noise. So every single frame, forever, for as long as this broadcast runs, has to get reprocessed on the fly before it hits their own displays."

"Sounds like you fixed it, then." Kaylee didn't look up from the panel she had half her arm inside of, three consoles over.

"I fixed it. I can't name it. 'The conversion' isn't gonna cut it. Nobody's saying 'the conversion' for the next however-many years of broadcasts. It's gotta be something people actually want to say." I fished a length of red licorice out of the bag beside my console without looking, pure muscle memory at this point, four hundred days deep into a habit that had started as a joke back at Canaveral and had long since stopped being funny to anyone but me. "You want a piece?"

"I want you to name the thing."

"I'm working on it. The licorice helps me think."

"Nothing helps you think. You've eaten an entire bag of that stuff watching a status light blink for ten minutes before." Kaylee thought about the actual question for exactly as long as it took to torque one more bolt. "Eridi-view."

"...What?"

"Eridi-view. Done." She still hadn't looked up. "Next problem."

I opened my mouth to argue, mostly out of principle, and found I didn't actually have an argument ready. It was short. It said exactly what it did. Nobody would ever need it explained twice.

"Eridi-view," I repeated, testing the shape of it, chewing through another inch of licorice while I did.

"Eridi-view," Kaylee said again, already moving on to the next connector, entirely unaware she'd just named something that was going to outlive both of us.

By the time the halfway broadcast went out, six weeks later, nobody on the whole crew remembered whose idea it had actually been. It was just what it was called. I still had four cans of energy drink and half a bag of licorice left over from that exact shift, which felt, in its own small way, like proof I'd earned the name fair and square.

Millie (POV): Four hundred some-odd days is a strange unit of time to measure a marriage's worth of hope against. It's exactly long enough to forget what home actually sounded like, and exactly short enough that you never stop counting.

I'd started keeping the count on the inside of my own wrist, early on, a single tally mark scratched in with whatever I had on hand each morning, more habit than actual necessity since the ship's own systems tracked the real number down to the second. I kept doing it anyway. There was something about a number you'd put there yourself, in your own hand, that a screen readout never quite managed to replicate. Davy would have laughed at me for it, gently, the way he laughed at all my smaller superstitions, and I found myself missing that particular laugh more than I'd expected to miss any one specific thing, out of everything I'd left behind to come out here.

We watched the broadcast together, all of us who weren't on shift, crowded into the common space the way we'd crowded into rooms our whole lives for news that actually mattered. Truman stood near the back, arms crossed, doing the thing he always did before something big, going still in a way that looked like calm and wasn't quite. Joe had found a spot near the front rail with two of the Eridian crew flanking him, close enough that I caught him translating something under his breath before the feed even started, some small kindness he'd started doing without anyone asking him to.

The halfway broadcast opened cold, no announcer, no countdown, just the actual footage, converted to eridi-view, of four men in matching suits crowded into a London recording studio in the year nineteen sixty-seven, a room full of friends and flowers and hand-lettered signs behind them, singing a song simple enough that it had once crossed every language barrier a fractured, anxious world could throw at it, live, beamed out to the entire planet at once over a brand-new satellite link nobody had ever tried using quite that way before. All You Need Is Love. I'd heard the recording a hundred times growing up. I had never once watched it land the way it landed that night, converted for eyes that had never belonged to a human being, broadcast simultaneously to two entire worlds at once for the first time in either species' history.

Nobody spoke while it played. Nobody, human or Eridian, seemed to want to be the one who broke whatever was happening in that shared, silent room. I watched one of the Eridians near Joe go very still in that particular way their whole species did, treating something moving and something true as the same category of fact, no drama layered over either one, and understood, watching it, that whatever this song was doing to it, it wasn't so different from what it was doing to me.

Only once it ended did Bartlett speak, his voice carrying across the same joint feed that had, for the length of one old song, made two separate planets feel briefly, impossibly, like a single room.

"Four hundred some-odd days ago," he said, "four people and a ship full of hope left everything they knew behind for the second time in as many years, with a fifth holding the ground steady back home so the other four could go. Tonight, they've crossed half the distance between here and wherever this next chapter of all our history actually leads."

I stood there, half a galaxy from the room he was speaking from, and felt the words land anyway, the same distance-defying way that song just had. Somewhere back on Earth, I knew, Davy was watching this exact same broadcast, on whatever screen Sterling's people had rigged up for him, and I let myself imagine, for exactly as long as the thought took to form, that the four and a third light-years of delay between his own reaction and mine didn't actually exist, that we were both hearing this sentence land at precisely the same instant, together, the way we'd have heard it if he were standing right beside me.

"We built a promise a while back," Bartlett went on, "that you would never be reading about any of this after the fact. Live, starting the moment we had something worth showing you, for as long as it takes. I intend to keep making good on that promise tonight, same as every night since. So: halfway there. Numbers on-screen now for anyone who wants them. And for everyone who's spent the last four hundred some-odd days wondering whether any of this was worth the wait." A pause, and I could hear, even through the light delay, something genuinely moved in it. "Nobody's ever had to figure out how to film honesty at this scale before tonight. I think we're doing all right."

The room held its own quiet a moment longer after he finished, and I added one more mark to the inside of my wrist, the same as I did every morning, and thought, not for the first time, that halfway didn't feel like halfway at all. It felt like the exact moment the whole thing finally became real enough to actually count.
