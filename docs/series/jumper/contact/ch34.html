Millie (POV): The chime was quiet, almost apologetic, and I had already learned to associate it with exactly one thing: The One Who Stands had decided to be somewhere.

I looked up from the tablet I'd been carrying around for two days without actually opening, and there he was in the doorway, unhurried, the raccoon-adjacent tilt of his head giving nothing away. "The gate finished decoding four days ago," he said, without preamble, the way he said most things. "Daniel has the full transcript. Carter has verified it against three independent methods. You have not read it."

It wasn't an accusation. He didn't seem to have an accusing register at all. But it landed anyway.

"I've been busy."

"You have been avoiding it." He said this with the same flat gentleness he'd used for the fact of the transcript existing, no more weight on one sentence than the other. "That is allowed. I am not here to correct you. I am here because I would rather you understand it than read it, the first time. Reading strips something out. I have watched it happen to every one of you."

I set the tablet down. "Then say it."

He did.

"All that we are, all that we have been, we gift to you who come after us, that you may avoid the events that so nearly cost us everything."

I'd seen those words already, secondhand, in a mission brief Daniel had written three days ago, clinical and careful the way Daniel wrote everything. I had not, until this exact moment, actually heard them, and something about a voice saying them, even a voice built rather than born, undid the careful distance the brief had let me keep.

"Say the rest isn't as bad as that sounded."

"I cannot say that." No hesitation, no softening either. "But I can give you the part the transcript does not have, because it is not a translation, it is a memory, and Daniel has no way to read those yet." A pause, the closest thing to consideration I'd seen from him. "This record. Where it began. I can tell you the name of the world."

"Tell me."

"Prism." He said it the way he said everything, entirely without ceremony, a name offered the same as any other fact. "You would not know it. It is nowhere near here. That is simply our own name for our own world, whatever your language would eventually render it as." A pause. "It is where this began."

I sat with that a moment, the actual concreteness of it, a real world with a real name instead of an abstraction called the Builders' homeworld. "You remember it. Firsthand."

"No." Plain, uncomplicated by any embarrassment at the correction. "I was not there. I was built with the memory already intact, given to me whole by someone who was. Their designation is the Library." A beat, nothing performed about it, just fact laid down next to fact. "I can show you, if you'd like."

"Show me how?"

"All thinking beings have a Plug." He said it exactly the way he might have said all thinking beings have a name, no drama in it whatsoever. "It is where a mind actually lives, the part that receives. It does not matter what shape carries it, carbon, silicon, whatever word your own science eventually settles on for what an Eridian is built from underneath. Every one of us has one. You have simply never had cause to use yours for anything but yourself."

"A plug for what?"

"For this." He tilted his head very slightly, the closest he came to something like warmth. "Connection is not communication. Communication is words trying to describe an experience secondhand, and something is always lost in that trade, every time, in every language I have ever watched try it. Connection is simply handing the experience across directly, the way you might hand someone a cup instead of describing water and hoping they understand thirst. I have done this so many times I no longer remember the first. It is not a large thing, to me. I understand it may be a large thing, to you."

I looked at him for a long moment, the tablet still sitting closed on my lap, the transcript still unread, and heard myself ask the only question that actually mattered.

"Will it hurt?"

"No." Simple. Certain. "It will only be true."

I set the tablet aside completely.

"Show me."

"Please have a seat first." Something almost gentle underneath the flatness of it. "This may be disorienting."

I sank into the nearest chair, hands flat on my own knees, bracing for something I had no actual frame of reference for bracing against.

Something extended from him then, thin and glinting and unhurried, a tendril no thicker than a cable uncoiling from somewhere along his forearm, drifting toward me with no urgency at all, as if it fully expected me to change my mind before it arrived. I almost did. Some old, animal part of me flinched at the sight of it, at the sheer wrongness of inviting something like that anywhere near my own ear. I made myself hold still anyway, made myself remember every reason I'd walked in here in the first place, and didn't pull away when the tip of it finally found the edge of my ear and settled there, light as a fingertip.

The tendril itself barely registered, a tiny tickle, the exact same faint itch I'd expect cleaning my own ear out with a q-tip, and then nothing further, no sensation of anything actually reaching into me, no countdown, no warning hum, nothing like the medical scans I'd braced for out of pure habit. There was me, and then, with no seam between the two states at all, there was Prism.

I had a body that wasn't mine, low and quick and close to the ground, and the light overhead came in wrong, harder and bluer than any sun I'd ever stood under, refracting through a canopy of something crystalline instead of green, so that the whole understory shattered into color the way light does through a cut stone. I understood, distantly, why The One Who Stands had always looked the way he did, that same glasslike quality I'd stood close enough to touch a dozen times without once knowing why it was there. It wasn't a design choice. It was simply what a body was, on a world lit like this one. I could feel, too, that this body loved this light, had grown up trusting it completely, and that trust was already, somewhere underneath the memory, a thing I knew was about to be spent.

There were others near me, dozens, a work-yard of some kind, purposeful and unhurried the way Yeth had been purposeful and unhurried over a mold four hundred and some times in a row, and for one full, aching moment the memory simply let me feel that: the calm satisfaction of doing, on an entire world, going about an entire world's version of an ordinary day.

Somewhere underneath even that ordinary calm, older than the calm itself, ran a second, quieter vigilance, the kind that never fully switched off. Every member of this Associative ran on sunlight, plain and literal, photovoltaic hearts that dimmed and stilled the moment the sun finally went down, and there was nothing anyone could do about the part of the day when the whole compound simply stopped, defenseless, waiting for morning to switch them back on. The Vwacorites knew it too. Organic under their own borrowed silicate shells, awake exactly when nobody else could be, and what they left behind by first light was never something the Library's memory tried to soften: walls torn open and left in fragments, warriors found the next morning with their limbs twisted off and carried away in baskets, ordinary and expected and never, not once in all the memory's own long life, stopped being awful. There was a saying for it, older than the Library itself. The night belongs to them.

Then the light changed.

Not the sun. Something closer, spreading low across the ground the way I now understood, with the Library's own bone-deep certainty, no one should ever have to understand anything: bright, wrong, alive, moving the exact branching way Ash had traced with one claw across a display two hundred years and four and a half light-years removed from this exact ground. It didn't roar. It didn't announce itself at all. It hadn't started tonight either, or this year, or however many years before this one nobody had thought to count: something patient enough to grow like this didn't begin the moment somebody finally noticed it, it had simply spent however long it needed growing quietly enough that nobody had a reason to look, and by the time it was visible at all, it was already the only story left to tell. It simply kept being more of itself, every hour, everywhere it touched, patient in a way nothing this small should have been able to afford to be.

I felt the Library run. Felt the physical shame of running, a body built for calm confronting something that made calm impossible, and worse, felt the moment running stopped being about self-preservation and became about carrying word of it fast enough for anyone else to have a chance. I felt a hand, not my own, not quite the right number of fingers, close briefly around the Library's own before letting go, a goodbye with no time left over for saying it out loud. I never learned whose hand it was. The memory didn't know either. That was, somehow, the worst part of all of it: some losses didn't even get to keep their own names.

And then, much later, in a room gone quiet the way rooms go quiet after everyone who was going to scream already has, I felt the Library sit down in front of a recording device that hadn't existed an hour before this one had been built for exactly this purpose, and begin, with perfect, deliberate calm, to speak the words that would eventually cross species, and centuries, and four and a half light-years, to land on a woman who hadn't even been born yet in a body that hadn't evolved yet on a world the Library would never live to see.

All that we are, all that we have been, we gift to you who come after us.

The Connection ended the same way it began: no seam, no warning, just me, alone in my own body again, on my own knees on an observation deck floor I didn't remember kneeling on, gasping like I'd actually been the one running.

The One Who Stands hadn't moved. "I told you," he said, gently, "that it would only be true."

I wiped my face with the back of one hand, found it wet, and didn't bother being surprised by that either.

"Get me Sterling," I said, once I trusted my own voice again. "Get me Gray. Get me everyone." I was already pushing myself back to my feet. "We are not watching this happen to someone else's world twice."

"And there's something else." I was still catching my breath, but my voice had already found its footing again. "The Vwacorites. Whatever's left of the Associative, wherever the Library actually is right now, however we eventually find them. Your whole civilization ran on the sun going down and just... stopping. Defenseless. Every single night, for I don't even know how many generations." I looked back at The One Who Stands. "We don't run out of power when the sun goes down. We haven't for over a century. Batteries, first, that's the easy part, something to hold a charge straight through the dark. And if that's not enough, we'll hand over the plans for real generators, something that doesn't care whether the sun's up or not."

The One Who Stands considered this with the same unhurried attention he gave most things. "That is..." A pause, unusual for him. "I do not believe anyone has offered the Associative anything, in longer than either of us could easily count."

"Good," I said. "Then it's overdue."

"We will accept the plans for those." Something almost amused threaded underneath the flatness now. "But we will do a much better job growing our own."

I blinked. "Growing them."

Something in my brain briefly refused to process the sentence at all, caught on the sheer, literal strangeness of it: a power plant that actually thinks about its purpose, a battery implanted like a pacemaker instead of bolted into a housing. I'd spent my whole life around machines built the way Commander Voss built a corridor, one measured joint at a time, bolted and welded and argued over, and the idea of any of it simply growing, cell by patient cell, out of something closer to seed than schematic, sat strangely in my chest, not quite believable even three feet from proof that it was.

"Yes." Matter-of-fact, no boast in it, simply correction offered the way he corrected everything else. "You build. We grow. It has always been the difference between us, and I do not say that to diminish what you are offering, only to tell you honestly: give us the shape of the idea, and the Associative will make something better suited to itself than any device your own hands could ship across four and a half light-years. That is not pride. That is simply what growing things are for."

I almost laughed, startled into it. "Then it's overdue, and it's going to outgrow us. Both things can be true."
