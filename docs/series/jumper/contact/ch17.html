Frank Delgado: I'd thrown up exactly once in twenty-six years, at my own wedding reception, on three fingers of my father-in-law's homemade mezcal. Never on the job. Not two hundred feet down, not topside in a following sea rough enough to put half the crew on the rail. You didn't get to be the guy who got sick, not in my line of work. The reputation followed you, and once it followed you, it never really let go, not even after you'd earned twenty years of evidence to the contrary.

I hadn't always been this hard to rattle. There'd been a version of me, twenty-four years old and still soft around the edges, who'd panicked once on a sat dive off Norfolk when a hose kinked at a hundred and eighty feet, real panic, the kind that makes your own hands stop belonging to you, and the diver who'd talked me back down through the comm line had died eleven months later on a job I wasn't even assigned to, a routine hull inspection that stopped being routine the way they always did, without any warning worth the name. I'd built the rest of my career, all twenty-six years of it, on one plain promise: nobody was ever again going to have to talk me down through a comm line. Crusty wasn't a personality trait. Crusty was a debt I'd been paying off since I was twenty-four, one calm decision at a time, to a man whose name I still said out loud every year on the anniversary, alone, where nobody could turn it into a moment.

And never once had I thrown up like this.

Nobody warned me the jump itself didn't feel like anything, not really, but that the half-second of nothing under your boots right after did. Twenty-six years running structural welds on Navy hulls, four of those on saturation dive work two hundred feet under the Atlantic, and none of it had prepared me for what my inner ear did the moment gravity stopped being a rumor and became a math problem. I'd trained for six weeks before they let me anywhere near real hardware, pressure chamber work, centrifuge runs, a medical clearance battery that made my old Navy dive physical look like a formality, and every single briefing had used the same word: disorienting. Nobody had used the word that actually applied, which was betrayal. My own body had spent forty-eight years agreeing with gravity. It didn't appreciate finding out the arrangement had never been permanent.

The kid floating next to me, tether already clipped, didn't laugh. Just handed me a wipe and looked politely somewhere else while I cleaned my own visor, giving me exactly as much dignity as the situation had left available.

"First-timers get one freebie," the kid said. "Nobody logs it."

"Kid" was generous. The guy had a NASA patch, a PhD he'd mentioned twice in the elevator back at Canaveral, and couldn't have been past thirty. His name tape said ROSSI. He moved through the harness rigging like he'd been born into it, easy and unhurried, the exact opposite of the careful, deliberate way I was still relearning how to move my own arms.

"How many of you are out here already?" I asked, once my stomach signed a truce it wasn't fully committed to.

"Forty-one, as of this morning's run. You're forty-two." Rossi pointed, and I turned, and that was when it actually landed.

I'd seen the renders. Everybody had, the classified-briefing versions and then the version that hit the news three weeks later once the White House stopped sitting on it. Renders didn't do it. What hung against the black wasn't a building or a ship, not yet, it was a skeleton, ribs of scaffold strut running out further than my light could reach, sparks walking down one truss where somebody's rig was already cutting stock to spec, and past all of it, close enough to touch if the numbers were different, a second star doing the job my own sun did back home, brighter and colder both at once. I'd worked under a lot of lights in my life, work lights, dive lights, the sick fluorescent hum of a Norfolk dry dock at three in the morning. None of them had ever made me feel this small on purpose.

"That's Centauri," Rossi said, unnecessary, watching my face do the thing everybody's face did.

"I know what it is." I didn't take my eyes off it. "Just didn't figure I'd be standing this close."

"Nobody did. Six months ago there wasn't a construction firm on Earth with a bid packet for 'permanent structure, deep space, crew of five thousand eventual, five-jumper synchronized delivery only.' We had to write the RFP category from scratch."

I'd read that RFP, or the parts of it that made it down to my own union hall before the government scrubbed the rest. Every welder who'd touched Navy steel in the last decade had gotten the same call, some version of the same pitch: we need people who can put a bead down that holds when nobody's checking your work twice, because there might not be a twice. No astronaut corps required. No flight hours. They didn't want pilots. They wanted people who already knew how to do the actual job in conditions that wanted you dead, and just needed the "in space" part bolted on after. I'd figured that last part was a formality too, right up until the vomit.

"You ever weld in a wet suit?" I asked.

"Can't say I have."

"Water's worse than this. Water fights you the whole time, current's always pulling, visibility's garbage, and you got maybe forty minutes before your gas runs the math against you." I flexed my glove, testing the give in the joint, feeling out exactly how much dexterity thirty layers of pressure-rated fabric was willing to give back. "This is quiet, at least. I'll take quiet."

"You always this cheerful after throwing up?"

"I'm not cheerful. I'm working." Twenty-six years of foremen and dive supervisors had all learned the same thing about me eventually, usually the hard way: I didn't do encouraging, and I didn't do rattled, and anybody looking for either one from me was going to walk away disappointed both times. "Cheerful's for people who haven't figured out the job yet. I figured it out a long time ago. You do the next thing right, then the thing after that, and you don't waste breath on how you feel about any of it. Feelings don't hold a bead."

Rossi handed me the torch rig and ran me through the striker sequence, twice, patient about it, and I listened the way I'd have listened to anybody handing me gear I hadn't used before, no ego in it, because dead was dead whether the mistake happened two hundred feet down or two hundred trillion miles out. Then I lit the arc, and the puddle behaved wrong, flatter than it should, no gravity pulling it into the bead the way four decades of muscle memory expected, spreading out lazy and shallow instead of cupping the way a good weld pool always cupped. My first instinct, straight out of the Navy manual living somewhere behind my eyes, was to slow the travel speed and add filler, exactly what I'd have done underwater when a current started dragging a bead thin.

It made it worse.

"Careful," Rossi said, quiet, not taking the torch out of my hand, just close enough to catch me if I needed catching. "It's not behaving like gravity's helping you or fighting you out here. It's not doing anything at all. You're not compensating for a force anymore. You're compensating for the absence of one."

That took a second to actually land, the way the good advice always did, a half-beat behind when I actually needed it. I sped the travel back up instead of slowing it, let the arc do the work surface tension alone would do without a downward pull helping it settle, and the puddle finally cupped the way it was supposed to, tight and even, holding its own shape out of pure stubborn chemistry instead of gravity's cooperation.

"There it is," Rossi said.

"There it is," I agreed, and kept going, the second joint easier than the first, the third easier than the second, the same curve every new skill ever taught me since I was nineteen years old and terrified of my own torch. Forty-one other people were doing the same thing right now, scattered across a mile of scaffold, most of whom I'd never meet, building something none of the news anchors could fully explain yet. I didn't need it explained. I needed the bead to hold. I struck the next arc and got back to work, and somewhere around the sixth joint I noticed my stomach had finally stopped arguing with the rest of me about whose universe this actually was.

Rossi drifted off to check on somebody two bays over, and for a while it was just me and the arc and the black, and I let myself think about the reputation for the first time since the wipe. Twenty-six years of never being the guy who got sick, and here I was, forty-two of us total on the whole project, the newest hand on a job that hadn't existed six months ago, and not one of them had watched it happen. That was the part that actually mattered, I decided, striking the next joint clean on the first pass. Not whether you threw up. Whether you got back to work after.

Rockhound (POV): I had just started putting the moves on this hot number at the bar when in walk a couple of yahoos in Air Force whites.

I want to be clear that the moves were working. I'd gotten as far as the origin story of the Westinghouse Prize, which is objectively the best part of my whole routine, timed perfectly to land right around the second round, and she was leaning in, actually leaning in, not the polite kind of leaning in people do when they're calculating an exit.

They walked right up to me. "We need to talk." That was the woman, dark hair, command presence you could've bottled and sold, not asking permission to interrupt anything.

I turned to the hottie. "How old are you again?"

"Twenty-six," she said, half laughing, already figuring out I wasn't actually worried about carding her at this point in the conversation, more just establishing for the room that I had my priorities in order.

"Good. Hold that thought." I turned back to the whites. "You've got sixty seconds before I go back to something more interesting than either of you."

"Rockhound." The woman again.

Not a question. That stopped me for half a second, because "Rockhound" isn't the kind of thing that ends up on a government form unless somebody actually did their homework, talked to my crew, dug past the diploma name into the name that actually gets used. Most guys in suits lead with "Dr. Choi" and I have to correct them, and half of them look vaguely offended when I do, like I've somehow made their briefing packet look sloppy. She skipped straight past that, which told me more about the seriousness of the operation than the folder did.

"Maj. Rebecca Childs," she said, like it was already on file somewhere and she was just confirming receipt. The guy next to her, older, unhurried, the kind of calm that's either senior rank or just genuinely doesn't care what time it is, nodded once. "Cmdr. Bob Iverson."

"You've got somebody's attention now," I said. "Fifty-two seconds, still burning."

"Your file's got an entire addendum," Childs said, dry, "just for personal conduct incidents. Three continents. I read the Reykjavik one twice, because I genuinely didn't believe the first pass."

"Everybody reads the Reykjavik one twice." I've heard my own crew explain the nickname to newcomers more times than I can count, and it's always the same delivery, same little pause right before the punchline lands: "We call him Rockhound because... well..." and then they just let the silence do the rest, like the double meaning's too obvious to bother finishing out loud. I've never once corrected them. Free advertising, both halves of it.

"The Reykjavik one is criminally understated in whatever paperwork you're reading. There was a hot spring involved, and a minister of tourism, and I'll tell you the whole thing properly sometime, but the short version is I don't apologize for it and neither did the minister of tourism." I straightened my collar, mostly for effect. "You want a guy who reads rocks right, or you want a guy who behaves himself at conferences? Because I've genuinely never once, in my entire career, been asked to be both at the same time, and I'm suspicious of anybody who thinks that's a reasonable request."

"Nobody's asking you to behave," Iverson said, entirely too fast for a man supposedly meeting me for the first time.

Iverson slid the folder onto the bar himself, unbothered, the way a man slides a folder onto a bar when he's done this exact thing more than once. More redactions on the cover sheet than an FBI file on Castro. "We need someone who can read ore bodies in an environment nobody's ever mined before."

Now that got my attention, mostly because it was a genuinely weird thing to say to a guy at a bar, and I've had a lot of weird things said to me at bars.

"Come again?"

"Off-world," Childs said. "We're not at liberty to say more here."

I laughed. Out loud, loud enough that a couple of heads turned two stools down. Iverson didn't even blink, which told me more than the folder did, because guys who don't blink at "off-world" have already seen enough that "off-world" stopped being the craziest thing on their briefing sheet a while back. I'd met plenty of people over the years who wanted something from me, industry recruiters mostly, the occasional government type looking for a consultant's signature on a report nobody outside a subcommittee would ever read. None of them had ever opened with a phrase that made the hairs on my arm actually stand up before the follow-up sentence.

"Look," I said, mostly to buy myself a second to actually think, "I've had guys try to get me into some stuff before. Vegas timeshares. A pyramid scheme selling supplements to actual pyramid enthusiasts, which, ironic. So before we go any further, you should know I don't respond well to being condescended to, and I definitely don't respond well to it from a man who thinks a redacted folder impresses a guy who won the Westinghouse Prize at twelve. You wanna compare brainpans? Published at nineteen. Double doctorate outta MIT at twenty-two, chemistry and geology, and I taught at Princeton two and a half years before I remembered rocks don't lie to you the way faculty meetings do. So whatever this is, you're gonna have to actually tell me. I already know I'm smart enough to hear it."

Childs looked at Iverson. Iverson shrugged, the universal gesture for he's your problem now, and opened the folder.

I'm not going to pretend I remember every word of the next forty minutes in order, because that's not actually how my brain works when it gets handed something this good. What I remember is the shape of it: a rock that wasn't from anywhere in this solar system, a mission patch with a star pattern I didn't recognize, a departure date that made my stomach do something I hadn't felt since the Westinghouse committee first called my house when I was twelve years old and convinced it was a prank. Somewhere around minute twenty the twenty-six-year-old excused herself, politely, correctly reading the room for exactly what it had become, and I barely noticed her go, which tells you everything about how good the pitch actually was.

Forty minutes later I still didn't have her number. But I had a mission patch nobody outside a three-letter agency was supposed to have seen yet, a departure date, and the distinct, giddy sense that Getchell's gold was about to become the least interesting rock in my professional life.

*

I've been called a lot of things over the years. Show-off. Prima donna. "That guy from the bar who apparently actually is a genius, huh." But nobody's ever once called me wrong about rock, and I intended to keep that streak alive on a planet that wasn't even mine.

The asteroid was garbage, on paper. Standard spectral pass said low-yield silicate, nothing worth the fuel to redirect a hauler its way. Every other geologist on the survey team had already flagged it and moved on, filed it, forgotten it, moved their attention to targets with better headline numbers. I hadn't moved on, because I'd spent twenty minutes staring at one anomaly in the return that everybody else's software had quietly smoothed over as noise, the kind of thing a good algorithm is specifically built to throw away, because algorithms are built by people who've never once been embarrassingly, spectacularly right about a hunch nobody else believed.

"It's not noise," I said, mostly to myself, mostly for the fourth time, because repetition's a hell of a drug when you're right and nobody else believes it yet.

"Rockhound, we've got six other targets queued and you're on number one for forty minutes." That was Delgado, on the comm, patient the way career tradesmen get patient with people who talk more than they work. Frank, I'd learn later, though right then he was just a voice with zero interest in my process.

"Forty-one minutes," I said. "And I need forty-two, because this thing's carrying a silicate lattice structure I have genuinely never seen in a natural formation before, and either my instruments are broken or this rock is doing something on purpose."

"Rocks don't do things on purpose." That was somebody else entirely, cutting in on the shared channel, a survey tech named Osgood who'd apparently decided this was worth breaking radio discipline over. "Also, hi, I've got the actual queue in front of me, and you're holding up three other people's afternoons, not just Frank's."

"Then those three other people are welcome to hate me from a comfortable distance while I make all of us richer."

"He talks like this constantly," a third voice put in, dry, female, somebody named Alvarez running one of the other survey drones two klicks out. "I want the record to reflect I warned everybody in the crew briefing. Nobody listened."

"The record can also reflect that I've been right roughly one hundred percent of the times anybody's bothered to check." I ran the spectral trace one more time, and there it was again, the same impossible regularity, crystal lattice repeating at a precision no natural cooling process had any business producing. Natural cooling is messy. It leaves fingerprints, inclusions, the geological equivalent of a shaky hand. This lattice looked machined, if rock could look machined, which by every textbook I'd ever taught from, it couldn't. "You know what refines into xenonite clean, first pass, no secondary processing? A lattice structure exactly like this one. I'd bet my double doctorate on it."

There was a pause on the line long enough that I could hear somebody else breathing on it.

"You'd bet your double doctorate on a rock."

"I'd bet my double doctorate on a lot of things, that's kind of the whole deal with having one." I was already redirecting the survey drone, already plotting the extraction path in my head before the numbers had even finished confirming what I already knew in my gut, the same certainty that had gotten me laughed at by an entire tenure committee once and vindicated eighteen months later, which is a story I will tell absolutely anyone who gives me more than ninety seconds of their attention. "Get me a hauler. This whole asteroid's a xenonite mine that decided to arrange itself for our convenience, and I am not walking away from free money because somebody's spreadsheet called it garbage forty minutes ago."

"You've got six other surveys backed up behind you."

"Then run them in parallel, because I am not moving off this rock until somebody hands me a hauler or a better argument, and I've been doing this a long time and nobody's ever once had a better argument."

"For the record," Osgood said, "I'm starting a pool. Five bucks says he's wrong for once."

"I'll take that bet," Alvarez said immediately. "Not because I think he's wrong. Because watching him be right one more time is worth five dollars of pure spite."

"You people have no idea how insulting it is to be doubted by a betting pool," I said, entirely delighted, because being talked about, even skeptically, had never once bothered me the way it apparently bothered other people.

They got me the hauler.

Three days later, when the assay came back and it turned out I hadn't just been right, I'd been spectacularly, embarrassingly right, forty percent above projected yield, enough xenonite in a single low-yield rock nobody else had bothered surveying twice to reframe the entire mining schedule for the next quarter, my whole team ended up crowded onto the same channel at once, everybody talking over everybody else. Osgood, first, groaning theatrically about the five dollars he now owed Alvarez, who was already demanding payment in a tone that suggested she'd expected nothing less all along. Delgado, quieter than the rest, just saying "huh" in a way that somehow carried more respect than anybody else's actual words did. And underneath all of it, a dozen other voices I only half recognized, survey techs and haulers and the two other geologists who'd flagged this same rock and moved on, congratulations and half-serious accusations of witchcraft tangled together into something I genuinely couldn't parse word for word, and I let them have their moment before I said the only thing that actually needed saying.

"Look, I get the compliments, I do, I've heard 'em all before and I never get tired of hearing 'em again." I meant it, mostly. "But I wasn't the one running six other surveys in parallel while I sat here arguing with a rock for forty minutes. That's on all of you. I'm only as right as the people making sure I've got the time and the room to be right. Best crew I've ever worked with, and I've worked with some real geniuses. So don't let it go to your heads that I'm the smartest guy on this operation." A beat, because I couldn't help myself, because I never could. "I'm just the only one dumb enough to argue with a rock for forty minutes and win. There's a difference, and it's a difference you should all be grateful for, because arguing with rocks is a genuinely terrible way to spend an afternoon if you don't already know you're going to win."

Yeth (POV): The panel came off the line true on the four hundred and twelfth attempt in a row, and that number meant nothing to anyone but me.

My hands already knew before my eyes confirmed it: the resonance through the mold when a pour set correctly ran different from a pour that hadn't, a hum through the fingers, not a sound so much as a certainty, and four hundred and twelve times now the hum had been right. I lifted the panel free, ran my palm along the seam anyway, because checking wasn't about doubt, it was about being present for the moment the work finished becoming itself. Eridian hands read a cooling pour the way I understood humans read each other's faces, some information arriving faster than words could carry it, already known before it had a name.

"You ever get bored?" The human doing quality intake, young, still new enough to make conversation instead of just logging numbers, watched me set the panel in the queue rack.

I considered the question honestly, the way I considered all human questions, since most of them turned out to be worth more than they first sounded. "Bored is when hands have nothing to do. Mine have something to do."

"Sure, but four hundred of the same panel..."

"Four hundred and twelve." Not a correction. A fact, offered because facts were worth offering accurately. "Each one is not the same panel. Each one is this panel, made now, by these hands, out of this xenonite, and then it is done, and it goes there..." I nodded at the queue rack, at the hull section slowly assembling itself three bays over out of pieces exactly like the one I'd just set down, "...and becomes part of something none of us could make alone. Load-bearing has to be xenonite, every piece of it, or it isn't load-bearing at all. That part where it stops being mine and starts being the ship's, that part is good. I don't get tired of the part that's good."

The human didn't have anything ready for that, which was fine. Not everything needed an answer. I was already reaching for the next mold, checking the pour temperature against the back of my own wrist out of habit before the gauge had even finished settling, a redundant check, the gauge was always right, but redundant didn't mean pointless.

He watched me work three more, silent this time, and on the sixth one his handheld chirped a reading and he actually laughed, short and surprised. "Four hundred eighteen. Still true. You want to know something? Tolerance spec on these is a hundred microns and you're running inside forty. Every single one."

"Why do you know that it is four hundred eighteen?" I asked, genuinely curious rather than making a point of it. "Not the tolerance. The number itself. You have said a specific count out loud four times now since I began working, and each time it was correct, and each time you looked almost surprised to be saying it, as if the counting itself were the interesting part instead of the panel."

He blinked, like the question had come from an angle he hadn't braced for. "It's just... the job. You count. Every unit off a line gets a number, and the number's how you catch a drift before it becomes a defect. If panel two hundred was clean and panel three hundred's drifting half a micron off, the number's the only way anybody ever notices before it's too late to matter."

"But you are not comparing panel four hundred eighteen to panel four hundred seventeen with your own hands. You are comparing a number to a number." I turned the panel in the light again, unable to fully shake the strangeness of it. "My people have made things with our hands for longer than either of us can easily count. We do not count the things we make. We know them, each one, the way I know this one, and if a piece is wrong, the hands know it is wrong without needing to first know which piece it was in order among all the others. I am trying to understand what the counting gives you that the knowing does not already provide."

"Distance, maybe." He said it slowly, like he was arriving at the answer for the first time himself, not reciting something he already had ready. "Your hands know when something's wrong because your hands were there. Mine weren't, half the time, not really, not the way yours are. I'm watching four different lines today. I can't be inside every single pour the way you're inside this one. The count's how I stay honest about something I can't actually feel for myself. It's not better than what you've got. It's what you do instead, when you don't get to have what you've got."

I considered that for longer than the panel in my hands strictly required. "That is not a smaller way of caring. It is only a different one."

"Yeah." He seemed almost relieved to hear it put that way. "Nobody's ever said it back to me like that before."

It made sense to me now, all the way through, the way most human things eventually did once somebody was willing to actually explain the shape of the need underneath them instead of just the habit. I still knew the feel, not the number, would probably always know the feel first. But I understood, finally, that the number wasn't a lesser thing standing in for what I had. It was the only thing available to a man who couldn't stand inside four hundred pours at once the way my own hands could stand inside exactly one.

"I know the feel," I said. "The number is just what the number is once someone measures it." I held the seventh panel a moment before setting it in the queue, turning it once under the light, not for inspection, just to look at it the way I'd look at anything finished and good. "Humans keep asking if the work is enough. If we need more than the work. I don't understand why it wouldn't be. The ship needs to be true. My hands can make it true. That the ship will fly to a place none of us have seen, that people I love will stand on this exact panel someday light-years from here, that's not separate from the work. That's what the work is for. Being seen doing it was never the part that mattered."

"Where I'm from," he said, slower now, like he was working it out as he said it, "somebody doing this exact motion four hundred times would've quit around panel thirty. Or they'd be phoning it in by panel two hundred. You're not phoning anything in."

"Phoning it in requires wanting to be somewhere else while your hands are here. I don't want to be somewhere else. This is already the place." I set the panel down, careful, precise, the same care I'd given the first one and the four hundred and eleventh. "You keep looking for the moment I get tired of it. I keep not finding that moment. Maybe it doesn't exist for me the way it exists for you. Or maybe you haven't yet found the thing that doesn't tire you either, and you're measuring my hands against your own experience of everything else you've ever had to do four hundred times."

He wrote something down, and I didn't ask what, because it wasn't my work to know, and went back to mine.

Panel four hundred nineteen came off the line true. So did four hundred twenty, and four hundred twenty-one, and somewhere in the low four hundred forties, long after the human had gone quiet and simply watched, I realized I'd stopped counting out loud and started counting only because the number itself had become a kind of company, a private tally that meant something only to the hands doing the work and nobody else, which was, I decided, exactly how it should be.
