Chapter 23 of 53

Cent (POV): Not every trip through this family's own courier system carries a crisis. Some of them just carry a fistful of thumb drives and a very good idea.

The White House hallway was busy the way it always seemed to be busy, staffers moving in every direction, each one genuinely believing their own errand was the most important thing currently happening in the building. I found Leo mid-stride, coffee in one hand, a folder in the other, and fell into step beside him without slowing either of us down.

"OK. Give me just a sec to drop these off..." I held out a fistful of thumb drives, the unglamorous reality of most Pony Express runs, "...and we'll be ready to rock."

He took them on reflex, the way you take anything handed to you by someone moving with that much confidence. "Ready to rock for what?"

"Also, heads up, I'm about to take my eighty-something grandmother past the edge of the solar system for the first time in human history. Wanted somebody to know, in case anyone asks where she went."

Leo's hand froze halfway to setting the drives down.

"You're going to what." Not even a question by the end of it. "Does the President know you people just..."

I was already gone before his second sentence finished leaving his mouth.

Behind me, I heard the beginnings of what I understood, secondhand and much later, turned into genuine pandemonium. Leo starting for the Oval, doubling back for the comms desk instead, abandoning that plan too the instant it hit him that there was exactly one faster route available. "Josh! Josh! Donna, where the hell is Josh? I need CJ!" Doors opening. Someone dropping a stack of press releases. Donna and Josh converging from opposite directions, CJ arriving last, already irritated on principle before anyone had even told her why.

Leo didn't wait for any of them to catch up before he reached the one person who actually needed to hear it first. No knock.

"Sir. You're going to want to sit down for this one."

The whole gang followed him in anyway, too curious and too rattled by whatever had Leo running to just wait in the hallway to be told secondhand.

Bartlett was at the window when they came in, not the desk, staring out at the grounds the way he did when a thought hadn't finished settling yet, hands loosely clasped behind his back. He didn't turn around right away, even at the sound of that many feet behind him.

"Sir. You're going to want to be sitting down for this one."

That got him to turn. "Leo. You've brought an audience this time."

"They followed me, Sir. I didn't invite anybody."

He didn't turn around to confirm it, just accepted it as a fact of the universe he'd long since stopped fighting. "All right." He crossed back to his desk, didn't sit, just rested a hand on the back of the chair. "Let's hear it."

"Cent Rice just told me, in passing, on her way somewhere else, that she is currently in the process of taking her eighty-something-year-old grandmother past the edge of the solar system. For no operational reason. As a personal favor."

It broke the room open all at once.

Toby didn't so much speak as detonate, pacing a tight furious line in front of the Resolute desk like the carpet personally owed him an explanation. "Is that girl insane? She can't just... people don't just... there are laws, there are precedents, there is a reason we have a process..." his hands doing half the arguing his mouth didn't have time for.

CJ talked straight over him without missing a beat, long since immune. "We gotta get this on a release before somebody else does," already reaching for a notepad.

Sam, over both of them: "Mr. President, I'll start drafting..."

Nobody actually finished a sentence. Josh tried to ask a question nobody answered. Donna told everybody at once to just wait a second. Toby was still going, quieter now only because he'd run out of air, not out of outrage.

Bartlett let it run a moment before cutting through it, quiet rather than loud, the kind of quiet that had learned exactly how much room it needed to take up a whole office.

"Is the grandmother aware this is happening?"

The room stopped like someone had pulled a plug. Even Toby.

"I don't know." Leo, into the sudden silence, hands spread slightly, genuinely at a loss. "She dropped that on me and disappeared before I could get a question out."

"Naturally." A real laugh, the kind that had been building since long before this particular Tuesday. "CJ, I don't suppose there's a press posture for 'a private citizen briefly left the solar system and nobody signed off on it.'"

"There is not, Mr. President." CJ, dry as anything, notepad already forgotten. "I'd love five minutes' warning next time, is all I'm asking."

"I don't think warning's really how this family operates." Bartlett, still smiling, looking for a moment less like a president absorbing a security concern and more like a man genuinely delighted by the world he'd found himself governing. "Leo. Find out if she made it back safe, and then let's not make this into more than it actually was. A woman had an adventure. Good for her."

*

I found my grandmother mid-jog on Kristen Station's inner hull, exactly where I'd expected to find her, tacky-soled shoes gripping the loop she'd walked and run for years now, ever since a health scare that had, apparently, convinced her that the smartest thing an old woman could do with whatever time she had left was move through it as fast as her own legs allowed.

"Cent!"

She slowed, winded, hands on her knees for a second before straightening out of sheer stubbornness, the same stubbornness I recognized in my own mother, and in myself, deep enough now that I understood it hadn't started with either of us.

"Grandma. How would you like to be the oldest person to ever leave the solar system?"

"Give an old lady a chance to catch her breath before a question like that!" Already slowing to a walk, eyes sharpening despite herself. "You know I've been in space more than most astronauts at this point. What makes this one special?"

"Distance. Not the getting-there part. You've got that part beat already."

A long look, weighing it slow and sure, then something young breaking loose in her face. "Well. It's about time somebody asked me to do something interesting."

The jump took us somewhere with no hull, no loop, no borrowed spin, and she didn't need one, having long since made peace with weightlessness as just another kind of ground. Earth hung there ahead of us, small enough to cover with one thumb.

"Well," she said, quiet, turning slowly in place to take in every direction at once. "I suppose you could say I've never felt lighter on my feet."

I laughed, and didn't let go of her hand the whole way back.

*

I was still thinking about it three jumps later, back on the hull, watching Osei run the frame's daily transfer window like she'd run a hundred before it, and it hit me sideways, the way the good questions always did, nothing to do with whatever I'd actually been looking at when it arrived.

I'd just jumped Grandma home. Before that, the White House. Before that, this exact hull, cruising at whatever fraction of light we were up to by now, deep enough into the crossing that Earth and Wayfarer's own clocks had to have stopped agreeing with each other days ago, if Reach's own numbers meant anything at all. And none of it had ever once felt strange. No lag. No catching up. I just arrived, every time, present tense, wherever I'd aimed myself, the same courtesy the jump had always extended crossing a latitude: no residual speed, no fight with whatever frame I was leaving or landing in.

"Osei." I said it before I'd fully finished the thought, which was usually a sign the thought was worth saying out loud instead of finishing first. "Wait. Shouldn't there be a dilation effect when we're on different clocks?"

She didn't look up from the console right away, but her hands slowed, which from Osei meant the question had actually landed somewhere. "Come again?"

"I've been jumping back and forth between here and Earth all day. We're moving fast enough now that Wayfarer's own proper time and Earth's have to be diverging, same as they always do on a real interstellar crossing. But I never feel it. I don't arrive early, or late, or off by some number of hours I have to account for. I just arrive." I turned to look at the frame itself, still holding its own quiet connection open a few feet away, Osei's daily transfer window running same as it had for months. "It's the same thing that keeps a jump from flinging you sideways crossing a latitude. The mechanic just... reconciles it. Every time. I've never once had to think about it, which is probably exactly why I never thought about it until right now."

"You're saying the same trick that fixes momentum might be fixing time too."

"I'm saying I don't actually know where the line is. Momentum, I understand, more or less. Nobody's ever explained the rest of it to me, because nobody's ever had a reason to ask." I looked back at the frame, and for the first time in months of watching it work, actually felt the size of the question underneath it. "That thing's been held open for hours at a stretch, for months, connecting a point on a ship that's been quietly outrunning its own reference frame this whole time to a point that hasn't moved an inch. If it were just punching a hole between two clocks that disagreed with each other, we'd know by now. Something would have come through wrong."

Osei was quiet for a moment, turning that over with the same care she gave any number that didn't close cleanly. "Nothing's ever come through wrong."

"Nothing's ever come through wrong," I agreed. "So either we've been lucky in a way that should terrify both of us, or the frame's doing something a lot stranger than moving cargo. I think it's coupling the two ends together while it's open. Not bridging them. Locking them, for as long as the connection holds, so there's never actually a gap for anything to notice." I found I couldn't quite explain why I believed that, only that I did, the same unexamined certainty that had always told me how a jump would land before I'd ever worked out the math for it. "Reach never asked this question. We had reason to, plenty of times, and it just never came up. I don't know if that means it never mattered before, or if it just took someone finally holding a connection open long enough for the question to have anywhere to land."

"That's either the most reassuring thing you've said to me all week," Osei said, "or the least, and I genuinely can't tell which."

"Yeah." I looked back out at Earth, small enough now to lose behind one raised thumb, and felt the old familiar shape of not knowing settle in next to the newer, stranger shape of finally knowing what I didn't know. "Welcome to the club."
