Chapter 13 of 53

Davy (POV): I've spent most of my life asking forgiveness instead of permission. Old habits, I was learning, didn't die just because you'd finally seen what they cost.

I found Sterling in his office two days after I'd walked out of Bonneville with a verbal yes from a woman I hadn't cleared with anyone, a job that didn't officially exist yet, and a growing, uncomfortable awareness that "give me a couple days" had, somewhere between the tarp and my own front door, quietly turned into "I already did it."

"You look like a man about to confess something," Sterling said, before I'd even sat down.

"I already hired somebody for the Bonneville fieldwork position."

He set his pen down slow, which from Sterling I'd learned to read as the opposite of casual. "The position we haven't actually created yet."

"That one."

"Talk me through it."

I did, start to finish. Wanda Chappell, thirty years of desert fieldwork across four continents, already on-site at Bonneville working somebody else's contract when I found her, already three steps ahead of Osei's own team on a data anomaly nobody else had caught yet. I told him about the drift in the laser return, the crust-resonance theory, the fact that she'd clocked an error signature in hardware she'd never even been briefed on. I did not, at first, mention that I'd offered her the job before I'd said a single word to him about it.

Sterling let me get all the way through the technical case before he asked the actual question. "Did you clear this with anyone first?"

"No."

"Why not?"

I thought about dressing it up as urgency, and decided he'd earned better than that from me by now. "Because six weeks ago you stood in that conference room and told us the whole point of doing this right was that nobody makes the call alone anymore. And then I found the exact person we needed sitting right there in front of me, and some old part of me just went and did it anyway. Same as I always have. I didn't even think about asking first until I was already halfway back to my own truck."

He didn't say anything for a moment, and I made myself sit in the silence instead of filling it.

"That's the part I actually needed to hear," he said finally. "Not the résumé. The part where you tell me you know exactly what you did, and why it isn't the version of this program we're building." He leaned back. "Here's what happens now. I run her background, same as I'd run anybody's, because that part doesn't get skipped for anybody, family recruit or not. Assuming it comes back clean, which from what you've described I expect it will, she's hired, retroactive to the day you offered her the job. I'm not interested in punishing good judgment about who to hire, only bad judgment about how you got there." He held up a finger before I could relax. "And you're going to sit through exactly one conversation with the review board about process, because they need to hear it from you directly, not secondhand from me. Not a punishment. A correction. There's a difference, and I need you to actually feel it."

"That's fair."

"It's more than fair. It's what you'd have wanted for anybody else on this team who skipped the same step, and you know it." Something almost like a smile crossed his face. "For what it's worth, Davy, the instinct that found her wasn't the problem. It's the same instinct that's made every good call this family's ever made, going back to Reach. I'm not trying to train that out of you. I'm trying to teach you to run it through one more room before it lands as a decision instead of a headline. That's all."

I nodded, something loosening in my chest that I hadn't quite noticed I was still carrying. "She's bringing an assistant. Kid's been out there with her three postings running. Might as well clear him too, save us both a second version of this conversation later."

Sterling almost laughed. "Noted. Send me his file today." He was already reaching for his phone, already moving on to the next thing on a list that never seemed to get any shorter. "And Davy. Next time, four minutes on a phone call before you do the thing, not after. That's genuinely all I'm asking for."

"Four minutes. Got it."

It wasn't much of a rule, as rules went. It was, I thought, walking back out into the Florida sun, exactly the right size for exactly the amount of change any of us could reasonably make at once.
