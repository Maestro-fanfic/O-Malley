Chapter 8 of 53

Truman (POV): We didn't need a new capability. We needed to stop pretending we didn't already have the old one available to us.

Sterling called the meeting for eight in the morning, two days after Gray walked out of the Oval Office, which for backing that had gone from "space agency program" to Joint Chiefs-level in under forty-eight hours, felt almost aggressively ordinary. That was the thing nobody warned you about a presidential edict with the full weight of the Joint Chiefs behind it: it didn't take weeks to stand something up. It took a phone call, and then everyone simply showed up. Conference room, coffee, a whiteboard nobody had erased from whatever meeting had happened in this room the day before. He waited until all five of us were seated before he said anything.

"I want to be clear about something," he said, "before we go one step further with any of this. What almost happened to Millie wasn't a failure of your capability. Your capability's fine. Better than fine. It's a failure of process, and process is the single easiest problem in this entire situation to actually fix, because NASA's had the answer sitting on a shelf for sixty years."

"Testing discipline," Cent said. Flat. Not a question at all, and Sterling caught it, the certainty of someone who'd already lived through this exact conversation once, years ago, in a conference room in Houston with a sling on her arm.

"Testing discipline. Safety margins. Abort protocols. Independent verification before anybody signs off on a live run." Sterling ticked them off on his fingers like items on a grocery list, plain and unglamorous, mostly for the rest of us this time, since Cent already had the whole list memorized. "None of that's new. None of it requires inventing anything. It requires the five of you actually using it, instead of five people making the call by themselves in real time because that's what you'd always done and nobody had ever told you there was a better way."

I felt that land somewhere uncomfortable, because he wasn't wrong, and because some part of me had known he wasn't wrong for longer than I wanted to admit. We'd built this whole program the way you build something in a garage, instinct and iteration and hoping the math held, and it had held, right up until it very nearly hadn't.

"So what does that actually look like," Davy asked. "In practice."

"In practice, it looks like every test from here forward getting reviewed by an actual engineering team before anybody so much as suits up. It looks like abort criteria written down in advance, agreed on by everyone in the room, not decided in the moment by whoever's closest to a problem when it happens. It looks like margins. Real ones. Not 'we think this will probably work,' but 'we've run this scenario forty different ways and here's the actual envelope we're staying inside of.'" Sterling looked at each of us in turn. "It looks like slower. I won't pretend otherwise. It's going to feel slower than what you're used to, and some of you are going to hate that, at least at first."

"I don't hate it," Millie said, quiet. Everyone looked at her. She hadn't said much since that second test, not about the test itself, and I think all of us had been quietly waiting to hear what she actually thought instead of just how she was holding up. "I almost died because eleven milliseconds went wrong and nobody caught it in time to stop it. I'll take slower."

Nobody argued with her.

"All right." Sterling turned to the whiteboard, already sketching out the shape of what came next, real structure finally replacing the informal scaffolding we'd been running on for two years. "Then let's build this the way it should've been built from the beginning." He underlined one word twice, hard enough that the marker squeaked. Synchronization. "That's where we start. Not margins, not abort criteria, not any of the rest of it yet. Eleven milliseconds is what put Millie in a tumble with no anchor point, and eleven milliseconds is what broke that hull apart before it. Every single problem we had was a timing problem before it was anything else. We fix the count before we fix anything downstream of it, or we're just building better safety nets under the same bad math." He capped the marker, already turning something over. "A verbal count's never going to close that gap all the way, not past a certain mass. Human reaction time's got a floor to it we can't train our way under. If we actually want zero, we're going to need something that doesn't route through a voice at all. I've got people who've worked biofeedback triggers for exactly this kind of problem."

"No."

Everyone looked at Davy. He hadn't meant to say it out loud, I could tell from his own face the second it landed, but it was already out there, flat and immediate.

"Something that doesn't route through a voice," he said again, slower this time, like he was making himself hear it back. "Something in my own body deciding for me, without me. I've done that before. It didn't end well the first time."

Sterling didn't flinch, but he did stop moving, marker held still against the board. "This isn't that."

"You don't know that yet. Neither do I." Davy's jaw was tight. "I need to actually understand what it is before I let anybody wire something into my own reflexes again."

"That's fair," Sterling said, quiet, no defensiveness in it at all. "Nobody's building anything without you in the room for every part of it. You'll see exactly what it does before it ever does it to you."

Davy didn't answer right away. When he did, it wasn't a yes, not yet, just enough to keep the door open. "I want to meet the engineer first. Before anything gets built."

"Done." Sterling was already reaching for his phone. "They start with you tomorrow morning. Everything else waits until this is solved."

I watched him work, marker moving fast and sure across a board that had, until about six weeks ago, never needed to hold anything more complicated than a budget line item, and felt a knot I hadn't realized I was still carrying finally come loose. I watched Davy too, out of the corner of my eye, and didn't feel the same relief yet on his behalf. That one, I understood, was going to take longer to untie.

We weren't a family running an experiment out of our own backyard anymore. We hadn't been for a while, if I was honest with myself. We'd just been slow to admit it, the way people are always slow to admit the exact moment a thing they built alone stopped belonging only to them.

"One more thing," Sterling said, not turning around. "Whatever we do from here, we do it in front of everyone. Cameras running, doors open." He paused half a second, correcting himself before anyone else could. "Doors open, that part's not new, that's been true since before I was back in this family's life. The cameras are what's new, the same live standard you all built back at Reach. I'm not interested in running this quietly just because it's now official. Quiet is how we almost lost Millie. Open is how we don't do that again."

Cent caught my eye across the table, and I saw the same thing in her face I felt in my own chest: relief, mostly, and underneath it, something that felt almost like coming home to a version of this work we should have been doing from the start.
