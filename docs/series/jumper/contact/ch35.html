Chapter 35 of 53

Daniel (POV): Twenty years of arguing that the math was there if anyone bothered to look had not prepared me for the vertigo of finally being the one who found it.

The main gate had been fully cracked for weeks by then, the phage-warning segment translated, verified, delivered, its worst weight already carried home on Millie's own shoulders. What was left was the unglamorous part nobody outside this work ever pictures: the archive kept talking long after the part anyone needed had already been said, and somebody had to keep listening anyway, because you never know in advance which paragraph was the one worth the whole excavation.

Carter found me still at it, three consoles deep into a stretch of the record that read less like warning and more like bookkeeping.

"You're supposed to be off shift."

"I know." I didn't look up. "This isn't the warning. I already gave you the warning. This is just... numbers. Migration data. How the thing actually moves once it's loose, star to star, over time." I turned the display toward her anyway, because explaining it out loud was the only way I'd ever learned to trust my own math. "Photophage isn't a weapon somebody aimed. It's alive, in the most basic sense that word has. It breeds. It travels, the way anything alive travels: toward breeding ground, away from predators. A Petrova line isn't a signature something left behind on purpose. It's just what a trail looks like, from far enough away, once something's been feeding long enough to leave one."

"You said that part already. The warning covered that."

"The warning covered that it happened once, to them." I pulled the second chart up, the one I'd built and rebuilt four times over the last two days because I kept not trusting my own arithmetic. "This is what happens after. Once it's loose, with nothing keeping it in check, it doesn't stop at one star. It keeps doing exactly what it already knows how to do, migrating, breeding, migrating again, for as long as there's anywhere left to go and nothing standing in the way." I tapped the radius on the display, a soft, spreading bloom of light centered on a point neither of us had a name for yet. "Run that outward across the kind of time this record's already told us it's had. It doesn't stay local. It reaches every star inside a few hundred light-years. All of them. Not maybe. Not some of them if the timing worked out unluckily. All of them, the same way water reaches every low place in a basin, given enough time and nothing to dam it."

Carter went very still, the stillness of someone doing the same arithmetic herself, fast, checking whether she wanted it to be wrong.

"Earth's inside that radius."

"Earth's inside that radius." I said it as gently as I knew how to say a sentence like that, which wasn't very. "Erid should be too, if the math holds the way I think it does. I haven't confirmed that part yet. I don't have anything like clean access to Erid's own deep genetic reference data, not the kind you'd need to actually go looking for a marker this old. That's not a gap I can close alone, not with anything sitting in this archive."

"You need someone who can read it the way you're reading ours."

"I need someone who can read it at all." I rubbed at my eyes, the fatigue of a man who'd been right about a math problem for twenty years and still somehow never learned to enjoy the moment it finally paid off. "If this is real, it isn't two worlds that got unlucky in isolation from each other. It's one seeding event, one root, with however many branches actually took, scattered across however much of the sky falls inside that circle. But I can't say 'we're two things that grew out of the same spill' with any honesty until somebody who actually knows Erid's own genetic record confirms it from their own side, not mine."

"That's why the Watchers exist." She said it slowly, working it out loud the same way I had two nights ago, alone, at this same console, at an hour I wasn't proud of. "Not because somebody's guarding two specific planets. Because somebody built a whole network to watch an entire region, because the region is the actual size of the problem."

"An entire seeded region. Every star that ever ended up inside that basin, whether anything ever grew there or not." I sat back, feeling the exhaustion of a man who'd spent his whole career defending the idea that the math would eventually matter, now finding out exactly how much it mattered, and how little comfort that turned out to be. "There's something else. I don't have it locked down yet, so don't hold me to it. Earth has the jump trait, and Earth's already a confirmed branch of this root. Erid has the jump trait too, obviously, that part's not in question. Whether Erid's branch and this root are actually the same root, that's the part I haven't confirmed yet. Erid may have both, the trait and this exact root underneath it. I just can't say that with any honesty until I've actually checked."

"That's not nothing. It's also not proof of anything." Carter said it the way she said most things she wasn't ready to let herself believe yet, careful, holding the door only halfway open. "Two data points isn't a pattern, Daniel. It's a coincidence with good posture. And phage itself has never once done anything that looks like jumping. Not once, not anywhere in this whole record. You're asking me to believe a trait rides in an organism that's never actually demonstrated it."

"You're right, and that's exactly the part that's been bothering me for two nights. If it were a gene, that objection would kill it outright." I pulled up something else instead, an old file I hadn't expected to need again this soon. "So it isn't a gene. Think about Joe. Think about Truman. Neither one has a drop of blood relation to Davy, and both of them ended up jump-capable anyway, off nothing but sustained exposure to somebody who already was. That's not how heredity works, Carter. A gene doesn't rub off on you from standing near somebody long enough. We already had proof, before today, that whatever this actually is, it isn't simple inheritance passing down a bloodline."

"You're saying it's not in our DNA."

"I'm saying that's the wrong question to even be asking, and I only just realized why on the walk over here." I made myself slow down, because this was the part I actually needed her to follow, not just accept. "It's not phage versus our own DNA. Phage already is our own DNA, at the root of it, and if I'm right, it's the root of Erid's too. There's no version of 'evolved independently' available to us here, Carter, not if that root holds. So the real question isn't why phage instead of us. It's whether the trait is something that sat there once, near the root, before our two branches ever split off and went their own way, or whether it's something each branch reinvented separately, twice over, after we'd already stopped being able to share anything with each other at all."

"Convergent evolution versus common descent." Carter said it slowly, testing the shape of it. "Same problem biologists have always had sorting out wings."

"Same problem. Except we actually have a way to test this one that most convergent-evolution questions never get." I leaned forward. "If both lineages independently reinvented the same capability after they'd already split, the actual underlying mechanism should come out different each time. Different solutions to the same problem, dressed up to look similar from the outside, same as a bird's wing and a bat's wing solve the same problem with completely different bones underneath. But this trait doesn't behave like that. It spreads sideways. Exposure alone woke it in Joe, in Truman, zero blood relation to Davy, nothing shared between them except proximity to somebody already carrying it. You cannot catch somebody else's independently-evolved mechanism just by standing near them long enough. That only works if the underlying thing is literally the same thing in both people, not two separate answers that happen to look alike." I sat back. "That's not proof it traces to phage instead of reinventing itself twice, once on each world. But it's real evidence against the twice-over version, and I don't have anything pointing the other way yet."

"That's still a hypothesis. Not a finding."

"I know. That's exactly why I'm not calling it locked." I held up both hands, half surrender, half plea for patience. "But two branches off the same root, both independently ending up with the same trait phage itself never shows, is at least worth asking the question out loud instead of filing it under coincidence and moving on. If it turns out to be three for three instead of two, that stops being posture and starts being an actual answer. I just don't have a third data point yet."

"Find one," Carter said. "Before you say this to anyone else."

"I intend to." I meant it. It was, if nothing else, a question I finally had a real reason to go looking for an answer to. "And I need to close the Erid gap first, properly, before any of the rest of this is worth saying out loud to anyone. I know who to ask."

"Who."

"Rocky." I was already thinking through how to actually phrase the question in a way that wouldn't sound insane the first time I said it out loud. "If anyone's going to know whether Erid's own history has room for something like this in it, legend or record or anything in between, it's him."

She didn't say anything else for a long moment after that, looking at the slow bloom of light on the display the way you look at a thing that's technically just data and doesn't feel like just data at all.

"Does the record say why it stayed contained here." She nodded at the translated warning, still sitting open in the adjacent window. "Whatever 'here' actually was for them. Why didn't it spread the same way for the Builders that it's apparently ready to spread for everyone downstream of them?"

"I don't know yet." That much, at least, was true, and I let it stay exactly that plain instead of dressing it up. "There's more archive left. I haven't finished listening." I looked back at the bloom of light, the whole quiet, patient shape of it, centered on a point I still couldn't name. "I have a feeling I'm not going to like the answer, once I find it. But not liking an answer has never once been a reason not to go looking for it. That's sort of the whole job."

Carter was quiet a moment longer, then reached for the comm.

"Get this to Sterling," she said. "And Millie. Tonight, not tomorrow." A beat, and something in her voice softened, just slightly, the way it does for anyone who's just watched somebody else's entire world get quietly, permanently larger. "You did good work here, Daniel. I know it doesn't feel like the kind of good news anyone throws a party over."

"It isn't." I saved the file anyway, because saving the file was the part of the job that didn't require feeling anything about it yet. "But it's true. And apparently that's still worth something, even on the nights it doesn't feel like it."
