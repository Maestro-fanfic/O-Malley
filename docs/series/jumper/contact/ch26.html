Chapter 26 of 53

Cent (POV): Some kinds of homework you do for a grade. Some kinds you just do because you can't stop, decades before you'd ever guess it might matter this much.

I'd never been to Mr. Sagan's house before, but twenty-plus years of reading about him and his works told me exactly what the address was, the way a life spent following someone's writing leaves you knowing things about them you never set out to memorize on purpose. No shortcut needed, no records request, no favor called in.

I transitioned into a handy spot at Ithaca Tompkins International Airport, the kind of place a person just appearing counts for nothing more than one more traveler nobody bothered to watch come off a jet bridge, and went outside and grabbed the first taxi sitting on the stand, same as anybody else who'd actually flown in.

The dropoff was ordinary too, an ordinary curb on an ordinary street, and I made myself walk the length of the sidewalk instead of just materializing at the door the way some part of me badly wanted to skip straight to the hard part. I stood there a moment once I reached it, working up the nerve to actually knock on a door I had absolutely no earned right to be standing in front of. Then I knocked anyway. Some things don't get any easier to do just because you've thought about them for twenty years first.

*

Mrs. Sagan: Four years is long enough to stop flinching every time a knock lands on the door after dark. It is not long enough to stop missing the sound of his own key doing it instead.

I heard it a little after eight, ordinary enough that I was already halfway across the front hall before I'd even finished deciding to wonder about it, the television still going low behind me in the front room, some old clip of him I'd stopped really watching an hour ago and never quite gotten around to switching off.

I opened the door, and for one long second I just stood there, because the face on my own front step wasn't a stranger's face at all. It was a face I knew the way the whole country knew it, from four and a half years of screens, closer up now than it had ever once been, and close enough to actually be strange for exactly that reason.

"Good evening, Mrs. Sagan. My name is..." she started, already halfway into whatever careful, rehearsed sentence she'd clearly practiced the whole taxi ride over.

"I know who you are," I said, cutting her off before she could finish it, the words arriving before I'd fully decided to let them. There wasn't a person left in the country who didn't. "You're taller than the cameras make you look." Which was a foolish thing to say to the first person to actually cross a room and knock on my door in longer than I wanted to admit, but it was the only sentence that arrived after the first one.

Something in her face went soft at that, unguarded in a way I hadn't expected from someone who'd apparently flown four and a half light-years and back since breakfast. "I get that a lot."

I stepped back and held the door open wider, the way you'd hold it for someone you'd somehow, impossibly, already been expecting, and she followed me in past the television, past the old clip of him still playing itself out to an empty room, four years gone now and none the worse for it. Neither of us moved to touch it. I didn't apologize for it either. There didn't seem to be any point pretending, to a stranger who'd clearly already guessed, that I'd left it on for any reason other than the one she'd have guessed.

My daughter came in from the kitchen first, drawn by voices that didn't belong to either of us, and stopped dead in the doorway the same way I had. My son came in from the study a minute after that, reading glasses still pushed up into his hair, and didn't bother hiding his own version of the same look. Grown as both of them were, well into their forties now with lives and kitchens of their own, neither one looked any less like the two kids who used to come running at the sound of an unfamiliar voice in this house. Nobody needed introductions. That face didn't require them.

We sat together at our own kitchen table, all four of us, and she waited until everyone had actually settled before she said anything at all, which I noticed, and was grateful for, more than she probably knew.

"I want to tell you why I'm here before you have to guess at it," she said. "It's not bad news. I promise you that much before anything else."

"That's usually the first thing people say right before it turns out to be bad news," my son said, not unkindly, just tired the particular way anyone gets tired after enough years of bracing for the worst version of any unexpected visitor.

"Fair," she said. "Then I'll just say it." She folded her hands on the table, the way you do when you want your own hands to hold still for something. "We found something out past Alpha Centauri. Something built, older than anything we've ever found before it, and important enough that it's going to need a name people actually use for the rest of however long anyone remembers any of this. We picked one already. The crew agreed on it inside of a minute, no argument, no vote even needed." A beat, and I watched her decide to just say the rest of it plainly instead of working her way toward it. "But nobody wanted to make it official without coming here first. Without asking."

"Asking what," I said, though some smaller, quieter part of me had already started guessing, the way you guess at the ending of a story from two chapters out and don't quite let yourself believe it until the page actually says it.

"I've come to invite your input on the Station we've ridden," she said, "all the way under the flag of one of your husband's most renowned books."

I burst into tears before I'd found any way to stop myself.

I'm not sure I could explain, even now, which part of it did it, whether it was the plainness of the sentence itself, or the fact that a stranger had crossed a distance I couldn't actually hold in my head to ask a question she could have just as easily skipped, or simply four years of missing him arriving all at once in a kitchen that had gotten too quiet a long time before tonight. My daughter's hand found my shoulder before I'd even registered I needed it there. My son didn't say anything. He didn't need to. He just reached over and took my other hand, the way he used to when he was small enough that the gesture meant something entirely different.

"Take whatever time you need," she said, quiet, not rushing a single second of it. "I didn't come here for an answer tonight. I came here to ask the question in person, the way it deserved to be asked, and I'll sit at this table as long as that takes."

"You don't have to sit," I managed, once I trusted my own voice again, wiping my face with the back of one hand and not bothering to be embarrassed by it. "You already have your answer. You had it before you finished the sentence."

"I'd still like to hear you say it," she said, gentle about it, "if that's all right. Some things are worth actually saying out loud, even when everyone in the room already knows the shape of them."

"Yes," I said. "Of course. Yes." I looked at my daughter, at my son, and found the same answer already sitting in both their faces, the same unguarded, immediate yes I imagine she'd seen plenty of times before, in plenty of other kitchens, asking plenty of other families plenty of other impossible questions. "He'd have loved that. He'd have loved it so much he'd have made everyone in the room uncomfortable talking about it for an hour."

That got something close to a laugh out of her, real and a little startled, like she hadn't expected to laugh tonight and was glad to be wrong about that. "From what I've read of him, I believe that completely."

"There's one thing," my son said, before she could stand to go. "We don't want to just read about this afterward. Everyone else gets to watch this happen live. We'd like the same thing. To see it ourselves, whatever it actually looks like out there, in person and not secondhand."

"You never had to ask," she said, something almost fierce underneath how gently she said it. "That was always in the plans, no matter your decision tonight. Anything less would be an insult." A beat, softer. "I'll bring you out myself. However many of you want to go."

"All of us," my daughter said, quiet and certain.

"Then all of you," she said.

*

She kept that promise three days later, one arm around each of us in turn for the whole four-and-a-half-light-year length of it, the same unhurried courtesy I'd later learn she extended to anyone she carried that far. Nobody rushed us on the other end. The observation deck had been cleared out already, whether for our benefit or simply out of habit I never asked, and I was glad either way not to have to share the moment with anyone who hadn't earned a reason to be standing there.

We stood a long while just looking at it, dark and angular and larger than anything I'd let myself picture beforehand, whatever their instruments had actually named it before tonight already forgotten, already replaced by the one word that mattered. Nobody in that room said much of anything. There wasn't much that needed saying.

A man I hadn't met yet, Sterling, I'd learn later, read the designation off the plate himself once it was actually fixed to the hull, plain block letters, no ceremony beyond the letters themselves.

SAGAN STATION.

I touched the plate before anyone else did. Nobody suggested I shouldn't. It seemed, standing there with my own hand against cold metal four and a half light-years from my own kitchen table, like the only order any of it could have possibly gone in.

Sagan Station became official that same afternoon. I don't imagine anyone who stood on that deck ever called it anything else again. I know I never have.
