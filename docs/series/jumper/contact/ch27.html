Chapter 27 of 53

Millie (POV): A promise made over half a bottle of vodka is still a promise. I'd never once considered breaking this one.

The station coasted to a stop relative to the Object, close enough now to shuttle back and forth without needing the four of us for every trip, and before any other arrival business got underway, I went to make good on the one promise I'd made that actually mattered more to me than almost anything else on this whole mission.

I found him exactly where I expected to, Kristen Station's own physical therapy bay, mid-stretch, his back to the hatch, entirely unaware anyone had just arrived behind him.

"Hey, смерд," I said: son of a stinking one, the old Russian insult carrying all the affection it had always carried between us, ever since a school literature unit had taught me the word decades ago and I'd never once found a better use for it than exactly this. "Ready to blow this popsicle stand?"

He turned so fast he nearly lost his own balance, and his whole face did something too wide for a man his age to have any business managing, real, unguarded eagerness, the kind a man doesn't bother hiding from someone he trusts enough not to need to.

"I never smelled you like that, смерд," he said, turning the word's own literal root right back on me, and for a moment it was like no time at all had passed since that centrifuge trial, since the vodka, since a promise made on the edge of a pod bay that had felt, at the time, like the only thing either of us had left to offer each other.

"We made it," I said. "Just like we said we would."

"You made it." Something quieter under the joke now, real, one hand still braced against the wall like he needed the actual physical fact of me to finish landing before he trusted it. "You are standing right in front of me. I thought I would spend this whole conversation watching your face on a screen, four light-years delayed. I did not think you would actually spend the jump on me first."

"It's not nothing," I agreed. "You're the reason half this crew knows how to do their own jobs. That's still true from here. That's going to keep being true no matter how far out we get."

He was quiet a moment, and I let him have it.

"Go," he said finally, dry again, the moment passing the way he always let moments pass, gently, without lingering past their welcome. "Go find whatever you flew four and a half light-years to find. And Millie." A beat. "Thank you for not forgetting an old man's foolish request."

"There's a place for you on Sagan Station. There always has been. Once it's built out properly, once your own heart stops arguing with the rest of you about whether it's allowed to go anywhere interesting. I'm not asking Sterling. I'm telling him. You've earned a seat same as the rest of us, and I intend to see you sitting in it. The very миг it's ready for you, same as the promise we already made."

"Millie..."

"Not without you." I said it flat, the same certainty I'd have used to state any other fact that wasn't up for negotiation. "You don't get to sign off on this from a physical therapy bay for the rest of your life and call that the whole story. There's a version of this where you get out here too, once it's actually safe to bring you, and I am not giving that version up just because it's easier to leave it unsaid."

He didn't argue that one either.

*

We nearly missed what the record device actually was.

It sat mounted low along the Object's own interior, on the "wall" immediately across from, apparently, the only way in: unmissable from that angle, planted directly in the eyeline of anyone who ever stepped through, and yet unremarkable up close, blending into the surrounding hull in a way that felt, in hindsight, almost deliberate, a plain structure easy to mistake for nothing more than surface texture unless you happened to be looking exactly the right way at exactly the right moment. The advance survey team had been standing in front of it since the moment they first stepped inside, and still needed a second, closer look before anyone actually noticed the faint, patterned irregularity that didn't quite match anything else on the Object's own skin.

"We were staring right at it," one of the surveyors said afterward, half laughing at herself, "before any of us understood what we'd actually seen." The same story, almost word for word, that this family had told about the Object itself, all those years ago at the very start of everything. Some things, it seemed, insisted on repeating their own shape no matter how much time passed between one discovery and the next.
