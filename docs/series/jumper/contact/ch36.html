Chapter 36 of 53

Rocky (POV): There are stories every Eridian child learns before they learn much of anything else. I had not, until the day Daniel came to find me, ever once considered that a story might turn out to be a record instead.

He found me in the materials bay, hands full of a spectral analyzer I didn't actually need for the sample in front of me, the exact busywork I reached for whenever my own thoughts wanted somewhere useful to go instead of circling. Daniel had the particular careful posture of a human trying very hard not to appear as though he was about to ask something enormous.

"Rocky. Do you have a few minutes?"

"I have exactly as many minutes as this question turns out to need." I set the analyzer down, because whatever this was, it deserved my hands as much as my attention. "You have the appearance of a man who already knows the answer and is afraid of it anyway."

"I don't know the answer. That's the actual problem." He walked me through it, patient, careful, the same way he explained anything, watching my hands after each piece to make sure I was actually following instead of just being polite about it, the same way I'd have watched his if our positions were reversed. The archive. The seeding radius. Earth, confirmed, inside it. Erid, mathematically likely, unconfirmed, because he had no clean way to check Erid's own deep genetic record without an Eridian's help. And underneath all of it, the smaller, stranger question: whether the jump trait itself traced back to the same root, riding in whatever phage actually was, at a level deeper than any single bloodline.

I stood very still for longer than I usually allow myself to, turning the shape of it over.

"There is a story," I said finally. "Every Eridian child learns it. I learned it from my own mother, she from hers, back further than anyone still living can actually trace by name. I never once thought of it as anything but a story, the same way your own people keep stories about floods and fires that happened to nobody in particular, everybody in general."

"Tell me."

"It is called the ones who were already gone." I said the full title in Eridian first, most of it landing well inside the range his own ears could actually catch, but not all of it, a thin layer of the chording sitting just past the edge of what a human body was ever built to hear. Humans, I had learned early, ran narrow that way even by ordinary standards, narrower than plenty of their own creatures back home, nothing unusual about him missing a note or two of anything. The translator carried the full meaning across to him half a beat behind the way it always did, so automatic by now that neither of us gave it any more thought than breathing. He would hear most of what I'd just said, honestly, directly, with his own two ears. Just never quite the whole chord. I said it anyway. Some things still deserved being spoken in their own true form first, even for an audience who could only ever receive most of them, because the saying was mine to do properly, whether or not every last note of it ever reached him. "Long before anyone counted years the way we count them now, our own ancestors were prey. Something hunted us, patient and thorough, the way water finds every low place in a basin. And a very small number of us, so few the story never bothers giving a number at all, simply were not there when the hunting came. Not hiding. Not fighting. Already gone, someplace else, before the danger had finished arriving." I looked at my own hands. "Grandmother told it to me as a lesson about vigilance. Watch closely enough, she said, and you will always be somewhere else before it is too late to matter. I never once considered that she might have meant it literally."

Daniel didn't say anything for a moment, and I sensed him do the thing his own species did when a piece finally clicked somewhere behind the eyes, a stillness of a very different shape than my own.

"Predators," he said slowly. "Not by chance. The pattern you're describing, prey animals surviving by simply not being where the danger arrives, that's not a metaphor for vigilance. That's a population under real, sustained predation pressure, over however many generations it took before something in a few individuals just... started working differently."

"You are saying my grandmother's lesson was a report."

"I'm saying if this story is old enough, and if it's describing something real instead of just useful, then Erid didn't develop the trait as a comfortable inheritance sitting quietly in everyone's blood. It developed it because something was actively killing the Eridians who didn't have it, generation after generation, until the ones who survived to keep telling this story were disproportionately the ones who could already do what your grandmother's lesson describes." He was talking faster now, the particular fast of someone thinking out loud instead of performing certainty. "That's not just consistent with a shared root. That's a mechanism. Real selection pressure, not passive coincidence. It would explain why the trait shows up at all instead of just sitting there unused. Something made it worth having."

"You want to check this against the genetic record."

"I want to see if there's a marker sitting under Erid's own reference entry, the same one already sitting under Earth's. If your story and Earth's math land on the same place independently, that's not folklore backing up science. That's two completely different kinds of evidence pointing at the same root, which is a much harder thing to explain away as coincidence than either one on its own."

I considered this a long moment, feeling something unfamiliar settle into place, the vertigo of a childhood lesson quietly becoming a data point.

"I can get you access," I said. "The reference archive is not classified, only unused, because nobody until this moment has ever had a reason to actually examine it this way. Come. I will show you where our own dead have been keeping their own records the whole time, waiting for someone to finally ask them the right question."

We found the marker before either of us had finished sitting down properly at the console, sitting exactly where Daniel's own instinct had already told him it would be, older than either the human or the Builder entries, the same signature, the same root, unmistakable.

"Two for two," Daniel said quietly, once he was fully certain his own eyes weren't lying to him. "I still need a third branch out there somewhere, and I don't have it yet. But this." He gestured at the marker, at the story, at the whole shape of what we'd just confirmed together. "This isn't posture anymore, Rocky. This is real evidence."

"Then go tell Carter it is real evidence," I said. "And go find your third branch. I have a feeling, now, that you are not going to have to search nearly as hard for it as you think."
