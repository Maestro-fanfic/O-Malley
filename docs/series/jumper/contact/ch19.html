Chapter 19 of 53

Sket (POV): The alarm went off for the ninth time in the same four hours, and I still didn't understand why anyone was pretending it meant something new.

"Depressurization, section four," Childs called out, already moving, already reaching for the emergency seal kit bolted to the wall exactly where it had been bolted the last eight times. "Go."

I went, because going was the assignment, and reached the breach panel two full seconds before the nearest human, and had the seal deployed, unhurried, already knowing, on some level below language, exactly how the material would behave under pressure. Childs called time. My number came back good. It was always good. It had been good all nine times.

"Again," Childs said.

"Why." I didn't move yet, hands still on the seal kit, genuinely asking rather than resisting. "The breach is the same breach. My response is the same response. Repeating a thing that is already correct does not make it more correct."

Childs pulled her hair back out of her face, breathing harder than the drill should have earned from someone this many reps in. "For you, maybe. You people show up already calm. I've watched it. Something goes wrong, and it's like a switch, focus just drops onto you from somewhere, no ramp-up, no fight to get there."

"That is not special. That is only what a body does when a body is not spending half its effort convincing itself not to panic."

"Right, well, my body spends that half its effort every single time, and the only thing that's ever gotten it to spend less is doing the exact same drill so many times that my hands stop waiting for my brain to catch up. That's the whole secret. There isn't a switch. There's just enough repetition that panic doesn't get there first." She nodded at the panel. "You want to know why we do it nine times instead of once? Because on the real day, whatever the real day turns out to be, I don't get to choose whether I'm scared. I only get to choose whether scared gets there before my hands do."

I considered this with the frank attention that had, over four months of drills, come to actually mean something instead of just meaning polite tolerance. "You are describing training a thing that is broken to work anyway."

"Yeah. Kind of exactly that."

"That is either the saddest thing I have heard a human say, or the bravest, and I genuinely cannot tell which."

"Little of both, probably. Most useful things are." Childs wiped her palms on her thighs, one at a time, a habit I'd catalogued weeks ago as the tell she gave right before saying something she'd actually thought about instead of something she'd said a hundred times to a hundred other trainees. "Panic's not a defect, is the thing. It's not broken. It's a survival system that showed up a few hundred thousand years too early for the kind of problems we actually solve now. Great system if the emergency's a lion. Terrible system if the emergency's a checklist. So we don't get rid of it. Can't. We just teach the hands to finish the checklist before the lion-brain finishes arguing about whether there's actually a lion."

"You are training around a flaw instead of removing it."

"You can't remove it. Believe me, people have tried. Best you can do is get your hands moving faster than it can talk." She glanced at me, something almost wry in it. "You people don't have the argument in the first place. I'm a little jealous, not gonna lie. But jealous doesn't get the panel sealed. Repetition does."

"Again," Childs said, resetting the panel herself this time, hands moving through the motion with the flat, unbothered economy of someone who'd stopped noticing she was doing it. "Go."

I went. Two seconds ahead again. Good numbers, same as always. But this time, hand on the seal, some small curious part of me counted the humans arriving behind, felt their tension ease between rep six and rep nine, less strain, less of that thing Childs called half-effort, closer every time to whatever I'd never had to build in the first place.

Rep ten went wrong.

I don't know, even now, exactly why. The alarm sounded the same as the other nine times. My hands moved the same way they had moved the other nine times. But somewhere between the sound and the seal kit, a thought arrived that had never arrived before, small and unwelcome and entirely new: what if this time is different. Not fear, not the way Childs meant it, nothing my body recognized enough to call by that name. Just a half-second of hesitation, my hand hovering a fraction too long over the kit before actually closing around it, and that half-second was enough. I reached the panel three full seconds behind where I should have been. The seal went on crooked, had to be repositioned, cost another two seconds correcting it that should never have needed correcting at all.

Childs called time. I already knew, before she said it, that the number wasn't good.

"Five point one," she said, flat, reading it off her own handheld without any particular judgment in her voice, which somehow made it worse. "First bad number all day."

I stood there with my hand still on the crooked seal, feeling something I didn't have an immediate word for, not in my own language and not in hers, a strange hollow unsteadiness that had nothing to do with the actual physical task and everything to do with the half-second that had come before it. "I do not understand what happened."

"Welcome to the club." Childs didn't say it unkindly. If anything, there was something almost gentle underneath the flatness, the tone of someone recognizing a landmark she'd passed herself a long time ago. "That's the thing nobody can drill into you from the outside. You just found out what it feels like when the lion-brain gets there first, even for half a second, even when you didn't ask it to. Congratulations. You're a little more broken than you were this morning, in exactly the useful way."

"That does not sound like a congratulations."

"It is one. I promise." She reset the panel again, unhurried this time, giving me a beat longer than usual before she called it. "You want to know the actual secret now? It's not that the next one's going to be perfect again. It's that you're about to find out whether you can move your hands anyway, with the hesitation still sitting right there behind them, instead of waiting for it to go away first. It never fully goes away. You just stop waiting on it."

"Go," she said, and I went.

The hesitation was still there, I could feel it, a half-formed shadow of the same thought, what if this time, rising up before my hand had even fully closed on the seal kit. But this time I recognized its shape before it finished forming, and moved through it instead of around it, the way I imagined Childs had described her own hands doing a thousand times before mine ever had to learn it once. The seal went on clean. Two seconds ahead, same number as every rep before the bad one.

It did not feel the same as the eight good numbers that had come before it.

Those eight had been simple, uncomplicated, my body doing the only thing it had ever known how to do, no more remarkable to me than breathing. This one had cost something. I had felt the wrongness rising and moved anyway, hands finishing the job before the small unwelcome thought could finish making its case, and the resulting seal, identical in every measurable way to the eight that came before it, sat under my palm like something I had actually built instead of something that had simply always been true about me.

"There it is," Childs said, quiet, watching my face with the same attention she'd have given a readout.

"That felt different." I turned the seal panel slightly under my hand, the way I might have turned a finished xenonite panel, checking it not for correctness but simply to look at it. "The number is the same number. It does not feel like the same accomplishment."

"No," Childs agreed. "It never does. That's the whole reason we keep doing this the hard way instead of just handing everybody a good number and calling it a day."

By rep twelve, the youngest of them beat me to the panel by half a second.

Childs didn't call time on that one. Just watched my face do something it hadn't done in eleven reps.

"Amaze amaze amaze!" The words came out before I'd decided to say them, the same triple-stack every Eridian reached for when a single amaze wasn't going to cover what needed covering. "I did not expect that. I did not expect that at all."

"There it is," Childs said, not unkindly.

"You want to know what we call that in the corps? The moment training actually earns the name." She clapped me once on the shoulder, easy, like it was nothing, like it hadn't just quietly rearranged something. "Congratulations. You just got outdrilled by a rookie. Welcome to the only club where that's actually good news."

"...I would like to run it again."

"Yeah," Childs said, grinning now. "There it is."
