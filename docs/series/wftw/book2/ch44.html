Chapter 44

I spent the following two days in our converted spare bedroom, watching Willow and Tony work through financial records with a thoroughness that made my earlier assumption — that this kind of investigation happened in dramatic, quick breakthroughs — feel naive.

"Okay, here's lead one," Tony said, the first afternoon, pulling up a spreadsheet dense enough that my eyes actually hurt looking at it. "Shell corporation registered eight months ago, funding pattern roughly matching the profile from the first facility. Ownership traces back to a holding company in Delaware, which traces back to a second holding company, which traces back to—"

"Let me guess. Another holding company."

"You're getting good at this already." He didn't look up. "Delaware's basically designed for exactly this kind of layered obscurity."

Willow, cross-referencing something on her own laptop, made a small, frustrated sound. "This one dead-ends, though. The Delaware entity's been dormant for three years. No activity, no connection to anything current."

"So that's lead one, gone." I felt something deflate slightly.

"That's how this works, mostly," Tony said, not unkindly. "You run down ten leads to find the one that actually goes somewhere."

We moved to lead two, then three, both dead ends within the hour.

"This is actually tedious," I admitted, somewhere around the fourth hour.

"Welcome to actual investigative work," Willow said. "It's not like the movies. It's mostly just careful, patient elimination, until the thing that's actually real finally stands out from everything that isn't."

By the second day, we'd worked through eleven leads, all dead ends, and I found myself, watching Tony's usual boundless energy visibly flagging for the first time since I'd met him, worried we might not actually find anything useful at all.

"I hate to say this," he admitted, late that second evening, "but I think we might be looking in the wrong direction entirely. Every lead assumes the funding structure mirrors the first facility's pattern. What if it doesn't? What if whoever's actually in charge learned from that exposure and restructured everything specifically to avoid the same signature?"

"That's an unsettling possibility," Willow said.

"Also probably accurate, honestly, given everything we know about how patient this organization is." Tony rubbed his eyes. "Think we need a different approach. Not financial structure. Something else entirely."

That stayed with me a moment, and I found myself, unexpectedly, thinking about my own recent work with Cory — the way he'd taught me to approach an engineering problem from multiple angles simultaneously, rather than fixating on the first plausible approach.

"What if we're thinking about this too narrowly," I said, slowly. "We keep looking at money. What about communication patterns instead? The analyst had to actually talk to someone, right, to report what he found. Even if the money's clean, restructured, whatever — the communication itself has to have left some kind of trace."

Tony went very still, something sharpening in his expression. "That's actually a good idea. We've been so focused on the financial angle we haven't properly cross-referenced his known communication devices against the timeline."

"I can pull that," Willow said, already reaching for her own laptop. "SHIELD's got his phone records from the initial investigation. Never fully analyzed them against the broader timeline, though."

We worked through the night on that instead, and by early morning, exhausted but energized, we'd found something — a pattern of calls to a burner number, active only during specific windows, always brief, always followed by a period of complete radio silence lasting exactly six days.

"That's a schedule," I said, staring at the pattern. "That's not random. That's someone checking in on a fixed rotation."

"That," Tony said, something like true excitement finally breaking through his exhaustion, "is our actual lead."
