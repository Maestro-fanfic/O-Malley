Chapter 38

Clara found me alone near the settlement's eastern perimeter that same evening, having gone looking once she realized I'd slipped away from the coordination center's planning session.

"Thought I'd find you out here," she said, settling beside me on the low stone wall overlooking the defensive gardens Home's people had built into every available margin of space. "You okay?"

"Scared, honestly. Really scared." I turned to look at her, grateful for how easily she could tell when I needed the real answer. "Watching my mom face this — watching the whole possibility that everything Home's rebuilt might be threatened again — it's bringing back stuff I thought I'd already processed."

"That makes sense. You don't process something this size once and get to be fully done with it forever." Her hand found mine, steady and warm. "You're allowed to be scared again, Charlie. Doesn't mean you're not handling it. Just means it's still scary."

"I know. Logically, I know that." I leaned into her. "Just hard to actually feel it sometimes, underneath all the fear."

"That's what I'm here for, then. Reminding you." A gentle kiss to my temple. "Whatever happens, you're not facing it alone. I'm not going anywhere, Charlie. Not now, not when things get scary."

Something eased in my chest.

"Thank you," I said, quiet. "For coming out here instead of just waiting for me to come back on my own."

"Always." She squeezed my hand. "You'd do the same for me. You have, plenty of times."

We sat together a while, watching the settlement's evening rhythms continue — guards on patrol, gardens tended even amid crisis.

"Can I tell you something?" I said, eventually. "Something I haven't fully admitted to myself yet."

"Of course."

"Think part of why this is hitting me so hard is I finally understood, sometime this past year, watching everything with Xander's family and Cent's, that Home isn't as fragile as I always assumed growing up. That we've actually built something lasting. And now, facing this threat, some old part of me's terrified that assumption was wrong all along."

Clara considered that, real thoughtfulness in her face. "I don't think it was wrong. Think what you're watching is proof of the opposite — that Home's strong enough now to face a real threat with real allies, instead of facing it alone and unprepared, the way your mom's generation had to."

"That's a good way to think about it."

"Had practice, thinking through your specific brand of anxiety spirals." A grin, gentle teasing, real warmth underneath. "Comes with the territory of loving someone who processes fear by imagining every possible worst-case scenario in exhaustive detail."

"That's fair," I admitted, a real, tired laugh despite everything.

"You do. It's part of why I love you, though, weirdly. Means you actually take things seriously instead of just assuming everything works out fine without effort." She leaned into me fully. "We're gonna get through this. All of it. Together, the way we've gotten through everything else."

"Together," I agreed, meaning it, warmth and hard-won courage settling alongside the fear still present underneath.

We stayed until the evening deepened into full dark, and I finally headed back toward the coordination center with Clara's hand still in mine, considerably steadier than I'd felt in hours.

The breakthrough came two days into the investigation, and it came, unexpectedly, from Willow.

"Okay, so," she said, striding into the coordination center with her laptop clutched against her chest like a shield, "I think I found something, and I want to preface this by saying I might be completely wrong, but I also think I might actually be right, and either way I needed to tell somebody immediately instead of sitting on it."

"Go ahead," Natasha said, already giving her full attention.

"So the gate-crossing signature Ilsa's team traced — the improvised one, six hours after the breach — I've been cross-referencing it against every anomalous energy reading we've got on file, going back to the original Convergence data. There's a match. Not perfect, but close enough I don't think it's coincidence." Willow pulled up her screen, a comparison of two waveforms that looked, to my untrained eye, almost identical. "This pattern matches a location we already know about. The same abandoned agricultural facility Dad and Steve did recon on, weeks ago, before the raid."

The room went very subdued.

"He went back to the original facility," Steve said, working through it aloud. "Or somewhere connected to it. That's either remarkably stupid, or—"

"Or he doesn't know we already found it," Fury finished. "The original cell's leadership was captured before they could confirm the facility was compromised. If our missing analyst wasn't part of that initial cell's direct communication chain, he might honestly not know we already hit that location once."

"Which means he might be there right now," Faith said, already tensing.

"It's the strongest lead we've got," Natasha said. "Want eyes on that facility again, immediately, before we commit to anything larger."

"I'll go," Dad said, already standing. "Know the terrain. Can get eyes on it fast, same as last time."

"Not alone," Steve said, already moving to join him. "Same protocol as before."

I felt the old fear rise, watching my father prepare to walk back into exactly the kind of danger that had nearly cost us everything weeks earlier, and made myself trust the process this time.

"Be careful," I told him, before he left.

"Always," he said, and meant it, and I watched him and Steve disappear into the gathering dusk, my mother's hand finding mine.

They were back within the hour.

"He's there," Dad confirmed, breathless but steady. "Alone, from what we could tell — no additional personnel, no sign of the broader operation we feared. Working out of a single room, some improvised lab setup. Equipment looked technical. Consistent with someone trying to build something from stolen schematics."

"Alone changes the calculus considerably," Natasha said, already moving into planning mode. "Not the multi-person operation we faced before. One man, isolated, likely working against a deadline he's set for himself."

"Which means he's vulnerable," Fury said. "And also, potentially, more dangerous in a different way. Desperate men working alone sometimes make choices a well-organized cell wouldn't risk."

"What's the plan," I asked.

Fury weighed it. "Fast, decisive action. Don't give him time to complete whatever he's building, or call for reinforcements if he's got any contingency for that. In and out, minimal engagement, prioritizing capture over confrontation."

"I want to be part of this one," Faith said, already reaching for the hammer. "Wasn't there for the actual takedown last time in the way that mattered."

"You'll have your role," Natasha confirmed. "This time, given the scale's smaller, I want a tighter team. Steve, Faith, myself, Thor for backup capability."

"And me," I said, before anyone could argue.

The room went quiet, several pairs of eyes settling on me, my parents' among them.

"I can jump in fast, jump out fast, if extraction becomes necessary," I continued. "Got the same skill Dad has, just younger, less experienced, sure, but real. I've earned the right to actually use it for something that matters."

Mom and Dad exchanged a long look.

"Backup extraction only," Dad said, finally. "You don't engage directly. You're there specifically in case something goes wrong and we need a second, independent evacuation option. That's the condition."

"That's fair," I said, relief and fierce determination tangling together.

"Then it's decided," Natasha said, already moving toward the door. "We move within the hour."

I found Charlie before we left, his expression carrying a complicated mixture of pride and fear I recognized immediately.

"Come back," he said, simply.
"I will," I promised, and meant it, and turned to join the team assembling for what I honestly hoped would be the last confrontation this whole terrifying month required.

I jumped myself to the staging position half a mile from the facility, close enough to reach the team fast if extraction became necessary, far enough to stay clear of the actual engagement, and settled in to wait with the hard-won patience twenty years of watching Dad do exactly this had somehow finally taught me too.

The comm crackled to life after twenty minutes, Steve's voice carrying the same controlled calm I'd come to associate with him. "In position. Faith and Natasha approaching from the east, Thor and I from the west. No visible additional security since Davy's earlier observation."

Another ten minutes passed, tense and silent.

"Entry made. He's here, alone, exactly as observed. Attempting to establish contact rather than force confrontation."

I held my breath, the old fear and fierce hope tangling together.

"He's not responding to reason," Natasha's voice, tighter now. "Says he'll destroy everything before he lets us take him. Faith, hold position, don't engage yet."

Something in the tone made my stomach drop, and despite every instruction to stay clear, I found myself edging closer to the facility's perimeter, close enough to actually see the low, unremarkable building where my father's family's danger had, apparently, finally traced back to one desperate, isolated man.

The comm crackled again, urgent. "He's got some kind of device. Might be a weapon, might be something else — hard to tell. Thor, can you—"

A sound reached me even from this distance, something between an explosion and a tearing, wrenching wrongness that made my whole body go cold with recognition — the unmistakable sound of reality itself being forced somewhere it wasn't meant to go, the same fundamental wrongness I'd felt once before, at the edge of the Convergence crisis.

"He's triggered something," Steve's voice, urgent, cutting through static. "Some kind of makeshift gate mechanism, unstable, could be dangerous to everyone in the vicinity. Cent, if you can hear this, get clear, now."

I didn't get clear. I ran toward it instead, every careful instruction forgotten in the face of the same instinct that had, weeks earlier, carried me safely across a school parking lot — assess fast, act faster. This time I wasn't running from danger. I was running toward people who needed exactly what I could offer.

I arrived at the facility's edge to find chaos — a jagged, unstable tear in the air itself, smaller than the Convergence's own catastrophic breach but wrong in exactly the same fundamental way, energy bleeding out in visible, distorting waves. Faith stood near it, hammer raised, clearly trying to determine whether direct force would help or catastrophically worsen the situation. Thor and Steve had the analyst restrained, the man struggling weakly, some of the fight already gone out of him now that his desperate gambit had spiraled beyond his own control.

"It's destabilizing," Natasha shouted, over the growing wrongness of the sound. "Whatever he built, it's not holding. Need to shut it down before it—"

I didn't fully hear the end, my attention narrowing onto the tear itself, some instinct I couldn't fully name recognizing exactly what needed to happen.

"I can close it," I said, moving forward before anyone could stop me. "Felt this before. During the Convergence. I know what a stable boundary feels like, and I know what this feels like. Think I can help stabilize it, the same way we did back then."

"Cent, that's not confirmed, that's dangerous—" Faith started.

"I know. Doing it anyway." I reached the edge of the tear, close enough to feel its wrongness pulling at something fundamental in my chest. "Faith. Your hammer. I think that's part of what worked last time — not just force, but worthiness meeting the boundary directly. Don't know if I can do this alone."

Faith didn't hesitate, moving beside me immediately, hammer raised, and I reached out — not physically, but with whatever strange, inherited sense my own ability actually operated on, feeling for the tear's actual structure the way I'd felt for a thousand ordinary jump-points across a hundred different worlds.

"Now," I said, and together, Faith's hammer meeting the boundary's edge and my own instinctive sense of its shape working in concert, we pushed.

The tear resisted, fighting back with a wrongness that made my whole body ache, and for a terrifying moment I believed we might not manage it — then, slowly, agonizingly, it began to close, folding in on itself until, finally, with a sound like a held breath released, it sealed shut entirely.

I stumbled backward, exhausted in a way that went far deeper than ordinary physical tiredness, and found Faith's arm catching me before I fully fell.

"You did it," she said, something like awe in her voice. "Cent, you actually did it."

"We did it," I corrected, breathless. "Couldn't have managed it alone. Same as you couldn't have managed the Convergence alone, probably."

Steve and Thor secured the analyst fully, his resistance entirely spent, and Natasha moved through the aftermath with quick, efficient assessment, confirming no lasting damage beyond the immediate structural mess of the facility itself.

"Threat neutralized," she said, into her comm. "Analyst in custody. Cent and Faith managed to stabilize and close an improvised gate breach before it could escalate further. Want full medical assessment on both of them the second we're back."

I sat down heavily on the facility's cracked concrete steps, the adrenaline draining out all at once, and felt, in the sudden quiet aftermath, proud — not just of surviving, but of contributing something real, alongside people who'd trusted me enough to let me try.

Dad found me there minutes later, having jumped in the second word reached him, and pulled me into a hug fierce enough to actually hurt.

"You weren't supposed to engage directly," he said, into my hair, voice carrying equal parts relief and residual fear.

"I know. I'm sorry. Saw an opportunity to help, and I took it." I pulled back enough to look at him properly. "I'm okay, Dad. Better than okay, actually. Did something real today. Something that mattered."

He studied me a long moment, something shifting in his expression — the same complicated mixture of fear and fierce pride, except this time a lot more settled, more proud than afraid.

"You did," he said, finally. "You really did. I'm proud of you, Cent. More than I think I've properly said yet."

"You've said it plenty," I told him, leaning into him despite my exhaustion. "I heard every single time. Just wanted to actually earn it too, this time. Not just survive something scary. Actually help fix it."
"You more than earned it," he said, and held on, and together, exhausted but whole, we waited for the rest of the team to finish securing the site before finally, gratefully, heading home.

40

We returned to Home to a reception far warmer than I'd expected, word of the analyst's capture having spread through the settlement faster than our own arrival, and found Osei waiting at the coordination center with an expression that carried hard-won relief.

"Confirmed," she said, once we'd relayed the full account. "The immediate threat's resolved. We've begun a full review of everything he might have accessed, and preliminary analysis suggests the schematics he stole were far less complete than we initially feared. Building anything functional from what he actually took would have required years, not weeks."

"That's good news," Fury said, some of his own tension visibly easing. "Doesn't mean the wider threat's fully gone, but Home's immediate exposure was less severe than the worst-case scenario."

"I understand your organization's wider concerns remain," Osei said, careful. "I want to discuss what an ongoing relationship between Home and Earth's defense structures might actually look like going forward. Not full integration — don't believe either of our civilizations is ready for that. But something more than the isolated caution we've maintained until now."

"I'd welcome that conversation," Fury said. "Whenever you're ready."

Miriam found Mom and me shortly after, pulling us both into a fierce hug.

"Thank you," she said, to me specifically. "What you did — closing that breach, alongside Faith — don't think you fully understand yet what that meant, to all of us. A visitor, choosing to risk herself for a civilization she's only just properly visited. That's not nothing, Cent. That's everything, to people who've spent decades learning exactly how rarely outsiders extend that kind of care."

"Charlie's basically family at this point," I said, a little embarrassed by the weight of her gratitude. "Wasn't really a hard choice, once it came down to it."

"Perhaps not for you. But it mattered enormously anyway." Something deeply moved in her face. "You're welcome here, always, exactly as you'd be welcome in your own home. All of you."

I spent most of that final day with Charlie and the rest of the Five, the six of us — counting myself now, folded properly into their group despite technically belonging to an entirely different reality.

"So," Joey said, at one point, "just to confirm I've got this straight. You helped close an actual tear in reality. With a magic hammer girl. That's a thing that happened, in my actual life, adjacent to me."

"That's accurate, yeah."

"I need you to understand I'm never going to stop bringing this up. This is my new permanent personality trait. 'Knows the girl who helped seal a reality tear.' Going on my future dating profile."

"That's deeply concerning, Joey," Marie said, though her voice carried real warmth underneath.

"Everything about me is deeply concerning, Marie, we've established this."

"We have," Rick agreed, quiet but fond.

Clara, ever practical, was already discussing with Charlie whether Wildside Investments should formally offer resources toward Home's rebuilding security infrastructure.

We said our goodbyes that evening, the whole assembled team gathering at the gate.

"Same time next month?" Charlie asked, echoing our earlier promise, though this time carrying far more weight.

"Same time next month," I confirmed, and meant it, stepping through the gate back toward home, exhausted, proud, grateful.

Dad found me on the porch that evening, both of us finally alone.

"How are you actually doing," he asked. "Not the version you give everyone else."

"Tired. Proud. A little scared, still, thinking about how close that could have gone wrong. But mostly settled, I think."

"That's a good way to put it." He was still a moment. "Keep thinking about the parallel. Me, twenty years ago, alone, terrified, nobody to help me survive Wyoming. You, this month, terrified too, but never actually alone."

"I got lucky. Having you and Mom as parents."

"Luck's part of it. But you also built it yourself, Cent. Every relationship — that's not just luck. That's you, choosing, over and over, to let people in instead of facing things alone."

"Learned it from watching you finally learn it," I said, echoing something I'd told him weeks earlier.

We sat together a while longer, the evening settling into comfortable, hard-won quiet.

Not alone. Never alone. Not as long as there was this much love willing to show up, again and again, for exactly as long as it took.
