Chapter 41

Osei began the meeting by setting a small, worn ledger on the table between them, and Fury, watching the gesture, seemed to understand immediately this wasn't merely procedural.

"This is the Council's own record," she said, "of every outside contact Home has made since the collapse. Thirty-one years of careful, deliberate isolation, broken only a handful of times, each weighed for decades before we allowed it. I want you to understand the weight of what I'm about to propose, Director, and that it isn't offered lightly."

"I understand that," Fury said, careful. "I'd be a poor negotiating partner if I didn't."

"Good." Osei opened the ledger to its final page, mostly blank. "I'm prepared to formally recommend an ongoing relationship between Home and your organization to the full Council. Not because this crisis frightened us into it — we've survived considerably worse without abandoning our own caution. Because your family, and Cent's family, demonstrated something we haven't observed from outsiders before. Genuine reciprocity. You didn't simply offer help and expect gratitude. You extended trust, and accepted the risk that came with it, the same way we'd expect from our own people."

"I appreciate that assessment. I want to be equally direct about what I can actually offer, though, given recent events." Real weight in his face. "My organization's had two confirmed breaches this past month. I won't propose full integration, or anything approaching it, until I've demonstrated we can actually secure what we're already responsible for."

"That's precisely the kind of honesty that earns the recommendation in the first place." Osei considered her next words. "I'd propose something incremental, then. Limited, verified information exchange, expanding only as trust is actually demonstrated. Quarterly review, both directions — either party may pause the arrangement if concerns arise, without that pause being treated as an act of bad faith."

"I can agree to that. I'd add one further condition." Fury leaned forward. "Given what happened to Samantha Rice, given the vulnerability that exposed — any joint technology, any shared research, gets reviewed for exactly that kind of exploitation risk before either side deploys it. Don't want to build this partnership on the same blind trust that got a tracking device implanted in an elderly woman's hip for two years without anyone noticing."

Osei's expression tightened, something old and furious crossing it despite her careful diplomatic composure. "That detail hadn't reached me yet, in full. I want to hear it properly, before we continue."

Fury relayed it carefully, and Millie watched Osei's expression shift through actual horror into something harder, more resolved.

"This is precisely the kind of threat Home has spent thirty-one years trying to prevent," Osei said, quiet. "Not the dramatic kind. The patient kind. The kind that waits years inside something trusted before it ever activates." Her hand had gone, without her seeming to notice, to the old, faded scar along her left forearm. "I lost my own mother to precisely that patience, thirty years ago. A supply contact, trusted for years, who turned out to have been reporting our settlement's location the entire time. I don't discuss it often. I mention it now because I want you to understand this isn't abstract policy for me. It's personal, every single time it comes up again."

"I'm sorry," Millie said, still.

"Thank you. It was a long time ago, and grief that old mostly just sits quietly now, most days." Osei closed the ledger with careful finality, her hand finally leaving the scar. "I want that condition included formally. Any shared technology undergoes exactly the review you've described, on both sides, without exception."

"Agreed."

They spent another two hours working through the actual mechanics — communication protocols, the exact scope of information Home was willing to share, the careful limits SHIELD would observe in return.

"I want to add something personal, before we conclude," Osei said, as the formal negotiation wound toward its close. "I watched your daughter, Millie, risk herself for a civilization she'd only just properly visited. I watched your husband use an ability he spent twenty years learning to fear, in service of protecting people who weren't his own family, not by blood. I don't extend the recommendation I'm about to make to the Council lightly. I extend it because your family has already, repeatedly, proven exactly the kind of trustworthiness this arrangement requires."

"Thank you," Millie said, moved. "That means more than I think you realize."

"I suspect it means exactly as much as I intend it to." Something almost like warmth crossed her careful face. "The Council will deliberate formally within the week. I don't anticipate significant objection. But I wanted the two of you to hear the recommendation directly, from me, before it becomes simply another item in a formal record."

They shook hands on it, an oddly ordinary gesture given the weight of what it represented.
