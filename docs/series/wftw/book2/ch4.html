Chapter 4

Charlie invited me to an actual Five gathering — Six, now, though the name hadn't caught on yet — three weeks before anything with the gray sedan started. I walked through the gate nervous about what he'd called "just a regular hangout, nothing special."

Regular hangout meant Rick's family barn, converted into something between a workshop and a living room. String lights strung crooked across the rafters. A couch that had clearly survived at least two other households before landing here.

"Cent!" Joey called out the second I stepped through the door, already crossing the barn. "The Sixth Member arrives. This calls for ceremony."

"You said you weren't doing the ceremony thing, Joey," Marie called from a low table where she was setting up a board game.

"I said I was workshopping ideas. This is the workshop's conclusion."

"His workshop concluded on finger guns," Clara said, not looking up from whatever she was tinkering with. "Which, honestly, might be the least embarrassing thing he's proposed. I'm allowing it."

"High praise," Rick said, appearing from the back with an armful of snacks. "Hey, Cent."

"I'd have picked you up myself if my license situation weren't currently revoked," Joey added, unbothered. "Long story. Involves a DWI and a mailbox that had it coming."

"The mailbox did not have it coming," Marie said, still not looking up. "You've told that story four different ways and the mailbox is blameless in every one."

"Details."

I sat down on the couch next to Charlie.

"So what's the plan tonight?"

"Board games, mostly," Marie said. "Movie after, depending how competitive this gets. Fair warning — Joey takes board games unreasonably seriously."

"I take winning seriously. Different thing."

"Not really," Rick said, already settling into the game.

"At least Marie's got an excuse for not driving Joey around," Charlie said, grinning. "Girl's got a pilot's license and no interest in a regular one. Some of us just carry the mailbox forever."

"I have priorities," Marie said. "Planes don't hit mailboxes."

Two hours of it — Joey's trash talk getting more elaborate by the round, Marie's sharp strategy, Clara's careful plans occasionally wrecked by one of Rick's quiet moves nobody saw coming until it was too late.

"Didn't expect Rick to be this good," I said, watching him take a win without changing expression.

"Nobody expects it," Charlie said. "That's the whole strategy. Let everyone underestimate him, then quietly take the whole board apart while they're arguing about something else."

"Solid strategy," Rick said, unbothered.

We put a movie on eventually, everyone crowded onto the couch and whatever chairs had survived, Joey narrating the entire thing whether anyone asked him to or not.

"Can I ask something," I said, during a lull. "What's it actually like, growing up here? Not the crisis version. Just the regular days."

"Honestly? Pretty ordinary, most of the time," Marie said. "School, friends, normal teenage stuff. The collapse-recovery thing's more like background noise most days."

"Except the resource drills," Joey added. "Those I think about constantly. Mostly because they're boring and I resent every one."

"They're important, Joey."

"They're important and boring. Both true."

Clara, practical as always: "I think it shaped how all of us think about the future. We don't take resources for granted the way I hear kids on your side sometimes do. Every choice feels a little more deliberate, given what our parents' generation lost."

"Sounds like a lot to carry."

"Sometimes. Mostly it just feels like how things are." She shrugged. "Normal's whatever you grew up with, I think. For anybody."

We finished the movie well past midnight. Nobody in a hurry to end it.

Charlie walked me back to the gate.

"Thanks for coming," he said. "I know it's a lot, still, crossing over for something this ordinary."

"It's not a lot. It's exactly what I wanted more of."

"Same time next week?"

"Same time next week," I said, and meant it.
