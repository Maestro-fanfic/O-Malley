Chapter 20

Grandma Samantha called on a Sunday, bright the way she always was on the phone, though Mom had learned over the years to listen underneath it. I was doing homework at the table, close enough to catch her side of the call.

"How are you feeling?" Mom asked, watching Dad through the window working on something in the yard.

"Oh, fine, fine. Hip's still a bother, but the therapist says I'm making good progress." A pause, something more honest slipping through. "Harder days than others, if I'm honest. The hardware from the surgery aches worse in cold weather than the original break ever did. Doctors are talking seriously about the low-gravity option again — my case is apparently exactly what their research points toward."

"Which kind of day is today?"

Samantha sighed, dropping the performance. "A harder one. Getting old is an undignified business, Millicent. Don't recommend it."

"I wish I could help more directly."

"You help plenty. These calls matter more than you realize." Her voice warmed. "How's my granddaughter? Still finding remarkable things in that ability of yours?"

"All three of us are, actually. Cent especially. Found a whole new dimension to it I never even suspected, after decades watching Davy use it."

"Sounds like Cent. Never content with how something works when there's more underneath." Samantha's laugh carried real warmth. "Gets that from you, you know. Your father used to say the same about your own curiosity."

Mom went quiet a moment, something warm and a little sad crossing her.

"I miss him," she said.

"So do I. Every day." Samantha's voice carried real grief alongside real peace with it. "But I think he'd be proud, watching what this family's become."

They talked a while longer. Hanging up, Mom looked grateful.

She told Dad about it that evening, both of them on the porch.

"She sounded tired," Mom said. "More than she let on."

"Want to visit? In person."

"I think I do. Bring Cent too. Been too long since they've had real time together."

"I'd like that. She's always been good for Cent."

They planned it over the following days. Once Mom told me, I felt genuinely excited.

"I want to ask her about Grandpa," I told Mom, the day before we left. "What he was actually like. Not just the stories I've heard a hundred times."

"I think she'd love that. Nobody asks about him directly anymore. People assume old grief doesn't need tending."

"That seems wrong."

"It is, I think. Grief doesn't have an expiration date. Just changes shape." Mom carried her own weight, saying it. "I think your grandmother would like someone actually asking."

We visited that weekend. I spent hours with Grandma Samantha hearing stories about my grandfather I'd never heard — his dry humor, his patient love for her across decades, the way he'd doted on Mom as a kid.

"You remind me of him, sometimes," Grandma Samantha told me, near the end, both of us on her porch. "That same curiosity. Same refusal to accept a surface answer when there's more underneath."

"I like that."

"You should. It's a wonderful inheritance, whatever else you've got from your parents' side of things." She squeezed my hand. "I'm glad you asked about him, Cent. It's good, keeping someone alive in memory instead of letting the details fade."

We left with real warmth settled in my chest.
