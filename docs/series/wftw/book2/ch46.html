Chapter 46

The burner number itself led nowhere directly — disposable, purchased with cash, no registration trail worth pursuing — but the tower data told a different story entirely, and Natasha joined us the following morning once Tony flagged the pattern as promising.

"Six-day rotation," she said, studying the map Willow had built, cell tower pings marked in careful chronological sequence. "That's a courier pattern, not a direct communication link. Whoever's actually running this doesn't trust electronic communication for anything sensitive. Using a physical intermediary, meeting at fixed intervals, probably somewhere considered secure enough to risk direct contact."

"Can we narrow down the actual meeting location?" I asked.

"Maybe. Tower triangulation's not perfect, but if the pattern's consistent enough, we should be able to narrow it to a reasonable search radius." Natasha considered the data further. "Willow, can you cross-reference the tower pings against the actual physical geography? Want candidate locations, not just coordinates."

Willow spent the next several hours doing exactly that.

"Okay, this is interesting," she said, finally, pulling up a satellite image. "There's a rural property here, changed ownership four times in the past decade, always through entities that don't quite make sense for what's actually on the land — registered as agricultural, but essentially no farming activity visible in any of the satellite imagery going back years."

"That's exactly the kind of quiet, unremarkable footprint this organization favors," Natasha said, studying the image. "Doesn't announce itself. Just sits there, patient, doing whatever it's actually doing behind a plausible cover."

"Could be nothing," Tony said, though something in his voice suggested he didn't believe that. "Could also be exactly what we've been looking for this whole time."

We spent another full day verifying the lead properly before committing to anything larger.

"I want to be honest," Natasha said, once the verification work concluded, gathering the whole team. "This is considerably better than any lead we've had so far. But I've also watched leads this promising turn out to be nothing, and I don't want anyone walking into this assuming it's confirmed until we've actually got eyes on it."

"What's the actual scale, though," Steve asked, studying the satellite imagery.

"Bigger. Considerably bigger, based on the property size alone, and the utility usage pattern suggests real, sustained operational activity rather than periodic use." Grim, focused. "If this is what we think it is, we're not looking at another operational cell. We're looking at something closer to regional command."

The room went subdued.

"Then we need eyes on it properly," Fury said, arriving partway through. "Same protocol as before. Careful reconnaissance first."

I looked around the room — at the exhausted, determined faces of people who'd spent two full days on tedious, unglamorous work before finding anything worth actually pursuing.

"We did that," I said, mostly to myself, though Willow caught it and smiled, tired but real.

"We really did," she agreed. "Eleven dead ends, and then the one that actually mattered."

Dad found me afterward, both of us finally stepping outside for air. "You did good work in there. That idea about communication patterns instead of financial structure — that's the thing that actually cracked this open."

"Learned it from Cory, honestly. Approaching a problem from multiple angles instead of exhausting one approach completely first."

"Good lesson to have learned properly." He considered the sky a long moment. "Feels like we might actually be getting somewhere real, this time."

"Yeah," I agreed. "Yeah, I think we might."

47

I didn't sleep properly for three days after the briefing, the old fear resurfacing in ways I thought I'd fully processed weeks earlier, and finally admitted as much to Willow during one of our now-regular video calls.

"I thought I was past this part," I told her, curled up on my bed with my knees pulled to my chest. "The whole scared, jumpy, can't-sleep-properly part. I thought I'd actually processed it, moved through it, come out the other side steadier. And now it's just back. Like none of that progress actually mattered."

"It mattered," Willow said, firm despite the gentleness. "Progress isn't a straight line, Cent. Setbacks don't erase what you've already built. They're just proof the situation actually got harder again, not proof you got weaker."

"It doesn't feel that way at three in the morning."

"No. I imagine it really doesn't." Willow's expression softened. "For what it's worth, I don't think there's anything wrong with being scared again, given the actual threat did get scarier. That's not regression. That's just accurate emotional response to accurate new information."

I turned that over. "Keep thinking about Charlie. About Home. About how this might not even really be about my family anymore, not primarily — how we might have just been the opening move in something considerably bigger."

"That's a heavy thing to carry."

"Yeah." I was quiet a moment. "I want to help, though. Properly, not just sitting on the sidelines being scared. Proved I could actually contribute, back at Home, sealing that tear. Don't want to lose that, just because the fear's come back sharper."

"You won't lose it. You'll just have to do the hard, unglamorous work of being scared and still showing up anyway, same as your dad's had to do his whole life."

I managed a small, tired smile. "Learned it from watching him, I guess. Full circle."

Dad found me in the kitchen the next morning, both up earlier than usual.

"Couldn't sleep either," he said, more observation than question.

"No. Been having a hard few nights, honestly. Talked to Willow about it last night. Helped some."

"I'm glad you talked to someone. That matters." He studied me. "You know it's okay, right? To be scared again, even after everything. Doesn't undo the progress you've made."

"That's basically what Willow said too."

"Good advice tends to repeat itself, apparently." A tired smile. "For what it's worth, I've been having a hard few nights too. Old instincts resurfacing. Think that's just honest. This really did get scarier again."

We sat together in comfortable, tired low-key, before his phone buzzed with a message that shifted his expression immediately from tired to alert.

"Natasha," he said, already reading. "They've got something. A lead on the internal breach — traced through financial records connected to whoever paid off certain personnel inside SHIELD's holding facility. Wants everyone at the Magic Box within the hour."

The old, familiar adrenaline surged through me.

"I'm coming," I said, already standing.

"I know," Dad said, and this time, there wasn't even a flicker of hesitation in his agreement.

We jumped straight to the Magic Box, arriving to find the whole team already gathering.

"Financial trail leads to a shell corporation," Fury said, no preamble. "Different structure than the last one — more sophisticated, more carefully insulated. But the same underlying pattern Tony flagged weeks ago. Connected, definitively, to the same broader network."

"Does it lead anywhere specific," Steve asked.

"It does." Grim, focused. "A facility. Considerably more significant than the one we hit before. Based on the resource allocation we're tracing, I believe this might actually be closer to the organization's regional command structure."

The room went very still.

"That's a bigger target," Natasha said, closely.

"It is. Which means bigger risk, and considerably bigger potential reward, if we handle it properly." Fury looked around the room. "I want to be honest — this isn't going to be like the last operation. This target's better defended, better prepared, and likely aware, given the analyst's death, that we're actively hunting them now."

"So what do we actually do," I asked.

Fury's gaze settled on me. "We prepare a lot more carefully than last time, and go in with everything we've got. This is likely our best chance at actually ending this rather than facing another partial victory that just delays the next crisis."

I looked around the room — at my parents, at Faith and Thor and Steve and Natasha, at the whole assembled, fierce, determined family that had grown out of crisis into something permanent.

"Then let's actually finish this," I said, and meant it completely.

The preparation for this operation carried a different weight than the first one had. Millie watched the whole assembled team move through planning with a grim thoroughness that had replaced the hopeful urgency of a few weeks before.

"I want full medical contingencies this time," she told Natasha, during a break in the tactical planning. "If this facility's a regional command structure, we should assume more resistance, more potential injuries. Want to be prepared for that, not just hoping for the best."

"Agreed. I'd value your input on the medical staging plan directly, if you're willing." Real respect in Natasha's face. "You've proven, this past month, you're a good deal more than just support staff in these situations."

"I intend to keep proving that," Millie said, and meant it.

She spent the following two days deep in preparation alongside the rest of the team — reviewing medical protocols with Steve, coordinating extraction logistics with Davy, working through worst-case scenarios with the same care she'd have brought to any hard case in her own practice.

She found Davy in the workshop late one evening, sorting through gear with focused attention.

"How are you actually holding up?" she asked, settling beside him.

"Scared. Determined. Both, same as always, apparently." He set down the item he'd been examining. "Keep thinking about Cent, though. How much this is asking of her again, so soon after everything already cost her so much."

"She wants to be part of it. You know that."

"I know. Doesn't make it easier, watching her walk toward danger again, even knowing she's earned the right to make that choice herself." A parent's fear, complicated by real, hard-won respect for his daughter's own agency. "That's the hardest part of all this, honestly. Not the danger itself. Watching someone I'd protect with my entire life choose, deliberately, to face danger anyway, because she's decided it matters more than her own safety."

"That's called courage, Davy. The real kind, not the reckless kind. She's not running toward danger blindly. She's choosing it, with full understanding of what it might cost, because she's decided the people and places she loves are worth the risk."

"I know. I'm proud of her for it. Doesn't make the fear any smaller."

Millie reached for his hand. "It's not supposed to make the fear smaller. It's supposed to make it bearable, alongside real pride and real trust in who she's become."

They sat together a while, before Davy spoke again, something more vulnerable than he usually allowed himself.

"Keep thinking about what happens if this goes wrong. If we lose someone, this time. I don't know if I could carry that, Millie. Not the way I've learned to carry everything else."

"You wouldn't carry it alone, if it happened. None of us would." She held his gaze, honest even about the hardest possibility. "Won't pretend the risk isn't real. It is. This is more dangerous than anything we've faced so far. But we're facing it about as prepared as any group of people possibly could be."

He nodded slowly, something settling. "You're right. Doesn't make it easy, though."

"It's not supposed to be easy. If it were easy, it wouldn't take this much courage from everyone involved."

By the time the operation's start time arrived, Millie stood beside her husband and daughter in the pre-dawn quiet, fiercely proud of the family they'd built.

"Ready?" Natasha asked, gathering the team for final deployment.

"Ready," Millie confirmed, taking Davy's hand on one side, Cent's on the other.

49

I jumped Steve and myself to the staging position an hour before the operation began, both confirming, one final time, that the facility matched everything our intelligence had suggested — far larger than the first, better fortified, personnel numbers that made the earlier operation look almost modest by comparison.

"This is bigger than anything I anticipated," Steve said, low, scanning the facility through binoculars. "Reinforced structure, multiple defensive positions, personnel rotation suggesting honest military-grade training rather than just professional security."

"Regional command, like Fury suspected."

"Looks that way. Which means this isn't just an operational cell anymore. This is infrastructure they've invested real, sustained resources into protecting." He lowered the binoculars, grim. "Want to be honest with you, Davy — this is a lot more dangerous than what we faced before."

"I understand. I'm going in anyway."

"I know. I respect that. I just needed to say it plainly." He met my eyes directly. "Whatever happens today, your family, and everyone connected to this fight, has already accomplished something remarkable."

The full team assembled an hour later, far larger than our first operation — Steve leading the primary assault alongside Natasha and Thor, Faith and a dedicated SHIELD tactical unit handling the secondary breach point, Cent and myself staged for extraction and support, Bruce present this time too, a quiet presence carrying its own weight of contingency neither of us discussed directly.

"Final confirmation," Natasha said, addressing the assembled team with grim clarity. "This operation carries real risk beyond what we've faced so far. I want everyone here by choice, fully understanding what we're walking into. Anyone who needs to step back, now's the time."

Nobody moved.

"Then let's finish this," she said, and the operation began.

I felt it start through the comm relay, tense updates as the team moved into position. Waiting at the staging point with Cent beside me, I gripped her hand tight enough that I probably should have eased off.

"Entry team in position," Steve's voice, controlled despite the tension underneath. "Beginning breach."

The following minutes stretched impossibly long, fragmented updates coming through in short bursts — resistance encountered, more organized than the first facility had offered, Thor's voice occasionally cutting through with confirmation of threats neutralized, Faith's secondary team reporting steady progress toward the facility's actual command center.

Then everything changed.

"We've got a problem," Natasha's voice, sharper now, real alarm cutting through her usual controlled delivery. "Command center's rigged. Some kind of failsafe, possibly designed to destroy evidence, possibly worse. Bruce, I need your assessment, now."

Bruce's voice, tense but steady, cut in. "Based on what you're describing, this could be catastrophic if triggered. Need eyes on it directly before I can assess containment options."

"Then get here. Fast."

Old fear surged sharp and immediate, watching Bruce move toward the comm relay with grim purpose.

"Cent," I said, subdued, urgent. "Whatever happens next, you stay here. Full extraction ready, but you don't engage unless I specifically call for it. This is different from Home. Considerably more dangerous, less controlled."

"Dad—"

"Please." I met her eyes directly, all the fierce protective love I carried for her fully visible. "This one, I need you to trust my judgment. Not because I doubt yours. Because I've got more direct experience reading how bad this specific kind of danger can get."

She held my gaze a long moment before finally, reluctantly, nodding. "Okay. I trust you. But the second you need me, I'm there. No hesitation."

"Understood."

The comm crackled again, Bruce's voice, more strained now. "Confirmed. This is a real failsafe device, more sophisticated than standard demolition charges. If triggered, it could destabilize local reality structure — similar in principle to what we saw during the improvised gate incident, but larger in scale."

The full weight of that landed hard and cold in my chest.

"Can you disarm it," Steve asked, urgent.

"Working on it. Need time, and everyone clear of the immediate area, given the uncertainty involved."

"You've got maybe ten minutes before facility personnel regroup enough to interfere," Natasha said. "I'll hold the perimeter. Work fast."

I stood at the staging point, every instinct screaming at me to move closer, and forced myself to trust the plan.

"They can do this," Cent said, quiet, beside me. "They've got this."

"They've got this," I agreed, and held her hand tighter.
