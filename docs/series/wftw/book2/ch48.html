Chapter 48

Bruce reached the command center to find the device mounted directly into the facility's central support structure, and his first actual thought, studying it properly, was that whoever had built this had access to considerably more sophisticated theoretical physics than any HYDRA cell had any reasonable business possessing.

"Okay," he said, mostly to himself, running through the visible components with careful attention. "Core housing's standard enough — modified demolition charge casing, probably repurposed rather than custom-built. But this—" He traced a secondary component, smaller, more precisely machined. "—this isn't standard. This is doing something considerably more specific than just triggering an explosion."

"Talk to me, Bruce," Natasha's voice, tight, through the comm. "Need something actionable."

"Working on it. Give me a second to actually understand what I'm looking at before I start guessing." He examined the secondary component more closely, tracing its connections back toward the main charge. "There's a resonance chamber here. Small, but precisely tuned. If this is what I think it is, this isn't designed to simply destroy evidence through conventional explosion. Designed to trigger a localized boundary rupture, similar in principle to the improvised gate device, except contained and directional rather than expanding freely."

"In English, Bruce."

"In English — if this thing goes off, it's not going to level the building. It's going to tear a hole in reality itself, right here, and I don't know yet whether that hole closes cleanly or keeps growing." He forced his breathing steady. "Need to understand the trigger mechanism before I touch anything. If I disturb the wrong component first, I could trigger exactly the outcome I'm trying to prevent."

He traced the wiring methodically, building a mental map of the whole system before allowing himself to consider intervention. The resonance chamber connected to a timing mechanism, itself connected to a secondary failsafe — deliberate redundancy, the kind of engineering that suggested whoever designed this had specifically anticipated someone attempting exactly what he was currently doing.

"There's a secondary failsafe," he said. "If I disable the primary trigger without addressing this first, the secondary kicks in automatically."

"How do you get around that?"

"Need to disable both simultaneously, which means interrupting the resonance chamber's power supply at the exact same moment I sever the primary trigger's connection. Sequential won't work. Has to be simultaneous." Bruce studied the physical layout. "Need something to hold one connection steady while I work the other."

"I can do that," Faith's voice, having reached the command center herself, breathless. "Tell me exactly where and how hard."

Bruce hesitated only a moment. "The resonance chamber's power feed — this cable, the one with the blue housing. Need you to physically hold tension on it, exactly this much—" He demonstrated the pressure with his own fingers. "—no more, no less, the second I say go."

"Exactly this much tension. Got it." Faith's hands, remarkably steady, settled onto the cable with the same careful precision she'd have brought to holding a blade at exactly the right angle. "Ready when you are."

"On three. One—" Bruce positioned his own hands at the primary trigger's connection point. "Two—"

"Three," Faith said, and pulled, exactly the calibrated tension he'd specified, and in the same fractional instant Bruce severed the primary connection, both actions landing within a margin of milliseconds neither could have consciously calculated.

The device went dark, the resonance chamber's faint hum fading into honest silence, and Bruce felt his own held breath finally release all at once.

"Disarmed," he said, into the comm, considerably less steady than he'd have liked. "Confirmed safe. That was considerably closer than I'd have preferred, but it's resolved now."

"Bruce," Natasha's voice, something almost gentle underneath the professional relief. "Good work. Genuinely good work."

"Couldn't have done it alone," he said, glancing at Faith. "Needed a second set of exactly steady enough hands."

"Anytime," Faith said, something like real pride in the simple word. "Turns out slaying's got some transferable skills. Who knew."

The ten minutes stretched into what felt like a far longer stretch, Cent's hand tight in mine, both straining to hear every fragment of update coming through the comm relay.

"Progress," Bruce's voice, strained with concentration. "This is more complex than standard demolition rigging. Whoever built this understood more about interdimensional mechanics than I'd have expected from a purely military organization."

"Time estimate," Steve pressed.

"Don't have one. Need everyone to trust the process here, same as I'm trusting my own assessment under a lot of pressure."

I felt Cent's grip tighten, both of us holding our breath. In that suspended, terrifying stretch, I thought about everything this past month had taught our whole family — that real trust, extended even under real uncertainty, was the actual foundation everything else had been built on.

"Facility personnel regrouping," Natasha's voice, sharper now. "Need more time, Bruce, but I'm running low on ways to buy it."

"Understood. Working as fast as safety allows."

Another agonizing stretch of silence, broken only by occasional bursts of confirmed engagement from Natasha's perimeter defense, Thor's voice cutting through now and then with grim confirmation of threats neutralized.

Then, finally, Bruce's voice again, carrying something closer to relief. "Disarmed. Confirmed safe. That was closer than I'd have preferred, but it's resolved."

The relief that flooded through me was so intense I actually had to steady myself against the nearby rock, Cent's own breath releasing in a shaky exhale beside me.

"They did it," she said, something wondering in her voice.

"They did it," I confirmed, and felt, for the first time since the operation began, something like real hope settle in.

The comm crackled once more, Steve's voice, steadier. "Command center secured. Finding extensive documentation — operational records, personnel files, more than we expected to recover intact. This could give us everything we need to trace the organization's full leadership structure."

"Casualties," Natasha asked, direct.

"Minor injuries on our side, nothing critical. More resistance than the first operation, but we came through it."

Something loosened fully in my chest.

Millie's voice came through next, having coordinated medical support from a secondary position. "Wounded stabilized. Nothing life-threatening, thankfully. Want everyone processed before we call this concluded."

"Understood," Natasha confirmed. "Beginning full extraction now."

"Can we go help now," Cent asked, already moving toward the facility.

"Let's go help now," I agreed.

We found the facility in the careful process of being secured. Faith emerged from the secondary breach point, visibly exhausted but carrying real satisfaction.

"We got them," she said, catching my eye. "The actual leadership structure. Names, locations, financial records. This isn't just another cell. This is significant."

"That's incredible work," I said, meaning it.

"Wasn't just me. Whole team, working together the way we've been learning to do this whole month." A tired, real smile. "Feels good, honestly. Finishing something, instead of just delaying the next crisis."

Bruce found us a while later, still visibly shaken from the intensity of disarming the failsafe.

"That was more intense than I'd have preferred," he admitted. "But we came through it. All of us."

"Thank you," I told him. "For staying calm enough to actually handle it."

"I've had practice staying calm under pressure," he said, wry. "Turns out it's useful for more than just avoiding a particular kind of transformation."

We spent the following hours in thorough processing — documentation secured, personnel detained, the whole facility gradually transitioning from active threat into evidence. Fury arrived personally to oversee the operation's conclusion, and confirmed this was the most significant breakthrough the investigation had achieved.

*
"This is it," he said, reviewing initial reports with careful satisfaction. "The actual leadership structure, exposed. Doesn't mean the wider organization simply disappears — these things rarely resolve that cleanly. But it's a real blow. More substantial than anything we've managed so far."

"Does this mean it's actually over," Cent asked. "For us, specifically."

Fury considered the question with the same careful honesty he'd always extended our family. "I believe the direct threat to your family and to Home is substantially reduced by what we accomplished today. Won't insult either of you with false promises about permanent safety — no threat this size ever fully disappears. But I think you've earned real relief this time, rather than just another pause before the next crisis."

I looked at Cent, at the exhaustion and pride in her face.

"Then let's go home," I said. "Let's actually rest."

Osei called a formal Council session three days after the operation concluded, inviting our full extended team to attend in person, and Millie found herself, standing in Home's central meeting hall alongside people from three separate realities, struck by the real historical weight of the moment unfolding around her.

"We have reviewed everything your combined efforts accomplished," Osei said, addressing the assembled Council and their visitors both. "The leadership structure exposed, the immediate threat to both our peoples substantially reduced. I want to say, formally, on behalf of this Council, that our earlier caution about outside involvement, however historically justified, would have left us a lot more vulnerable had we maintained it through this crisis."

Miriam, standing near the front, looked visibly moved. "This wasn't a small thing, Osei, choosing to trust that caution had limits."

"No. It was not small. But it was, I believe, correct." Osei's gaze swept the room. "I wish to propose something more formal than the limited cooperation we extended during this crisis. A real, ongoing alliance — intelligence sharing, mutual defense commitments, careful but real integration between our security structures."

Fury stepped forward. "SHIELD would welcome that formalization. Want to be clear — this isn't about control, or oversight, or anything resembling the exploitation your people rightfully fear. This is about genuine partnership between equals, built on exactly what we've demonstrated together these past weeks."

The Council deliberated briefly before Osei spoke again. "We accept this proposal, formally, effective immediately. Home will no longer face threats to our existence in isolation, provided this partnership continues to honor the trust we're extending today."

The formal proceedings gave way, eventually, to something warmer — a celebration properly earned this time, spanning Home's central square.

Cent found her among the crowd, Charlie beside her.

"Can you believe this?" Cent asked, gesturing at the whole sprawling celebration. "A month and a half ago, this was just us, scared, watching a car outside our house. Now it's this. An actual alliance between civilizations."

"I can believe it, actually," Millie said, real warmth. "I think that's exactly what happens when people choose connection over isolation, again and again, even when it's hard."

Charlie, listening, nodded with real feeling. "My mom keeps saying something similar. That courage isn't really about facing danger alone bravely. It's about choosing, deliberately, to let people in, even when every instinct says isolation would be safer."

"Your mom's wise," Millie said, meaning it.

They spent the evening moving through the celebration together, the whole assembled family — Sunnydale's crew, the Avengers, Home's own careful, rebuilding population, the full Wildside Five — mingling with real, hard-won ease.

Davy found her as the evening wound down, settling beside her with visible, true peace in his expression.

"How are you doing?" he asked.

"Good. Really, honestly good." She leaned into him, watching the celebration continue around them. "Keep thinking about how this actually started. Fear. A parked car. And now this. An alliance between civilizations, built out of exactly the kind of choice you and I spent twenty years learning to make."

"Been thinking that too, honestly." He wrapped an arm around her. "Think that's the actual lesson worth taking from all this. Not that danger stops mattering. Just that connection, chosen deliberately and repeatedly, builds into something considerably stronger than fear ever manages to tear down."

They stayed together at the celebration's edge a while longer, watching their daughter laugh with Charlie and the rest of the Five, watching Fury share what looked like honest, easy conversation with Osei.

"I love you," Millie said, simply.

"I love you too," Davy said, and held her closer.

Faith found me on Joyce's porch a week after everything with Home finally settled, dropping down onto the steps beside me, hammer thumping down next to her like an afterthought.

"You've been quiet lately," I said. "More than usual, even."

"Been thinking a lot. About everything, honestly." She turned the hammer over where it rested against her knee. "This whole thing with your family, then Home — biggest sustained fight I've ever been part of. Bigger than the Convergence, weirdly, even though the actual stakes started smaller."

"How do you mean?"

"Convergence was reactive. Something huge happened, we punched it until it stopped happening. This was different. Had to keep showing up on purpose, over and over, even when it kept getting worse instead of, you know, resolving like a normal apocalypse." She snorted at her own phrasing. "Learned something about myself doing that. Turns out I'm not just the girl who takes a hit and keeps standing. Apparently I've also got a functioning brain I can use for the slow, boring, actually-useful part of a fight instead of just the hitting."

"That's real growth, Faith."

"Don't make it weird." Easy, no real bite in it. "Yeah. I think it is, though. Couldn't have gotten there without you three."

I felt something warm settle in my chest. "I'm proud of you."

"Same. Watching you grow up through all this — actually watching it, not hearing about it secondhand — that's been something. You went from a scared kid staring at a car outside your house to someone who helped close an actual tear in reality and then walked straight into round two without flinching."

"I flinched plenty. Just kept moving anyway."

"That's literally what courage is. Nobody tells you that part." She bumped my shoulder, harder than strictly gentle.

We sat a while in comfortable still before she spoke again, something rawer under the usual armor.

"Gonna tell you something I haven't said out loud to anybody yet."

"Go ahead."

"Been thinking about Home. What Osei said, about choosing connection over hiding in a hole and calling it strength. Made me think about my own deal — how long I spent completely alone before any of this, before your family, before this whole ridiculous extended clan we've apparently assembled. Didn't understand till this ordeal exactly how much that isolation actually cost me."

"What changed?"

"You did. This whole family did. Watching people actually choose each other, over and over, even when it's inconvenient or scary — taught me something I never properly learned growing up in group homes and motel rooms. Connection's not the soft option. It's the only thing that actually holds when the real stuff comes for you." A beat, then, dry, "Also I make honestly excellent bread now, which nobody saw coming."

I laughed, reached over, took her hand. "I'm really glad you're part of this family, Faith."

"Same." Something moved in her voice despite the effort to keep it light. "Don't get used to me saying stuff like this. Once-a-decade special occasion only."

53

Buffy found Xander and Willow at the Magic Box a few days after everything with Home finally settled, both deep in quiet conversation over cups of tea gone lukewarm, and dropped into the chair across from them.

"Please tell me this is a fun conversation," she said. "I've had a day. Mom found out I used the guest towels for slaying purposes. I'm currently on towel probation."

"Towel probation sounds like a honest punishment," Xander said.

"It is deeply unfair and I stand by my choices."

Giles emerged from the back room, removing his glasses.

"I couldn't help but overhear," he said, settling into his own chair. "Buffy, I do hope you're not using household linens as combat equipment on a regular basis."

"Only in emergencies, Giles."

"Every incident is, apparently, an emergency."

"That's what makes it slaying and not, like, laundry scheduling." Buffy grinned, unrepentant, then let it settle into something more real. "Actually, real talk for a second — keep thinking about how different this whole HYDRA thing was from what we usually do. No apocalypse. Just a family, scared, needing people to actually show up. And we did. Every single time it got worse instead of resolving nicely."

"I confess I've found the whole ordeal rather clarifying," Giles said, something reflective replacing his usual dry formality. "We've operated in relative isolation here for years — a small, dedicated group, rather proud of managing that way, if I'm honest. This rather thoroughly demonstrated that the isolation was never actually necessary."

"That's either very wise or you've just discovered friendship, Giles, four years into knowing all of us."

"I am capable of learning things slowly, Buffy. It is one of my few consistent qualities."

Faith wandered in partway through, dropping into the last chair and immediately stealing Xander's tea without asking. "This the deep-conversation table? I want in. Also this tea's cold, somebody needs to own that failure."

"That would be Xander's failure specifically," Willow said.

"Figures." Faith took a long drink of it anyway. "I've got a thought, if the group's taking submissions. Spent a long time thinking family was just whoever happened to be standing around you, whether you picked 'em or not. This whole thing taught me it's actually a choice. You gotta keep making it, too, not just once."

"That's beautifully put, Faith," Giles said, moved despite himself.

"I have my moments." Faith took another swig of the stolen tea, entirely deadpan.

The table dissolved into real, unguarded laughter, and Buffy leaned back, watching her friends — her family, fully, messily, permanently.

"Same time next crisis?" Xander asked, only half joking.

"Same time next crisis," Buffy confirmed, stealing her own tea back before Faith could finish it.

54

Fury called a final formal briefing two weeks after the operation concluded, once his people had processed the full scope of what we'd recovered. Sitting once more at our kitchen table with Millie and Cent, I was curious what the actual final accounting would reveal.

"Here's the full picture," he said, spreading printed documents across the table rather than relying on Tony's usual digital displays, something almost ceremonial in the choice. "The leadership structure we exposed accounts for roughly forty percent of the wider network's known operational capacity, based on cross-referencing everything we recovered against existing intelligence."

"Forty percent," Millie repeated. "That's substantial."

"It is. More substantial than any single operation against this organization has achieved in over a decade." Hard-earned satisfaction in his face. "Want to be honest — sixty percent remains, distributed across structures we haven't yet mapped. This isn't a complete victory. Don't believe complete victories exist against an organization built the way this one is."

"But it's a real victory," I said.

"A significant one, yes. Enough that your family, and Home, can reasonably expect a substantially reduced direct threat going forward." He gathered the documents with careful precision. "I don't say this lightly, Mr. Rice. This was one of the most successful operations against this organization in my entire career, and it happened because your family chose, repeatedly, to trust the process instead of retreating into isolation."

Cent, low-key through most of this, finally spoke. "What happens to the people we captured? The actual leadership?"

"Extended legal process, considerable oversight given the scale of what they're responsible for. Won't pretend that moves quickly or cleanly. But they'll face real consequences, more substantial than anything HYDRA's typically managed to avoid in recent years."

"That's something," Cent said, though I could hear the complicated weight in her voice.

Fury lingered after the briefing concluded.

"I want to tell you something, before I head back to far less pleasant paperwork," he said. "I've run a great many operations over a long career. Watched a great many families get pulled into danger they never asked for. This is the operation I'm proudest to have been part of — not just because of what we accomplished, but because of how your family chose to face it. With honesty. With real trust extended, even when trust was hard."

"We had a lot of help," I said, the same thing I'd told him weeks ago, still fully true.

"You did. You also chose to accept it, repeatedly, which I've watched fewer people manage than you'd expect." He extended a hand, formal but warm. "It's been a privilege, working alongside your family through this."

"The privilege's been ours," Millie said, shaking his hand with real feeling.

He left the way he always did, unhurried, no dramatic exit.

Cent lingered at the window, watching his car pull away down the quiet street. "Do you think we'll ever fully know it's over? Like, completely over?"

I considered the question honestly. "I don't think so. Not in the way that means never worrying about it again. But I think we've learned something more useful than that kind of false certainty. We've learned we can carry ongoing uncertainty without it destroying our capacity for real peace. That's not nothing, Cent. Might actually be everything."

"That's a very wise, very tired-sounding thing to say."

"I am very wise and very tired," I said, managing a real smile. "Comes with the territory of everything we've survived together."

She laughed at that, unguarded, and the three of us settled into the kitchen's comfortable evening still.

Joyce insisted on hosting one final gathering, a proper close to the whole extraordinary ordeal, and by the time everyone had arrived, her backyard had once again transformed into something far larger than any ordinary family dinner had any right to become.

I stood near the grill with Thor, who'd apparently claimed it as permanently his own domain, watching the whole scene unfold — Tony and Willow deep in a conversation that had clearly been running continuously since their last parting; Faith and Buffy trading easy banter near the cornhole game, considerably better at it now than their first attempt months earlier; Cent laughing at something Joey had said, the full Wildside Five settled in around her like they'd always belonged there.

"You look thoughtful," Millie said, joining me, following my gaze across the yard.

"Just taking it in. Everything that's happened. Everyone who's here." I gestured at the sprawling gathering. "Two months ago it was just fear. A parked car. Now it's this — an entire, permanent extended family, spanning realities none of us knew existed a year ago."

"I've been thinking that too." She considered the gathering with visible pride. "That's the thing I'm proudest of, out of everything we survived. Not just that we came through it. That we came through it bigger, more connected, less afraid of letting people in than we were before it started."

"I feel that too. Watching Cent choose courage, over and over, even scared, that reminded me what actually mattered underneath all my own old fear."

Fury caught my eye across the yard, raising his glass slightly in acknowledgment. I raised mine back, feeling something closer to real warmth than mere professional respect.

Steve found me a while later, settling nearby with his own plate. "How's it feel? Actually being done, this time."

"Good. Different than after the first raid, though. That one felt like relief. This one feels more like arrival. Like we finally got where we were actually headed."

"That's a good way to put it." He was quiet a moment. "Keep thinking about the whole shape of this year. How much changed, starting with just a car parked on your street."

"Feels like it should've broken us, looking back. Instead it just made everything bigger."

"Think that's the actual lesson. Not that danger stops mattering. Just that connection, chosen deliberately and repeatedly, ends up stronger than whatever tries to threaten it."

The evening wound down slowly, goodbyes exchanged, promises made about future gatherings that carried now the weight of real, permanent commitment. Charlie and the Five headed back through the gate last, Charlie pausing at the threshold to look back.

"Same time next month?" he called to Cent.

"Same time next month," she confirmed, and I watched them go, Clara's hand already finding his as the portal sealed behind them.

I found Millie and Cent on the porch once the yard had finally emptied.

"Good ending," I said, watching the empty street.

"Good ending," Millie agreed, leaning into me.

"Not an ending, though," Cent said, thinking, I knew, about everything Fury had told us — about the sixty percent still out there, about the ongoing vigilance we'd all learned to carry alongside real peace. "More like a really good chapter break. Before whatever comes next."

"I can live with that," I said, managing a real, tired smile. "I think I actually can."

The call came through Fury's office three weeks after the operation concluded, and he brought it to us in person, given who exactly was on the other end.

"Wakanda's reached out," he said, settling at our kitchen table with the same careful gravity he'd carried through every significant development this whole ordeal had produced. "Their monitoring systems detected the failsafe device's activation and subsequent disarmament during your final operation. Vibranium-based sensors — a good deal more sensitive to interdimensional energy fluctuations than anything we've currently got access to."

"They've been watching us this whole time?" I asked, some wariness rising despite everything we'd already survived.

"Not watching you specifically. Monitoring for exactly this category of anomaly, as standard practice, given their own history with advanced technology falling into dangerous hands." Real respect underneath the careful delivery. "They want to send a delegation. Officially, to understand the scope of what happened. Unofficially, I suspect they're also interested in the broader multi-reality situation your family's become central to."

"Should we be worried?" Millie asked.
"I don't believe so. Wakanda's relationship with SHIELD, and with the wider world, has been complicated but good-faith for years. I'd trust their involvement far more readily than I trusted HYDRA's initial approach, if that comparison helps."

The delegation arrived two days later, and the second the door opened, Shuri breezed past the formal greetings entirely to plant herself directly in front of our bookshelf, already scanning the room with a small device before anyone had said a proper hello.

"Okay, this is a nice house, very cozy, very human," she said, not looking up. "But I'm gonna need everyone to hold that thought because your backyard just gave my sensors the best data spike of my entire year and I have several hundred questions."

"Shuri." T'Challa's voice carried the specific, long-suffering patience of an older brother who'd clearly had this exact conversation a hundred times before. "We have not even been introduced yet."

"We're being introduced right now. This is me introducing myself. Hello, I am Shuri, I make things that shouldn't be possible, and your reality apparently broke in a way I find deeply exciting." She finally looked up, grinning. "Also that's my brother, King T'Challa, he's very dignified, please ignore how unimpressed he looks, it's his default setting."

"It is not my default setting."

"It is extremely your default setting. I've seen baby pictures. You had this exact face at four months old." Shuri turned back to me, undeterred. "Anyway. Mr. Rice. I understand you can fold space using nothing but your own nervous system, which, frankly, some of our best engineers have been trying to do with machinery for over a decade, so you can imagine my level of interest."

"We appreciate you coming personally," I managed, still slightly disbelieving of who was actually standing in my kitchen.

"Forgive my sister," T'Challa said, fond underneath the formal delivery. "She has never once in her life allowed an introduction to properly conclude before her curiosity intervened."

"You say that like it's a character flaw and not literally the reason Wakanda has the best tech on the planet." Shuri didn't even look up from the device. "Also you're one to talk, Your Majesty. Last time we met new people you spent the first ten minutes doing the whole 'I am T'Challa, son of T'Chaka' thing before anyone got a word in."

"That is tradition."

"That is you liking the sound of your own introduction. Both things can be true."

Millie, recovering her composure faster than I had, gestured them both toward the table. "Please, sit. I'll make tea, and you two can continue relitigating your entire childhood, or we can discuss whatever you actually came here to discuss. Your choice."

"I like her," Shuri said, immediately, dropping into a chair. "She gets it."

Cent joined us partway through, having been video-calling Willow when the delegation arrived, her expression shifting fast from surprise to barely contained excitement.

"You're Shuri," she said, starstruck despite everything she'd already survived these past two months. "I've read about your work. The vibranium applications, the medical technology — it's incredible."

"You have good taste," Shuri said, pleased. "Most people just want to know if I can make their car fly. Refreshing to meet someone who appreciates the actual science instead of the car." She was already pulling data out of her device, screens unfolding in a way that made our kitchen table look, briefly, like the inside of a more advanced facility. "Speaking of the actual science — I want to show you what our sensors actually caught."
