Chapter 8

Ilsa asked for a proper research visit six weeks before HYDRA, careful to frame it as pure curiosity rather than anything operational.

"I've wanted to study the tame-side ecosystem for years," she admitted, at our kitchen table, a different kind of enthusiasm than the focused urgency I remembered from the Convergence. "We've kept separation so carefully that actual field research has always been limited. Feels like a real opportunity, now that the connection's stable."

"Glad to help however we can," Millie said.

She spent three days with us cataloguing local flora, pulling Cent into long conversations about ecological differences that left them both delighted. She also had, I noticed within the hour, an apparently uncontrollable need to correct imprecise terminology. Cent called something "just a regular oak tree" the first afternoon and got a full three-minute explanation of which oak it actually was, delivered with the same gentle immovable insistence she'd probably bring to a matter of life and death.

"I can't say 'tree' around her ever again," Cent told me later, exasperated and charmed in equal measure. "Corrected me on 'bug' too. There's an actual scientific difference between a bug and an insect. Did you know that? I didn't. I know it now. Forever."

"Your world's biodiversity is remarkable," Ilsa said, the second evening, reviewing her notes. "Far more varied than what's left on our side, post-collapse. I understood the scarcity intellectually. Seeing the contrast is different."

"Does that make you sad?" Cent asked. "Seeing what we've got, next to what Home lost?"

Ilsa thought about it. "Sometimes. Mostly it makes me hopeful. Proof recovery's possible. Our own ecosystems are recovering too, slowly. Nothing like this yet. Real progress, though."

She told us about it — reforestation, agricultural diversification, the kind of slow abundance that had once seemed permanently gone.

"I'd like to bring some of your field notes back," she said, her last evening. "Nothing sensitive. Ecological observation. Our scientists would find it useful."

"I'd be honored," I said.

That last evening she relaxed into something warmer than her usual careful manner. Watching her delight at a sunset, at Millie's bread, at an ordinary family dinner, I understood something about how precious ordinary abundance looks to someone who grew up knowing exactly how fast it can vanish.

"Thank you," she said, at the gate the next morning. "Not just the research access. The ordinary hospitality. I don't get much of that."

"You're welcome here anytime," Millie said. "Not just for research."

"I might take you up on that," Ilsa said.

She left through the gate, and I watched it seal behind her, thinking about how much our family's circle had grown this year without a single crisis making it happen.

Cent found me on the porch that evening.

"I liked her," she said, sitting down beside me. "Guarded, but underneath it, genuinely warm, once you get past the distance."

"Noticed that too. I think that's a lot of Home. Decades of caution, warmth sitting right behind it once you've earned the trust."

"I like that we're earning it. Slowly. The right way."

"I think you're right," I said.
