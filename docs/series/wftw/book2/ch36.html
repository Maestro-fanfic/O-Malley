Chapter 36

The call came from Tony at two in the morning, three days into the investigation, and Millie knew before she'd fully answered that whatever he'd found wasn't going to let any of them sleep properly again that night.

"I need you both awake for this," he said, no preamble, none of his usual easy swagger. "Cross-referencing the financial records we pulled from the analyst against medical supply contracts — standard due diligence, honestly, I almost skipped it — and I found something that's going to be harder to hear than anything else we've turned up."

"Just say it, Tony," Davy said, already pulling Millie close, some old instinct bracing for impact.

"The orthopedic hardware supplier used for your mother-in-law's hip surgery, years back — the actual plates and screws, Millie — traces back, through four shell layers, to the same funding network as the facility your family already hit. Don't think this was random. Think it was deliberate, planned well in advance. A foothold established years before anyone needed to use it."

Millie felt the floor tilt slightly, some clinical part of her mind already running the implications even as the rest of her went cold. "You're telling me they put something inside my mother's body. Years ago. Before any of this."

"Telling you it's a real possibility, and needs to be checked properly, immediately." Genuine, careful gravity in his voice. "There's a complication, though, given where she's actually living now. If there's a transmitter in that hardware, it hasn't just been broadcasting a ground location. It's been broadcasting from orbit, this whole time, since she moved up to Kristen Station. Which means, if this is real, they may know the station exists at all. Its position. Its orbit. Everything."

The room went silent a long moment.

"That's not just a personal violation anymore," Davy said, quiet, working through it. "That's a security breach on something we all believed was secret."

"Afraid so."

They jumped to the station that same morning, Cent joining the moment she heard, and Millie spent the whole ascent — that strange half-second of transition, Earth curving away beneath them into something impossibly small — running through every possible way to tell her mother something this frightening without losing the composure the moment required.

Samantha met them at the observation deck, sharp as ever despite the low-gravity shuffle she'd never quite lost the habit of moving through carefully. "You've all got the same face on. Raised one of you and watched the other two grow into people who can't hide anything from me either. What's happened?"

Millie considered lying, protecting her mother a little longer — then thought about everything her own family had learned this past month about what honesty actually cost versus what it saved.

"There's a real possibility," she said, attentively, "that something was hidden in the hardware from your hip surgery. A tracking device. We don't know for certain yet, but given where you're living now, if it's real, it means considerably more than we first realized."

Samantha went very still, something old and furious crossing her face that Millie recognized as her own temper. "Someone put a tracker inside my body, and it's been telling people where this entire station is."

"If it's true, yes."

"Well." Her jaw set, steely despite the vulnerability of the moment. "I moved up here specifically to be somewhere safer. I'd like to know exactly who's responsible for making that not true, so I can express my extremely undignified displeasure directly, gravity permitting."

The scan Bruce's trusted specialist ran that afternoon confirmed it — a small, sophisticated transmitter embedded directly into one of the plates, calibrated for minimal power, transmitting in narrow, infrequent bursts specifically designed to avoid standard sweep detection. The kind of dormant asset an organization plants years in advance and waits to need.

"This explains much more than I wanted it to," Davy said, once confirmed, the flat control Millie recognized as him holding real fury in check. "They didn't need active surveillance on the station itself. Had a fixed point already inside it, transmitting its position the whole time. We built this place to be untraceable, and it never actually was."

"We know now," Samantha said, firm, from the exam table, gravity-anchored straps the only thing keeping her still for the procedure. "And I'd like it removed, please, at everyone's earliest convenience. Carried enough undignified hardware in this hip already without also hosting a surveillance device broadcasting my address to the people terrorizing my family."

The removal happened within the week, a careful procedure run by the station's own small medical bay, Bruce's specialist supervising remotely. Samantha came through it with the same sharp, undiminished resolve she'd carried through the whole ordeal.

"Well," she said, once the anesthesia cleared, Earth turning slowly beneath the observation window. "I suppose that's one more thing this family's survived together. Feels almost routine, at this point."

"I wouldn't go that far," Millie said, though something in her chest had eased considerably, watching her mother sit up, honestly herself again.

"No. I suppose not." Samantha reached for her daughter's hand. "But I'd rather face something like this with all of you knowing about it immediately, the way you just did, than find out years later you'd been quietly protecting me from it. That's not protection. That's just a different kind of leaving someone alone with something frightening."

"I know," Millie said, thinking of Davy, of twenty years of exactly that lesson, finally, properly learned. "We're all still learning that one, I think. Every single time it matters most."

We deployed Fury's sensors first, tucked into the brush at three points along the ridge, each confirmed active with a blink of green light. The facility looked, from this distance, almost exactly as unremarkable as the satellite images suggested — low buildings, chain-link perimeter, a single dirt access road winding toward it from the highway.

An hour passed in quiet observation. Vehicle traffic more than an abandoned facility should reasonably see — three separate trucks in the first forty minutes, each disappearing behind the largest structure long enough to suggest a loading dock we couldn't directly observe. Personnel moving between buildings with efficient purpose. A perimeter patrol, timed, professional, rotating on a schedule tight enough to suggest real training — I counted the interval twice, coming up with the same eleven minutes both times.

"That's not a skeleton crew," Steve said, low. "That's an active operational base."

"Any read on numbers?"

"Minimum fifteen, based on rotation. Could be more inside the buildings we can't see into." He lowered the binoculars, jaw tight in a way I was starting to recognize as his tell for real concern. "More than I'd like to admit. HYDRA's always favored this kind of unremarkable footprint — nothing flashy, just still, patient infrastructure built to last far longer than anyone expects to find it." Old, weary familiarity. "The scale here concerns me more than the visible activity. This kind of setup usually means a lot more planning went into it than a single opportunistic grab attempt would require."

We stayed another forty minutes, gathering what we could.

"You're good at this too," I said, calmly, watching him track the patrol's movement. "The waiting part. Most people I've worked with struggle with this."

"Learned it the hard way, same as you, I imagine. Different war, same lesson." Something distant crossed his face. "Impatience gets people killed in this line of work. Lost people, early on, to exactly that lesson. Doesn't fade."

Then the patrol pattern shifted.

Not dramatically. Just enough — a guard pausing at a spot he hadn't paused at before, radio raised to his mouth, another guard converging from the opposite direction with a speed that suggested something other than routine, both scanning the tree line with a focus that hadn't been there thirty seconds earlier.

"They made something," Steve said, already gathering the binoculars. "Not us specifically, I don't think, but something's triggered a response."

"Extraction?"

"Extraction," he confirmed, and I didn't need telling twice.

I grabbed his arm and jumped, the ridge and the facility dissolving in the familiar disorienting rush, landing back in my own kitchen exactly nine minutes after we'd left, breathing hard.

Natasha was there within seconds, Millie right behind her.

"We're fine," I said, quickly. "Facility's active. Confirmed minimum fifteen personnel, real infrastructure. Something in their patrol pattern shifted right before we pulled out — might've been unrelated, might've been us. Sensors still active, still transmitting."

Natasha was already pulling up the incoming data. "This is good work. Real, actionable intelligence. Fifteen-plus personnel changes the calculus — this isn't something a small strike team handles alone."

"What's it mean," Millie asked, hand finding mine.

"It means we're not doing this small," Natasha said. "Full team. Thor, Faith, a lot more SHIELD presence, probably Steve leading the tactical side given his specific experience with exactly this kind of target." She met my eyes directly. "Also means I want to talk seriously about timeline. If they made something during your recon, even if it wasn't specifically you, that facility's threat assessment just went up. They might accelerate rather than wait for us to make the next move."

"So we move first," I said.

"We move first, with everyone we've got." Steady, focused. "Full operational briefing within the hour. Everyone. This is no longer a reconnaissance problem. This is the actual fight."

I looked at Millie, and underneath the very real fear of what was coming, something steady settled into place.

"Let's do it, then," I said, and meant it completely.

"We did that together," I said, mostly to myself.

"We did." She leaned into me, exhausted, real. "Twenty years, and I don't think we've ever actually fought side by side like that. Not properly."

"Feels different than I expected."

"Better, or worse?"

"Better," I said, without hesitation. "Considerably better."

We sat there as the operation wound down around us, and I felt, for the first time since that gray sedan parked on Cypress Street, something like actual, earned peace settle into my chest.

Not alone. Never again, if I could help it.

"Say that again," Millie said, her voice gone very careful. "You're certain it's the same analyst?"

She listened another long moment, her free hand gripping the counter's edge.

"We're coming," she said, finally. "Give us twenty minutes to reach Giles, coordinate, and we'll have people there as fast as anyone can manage."

She set the controller down slowly, and when she turned to face us, I saw the fear I'd really hoped we'd finally left behind for good.

"Someone breached Home's outer perimeter," she said. "Three days ago. Just confirmed it wasn't an accident, wasn't a malfunction — someone got in, accessed restricted archives, got out before anyone caught them."

"The missing analyst," I said, already knowing the answer.

"Miriam's team found a partial signature match. Not conclusive yet, but strong enough she wanted us to know immediately." Millie's hands were shaking now, the composure that had carried her through the entire ordeal finally cracking. "Davy, if it's him — if HYDRA's actually found a way into Home itself—"

"Then this was never just about us," I said, the full shape of it landing cold. "Never just about grabbing Cent for leverage. They wanted the technology. They wanted Home."

Cent had gone very pale, homework forgotten. "Charlie. Is Charlie okay? Is his family—"

"Don't know yet," Millie said. "Miriam didn't say anyone was hurt. Just that the breach happened, and whatever they accessed included technical schematics for the gate technology itself."

The weight of that settled over the kitchen like something physical. Twenty million people, once. A civilization that had spent decades rebuilding from catastrophic collapse, finally, cautiously reopening itself to the wider multiverse — and now, potentially, exposed to exactly the kind of predatory organization that had already proven, on our own Earth, precisely how far it was willing to go.

"We need Fury," I said. "Now. This isn't something we handle with just Natasha's old detail anymore."

"Already calling him," Millie said, dialing, her voice steadying into the same controlled calm I'd watched her use throughout this entire ordeal.

Fury picked up on the second ring, and Millie relayed everything in short, precise sentences.

"He's mobilizing everyone," she said, once she'd hung up. "Steve, Natasha, Tony, the whole team. Wants us at the Magic Box within the hour. He also—" She hesitated. "Wants to talk to Home's Council directly. Formally offer SHIELD's support, given the scope of what this apparently represents now."

"Do you think they'll accept it?"

"Don't know. Home's spent decades being deeply cautious about outside involvement, for reasons that make complete sense given their history." Real fear now, raw. "But Davy, if HYDRA's got their hands on gate technology schematics — if they understand, even partially, how to replicate or exploit that technology — this isn't a threat to one family anymore. Isn't even just a threat to Home. Could be a threat to every reality that technology could ever theoretically reach."

I looked at Cent, seventeen years old, her whole face carrying the same fierce, frightened determination I recognized as inherited directly from both of us.

"Then we go," I said, already reaching for my jacket. "All of us. Whatever this actually turns out to be, we face it the same way we faced everything else this past month. Together, with everyone willing to stand with us."

"I'm coming too," Cent said, already grabbing her own jacket, and this time, watching the steady resolve in her face, I didn't argue.

"You're coming too," I agreed, and meant it completely.

We jumped straight to the Magic Box, the familiar bell chiming overhead, and found Giles already gathering research, Willow's laptop already open, the whole household mobilizing with grim, focused efficiency.

"This isn't over," I said, low, mostly to myself. "This was never actually over."

"No," Giles agreed, grim but steady, looking up from his books. "I don't believe it was. But we've faced larger things than this together before, and come through them. I don't intend to believe, even now, that this time will prove any different."

I understand their history makes that a difficult offer to accept. I made it anyway, because I think the alternative — them facing this entirely alone, with security infrastructure clearly, demonstrably breachable — is considerably worse."

"As safe as anyone currently is, which I won't pretend is fully reassuring. Home's tightening internal security as we speak. But I want you to understand clearly — this isn't a contained threat anymore. If HYDRA's after the technology itself, everyone with access to it, including your friend's family, represents a potential target."

I felt something cold and furious settle into my chest, sharper than anything I'd felt during my own ordeal, because this wasn't just about me anymore.

"What can we actually do," I asked. "Right now. Today."

Natasha, hushed through most of the briefing, finally spoke. "We prepare to move, fast, the moment Home's Council gives any kind of green light. Meanwhile, gather what intelligence we can from our side — the missing analyst's known associates, any pattern in his movements since he went dark."

"And if Home says no?"

Fury's expression didn't soften, but something in his voice did. "Then we respect that decision, however difficult, and do everything we can from our own side — securing our own access points, hunting the analyst with everything we've got. We don't get to force help on people who've decided, for reasons rooted in true, hard-earned trauma, that the risk of trusting outsiders outweighs the benefit."

Willow, beside me, squeezed my hand once. "Whatever happens, we're not gonna just sit here and do nothing. You know that, right?"

"I know." I squeezed back. "Just keep thinking about Charlie. About how it felt when it was happening to me, knowing he might be feeling exactly that same fear right now, and I can't do anything about it yet."

"You can. You're doing it right now — being here, being ready." Real conviction. "That matters, Cent."

The briefing continued another hour. Miriam's response came through just before midnight, relayed via the old controller, and Mom's face, listening, shifted slowly from tense worry into something like cautious, hard-won relief.

"The Council's accepted limited support," she said, once the call ended. "Careful, controlled, on their terms. But they've accepted it. They understand, finally, that facing this entirely alone isn't actually the safer choice anymore."

"When do we move," I asked.

"Tomorrow," Fury said, already standing, already reaching for his phone. "First thing. Want everyone rested tonight, because tomorrow, we're doing something none of us have actually attempted before — coordinating a real, multi-reality security operation, on unfamiliar ground, against an enemy who's already proven exactly how dangerous they're willing to be."

I looked around the room one more time — Faith, Buffy, Xander, Willow, Thor, Steve, Natasha, my own parents, Giles bent over his research — and felt, despite the very real fear still sitting in my chest, something steady and certain settle into place alongside it.

Whatever came next, we weren't facing it alone.

I stood in our own backyard at seven in the morning, surrounded by an assembled team that included two Avengers, a Norse god, and a good deal more armed SHIELD personnel than I'd ever expected to see in one place, nervous about what actually waited on the other side.

"Just — this is real now. Actually real. About to see the place you've been telling me about my whole life."

The gate opened, that same familiar tearing-light sensation I'd experienced a hundred times crossing to Xander's worlds, and we stepped through into something that stole my breath entirely.

Home wasn't what I expected. I'd pictured something more obviously post-apocalyptic — ruins, scarcity, visible struggle. Instead we emerged into a settlement that looked, at first glance, almost peaceful: low, efficient buildings arranged with deliberate purpose, gardens threading between structures in patterns suggesting real, patient cultivation rather than mere survival, people moving with the unhurried purpose of a community that had learned never to waste resources on anything unnecessary.

But underneath the peace I could see it — the careful redundancy in everything, backup systems visible even to my untrained eye, defensive structures integrated so seamlessly into the architecture they'd clearly been designed by people who understood, in their bones, that peace was never fully guaranteed.

Director Osei met us personally, a woman whose bearing carried the gravity of someone who'd shouldered impossible decisions for decades.

"Director Fury. Captain Rogers. And the rest of you — welcome, though I confess I wish the circumstances were far less urgent." Her gaze settled on me last. "You must be Cent. Miriam's spoken of you often. Charlie especially has spoken of you often."

"Is he okay? His whole family?"

"Secured, for now. We've moved several key families to more protected housing while we assess the actual scope of the breach." Real weight in her expression, one hand drifting briefly to an old, faded scar along her left forearm before she let it fall still. "I won't pretend this hasn't shaken us badly. We built this place on the promise that we would never again be caught unprepared for exactly this kind of threat. Discovering our own security wasn't as thorough as we believed has been difficult, to say the least."

"We're here to help however we actually can," Fury said. "On your terms, within whatever limits you set."

"You understand correctly." Her gaze swept the group, something almost approving despite the circumstances. "The Council debated long into the night. What ultimately convinced us was Miriam's account of everything your organizations did for her family this past month — the honest care shown, not just the tactical support."

We were led through the settlement toward the coordination center, and I, walking through Home's careful streets, noticed small details — children playing some game I didn't recognize near a garden plot, an elderly man tending vegetables with the patient attention of someone for whom growing food had once meant the difference between survival and catastrophe.

Charlie found me before we reached the center, breaking away from a cluster of people near one of the residential buildings, and the relief on his face when he saw me landed somewhere deep in my chest.

"You're actually here," he said, pulling me into a hug before either of us fully processed the gesture. "I hoped, but I didn't actually expect you'd come in person."

"Of course I came. The second I heard, there was never a question." I held on a moment longer, taking in the exhaustion under his relief. "Are you okay? Genuinely?"

"Scared. Honestly, scared, in a way I haven't been since the whole Convergence thing." A tired, real smile despite it. "But better, now. Knowing you're all here. Knowing Home doesn't have to face this entirely alone this time."

"That's the whole point," I said, meaning it.

Marie found us next, followed by Rick and Joey and Clara, the full Wildside Five properly assembled.

"Okay, not gonna lie," Joey said, taking in our assembled Sunnydale and SHIELD contingent with wide, genuine awe despite the circumstances, "this is objectively the coolest and most terrifying thing that's ever happened to me simultaneously, and I include the actual civilizational collapse stuff my mom tells me about, because at least that one didn't come with a literal god standing in our actual settlement."

"Joey," Marie said, though there was less weariness in it than usual.

"I'm allowed one (1) inappropriate observation during an active crisis. It's a documented coping mechanism."

Despite everything, that got a real laugh out of several of us.

"Let's go help them fix this," I said, and together we headed toward the coordination center.

"How long would replication actually take," I asked, surprising myself, though given everything Tony and Bruce had taught me this past month, I found I understood enough to ask it meaningfully.

Ilsa considered it seriously, treating it with honest respect despite my age. "Depends heavily on their existing technical capability, which we don't yet know. If working from nothing, potentially years. If they've got existing research to build from, perhaps considerably less."

"We should assume less time, not more," Bruce said, quiet but firm. "Better to overestimate the urgency than underestimate it."

The room settled into focused, methodical planning after that — search patterns for the missing analyst, expanded security protocols, coordination between Home's forces and our combined Earth contingent.

Charlie found me during a break, both of us stepping outside for air, the settlement's hushed streets stretching under a sky that looked subtly different from home.

"Scary, right?" he said, watching my expression. "First time seeing an actually foreign sky."

"Yeah. Beautiful, though. In a strange way." I turned to look at him properly. "How are you actually holding up? Not the version you give the room."

He considered it seriously. "Scared, honestly. Not just for myself. For everyone here. For my mom, especially, given everything she already survived once. Watching her face the possibility of losing all this again — don't think I fully understood, before this week, how much weight she's actually been carrying, quietly, this whole time."

"I get that. Watching my own parents face something that threatened to reopen old wounds — different, seeing them scared, than just hearing about the history secondhand."

"Yeah." He was quiet a moment. "Keep thinking about what you told me, though. About your dad finally learning to accept help instead of facing everything alone. Think that's what's actually different this time, for my mom too. Not facing this in silence, hoping it resolves itself. Actually asked for help, even though every instinct in her clearly wanted to handle it alone."

"That's real growth. For both our families, I think."

"Yeah." A small, honest smile despite the fear still visible underneath. "I'm glad you're here, Cent. Not just for the tactical help. Having you here, specifically, makes this feel a lot more survivable."

"Same," I said, meaning it, and reached out to squeeze his hand.

We stood there together a while longer, watching Home's low-key streets, two teenagers from separate realities carrying weights considerably heavier than either of us should have had to carry at seventeen.

"We should get back," Charlie said, eventually. "They'll want everyone for the next phase."

"Yeah." I didn't move immediately. "Charlie — whatever happens, however this resolves, I'm glad we finally got to do this properly. Meet, actually be in the same place together, even if the circumstances are terrifying."

"Me too," he said, and together we headed back inside.
