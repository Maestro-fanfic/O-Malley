Chapter 1: Chapter 1



Three weeks before the gray sedan showed up on Cypress Street, I spent my first ordinary Saturday on Home.

Not a crisis visit. Not some careful diplomatic thing with a chaperone standing three feet back. Just a Saturday, the kind Charlie and I had been talking about for months. Mom dropped me at the gate with a list of instructions I only half listened to. Charlie was waiting on the other side, trying not to look like he'd been looking forward to it.

"Okay," he said, the second I stepped through. "Got the whole day planned. Actual tour this time, not the crisis-response version. Ready?"

"Ready."

"Fair warning, there's a lot of walking, and I'm not built for it." He patted his stomach. "So we're taking breaks. Dignified breaks. Not me being out of shape."

"I can work with dignified breaks."

Home in daylight looked nothing like the tense version I'd seen my first visit. Kids ran between garden plots. Old people worked the crops with the kind of hands that knew exactly what a single seed was worth.

"This is the market," Charlie said, leading me through a cluster of stalls around the settlement's central square. "No real currency. More of a distribution system, though people still trade personal stuff back and forth."

Vegetables I didn't recognize. Hand tools somebody was proud enough of to display like art. Vendors and customers who'd clearly known each other their whole lives.

"It's beautiful here," I said, and meant it. "Not despite what happened. Because of it, almost."

"Yeah. That's about right." Charlie thought about it a second. "My mom says losing almost everything taught this whole place something most people never learn — that ordinary safety isn't actually ordinary. It's a big deal, every single time it holds."

We spent the afternoon on it — the agricultural systems, a workshop where old people were teaching kids traditional crafts next to newer tech, a memorial garden neither of us lingered in long.

"Can I ask you something," I said, near the edge of the garden, afternoon light cutting gold across the rows of markers. "Does it ever feel strange? Growing up somewhere that's still rebuilding from something this big?"

Charlie thought about it properly this time. "Sometimes. I think everybody here carries some version of it, even the ones too young to remember the collapse. But it's not all bad. Makes the good days feel like something instead of just — days." He shrugged. "You notice a Saturday like this, growing up here. You don't get to take it for granted."

"That's a good way to live."

"I think so too. Took me a while, though. Used to just feel like weight. Now it's more like — I don't know. Knowing what it's actually worth."

We finished the day at his family's place, dinner with Miriam and his father. Miriam asked after my own parents, real interest, not politeness, and I found myself telling her more than I'd planned to.

"You should visit more," Miriam said, near the end of it. "Not just for crises. Saturdays like this one. I think that's actually the important kind."

"I'd like that."

Charlie walked me back to the gate.

"Thanks," I told him. "For the actual tour."

"Thanks for wanting the actual tour." Something shifted in his face. "Most people would've been fine just knowing Home was real."

"I'm not most people."

"No," he said. "You're really not."

I stepped back through into the ranch's hangar and jumped straight home from there — same reality now, no gate needed, just the ordinary trick I'd had my whole life — the day still sitting warm in my chest.

Chapter 2: Chapter 2



Two weeks before the gray sedan, Millie and I took an actual vacation. First one in longer than either of us could remember.

Nothing dramatic. Three days at a cabin two states over. Cent stayed with the Sunnydale crowd.

"I don't remember the last time we did this," Millie said, our first evening, both of us on the porch watching the sun go down over hills neither of us recognized. "Just us. No crisis. No working around Cent's schedule or my clinic hours."

"Feels strange." I turned my wine glass, watching the light move in it. "Keep almost reaching for the relay. Waiting on an emergency that isn't coming."

"Same. Old habits." She smiled, small. "Still can't quite believe we've got a machine Tony Stark built us sitting in a kitchen drawer, ready to holler if anybody on the other side needs us."

"He dropped off a matching set for us and Xander's whole household both, back when — muttering the entire time about not trusting either family not to almost die without a way to reach him. Same trick as the one he rigged for Thor, just smaller. Rides whatever door's already open instead of trying to punch a new one through."

"Never thought I'd be grateful for a billionaire's guilt complex."

"Takes all kinds."

Second morning we hiked a trail through unfamiliar woods.

"Can I tell you something," Millie said. "I've been thinking about Home. About Miriam, about what she's rebuilt there. Made me think about us differently."

"How so?"

"I think I've been treating our own peace like something fragile the whole time. Something to guard instead of actually live in. Watching how deliberately Home's people choose to notice what they've got, instead of just anxiously sitting on it — I think that's something I should've learned twenty years ago."

I thought about it, walking. "I think I've been doing the same thing. Treating every good year like it's borrowed."

"Maybe that's what this trip's actually about, then."

We hit a small overlook partway up, the valley spread out below, and stood there a while not saying much.

"I love you," I said.

"I love you too." She leaned into me, and we stayed like that a while, watching the light move across the valley.

The rest of the trip went the same way. Long talks. Quiet walks. Actually noticing it, for once, instead of just surviving it.

"I want to do this more," Millie said, the last night, both of us by the fire pit. "Not just wait for vacation to remember how."

"I'd like that." I took her hand. "That's the whole thing, isn't it. Not just accepting help when a crisis makes you. Building it in before the crisis shows up."

"Twenty years." She smiled. "I think we're finally getting good at this."

We drove home the next morning. Cent met us on the porch, full of stories — a movie marathon with Willow, an afternoon with Buffy learning some new patrol technique.

"Good trip?"

"Really good trip," Millie said. "We should do the family version of this more too. Not just crisis gatherings."

"I'd like that," Cent said, folding herself into a hug between us. "This whole quiet stretch we've been on. Hope it keeps going."

I held onto both of them a minute longer.

Chapter 3: Chapter 3



One week before the gray sedan, I spent a whole Sunday afternoon just practicing. Alone.

Not training. Not the drilling Dad built into me as a kid, the kind meant for actual emergencies. Just practicing. Playing, almost, with something I'd had my whole life and rarely got to enjoy without urgency stapled to it.

I started small. Jumping between spots around the property — the old oak at the edge, the flat rock by the creek, the rise where you could see clear across three neighboring farms on a good day. Nothing dramatic. Just moving through space in a way almost nobody else on the planet could, purely because it felt good.

I called Willow that evening.

"Best day," I told her, phone propped against a pillow. "Just practicing. Playing around with the ability, for once."

"That sounds lovely," Willow said. "I did something similar, actually. Just cataloguing for the fun of it, not tied to a crisis or a research project. Found a whole meadow of butterflies nobody in Xander's records had ever documented. Watched them three hours. Barely took notes."

"That sounds perfect."

"It was. I think we both needed it. Feels like this whole year's been one big thing after another — the Convergence, the HYDRA business with your family, everything with Home. Nice to remember why any of it felt magical to begin with."

We talked a while longer.

Dad found me on the porch later, both of us watching the sunset.

"Good day?"

"Really good day. Practiced. Jumped around the property for hours, just for fun. Felt good, remembering why I actually like this thing instead of just using it."

"I remember that feeling," Dad said, something distant in his face. "Used to do the same, before any of this was about anything except finding out what was over the next hill. Found some of my favorite spots that way. Still visit some of them."

"Will you show me? The actual favorites. Not the ones tied to something urgent."

"I'd like that."

We sat a while longer, the evening settling around us.

Chapter 4: Chapter 4



Charlie invited me to an actual Five gathering — Six, now, though the name hadn't caught on yet — three weeks before anything with the gray sedan started. I walked through the gate nervous about what he'd called "just a regular hangout, nothing special."

Regular hangout meant Rick's family barn, converted into something between a workshop and a living room. String lights strung crooked across the rafters. A couch that had clearly survived at least two other households before landing here.

"Cent!" Joey called out the second I stepped through the door, already crossing the barn. "The Sixth Member arrives. This calls for ceremony."

"You said you weren't doing the ceremony thing, Joey," Marie called from a low table where she was setting up a board game.

"I said I was workshopping ideas. This is the workshop's conclusion."

"His workshop concluded on finger guns," Clara said, not looking up from whatever she was tinkering with. "Which, honestly, might be the least embarrassing thing he's proposed. I'm allowing it."

"High praise," Rick said, appearing from the back with an armful of snacks. "Hey, Cent."

"I'd have picked you up myself if my license situation weren't currently revoked," Joey added, unbothered. "Long story. Involves a DWI and a mailbox that had it coming."

"The mailbox did not have it coming," Marie said, still not looking up. "You've told that story four different ways and the mailbox is blameless in every one."

"Details."

I sat down on the couch next to Charlie.

"So what's the plan tonight?"

"Board games, mostly," Marie said. "Movie after, depending how competitive this gets. Fair warning — Joey takes board games unreasonably seriously."

"I take winning seriously. Different thing."

"Not really," Rick said, already settling into the game.

"At least Marie's got an excuse for not driving Joey around," Charlie said, grinning. "Girl's got a pilot's license and no interest in a regular one. Some of us just carry the mailbox forever."

"I have priorities," Marie said. "Planes don't hit mailboxes."

Two hours of it — Joey's trash talk getting more elaborate by the round, Marie's sharp strategy, Clara's careful plans occasionally wrecked by one of Rick's quiet moves nobody saw coming until it was too late.

"Didn't expect Rick to be this good," I said, watching him take a win without changing expression.

"Nobody expects it," Charlie said. "That's the whole strategy. Let everyone underestimate him, then quietly take the whole board apart while they're arguing about something else."

"Solid strategy," Rick said, unbothered.

We put a movie on eventually, everyone crowded onto the couch and whatever chairs had survived, Joey narrating the entire thing whether anyone asked him to or not.

"Can I ask something," I said, during a lull. "What's it actually like, growing up here? Not the crisis version. Just the regular days."

"Honestly? Pretty ordinary, most of the time," Marie said. "School, friends, normal teenage stuff. The collapse-recovery thing's more like background noise most days."

"Except the resource drills," Joey added. "Those I think about constantly. Mostly because they're boring and I resent every one."

"They're important, Joey."

"They're important *and* boring. Both true."

Clara, practical as always: "I think it shaped how all of us think about the future. We don't take resources for granted the way I hear kids on your side sometimes do. Every choice feels a little more deliberate, given what our parents' generation lost."

"Sounds like a lot to carry."

"Sometimes. Mostly it just feels like how things are." She shrugged. "Normal's whatever you grew up with, I think. For anybody."

We finished the movie well past midnight. Nobody in a hurry to end it.

Charlie walked me back to the gate.

"Thanks for coming," he said. "I know it's a lot, still, crossing over for something this ordinary."

"It's not a lot. It's exactly what I wanted more of."

"Same time next week?"

"Same time next week," I said, and meant it.

Chapter 5: Chapter 5



I found a new place by accident, six weeks before HYDRA — jumped somewhere I'd never aimed for, one of the rare surprises this ability still throws after twenty-odd years.

I'd meant to jump to the Aerie, wanted the quiet after a long week, and landed instead somewhere I didn't recognize. A coastline. Cliffs dropping straight into water clear enough to see real depth from the edge. Seabirds wheeling overhead in numbers that meant a colony nesting close by.

I stood there a minute, working out what happened. Some quirk in the jump. Maybe my own head pulling me somewhere new instead of where I'd meant to go. It happens, rarely, and I'd learned a long time ago to treat it as a gift instead of a mistake.

I spent the rest of the afternoon just exploring. Seabirds nesting on the cliff face, more of them than I'd ever seen in one place, the whole afternoon full of their noise. Found a narrow path down to a cove, sheltered from the wind, water calm.

I called Millie that evening.

"You found somewhere new? After twenty years?" Real surprise in her voice.

"By accident. Some kind of subconscious redirect. Haven't had one of those in years." I described it — the coastline, the birds, the cove. "Want to take you and Cent out there this weekend, if you're both free."

"We're free."

We went Saturday, all three of us. Watching Cent's face at the cliff's edge, I remembered why I fell for this ability in the first place, decades back, before any of the danger piled on top of it.

"This is incredible," Cent said. "How'd you even find this?"

"Pure accident. Sometimes the best ones are." I settled beside her. "Used to find places like this all the time, when I was younger. Before responsibility crowded it out."

"I want more days like this," Cent said. "Not instead of everything else. Alongside it."

"I think that's right," Millie said, joining us at the edge.

We spent the whole weekend on it. Catalogued seabird species Willow would want notes on. Found a tide pool ecosystem in the cove that held Cent's attention most of an afternoon.

"I want to name it," Cent said, our last evening, the sunset doing something impossible to the coastline. "Feels wrong calling it 'the accidental cliff place.'"

"What would you call it?"

She thought about it, watching the birds settle for the night. "Something about the accident. About how the best things happen when you're not even trying."

"Serendipity Cliffs," I said, half joking.

"That's actually perfect." She grinned. "I like that a lot."

We jumped home that evening.

"Same time next month?" Cent asked — a phrase that had, over the past year, turned into something like a family motto. "Serendipity Cliffs, I mean. Not just Home."

"Same time next month," I said.

Chapter 6: Chapter 6



Cordelia cornered Willow at her locker on an ordinary Wednesday.

"Okay, I need an update," Cordelia said, no preamble. "It's been weeks since anything interesting happened, and I refuse to believe that's actually true. What's going on with the interdimensional family situation?"

"Cent's doing well, actually. Peaceful stretch, for once." Willow shut her locker. "Visited Home properly last weekend. Ordinary version, not the crisis one."

"That's disappointingly boring, Willow. I was hoping for at least a minor catastrophe."

"Sorry to disappoint."

Jesse showed up partway through. "Fine's good, though, right? We should celebrate fine. It doesn't happen much around here."

"I suppose," Cordelia said, already warming to it despite herself. "Fine deserves acknowledgment. Low bar. Still a bar."

"High praise," Jesse said, grinning.

"Don't get used to it."

They settled at the usual lunch table. Faith showed up partway through.

"Heard Cent's been having good weeks," she said, dropping into her seat. "Genuinely good. Not just performing okay for everybody."

"That's what I heard too," Willow said. "I think we're all just enjoying it."

"Been doing ordinary stuff myself lately," Faith said. "Actual patrol, nothing dramatic. Hanging with Buffy and Xander. Learning to cook."

"You're learning to cook?" Jesse said.

"Joyce is teaching me. Made a whole lasagna last week that wasn't a crime against food."

"That's a big deal, coming from you," Cordelia said. "I remember your first attempt at toast. It was concerning."

"I've grown, Cordelia. People do that."

Lunch went on the way lunch usually did.

"Can I say something slightly sentimental?" Willow asked, during a lull.

"Depends how sentimental," Cordelia said.

"I just — I love this. Ordinary lunch. Nothing urgent."

"That's fair," Faith said. "Been trying to practice that myself. Appreciating ordinary instead of just surviving toward the next extraordinary thing."

"Look at all of you, getting wise," Cordelia said, real warmth under the dry delivery. "Continue being grateful for boring lunches. Suits you."

Willow texted Cent that evening, ordinary details from an ordinary day, and read Cent's equally ordinary updates back, and felt something settle, quiet, in her chest.

Chapter 7: Chapter 7



Millie's Tuesday client list looked perfectly ordinary.

Her first client, Sarah, showed up with something different in her posture.

"You seem lighter today," Millie said.

"I feel lighter. I think I finally forgave myself for the divorce. Not just understanding I shouldn't blame myself. Actually feeling it."

"That's real work, Sarah. Give yourself a moment with that before we move on."

Her afternoon client, a teenager named Marcus working through a rough patch with his dad, showed less visible progress but kept at it anyway.

"I tried the thing you suggested," he said. "Talking to my dad instead of dodging it. Didn't go perfect. Went better than I expected, though."

"Tell me."

They worked through it. Millie thought, watching him, about Davy's own slow lesson on accepting help, about Cent learning the same thing in her own way — the same lesson, different people, different circumstances.

She finished the day with an older client, Frank, working through grief, and drove home.

Davy had dinner started.

"Good day?"

"Really good day." She sat at the counter, watching him cook. "Keep noticing how much my own work parallels what's happening in this family lately."

"Noticed that in myself too. Twenty years of you pushing me toward this, and I think I've finally caught up."

"Better late than never."

Cent got home not long after, dropped her backpack by the door.

"Good day?" Millie asked.

"Really good day. Ordinary. School, then Willow's. Nothing dramatic." Cent grabbed a snack, sat down. "I like these stretches."

"We've earned them," Millie said.

Dinner ran long and easy, nothing urgent on the table.

"I love this," Cent said at one point, watching her parents share a look across the table. "Ordinary dinner. Everybody here."

"Me too," Millie said.

Chapter 8: Chapter 8



Ilsa asked for a proper research visit six weeks before HYDRA, careful to frame it as pure curiosity rather than anything operational.

"I've wanted to study the tame-side ecosystem for years," she admitted, at our kitchen table, a different kind of enthusiasm than the focused urgency I remembered from the Convergence. "We've kept separation so carefully that actual field research has always been limited. Feels like a real opportunity, now that the connection's stable."

"Glad to help however we can," Millie said.

She spent three days with us cataloguing local flora, pulling Cent into long conversations about ecological differences that left them both delighted. She also had, I noticed within the hour, an apparently uncontrollable need to correct imprecise terminology. Cent called something "just a regular oak tree" the first afternoon and got a full three-minute explanation of which oak it actually was, delivered with the same gentle immovable insistence she'd probably bring to a matter of life and death.

"I can't say 'tree' around her ever again," Cent told me later, exasperated and charmed in equal measure. "Corrected me on 'bug' too. There's an actual scientific difference between a bug and an insect. Did you know that? I didn't. I know it now. Forever."

"Your world's biodiversity is remarkable," Ilsa said, the second evening, reviewing her notes. "Far more varied than what's left on our side, post-collapse. I understood the scarcity intellectually. Seeing the contrast is different."

"Does that make you sad?" Cent asked. "Seeing what we've got, next to what Home lost?"

Ilsa thought about it. "Sometimes. Mostly it makes me hopeful. Proof recovery's possible. Our own ecosystems are recovering too, slowly. Nothing like this yet. Real progress, though."

She told us about it — reforestation, agricultural diversification, the kind of slow abundance that had once seemed permanently gone.

"I'd like to bring some of your field notes back," she said, her last evening. "Nothing sensitive. Ecological observation. Our scientists would find it useful."

"I'd be honored," I said.

That last evening she relaxed into something warmer than her usual careful manner. Watching her delight at a sunset, at Millie's bread, at an ordinary family dinner, I understood something about how precious ordinary abundance looks to someone who grew up knowing exactly how fast it can vanish.

"Thank you," she said, at the gate the next morning. "Not just the research access. The ordinary hospitality. I don't get much of that."

"You're welcome here anytime," Millie said. "Not just for research."

"I might take you up on that," Ilsa said.

She left through the gate, and I watched it seal behind her, thinking about how much our family's circle had grown this year without a single crisis making it happen.

Cent found me on the porch that evening.

"I liked her," she said, sitting down beside me. "Guarded, but underneath it, genuinely warm, once you get past the distance."

"Noticed that too. I think that's a lot of Home. Decades of caution, warmth sitting right behind it once you've earned the trust."

"I like that we're earning it. Slowly. The right way."

"I think you're right," I said.

Chapter 9: Chapter 9



I found something genuinely new about my own ability seven weeks before HYDRA — twenty years in, and I hadn't expected there was still something fundamental left to learn.

Happened by accident, same as most real discoveries with this thing. I was jumping between familiar spots around the property, same easy practice I'd done a thousand times, when mid-jump I noticed something I'd never consciously caught before. A kind of peripheral sense — other potential destinations sitting just beyond my actual target. Like standing in a hallway and somehow knowing every door along it, even the ones you're not opening.

I stopped, standing in the middle of the backyard instead of the creek spot I'd meant to hit, and spent ten minutes trying to get it back.

Took days of practice before I understood it. Some kind of expanded spatial sense, it seemed, that only showed up when I jumped with real focused intention instead of the casual jumps I usually did. When I concentrated I could feel, faintly, the rough shape and distance of places well outside my visual memory. Not clear enough to jump there blind. Enough to change what I thought I understood about how this worked.

I called Millie out to the backyard.

"Found something new," I told her. "After twenty years. Some kind of expanded awareness, when I focus mid-jump."

"After twenty years?" Her surprise matched mine. "Show me."

I tried, though the sensation was harder to explain than to feel, and we spent the next hour on it, Millie listening with the same curiosity she brought to everything.

"This is remarkable, Davy." She turned it over. "Does this connect to what Strange and Wong were saying? About your ability touching something more fundamental than point-to-point movement?"

"Maybe. Might explain some things I always chalked up to intuition. Like knowing a destination's going to feel wrong before I even jump."

We spent the week on it, documenting, no crisis pushing us, just the wanting-to-know of it.

Cent joined in once she heard.

"You think I've got it too?" She asked, running her own careful jumps around the property.

"Worth finding out."

She spent an afternoon on it, and by evening had her own version — different from mine, enough to surprise all three of us.

"Not quite the same as what you're describing," she said. "For me it's less like sensing other doors down a hallway. More like sensing the walls themselves. The structure holding it all together, not just the destinations inside it."

Something clicked for me. "That might connect to what happened at Home. Sealing that tear. If yours is more structural than mine, that'd explain why you could actually touch the boundary instead of just moving through it."

"That's a real distinction," Millie said, already reaching for her notebook. "Two generations of the same ability, showing up two different ways. Individual variation, or something more systematic?"

We spent the following weeks finding new nuances in it, no urgency anywhere in the process. Watching my own kid find dimensions in an ability I'd thought I understood completely, I got a good reminder of how much stays unexplored even in something you've lived with your whole life.

"Best homework I've ever had," Cent said, one evening, comparing notes. "Actual research into something that matters, instead of memorizing for a test."

"Feels good, doesn't it," I said. "Finding something new just because it's there, not because a crisis made you look."

"It really does." She was quiet a second. "Hope we get more time for this. Pure discovery, not crisis-response."

I hoped for that too. Some old instinct underneath the hope kept wondering how long it'd actually last.

Chapter 10: Chapter 10



I made my own discovery three days after Davy's. Sitting in the kitchen doing paperwork while he practiced in the backyard, and it hit me out of nowhere.

I knew he was about to jump before he did it. No visible cue, no sound. Just a sudden certainty, a full second before I heard the displacement of air that always came with his transitions.

I sat there a minute, then called him inside.

"I think I just sensed you jumping," I told him. "Before it happened."

Davy's face went from casual to focused fast. "Show me. Let's test it."

We spent the next hour on it, Davy jumping at random intervals while I tried to catch the feeling before it happened. Seven out of ten by the end. Better than chance explained.

"This is remarkable," Davy said. "Twenty-three years, and I never once thought you might have some connection to this too."

"Not the same as your ability. I can't do what you do. Maybe some kind of resonance, built up over decades of being close to somebody who does."

We called Cent in to help test it, and by evening had a rough theory — some real if limited sensitivity I'd built over years of proximity to Davy's ability, something that seemed tied to emotional connection more than plain physical distance.

"Wonder if this connects to what you said about resonance affecting the Convergence readings," I said. "Maybe emotional bonds create some kind of measurable link to the ability itself, even in people who don't have it."

"That's worth digging into," Davy said, already reaching for his notebook.

We spent the following days testing the edges of it. Did it work at distance? Did it need Davy's conscious intention? Did Cent register differently than I did?

"I think it's tied to years of real partnership," I said, after several days. "Not just proximity. Not love in the abstract. Something built through decades of trust and shared weight."

"That's a good way to put it," Davy said. "Twenty-three years, and apparently our marriage was quietly building this the whole time."

I wanted to test whether it extended to Cent too, and it did — less precise than with Davy, given the shorter years behind it.

"Makes sense," Cent said. "Less time built up. Still remarkable it works at all."

We kept documenting it through the following weeks, alongside Davy's spatial sense and Cent's structural one, none of it tied to any crisis, just curiosity for its own sake.

"I love this season we're in," I told Davy, one evening, both of us going over notes. "Hope we get a lot more of it before something comes along to interrupt."

"Me too," Davy said. "Twenty-three years of learning to trust each other, and this is what it built toward."

I hoped, sitting there, that it would last longer than any of us could yet know.

Chapter 11: Chapter 11



I spent the next two weeks obsessed with my own version of the discovery — the structural-perception thing Dad and I had first found — testing it with the same method I usually reserved for homework, except this was actually interesting.

I started small, practicing familiar spots, trying to put words to the sensation. Not visual, exactly, though I kept reaching for visual words anyway. More like a pressure-awareness — reality's structure feeling taut in some places, slack in others.

"Try describing what you're sensing right now," Dad said, one afternoon, both of us in the backyard.

"Okay — imagine a blanket, stretched tight in some spots, loose in others. That's kind of what it feels like from wherever I'm standing. Our backyard feels loose. Comfortable. The Aerie feels different. Tighter."

"That's a remarkably precise description for something this abstract."

"Two weeks of practice trying to explain it."

We spent the afternoon mapping locations by their "tightness," as I'd started calling it, and found it tracked something — places we visited a lot felt looser, newer or more significant places felt more tense.

"Might connect to actual boundary integrity," Dad said. "Places you keep connecting to might build stronger pathways. Newer ones haven't caught up yet."

"That'd explain Home," I said. "Felt tight the first visit. Looser now."

We tested it over the following days and it held. Places I'd been once or twice carried more tension than places I visited regularly, and it eased with repetition.

I called Willow.

"This is fascinating," she said, already cataloguing implications in her head, I could tell. "Is it about the boundary structure itself, or your own emotional read on each place? Could be both."

"Don't know yet. Want to test some places I care about but haven't been to much."

We spent the week on it — Willow helping design proper tests, comparing places I visited a lot but felt neutral about against places I rarely visited but felt strongly about. Messy results. Both frequency and emotional weight seemed to matter, in ways neither of us could untangle yet.

"This could matter beyond the pure curiosity of it," Willow said, on one call. "If you can sense boundary integrity this well, that might turn out useful someday."

I hadn't thought about practical use, too deep in the wonder of it, but something in that sat uneasy next to the delight.

I mentioned it to Dad that evening.

"Does it worry you?" He asked. "That it might matter eventually?"

"A little. I like this part. The wonder. Nothing urgent attached. Don't want to lose that just because it turns out useful."

"I get that completely." He thought about it. "I think that's the gift of a season like this one. We get to actually understand the thing, instead of a crisis forcing rushed conclusions."

I found some comfort in that.

"Want to test one more thing," I said, a few days later. "What if I push against the tension instead of just sensing it?"

We tested it small and controlled, and I could — a little. Nothing like what I'd manage at Home later. But real. Not purely passive.

"That's remarkable, Cent," Dad said. "Never heard of anybody developing that particular application before."

"Feels good, understanding it instead of just having it happen to me in a crisis." I turned it over, proud of the work. "I like learning something about myself because I wanted to, not because I had to."

"I like it too," Dad said. "Hope we get a lot more time for exactly this."

I hoped for that too — though some part of me, remembering what Willow said, kept wondering how long it was actually going to last.

Chapter 12: Chapter 12



A journalism student named Derek caught me after class one afternoon, asking if I'd sit for an interview about "unusual family backgrounds" for some elective project.

I was buying hinges, replacing a cabinet door that had been sagging for months — the kind of ordinary Saturday errand that had become, the past several days, a small act of defiance in itself. Told Millie I wanted to run it myself, alone, no shadow. She argued five minutes before agreeing, on the condition I texted the second I left and the second I arrived. I'd texted both. Been in the store maybe fifteen minutes, comparing two nearly identical hinges, when I heard him.

The scrape of expensive shoes on a floor that mostly saw work boots. That registered first, before I even turned around. Twenty years of paying attention to exactly this kind of small wrongness had trained the reflex deep enough I didn't have to think about it anymore.

"Mr. Rice." Not a question. He already knew.

I set the hinges down slow, giving myself a second to look at him properly. Mid-forties, clean-shaven, the kind of composed that takes real practice — not natural stillness, cultivated stillness, someone trained out of every visible tell. Good suit, good shoes, nothing that screamed government or corporate or anything else you could name. That absence of a label was itself the most concerning thing about him.

"Do I know you?"

"Not yet. My name's not important right now. What's important is that I represent an organization with a real, professional interest in your family's unique circumstances." Easy, unhurried, like weather, standing a comfortable distance away, hands loose and visible — the posture of a man carefully trained in how to approach someone without triggering a defensive reaction, and who knew exactly why that mattered here. "We'd like to have a conversation. Somewhere more private than a hardware store, ideally."

"Not interested in conversations with people who don't give their names."

"That's fair." He didn't push, didn't crowd, just stood there patient in a way that felt more threatening than an outright threat would have. Patience deployed as a weapon. Something in my chest recognized the shape of it immediately, straight past thought, landing somewhere older. "Let me offer something instead. We're aware of the ability. Aware of the situation involving the reality you've recently reconnected with. We believe there's room for a mutually beneficial arrangement, rather than the kind of unfortunate misunderstanding that's marked your past dealings with less thoughtful organizations."

Hyacinth. Talking about her without saying her name. Dangling the comparison like bait, like I was supposed to be grateful he wasn't that.

"I've had this conversation before," I said, keeping my voice level. "Didn't go well for the people who started it."

"Aware of your history. Like to think we're offering something different." He reached into his jacket, slow, deliberate, wanting me to see every inch of the motion, and produced a plain card — nothing on it but a phone number. No name, no logo, nothing traceable. "Think it over. No pressure, no deadline. We're patient people."

I didn't take the card.

He set it on the counter beside the hinges, smiled like that settled something.

"We're not your enemy, Mr. Rice. I'd like you to believe that. But I'll be honest, since I think honesty's the only thing that'll actually work here — we're also not going away. Whatever you decide, this conversation's happening one way or another. I'd rather it happen with both of us being reasonable."

"And if I decide I'm not interested in reasonable, on your terms."

Something shifted behind his eyes, brief, gone almost before I caught it. "Then I imagine we'll find another way to have this conversation. I'd actually prefer this one, though. Lot more pleasant for everyone."

He left before I could answer, unhurried, disappearing around the end of the aisle like a man with nowhere urgent to be — the whole point, I understood immediately. A demonstration of control.

I stood there holding a box of hinges I didn't want anymore, twenty years falling away in about four seconds flat. The old fear didn't build gradually. Arrived all at once, complete, familiar — a cold, precise recognition that the ground I'd spent two decades learning to trust had just revealed itself considerably less solid than I'd let myself believe.

Jumped straight home. Didn't pay for the hinges. Left them on the counter with the card I hadn't touched. Found Millie in the kitchen, and the second she saw my face she set down what she was holding and said, "Who."

"Don't know his name. Don't know who he's with. But it's not nothing, Millie. Exactly what we were afraid it'd be." My voice came out steadier than I expected, given how badly my hands were shaking. "Card on the counter at the hardware store. Phone number, nothing else. Mentioned Hyacinth without saying her name. Talked like he already knew our whole history and wanted me to know it."

She didn't ask if I was okay. Twenty years, and she still knows exactly which questions actually help.

"Tell me everything," she said instead, pulled out a chair, and I sat and told her, word for word — the suit, the shoes, the careful patience, the way he'd left before I could push back — hands only shaking a little by the time I finished, Millie's own hands steady on the table the whole time.

"We need to call Natasha," she said, once I'd finished. "Now. Not after dinner. Now."

"I know."

"And Davy—" She reached across, took both my hands, held on until the shaking stopped. "We're not doing this the way you did it last time. Alone, quiet, hoping it resolves if you handle it deliberately enough. Together. Out loud. With everyone who actually cares what happens to us. You hear me?"

"I hear you," I said, and meant it, even as some old, stubborn part of me was already, quietly, calculating exit routes I had no intention of using.

7

I dreamed about the cell that night, hadn't in almost two years, and woke at three in the morning with my heart going too fast and my hands checking the sheets for restraints that weren't there, hadn't been there in a decade, probably never would be again — and none of that made the checking stop.

The dream itself wasn't even accurate anymore. I'd noticed that over the years, the way dreams drift from the actual memory into something worse and stranger, details shifting, the room getting smaller each time, the fear staying exactly the same. Millie's colleague explained it once, years back — the brain doesn't preserve trauma like a photograph. It preserves the feeling and lets the details reshuffle around it, trying and failing to make sense of something that never made sense in the first place.

Millie woke up when I did. She always does.

"Hyacinth?" Quiet, not turning on the light yet.

"Wyoming. The water thing." Didn't need to say more. She's the only person alive who knows the full shape of the water thing — not the summary I give everyone else, Fury, Giles, whatever paragraph of clinical language sits in a SHIELD file now.

"You're here," she said. "Not there. Here."

"I know."

"Say it anyway."

"I'm here. Not there." My heart was already slowing, just from the words. Twenty years of practice, and it still works better than anything else I've tried — better than the medication I hated in my late twenties, better than the breathing exercises that helped some but never reached the root of it. Just her voice, steady, saying the same simple true thing until my body finally believed it.

She turned the lamp on low, sat up against the headboard, pulled me in against her side. I let her. Some part of me still hates needing it — old, stupid, seventeen-year-old part that thinks needing help is the same as being weak. Twenty years hasn't killed that part. Just taught it to stay quiet while the rest of me does what actually works.

I thought about Brian. Not part of Wyoming — Brian was never in that room, never part of what Hyacinth did. The man who threw a chair through a window and carried me two blocks on his back before the bullets caught him. Last words about his wife and his boys, not about me at all. I don't think about him often enough. Some years Wyoming takes up so much room in my head there's none left for the man who actually died trying to keep me out of it.

"He didn't touch me," I said, into the still. "The guy at the hardware store. Didn't threaten me, not really. Just stood there being reasonable and patient, and it was somehow worse than an outright threat."

"Because reasonable and patient is exactly what Hyacinth was, right before Wyoming."

Hearing the name land, plain in the dark, still did something even after all these years — made the whole thing smaller, an actual name instead of formless dread, and worse too, somehow, both at once.

"Yeah." Hadn't put words to that part yet, not even to myself. She'd gotten there faster, same as always. "Reasonable's a tactic. I know that intellectually. Some old part of my brain still hears reasonable and files it under safe, right up until it isn't."

"You know the difference, though. Between then and now." Steady, professional in the way she gets, though with her the two have never fully separated. "Then, you were alone. No resources, no support, nobody who believed you, seventeen years old and completely on your own. Now you've got me. Cent. An actual network of people who'd move heaven and earth for this family. That's the whole difference, Davy."

"I know that. In my head, I know it." I pressed the heel of my hand against my eyes. "Body apparently didn't get the memo."

"Bodies are slow learners." She kissed the top of my head. "We'll figure out the card tomorrow. Tonight you just need to actually sleep, not white-knuckle your way through pretending you're fine."

"Don't think I can sleep."

"Then we'll just lie here. That counts for something too."

We stayed like that a long while, not talking, breathing together in the lamplight. Somewhere in there I found myself thinking about Cent, asleep down the hall, unaware how close her father had come to the edge tonight. I'd promised myself, every year of her life, that whatever happened to me would never touch her the same way — that I'd build something around her sturdy enough to hold even if the old danger resurfaced. Watching that promise get tested felt like watching a bridge you'd built with your own hands take its first real weight.

"I keep thinking about her," I admitted, finally. "What if this reaches her the way it reached me."

"It won't. Not the same way." Millie's voice stayed steady, earned, not naive. "She's not alone the way you were. She's got us. Sunnydale. A whole network of people who love her fiercely enough to move mountains, and she knows how to use it, which is more than either of us knew at her age. That's the whole point of everything we built. Not that bad things would never happen again. That when they did, she'd never have to face them the way you had to."

I let that settle. Found it held.

"Okay," I said, finally, tiredness starting to outweigh the fear. "I believe that. Mostly."

"Mostly's enough for three in the morning."

We drifted off eventually, before dawn, tangled together the way we always ended up after nights like this. I woke a few hours later much less rested but far less afraid, which felt, on balance, like the better trade.

8

It happened in the school parking lot, which is exactly the kind of ordinary place these things aren't supposed to happen. Which I guess is the whole point of choosing it.

I'd stayed late for yearbook committee, arguing with Jenna Park for twenty minutes about whether the sports section needed more candids or fewer — a conversation that felt, in hindsight, almost offensively normal given what happened forty minutes later. The lot was mostly empty when I came out, a handful of cars under sodium lights that made everything look faintly orange and wrong. I had my keys out, thinking about dinner, thinking about the security detail I'd complained about all week and would, within the hour, be deeply grateful for, when the van pulled up beside me way too close, way too fast for a parking lot.

I noticed the sound before the van itself — tires on wet asphalt, closer than any car should get to a person walking. Some old instinct, ten years of Dad's careful drilling, had me half-turning before my conscious brain finished processing something was wrong.

Two of them. Out before I'd even fully processed the van, moving with a coordinated efficiency that told me, even in that first half-second, this wasn't random.

Dad's voice in my head, automatic: *assess before you react, but don't take longer than a second to do it.*

I didn't take longer than a second.

The first one reached for my arm and I did the thing Dad taught me — didn't pull back, went with the pull instead, used his own grip to close the distance and drive my elbow into his throat. He made a sound like a kicked door and folded.

The second was faster, already grabbing for my other arm before the first had hit the ground. I twisted hard enough something in my shoulder screamed, broke his grip, ran. Backpack slipped off one shoulder, I let it go.

Not toward my car. Toward people. Toward the gym, where I knew there'd be a coach and a dozen kids finishing practice. Running toward crowds is rule two, right after rule one, which is don't freeze.

I didn't freeze.

I heard the van's tires behind me, too close, gravel spraying somewhere behind my shoulder, and I did the thing I'm not supposed to do except in honest emergencies — the thing that costs something every time, some toll on the body Dad's never fully described, like running a mile in a single breath.

I jumped.

Fifty feet, straight through the gym doors, into the middle of a very confused volleyball practice, hit the floor hard enough to knock my breath out, rolled once, shoulder screaming now that the adrenaline had a half-second to register the damage. Coach Reyes was already moving toward me.

"Rice? What the hell—"

"Call the police," I managed, between breaths that weren't coming easy. "Now. Parking lot. Two men, they tried to—" My voice cracked. The gym swam at the edges.

Coach Reyes didn't ask a follow-up. Barked at someone to call 911, got between me and the door, solid and immediate. A couple of the volleyball girls had clustered close, Mia Torres crouching beside me asking if I needed water.

I called Dad next, hands shaking so bad I fumbled the phone twice before it connected.

"Cent?" He knew immediately something was wrong.

"I'm okay," I said, first, because that's the rule. "I'm okay. Someone tried to grab me. Parking lot. I got away, I'm at the gym, called the police, I'm okay."

The silence on the other end lasted maybe half a second, but I heard everything in it.

"I'm coming," he said, flat and controlled, a way I'd never heard from him before, not once. "Thirty seconds. Stay where you are. Stay with people."

"Dad—"

"Thirty seconds, Cent."

He wasn't lying. I counted maybe twenty before he was there, materializing right in the middle of the gym in front of God and Coach Reyes and half the volleyball team, and nobody cared about the secret staying secret in that moment. He crossed the distance faster than I'd ever seen him move and just held on, both arms, tight enough it almost hurt, and I let him, shaking, finally feeling the whole thing instead of just surviving it.

"You did everything right," he said, into my hair, voice not fully steady. "Everything. You hear me?"

"Doesn't feel like everything right. Feels like I almost—"

"Almost isn't what happened. What happened is you got away. Used what I taught you, and it worked, and you're standing here because of it." He pulled back enough to look at me, hands steady on my shoulders even though I could feel what it was costing him underneath. "I am so proud of you. And I'm about to be a lot less calm about this once I know you're actually safe. Let's get you safe first."

Coach Reyes hovered, clearly deciding to worry later about the man who'd just appeared out of thin air. "Rice's dad?"

"Davy," he said, extending a hand, remarkably composed. "Thank you for what you did. Getting between her and the door. Won't forget it."

"Anybody would've done the same," she said, though her face suggested she understood this had been something more than an ordinary scare.

Police arrived eleven minutes later, took my description — the van, dark, no plates I'd caught, two men, mid-thirties, professional in a way that felt wrong for a random crime. By then the van was long gone. Dad stood close the whole time, one hand on my good shoulder, not interrupting, just present. Somewhere in my third retelling of the same three minutes, I actually started believing what he'd said. That I'd done everything right. That surviving counted for something even when it didn't feel clean or heroic. Just messy and scared and, somehow, enough.

By the time we left the gym, Mom already speeding toward us from the clinic, Natasha's car pulling in right behind hers, I was standing in a gym full of witnesses, breathing, mostly steady, alive. For the moment, that was the only math that mattered.

9

Millie got the call from Davy still ten minutes out and broke every speed law worth breaking getting there, the drive collapsing into a blur of red lights she barely registered, her whole professional composure abandoning her the second the words *someone tried to grab Cent* landed.

She found her daughter on a folding chair wrapped in someone's spare jacket, a paramedic checking her pulse, Davy crouched in front of her with both hands still on her knees like letting go might undo whatever had just gone right. The gym had gone strange and hushed, practice abandoned, a cluster of teenagers watching from a respectful distance.

She didn't say anything at first, just crossed the floor and got down on Cent's other side and held on, the three of them forming something that from the inside felt like the only structurally sound thing left standing in the whole building.

"I'm okay," Cent said again, into her mother's shoulder, smaller than Millie had heard her in years. "I keep saying it and it keeps not feeling true yet, but I promise it's true."

"It'll catch up," Millie said, meaning it both as a mother and as someone who'd spent two decades helping other people's trauma catch up to their survival. "Give it time. You did everything right tonight."

"Dad already said that part."

"He's right. Bears repeating." Millie pulled back to look at her properly, cataloguing, the same clinical attention that had kept her sane through every crisis Davy's own history had ever landed on their doorstep. "Anything really hurt? Not adrenaline-numb hurt."

"Shoulder. Where I twisted out of his grip." Cent rolled it experimentally, wincing, a small involuntary sound that made Davy's jaw visibly tighten. "Not broken. Just angry."

"We'll get it looked at properly." The paramedic nodded confirmation — full range of motion, no obvious fracture, likely soft tissue strain, ice and rest and several days of complaining. "But you're right. Not broken."

Police took statements for the better part of an hour — a description of the van, general enough to be nearly useless, two men, professional in a way that made the responding officer's eyebrows climb even before federal involvement got mentioned. Millie watched Davy field the questions with a careful, controlled calm she recognized — the mask he wore when he was one wrong word from coming apart entirely.

She caught his eye once, over the officer's shoulder. Didn't need words. *Hold on. Almost done. I've got you after this.*

He held on.

Natasha arrived twenty minutes after the police and took one look before her whole demeanor shifted colder, more focused.

"This shouldn't have happened," she said, quiet, to Millie, once Cent had been settled and the crisis had given way to exhausted aftermath. "I was watching the house. Wasn't watching the school. That's a gap I should have closed."

"You couldn't have watched everywhere."

"Should have anticipated they'd go for the softer target instead of the harder one." Her jaw was tight, voice controlled the way it gets when someone's furious at themselves rather than anyone else. "This is on me. Want that acknowledged, not smoothed over."

"Acknowledged," Millie said, meeting her eyes. "Now help us fix it instead of just carrying it. Guilt doesn't close gaps. Action does."

Something shifted in Natasha's face. She nodded once, sharp, already pulling out her phone. "Full detail from here forward. Cent doesn't go anywhere alone until this is resolved. I'll coordinate with Fury tonight — this changes the calculus considerably. An attempted grab on a minor isn't a gray-area concern anymore. Active operational threat, treated like one starting now."

"What does that actually mean, practically," Davy asked, voice steadier now.

"Means much more resources than we've got assigned. Means I'm done treating this as watch-and-wait. Means it's time to bring in reinforcements. People, not just protocol."

Millie watched her daughter across the gym, wrapped in a borrowed blanket, Davy still not willing to put more than three feet between them, and felt something old and furious settle into her chest — steadier than fear, more useful. The same anger she'd felt exactly once before, twenty years ago, watching Davy come home from Wyoming barely able to speak.

"Good," she said. "Because I want whoever did this found. And I want them to understand, very clearly, what they actually started tonight."

They drove home in a strange, exhausted muted, Cent wedged between them in the truck's single bench seat despite being seventeen and technically far too old for it. Nobody willing to put that much distance between them yet.

10

Fury came himself three days later, which Millie said meant something, and I figured she was probably right.

Three days that had felt like three months. Cent hadn't gone back to school yet — we'd agreed a few remote days made sense while the danger was still an open question — and the house had settled into a strange, watchful rhythm, Natasha's team rotating shifts, Thor still occupying an improbable amount of our living room, everyone waiting on the one piece of information nobody could produce yet: who wanted my family badly enough to send two men after a seventeen-year-old in a school parking lot.

He looked older in person than the file photo, or maybe just more tired. Arrived alone, no entourage, parking an unremarkable sedan at the curb and walking up our path with the deliberate pace of a man who'd learned that arriving too fast makes people nervous.

"Mr. Rice. Mrs. Rice." He nodded to each of us, standing on the porch like he was waiting to be invited in properly. "I'll get straight to it, since I imagine straight's what you'd prefer."

"Straight's good," I said, gesturing him toward the porch chairs rather than inviting him inside. Some old instinct wanting this conversation in a space I could see all the exits from.

"We found the leak." Plain, no drama, though I could see what it cost him underneath the flatness. "Analyst on my own Convergence oversight team. Eleven years with the agency. Nobody flagged him once. Either a credit to how good he was at hiding it, or an indictment of how badly we'd stopped looking. Probably both."

"HYDRA?" Millie asked, carefully neutral, though I could feel the tension in her hand on the arm of her chair.

Fury's jaw tightened at the word. "Confirmed, as of about six hours ago. Sleeper, activated sometime after the Convergence report went up the chain. Fed information out for weeks before anyone caught the pattern. My own oversight team, Mr. Rice."

"You're telling us this to your own credit or to warn us it might not be over."

"Both, if I'm honest. He's in custody. That part's handled." His eye held mine steady. "What's not handled is whoever he was reporting to. Cutting off one head doesn't guarantee the rest stops moving. Usually just makes them move faster."

"So the men at the school—"

"Almost certainly connected. Whether direct orders from someone still active, or freelancing off information he'd already leaked before we caught him, I can't say for certain yet." He glanced toward the house, where I could hear Cent's voice through the screen door, animated, deep in conversation with Tony about something scientific. "Won't lie to you and pretend this is over. More accurate to say we've identified the wound. Haven't finished cleaning it."

Millie's hand found mine.

"What do you need from us," I said.

"Patience, mostly. Continued cooperation with Agent Romanoff's security, which I understand's already tightened." Something almost apologetic crossed his face. "I'd also like your permission to bring in additional resources. A dedicated team, not just Romanoff alone. Don't like the odds of one person covering a threat this active, however good she is."

"You're asking permission." Hadn't expected that. "Most people in your position would just do it."

"Most people in my position learned the hard way that doing things to families instead of with them costs more trust than it's worth." He held my gaze. "Read your file, Mr. Rice. All of it. Know what it cost you, dealing with people who decided things about your life without asking first. Rather not repeat that mistake."

That landed somewhere I hadn't expected. Made me believe the man on my porch — not fully, twenty years of hard-earned caution doesn't dissolve in one conversation, but enough.

"Bring the team," I said. "But I want to meet them first. All of them. Before they're anywhere near my daughter. And I want the right to say no to any individual, without needing to explain myself."

"Fair. More than fair." Something like respect in the nod. "I'll have them here within forty-eight hours, personally vetted before they set foot on your property." A pause. "I'd also like to formally extend the offer Captain Rogers made through Agent Romanoff. He's requested to be looped in on the operational side. Hands-on, if you're willing."

"Why him specifically? Beyond the history."

"Because he's the best I've got at exactly this kind of threat, and because I trust his judgment on HYDRA implicitly. Fought them longer than anyone alive. If there's a pattern here, he'll recognize it faster than the rest of us combined."

I looked at Millie, who nodded slightly.

"Yes," I said. "Bring him in too. Properly."

"I'll relay that." He paused at the porch steps, halfway to leaving. "And Mr. Rice — I am sorry this happened. On my watch, to your family specifically. I intend to make it right."

He left before I could answer. Millie and I stood on the porch a long moment, watching his car pull away, the late afternoon light going gold and long across the yard.

"You believe him?" Millie asked.

"Mostly." I was honest with myself about the answer before I gave it to her. "Enough to let the team in. Enough to let Rogers in too, apparently, which is a sentence I never expected to say out loud. Not enough to stop checking every one of them myself, the whole time they're here."

"Good," Millie said. "That's the right amount of trust. Enough to work with him. Not so much we forget why we're being careful in the first place."

I nodded, reached for her hand again.

11

The security team turned out to be three people I actually liked, which surprised me more than it should have — a quiet, competent woman named Rosa who'd run protective details for foreign dignitaries before SHIELD recruited her, a former Army Ranger named Desmond who treated our backyard fence like a real tactical problem, and a younger agent, Priya, who Cent took to immediately, mostly because Priya was the only one who actually laughed at her jokes instead of just tolerating them.

Four days running, things settled into something almost like normal — normal with an armed agent near Cent's classroom door, normal with Natasha doing perimeter checks at hours I only half-noticed anymore. But normal enough that Cent went back to school, and I went back to sleeping through most nights, and Millie's shoulders came down from somewhere near her ears. Steve Rogers had arrived on the second day, quieter than I expected, reviewing files with Natasha in the makeshift operations room our spare bedroom had become.

"HYDRA's patient," he told me, the third evening, both of us on the back porch. "That's what people underestimate. Lost their big victories a long time ago. What they've gotten good at since is patience. Small moves. Long games. Whoever's running this isn't going to give up because the first attempt failed."

"Not exactly reassuring."

"Not meant to be. But it means something useful — patient people are predictable, in their own way. They don't panic. They regroup and try again." He looked at me, something steady and kind despite the grim subject. "You're doing better than you probably give yourself credit for. I know what it costs, fighting the instinct to handle everything alone. Fought that instinct myself, longer than I'd like to admit."

Hadn't expected that particular kindness. Stayed with me longer than the tactical assessment did.

It didn't last, the fragile normal. Four days, and then it didn't.

I felt it before I understood it — that particular subdued before a bad thing. I was in the kitchen, Millie at the clinic, Cent at school with her shadow, Rosa at the front of the house, Desmond doing his perimeter sweep, and the wrongness sat in my chest like weather moving in before the clouds show. Nothing concrete. Just twenty years of instinct screaming something had shifted.

I checked every window. Nothing. Checked the security feed. Nothing.

Then the phone rang. Our actual landline, the number almost nobody had anymore. Something in my gut went cold before I even answered it.

"Mr. Rice." Not the same voice as the hardware store. Rougher, less patient. "We're going to make this simple. Your daughter's currently at school. So is our man. If you want today to end without complications, you'll jump to the following coordinates alone, right now. No calls, no warnings to your security detail."

"You don't get to make demands after what you already tried."

"We don't need to make demands twice, Mr. Rice. We already know you'll come. Men like you always come. Take something precious enough, cooperation stops being optional." A deliberate pause, cruel in its patience. "Ten minutes. After that, we stop being reasonable."

The line went dead. Coordinates rattled off before hanging up, scrawled on the back of an envelope with a hand that wasn't fully steady.

I stood in my own kitchen maybe three seconds, every old instinct screaming to do exactly what he'd said — go alone, go quiet, protect Cent the only way I'd ever known how, by absorbing the danger myself. The exact reflex that had kept me alive at seventeen and had, for twenty years after, nearly kept me isolated from everyone who loved me.

Twenty years ago I'd have done exactly that.

I called Natasha instead.

"They called the house," I said, fast, controlled. "Claim they've got someone at the school. Wanted me alone. Gave me coordinates."

"Location on the caller?"

"Gave coordinates. Don't know if real. Might be a decoy."

"Already moving. Stay put, stay armed if you've got anything, do not go anywhere alone. That's exactly what they want." Flat, professional, a car door slamming somewhere behind her voice, an engine starting. "Two agents on Cent right now. If anyone so much as looks at her wrong, I'll know inside thirty seconds. Rosa and Desmond converging on your position. Rogers is pulling the coordinates to assess independently before anyone moves on them."

"And if it's real? If they've got someone inside the school?"

"Then we handle it properly. Together. Not with you walking alone into a trap built to exploit exactly the instinct you're fighting right now." Something gentled slightly. "I know what they're counting on, Davy. I've read your file too. They're counting on the version of you that existed before you had backup. That version doesn't exist anymore. Let the rest of us prove it."

I stood in my kitchen, hands shaking, every cell wanting to jump straight to those coordinates and end this myself — and made, instead, the harder choice, the one that had taken twenty years and a family and more therapy than I'd ever admit out loud to learn how to make.

I stayed. Waited. Let the people who'd actually shown up do their jobs, hands braced against the counter, breathing through every instinct screaming that waiting was the same as failing.

Rosa arrived first, at my back door within two minutes, weapon holstered but hand near it. Desmond followed shortly, having swept the property, confirming nothing currently threatened the house itself.

And I called Millie, hands still shaking, and told her everything — the actual proof of how far I'd come. A man standing in his kitchen, terrified, choosing honesty over silence and solitary sacrifice.

***

12

She was back in under two hours, which felt like much longer sitting on my own porch doing nothing but watch the road.

Millie sat beside me the whole time, not saying much, just present, her hand finding mine every few minutes. Rosa and Desmond had positioned themselves at opposite corners of the property. Inside, Priya kept Cent occupied with something that sounded, through the screen door, suspiciously like an argument about which Marvel movie had the best soundtrack — a small, deliberate kindness.

We didn't talk about what might be happening at those coordinates. Nothing useful to say that wouldn't just be borrowed fear dressed as conversation.

Natasha's car pulled in fast, gravel spitting under the tires, and she was out and moving before the engine had fully died, Steve Rogers climbing out of the passenger side a beat behind her.

"Empty building," she said, no preamble. "Decoy. They wanted you moving, alone, predictable, away from the house. When you didn't take the bait, that told us something."

"What," I said.

"Tells us the call was a test, not the actual plan. Old instinct against new support. Wanted to see if you'd still run alone, after everything." Her jaw was tight, controlled the way I'd started recognizing as her holding real fury just barely in check. "You passed. Which means whatever they actually intend next, they're gonna have to do it the hard way. Through all of us, not around you."

"That's not exactly comforting," Millie said.

"Not meant to be comforting. Meant to be accurate." Her eyes were already moving, scanning the yard, the tree line, the road, the same automatic sweep now without appearing to think about it.

HYDRA has tried this before, more than once, and failed far more decisively than they managed against your family. I believe combining our efforts could prove really valuable, for both our peoples."

"I'd welcome that partnership formally, on SHIELD's behalf," Fury said, though something careful in his tone suggested the actual terms would require more discussion. "I want to be equally direct, though. My organization's had a breach. Two, if we're being honest about the analyst and whatever compromised our holding facility. I won't ask you to extend trust to an organization that's already proven vulnerable, without addressing that vulnerability properly first."

"I appreciate the honesty. It's rarer than it should be, in this kind of conversation." T'Challa considered his next words. "What would you propose?"

"Limited information sharing, initially. Nothing that would expose Wakanda's own security infrastructure to risk. We build trust incrementally, verify it holds, before expanding the scope."

"That's a reasonable position." T'Challa nodded. "I would add one condition of my own. Any information regarding the Rice family's ability, or Home's technology, remains under joint custody — neither organization holds it unilaterally."

"That's fair. More than fair, honestly, given everything that's already gone wrong on my end." Fury considered it further. "I'd want the same protection extended to whatever Wakanda shares with us."

"Agreed completely."

They spent the next hour working through the actual mechanics — communication protocols, verification procedures, careful limits on what each organization would share and when.

"You're taking this seriously," I said, once they'd finally paused for a break.

"Alliances built carelessly tend to fail exactly when they're needed most," T'Challa said. "I've watched that happen, more than once, in my own country's history. I'd rather build this one properly, even if it takes longer."

"That's a good philosophy."

"It's an expensive one, in terms of time and patience. But I've found it consistently worth the expense." He studied me a moment. "Your family's shown remarkable capacity for exactly this kind of patient trust-building. That's not a small thing, given everything you've already survived that might reasonably have taught you the opposite lesson."

"We had good teachers. My dad, mostly. Twenty years of learning the hard way that isolation doesn't actually protect anyone."

Shuri found us there eventually, settling onto the porch railing.

"You two done being extremely serious and official?" she asked. "Because I've got the equipment loaded, and I promised Cent I'd actually explain the vibranium medical applications properly before we left."

"We're nearly finished," T'Challa said, fond and long-suffering. "Try to contain yourself a few minutes longer."

They left an hour later, the whole delegation's departure carrying much more warmth than its arrival had.

"This is a lot of new people," Millie said, quietly, once they'd finally gone.

"It is." I turned the observation over. "But it's the right kind of a lot, I think. Every new ally makes the whole network stronger. And watching how carefully T'Challa and Fury actually built this, rather than just assuming trust — that matters too."

"I think you're right." Millie leaned into me. "Feels strange, doesn't it? How much bigger our lives keep getting, every single time something scary happens."

"Strange good, though," I said, echoing something Cent had said herself, months ago, meeting Charlie for the first time. "Strange good, every time."

The portal opened in our backyard on an otherwise ordinary Saturday afternoon, gold light spilling across the grass, and Strange stepped through with Wong close behind, both carrying the focused intensity I'd come to associate with really significant developments.

"Sorry for the unannounced visit," Strange said, though his tone suggested the apology was mostly formality. "We felt something. Twice, actually — once during your daughter's involvement with the tear at Home, and again during the improvised gate device your team disarmed. Wanted to investigate the pattern directly."

"Reality carries certain reverberations, when something like a tear opens or closes, especially one involving worthiness-based stabilization rather than merely brute mechanical force." Scholarly interest underneath the gravity. "What your daughter and Faith accomplished at Home, and what Bruce managed disarming that failsafe device — both represent significant interactions with the fundamental structure connecting your various realities. Cent joined the conversation shortly after, engaging with Strange and Wong with the same confident curiosity she'd brought to her conversation with Shuri days earlier.

"I think it's because we chose to let people in, every single time," I said. "Every crisis, every actual connection extended and accepted — it just kept building into exactly this."

We held the real celebration a month after everything finally settled — not the tense, hastily-assembled gathering after the first raid, not the formal Council proceedings at Home, but something closer to an ordinary Sunday, everyone actually present, nobody bracing for the next call.

Joyce's backyard filled up gradually through the afternoon, the same sprawling, improbable cast that had become, somehow, simply how our family looked now. Thor manned the grill. Faith brought bread, unprompted this time. Natasha and Steve arrived together, a lot more relaxed than either had looked in months. Fury came too, standing near the fence with a plate he mostly didn't touch.

Joe drove up from Stanford for the weekend specifically for it.

"This is a lot of people who love you," he said, simple and true.

"Yeah. Took a while to actually earn all of it, but yeah." I leaned into him. "Feels different than the party after Home. That one still had fear underneath it. This one doesn't."

Dad found us a while later, watching Mom deep in conversation with Miriam near the porch. "How's it feel? Actually being done, this time."

"Good. Really good." I considered it properly. "Different than after the first raid, though. That one felt like relief. This one feels more like arrival. Like we finally got where we were actually headed this whole time."

"I keep thinking about the whole shape of this year," Dad said. "How much changed, starting with just a car parked on our street. Serendipity Cliffs. Apex Orbital. Everything with Home. All of it growing out of that one scary morning."

"Feels like it should've broken us, doesn't it. Instead it just made everything bigger."

Fury drifted over as the afternoon wound toward evening.

"I wanted to say something before I head out," he said. "This case — your family's case — it's the one I'll remember longest, out of a very long career. Not because of the scale, though that mattered. Because of how you all chose to face it. I don't get to see that often. Wanted you to know I noticed."

"We had a lot of help," Dad said, the same old refrain, still fully true.

"You did. You also chose to use it every single time, which is the part most people struggle with." Fury extended a hand. "Reach out anytime. I mean that. Not just for crises."

He left the way he always did, unhurried.

The evening settled slowly, guests drifting out in twos and threes. Charlie and Clara left through the gate last, both pausing at the threshold.

"Same time next month?" Charlie asked.

"Same time next month," I confirmed, and watched them go, Clara's hand already finding his as the portal sealed behind them.

I found my parents on the porch once the yard had finally emptied.

"Good year," Dad said, watching the last light fade.

"Good year," Mom agreed, leaning into him.

"The best," I said, and meant it completely — not because the danger was gone forever, not because nothing could ever threaten us again, but because I'd finally, properly understood the actual shape of courage my father had spent two decades learning to practice: not the absence of fear, but the deliberate, repeated choice to face it surrounded by people who'd proven, over and over, they weren't going anywhere.

Not an ending. Just a very good chapter, closing gently into whatever came next.
