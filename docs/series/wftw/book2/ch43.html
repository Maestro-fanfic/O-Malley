Chapter 43

I found Ilsa deep in technical argument with Tony and Bruce in the coordination center's back workshop, the three of them surrounded by diagrams that meant more to them than to me, and settled in to listen.

"The problem with your proposed shielding," Ilsa was saying, tracing a line across one diagram, "is that it assumes threat vectors similar to what your reality typically produces. Ours are different. We've spent thirty years building defenses against scarcity-driven desperation, not sophisticated technological infiltration. I don't think your standard countermeasures translate as directly as you're assuming."

"That's a fair critique," Tony said, studying the diagram with real consideration rather than his usual reflexive confidence. "Walk me through what you'd propose instead."

"Layered redundancy, but distributed rather than centralized. A single point of failure, however well-defended, remains a single point of failure. We learned that lesson considerably too well, historically." Ilsa sketched a different configuration, something Bruce leaned in to examine. "Multiple independent verification systems, none of which alone controls access, all of which must agree before anything actually sensitive gets shared or accessed."

"That's slower, though," Tony said.

"Slower and much harder to compromise through a single vulnerability, the way your organization's already been compromised twice this month." No real accusation, just plain fact. "I'd rather build something that costs us convenience than something that costs us everything, given what we've all already survived losing."

Bruce, quiet through most of the exchange, finally spoke. "I think she's right, honestly. We've been building security like we're the only ones who understand the threat landscape. Home's survived considerably longer, under far worse pressure, with a philosophy we haven't properly incorporated yet."

Tony considered that, visibly recalibrating. "Okay. Can work with distributed verification. Walk me through the actual implementation, though — want to understand how you're proposing to balance security against actually being able to use the system when it matters."

They spent the next several hours working through the honest technical complexity, Ilsa's careful, hard-won caution meeting Tony's rapid, confident engineering in a way that produced, gradually, something neither could have built alone.

"This is remarkable," I said, at one point, watching a design take shape that felt considerably more thoughtful than anything I'd seen from either side individually.

"Good collaboration usually is," Ilsa said, not looking up. "The best solutions rarely come from either side simply imposing their own methodology. They come from truly listening to what the other side learned the hard way."

"That's a good philosophy for more than just security architecture."

"I've found it applies rather broadly, yes." Something almost like a smile. "You'll notice I correct your terminology constantly, by the way. Same principle, smaller scale. Precision matters, in both directions. I'd rather be annoying and accurate than easy and wrong."

"I've definitely noticed. Know what a proper oak tree is now, for the rest of my life."

"You're welcome," Ilsa said, entirely serious, and returned to her notation.

By evening, they'd produced a genuine, workable framework — not finished, not perfect, but real.

"I want to say something," Bruce said, as they wrapped up. "This collaboration — Home's patience, Tony's speed, all of us actually listening instead of just defending our own approach — I think this is actually the model going forward. Not just for security. For everything this alliance is going to require."

"I think you're right," Tony said, uncharacteristically humble. "Don't usually say that. Enjoy it while it lasts."

Ilsa allowed herself a real smile at that, the first I'd seen from her all day.

41

We gathered at my place three days after everyone finally headed home, the full Five plus, for the first time, an actual sixth member none of us had ever expected to add — Cent, sitting cross-legged on our kitchen floor with the easy comfort of someone who'd become part of the group rather than just an occasional visitor.

"Okay, I need the full story," Joey said, for what had to be the fourth time since the crisis actually resolved. "Not the summary version. The full, dramatic, blow-by-blow version, with appropriate sound effects if necessary."

"I've told it four times already, Joey."

"And it gets better every time. That's how good stories work. Repetition with escalating drama."

"It's not escalating. I'm telling you the same true events."

"Escalating drama, Cent. Keep up."

Marie rolled her eyes, though there was real fondness underneath the exasperation. "Ignore him. We all just want to make sure you're actually okay, underneath the whole heroic-reality-sealer thing you apparently pulled off."

"I'm okay. Tired, still processing some of it, but okay." Cent turned her mug over in her hands. "Honestly, the scariest part wasn't even the tear itself. It was the half-second right before I decided to try closing it, where I didn't know if it would work or if I was about to make everything worse."

"That's the scary part of basically everything important, though," Rick said, quiet as always but carrying real weight when he spoke. "The half-second before you commit. Everything after that's just execution."

"That's weirdly profound, Rick."

"I have my moments. Mostly muted ones, but they happen."

Clara, characteristically, had already moved past the emotional processing into practical planning. "So what's the actual long-term plan? Home's apparently entering some kind of formal cooperation agreement with Earth's defense structures. More of this? More crises, more coordination, more danger, on an ongoing basis?"

"Probably some, yeah," I admitted. "Osei was pretty clear this doesn't mean the threat's fully resolved forever. Just that we're not facing whatever comes next in total isolation anymore."

"I can work with 'not total isolation,'" Marie said. "That's a considerable upgrade from where Home was even a month ago."

"Definitely an upgrade," Joey agreed. "Also, speaking of upgrades, can we talk about the fact that I met an actual Norse god and he laughed at one of my jokes? Been trying to bring that up organically for the past three days and nobody's given me a proper opening."

"You've mentioned it at least six times, Joey."

"Six organic opportunities. I stand by the strategy."

Despite everything — the real fear we'd all carried through the past month — the kitchen dissolved into easy laughter.

"I want to say something," I said, once the laughter settled. "This past month — watching all of you show up for something that started as my family's crisis and ended up touching all of yours too — I don't think I've ever felt luckier to have exactly the friends I've got."

"You'd have done the same for any of us," Rick said, simple and certain.

"I would. Doesn't make it feel less remarkable, though, having it actually happen."

Marie reached over, squeezed my hand briefly. "That's what the Five's always been, Charlie. Whatever this turned into — Six, I guess, now — that's the whole point. We show up. Every time, whatever it costs."

"Six has a nice ring to it," Cent said, something moved in her voice despite the lightness of it. "I like being part of a Six."

"Welcome officially, then," Joey said, mock formality. "There should probably be some kind of ceremony. I'm workshopping ideas. Give me a week."

"Please don't workshop a ceremony, Joey," Marie said, though she was laughing again despite herself.

We spent the rest of the evening in easy company, working through homework nobody particularly wanted to do, trading stories about our entirely different lives.

Cent left through the gate late that evening, promising, as always, to be back soon. Watching the portal seal shut behind her, I felt grateful for the strange chain of events that had led an ordinary kid with a family secret into friendship with someone from an entirely separate Earth.

42

I went back to the clinic the following Monday, my first day back since everything started, and sat across from my first client of the morning surprised by how strange and grounding the ordinary rhythm of the work felt after weeks of crisis management.

"You seem different," Sarah said, partway through our session, a client I'd been seeing for the better part of two years. "Not bad different. Steadier, somehow. Did something happen?"

I weighed how much to share, old professional boundaries against the growth I'd actually experienced this past month.

"Something did happen," I said, finally. "A family crisis. Frightening, for a while. But we came through it — together, in a way that taught me something I think I'd been circling for years without quite landing on."

"What's the lesson?"

"That accepting help isn't weakness. That letting people see you scared, letting them carry some of the weight alongside you, doesn't diminish your own strength. It just means you're not facing everything alone." A rueful smile. "I've been telling clients that lesson for fifteen years, professionally. Took my own family's crisis to actually finish learning it myself, in my bones instead of just in my training."

Sarah considered that. "That's helpful, actually. Hearing you say that. Makes the advice feel less like theory."

"It is real. Hard-won, but real." I held her gaze. "I think that's true for you too, Sarah. Whatever you're carrying about the marriage, about what comes next — you don't have to carry it alone either. That's not a failure of strength. That's what strength actually looks like, most of the time."

We worked through the rest of the session with that thread woven through it, and by the end I felt energized despite how tired the past month had left me.

Davy picked me up that evening.

"How was the first day back?" he asked, once I'd settled into the truck.

"Good. Strange, going from crisis management to ordinary clinical work, but good strange. Grounding." I reached for his hand across the console. "I told a client about what happened, actually. Not the details, but the shape of the lesson. Felt important to let the experience inform my work instead of keeping it entirely separate."

"How'd that feel?"

"Good. Real. Like letting two parts of my life that used to feel separate finally talk to each other." I turned the thought over. "I think that's true for us too. This whole ordeal — don't think it's something we just survive and move past. Think it's actually made both of us better at the ordinary work of living."

"I'd agree with that. Feel it too. Some kind of deeper trust in what we've built together. Twenty years, and this past month tested it more than almost anything else we've faced. It held. More than held. Got stronger."

We drove the rest of the way home in comfortable quiet, the late afternoon light going gold across the familiar streets.

Cent was waiting on the porch when we pulled in, phone in hand, deep in conversation with Willow or Charlie or one of the Five.

"Good day?" she asked.

"Really good day," I confirmed, settling onto the porch steps beside her, Davy joining a moment later.

"I love this," Cent said, quietly, mostly to herself. "This exact thing. Ordinary evening, ordinary porch, everybody actually here and actually okay."

"Me too," I said, and meant it, and the three of us sat there as the evening settled into dusk.

43

Steve stopped by alone, a week after everything at Home had finally settled, finding me in the workshop sorting through the same box of climbing gear I'd been organizing on and off since this whole ordeal started, apparently still no closer to actually using any of it.

"Figured I'd say a proper goodbye before heading back to more regular duties," he said, leaning against the doorframe with easy, unhurried presence. "Wanted to do it in person, not just a phone call."

"Appreciate that." I set down the carabiner I'd been holding. "You heading somewhere specific?"

"Fury's got me looking into a few other threads related to the wider HYDRA network. Nothing urgent, nothing connected directly to your family anymore, but work that needs doing regardless." He studied the workshop a moment. "Wanted to tell you, before I go — this assignment meant something to me. More than most of what I usually get pulled into."

"How so?"

"I've spent a long time fighting HYDRA in the abstract, mostly. Institutions, ideologies, faceless threats spread across decades and continents. This was different. One family, actually terrified, choosing to fight back together instead of letting fear isolate them the way it's isolated so many people I've watched HYDRA touch over the years." He met my eyes directly. "Watching you and Millie and Cent do that gave me something I don't get very often in this line of work. A good outcome, achieved by people choosing connection over isolation."

"That's generous of you to say."

"It's not generous. It's accurate." A small, real smile. "I don't offer empty compliments, Davy."

We talked a while longer, easier now, the professional distance from our first meetings dissolved into something closer to real friendship.

"For what it's worth," he said, eventually, preparing to leave, "if you ever need anything — not just crisis-level emergencies — you've got my direct line now. That's not standard practice."

"I appreciate that."

"Take care of your family, Davy. You've built something remarkable here."

He left the way he usually did, unhurried, no dramatic exit.

Fury called that same evening, his own version of a goodbye shorter but no less meaningful.

"Wrapping up my own involvement here too, mostly," he said, without much preamble. "Wanted you to know the analyst's fully processed through our system now. Cooperation's been extensive, given the alternative he's facing. Using what he's given us to trace far more of the wider network than we'd have managed otherwise."

"That's good news."

"It is. Doesn't mean the threat's fully eliminated — won't insult you with false comfort on that front. But we're a good deal further ahead than we were a month ago, and that's largely thanks to your family's willingness to engage with this properly instead of just hunkering down."

"We had a lot of help."

"You did. You also made the choice to accept it, repeatedly, which not everyone manages." Something almost warm crept into his usually flat delivery. "I'll keep you informed going forward, same as I promised."

"I trust that you will."

"Good." A pause, almost hesitant. "For what it's worth, Mr. Rice — this assignment reminded me why I took this job in the first place, a long time ago, before it started costing me more than I sometimes remember it's supposed to give back. Watching your family come through this stronger, not smaller — that's worth remembering, on the harder days this work usually brings."

"Thank you, Director. For everything."

"Fury's fine, at this point. I think we're past the formal titles." Something almost like warmth colored the words, gone as quickly as it arrived. "Take care, Davy. I mean that."

He hung up before I could respond. Millie found me there, settling into the chair across from me.

"You look thoughtful."

"Steve came by. Fury called. Both saying goodbye, in their own ways." I turned it over. "Think we've made friends, out of all this."

"That's a good thing, Davy."

"It is." I reached for her hand. "I don't think I ever expected, twenty years ago, that surviving would eventually mean this — an entire network of people really caring what happens to us, long after the danger's passed."

"I think you're right," Millie said, and leaned into me.

44

Xander found Willow in the Magic Box's back room late one evening, weeks after everything with Davy's family had finally resolved, both lingering after Giles had already headed home.

"You've been quiet," he said, settling onto the stool beside her, watching her flip through pages of notes — sketches of the reality tear Cent had helped seal, careful annotations about Home's gate technology.

"Just thinking. About everything, honestly." Willow set the notebook down. "It's strange, you know? A month ago, this was Cent's crisis. Scary, personal, contained mostly to her family. And then it just grew. Home. HYDRA. An actual reality tear. All of it becoming everyone's crisis, all over again."

"Yeah. Been thinking about that too." Xander considered his own notebook, still open on the table. "Feels like our whole life's built that way now. Every crisis eventually touches everyone we love, because we've accumulated so many people we love, across so many separate realities, that nothing stays small and contained for very long."

"Is that a bad thing? Genuinely asking."

He considered it. "No. Don't think it is, actually. Used to think the goal was keeping things simple, keeping danger contained to whatever corner of my life could handle it without spilling over. But watching this past month — watching Cent step up, watching Davy and Millie finally learn to actually ask for help instead of hiding — think the accumulation's actually the whole point. Not a burden. A gift, weirdly, even wrapped in real terror."

Willow smiled, warm and honest. "That's a good way to put it. Keep thinking about Cent too. How much she's grown, even just since I first met her. That scared, careful kid who barely trusted anyone outside her own family, and now — sealing reality tears alongside Faith, part of an entire found family spanning three separate Earths."

"She's remarkable. Always has been, honestly."

"Yeah." Willow was still a moment. "Do you ever worry, though? About how big this is all getting. More realities, more threats, more people we love scattered across places we can't always reach fast enough."

Xander turned that over, wanting an honest answer. "Sometimes. Genuinely, sometimes I worry about exactly that. But then I think about this past month — how fast everyone actually mobilized, how completely we all just dropped everything the second Cent's family needed us — and I remember the size of the danger's never really the thing that matters most. It's the size of the people willing to face it together. And that's only ever grown."

"That's optimistic."

"Had a lot of practice being optimistic. Comes with the territory of my particular life." A real smile, tired but real. "Also, I've got you. And Buffy. And Faith. And now, apparently, an entire extended family that includes literal gods and actual Avengers and a whole civilization's worth of people who've decided we're worth defending."

Willow reached over, took his hand. "I love you, you know. Especially right now, in this exact moment. I love how you think about all this. Not with dread. With something closer to gratitude, even given everything scary about it."

"Learned that from watching Davy, honestly, this past month."

Buffy found them there eventually, dropping by after her own patrol.

"You two look thoughtful," she said. "Good thoughtful, or worried thoughtful?"

"Good thoughtful," Willow confirmed. "Mostly just processing everything with Cent's family. How big it all got, how well it all actually turned out."

"Yeah." Buffy's expression softened. "Keep thinking about that too. About how much bigger our whole family's gotten, this past year especially. Used to be just us, basically. Now it's everyone. Everyone who matters, all connected, all willing to show up."

"Everyone who matters," Xander repeated, finding it exactly right.

45

The message came through Fury's private channel at two in the morning, and I only found out because Natasha called our house directly, her voice carrying an urgency I hadn't heard from her since the night of the school parking lot.

"The analyst's dead," she said, without preamble, the second I picked up. "Found in his cell four hours ago. Not suicide. Somebody got to him."

I sat up straight in bed, Millie already stirring beside me. "Inside a SHIELD holding facility?"

"Inside a SHIELD holding facility, with security protocols that should have made that functionally impossible." Tight, controlled fury barely restrained underneath the professional delivery. "Fury's treating this as confirmation of exactly what we should have suspected from the beginning. Our analyst wasn't running this operation independently. He was expendable — a resource HYDRA was willing to sacrifice the moment he became a liability rather than an asset."

"Who's actually in charge, then? If it wasn't him."

"That's what we don't know yet. What we do know is whoever it is, they've got reach inside SHIELD's most secure facilities. That's not a small operational cell anymore, Davy. That's something far larger, far more entrenched than anyone initially assessed."

Millie had turned on the bedside lamp by now, listening intently, her expression tightening with each new detail I relayed.

"What does this mean for us? For Cent?"

"Don't know yet, honestly. That's the truth, not a reassurance." Real uncertainty in her voice. "Could mean nothing further, if their goal was purely covering their own tracks. Could mean more, if silencing the analyst was also meant to send a message — that they're still active, still capable, still willing to reach anywhere they need to."

"You think it's a message."

"Given everything we've learned about this organization's patience and reach, we can't afford to assume otherwise." A careful pause. "Fury wants a full briefing at the Magic Box within the hour. Pulling in everyone again — Steve, Thor, the whole team."

"When does this actually end, Natasha? Genuinely asking."

She was quiet a long moment. "I don't know. Wish I could tell you it ends cleanly, with a specific date, a specific victory. Organizations like this rarely offer that kind of closure. What I can tell you is we're not facing it any less prepared than we were a month ago. If anything, we're better prepared — better intelligence, better coordination, an actual alliance with Home now instead of isolated defense."

I hung up and sat there a moment, the old fear rising again, sharper this time, tangled with something new — a weary, hard-edged determination.

"They killed him," I told Millie. "Inside SHIELD custody. Whoever's actually running this, they've got reach we didn't fully understand yet."

Millie's expression carried the same mixture of fear and fierce resolve I felt myself. "Then we do exactly what we did last time. Face it together, with everyone willing to stand with us."

"You're not scared?"

"I'm terrified, Davy." She reached for my hand, steady despite the fear visible in her eyes. "But I've also learned, this past month, that terror and capability aren't actually opposites. I can be scared and still show up."

We woke Cent together, sitting on the edge of her bed as she surfaced from sleep into immediate, sharp alertness.

"What happened," she said, already sitting up, already reaching for actual clothes rather than pajamas.

"The analyst who was cooperating with SHIELD — he's dead. Killed inside custody. Whoever's running this reached him there, which means this is bigger than we understood." I kept my voice steady, honest. "Heading to the Magic Box for a briefing. Wanted you to hear it from us directly."

Cent absorbed that with a steadiness that made my chest ache with pride even amid the fear.

"Okay," she said, already pulling on her jacket. "Let's go find out what we're actually facing this time."

We jumped straight to the Magic Box, and found the room already filling with familiar, grim faces.

"This isn't over," I said, quietly, the same words I'd spoken weeks earlier, this time carrying a good deal more weary certainty than surprise.

"No," Fury said, turning to face our arrival, something grim and resolved in his single visible eye. "But we're not facing it the way we were a month ago. We're facing it together, properly prepared, with allies we didn't have before."

46

The briefing stretched well past dawn, the whole assembled team working through fragments of intelligence with the grim focus of people who understood, now, exactly how much more dangerous this situation had proven to be than any of them had initially assessed.

"Here's what we've confirmed," Fury said, pulling up security footage on Tony's display, grainy, partial. "Whoever did this had access to our facility's internal systems — not hacked from outside, accessed from within. That means another mole, or a compromised system we haven't identified yet."

"Another one," Steve said, grim. "Inside SHIELD, again."
"Possibly. Or the same network runs deeper than a single analyst ever suggested." Fury's jaw was tight. "Not ruling anything out, including the uncomfortable possibility that our organization's been compromised at a level considerably higher than one mid-tier analyst."

"That's a terrifying possibility," Willow said, quietly, from where she'd been cross-referencing data on her own laptop.

"It is. I don't intend to sit with terror, though. I intend to find the source." Real determination underneath the exhaustion. "Already initiated a full internal audit, far more invasive than the last one."

Bruce, who'd been evenly examining the footage himself, spoke up. "This timing bothers me. The analyst dies right as Home and Earth are formalizing cooperation. That's either coincidence, or someone specifically wanted to disrupt that alliance before it fully solidified."

The room went quiet.

"You think this is about Home," Miriam said, having arrived partway through the briefing, tight with fresh worry.

"Think it's worth considering seriously," Bruce said, careful. "If HYDRA's actual goal was always the gate technology, and direct assault isn't working, disrupting the alliance that would otherwise protect Home more effectively might be exactly the kind of long-game strategy an organization this patient would pursue."

"Divide and conquer," Faith said, flat. "Classic move."

Fury considered that seriously. "That's consistent with everything we know about their methodology. Patient, indirect, willing to sacrifice immediate assets for longer-term advantage." He looked toward Miriam. "I want to be honest — if this assessment is correct, Home may be facing renewed pressure, possibly more sophisticated than the initial breach."

"We're prepared for that possibility," Miriam said, though I could hear the fear underneath her controlled voice. "The Council's already discussing additional security measures. I'll need to update them on this immediately."

"I can have a technical liaison ready within the hour," Tony offered.

"I'll bring that to the Council," Miriam said, something grateful in her exhausted expression.

The briefing continued another hour, contingencies building on contingencies.

"What do we actually do next," I asked, once the immediate planning had settled into something like consensus.

Natasha, subdued through much of the deeper strategic discussion, finally spoke. "We hunt properly this time. Not reactive defense. Use everything we've learned — the facility, the analyst's confirmed connections before his death, whatever pattern emerges — and trace it back to whoever's actually running this, however deep that trail goes."

"That could take considerable time," Steve said.

"It could. I'd rather take the time and get it right than rush and leave this family, and Home, exposed to whatever comes next." Steady, resolved. "This isn't going to be one breakthrough. It's going to be days of tedious work — financial records, communication patterns, personnel histories, most of which will lead nowhere."

"Then let's actually start," I said.
