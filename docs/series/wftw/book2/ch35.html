Jesse showed up unannounced four weeks before the sedan, having talked Xander into a group jump specifically to "personally inspect the rocket situation," and spent the first ten minutes examining our kitchen like it might be concealing an actual spacecraft behind the refrigerator.

"Okay, so where's the rocket," he said, opening a cabinet that contained, disappointingly, only cereal.

"There's no physical rocket, Jesse. Jump-based delivery system."

"Deeply anticlimactic. I came here expecting at minimum a small, tasteful rocket. Possibly with racing stripes." He closed the cabinet, undeterred, dropped into a kitchen chair with the sprawling comfort of someone who'd never once worried about overstaying a welcome. "So explain the actual mechanics like I'm an idiot, because Xander tried already and I got lost around 'orbital velocity.'"

"You are an idiot, for the record," Xander said, arriving behind him. "Wanted that established before Cent starts explaining physics."

"I contain layers, Xander. Some idiot-shaped. Doesn't mean all of me is."

"Never heard you use the word 'layers' unironically before."

"First time for everything. Growing as a person." Jesse turned back to me, real if slightly chaotic enthusiasm. "Seriously, though. Walk me through it."

I spent twenty minutes explaining it, and found myself charmed by how many sharp follow-up questions he asked once he settled in.

"Okay, that's cool," he admitted, once I'd finished. "Also deeply terrifying, given the whole teenager-solving-problems-NASA-hasn't-solved thing, but mostly cool."

"Thanks, I think."

"High praise from me. I don't hand it out easy." He grinned in a way that made me suspicious. "Unrelated follow-up. Does this mean you could, hypothetically, deliver something to orbit for a bet."

"What bet."

"Ongoing situation with Cordelia about whether a hot dog is a sandwich. I've floated that photographic proof of a hot dog reaching low Earth orbit constitutes decisive symbolic victory, on grounds that reaching orbit is objectively more impressive than being correct about sandwich taxonomy."

"That's the least logical argument I've ever heard."

"I contain multitudes, several illogical." Delighted with himself. "So is that a yes on the orbital hot dog."

"Extremely not a yes."

"I'll let you think it over. Offer stays on the table." He leaned forward with the specific, transparent energy he got right before asking something he already knew was inappropriate. "Unrelated. Purely scientific follow-up. This Avengers situation — is Natasha as intimidating in person as the internet says, or is that just very effective marketing?"

"Jesse."

"Purely academic reasons."

"Not academic reasons."

"Academic reasons buried somewhere under the other reasons." Unrepentant. "Also — Thor. Attractive in a normal human way, or attractive in a specifically alien-physics way that shouldn't technically be legal?"

"Not answering either question."

"Your silence speaks volumes." He turned to Xander, delighted. "Didn't even try to deny it. Basically a yes."

"I didn't answer because the question doesn't deserve one."

"Exactly what someone would say if it were secretly a yes." He held up both hands at my expression. "Retracting. Your face is doing a thing that suggests physical retaliation."

Xander groaned from across the kitchen. "Please tell me you didn't do the Natasha thing again."

"I did the Natasha thing again."

"Jesse. We talked about this."

"We talked, I heard you, I did it anyway, because growth is a journey and I'm apparently early in mine." He remembered himself. "Anyway, unrelated, need your help, Xander. Bribed Cordelia into a rematch on the Convergence trivia thing. Need backup, she's been studying, which feels like cheating given the original agreement was casual."

"You bribed her into a rematch of a game you already lost?"

"Bribed her into a rematch of a game I strategically lost, to lull her into false confidence before the real rematch. Whole plan. Had it for weeks."

"That's not how bribery works, Jesse."

"It's exactly how bribery works. You're just bad at long cons." He turned back to me. "Anyway. Rocket girl. Rooting for you, genuinely, orbital hot dog and inappropriate Avengers commentary notwithstanding. Objectively the coolest thing anyone our age has ever done, and I say that as someone who's personally watched a literal god eat an entire pizza in one sitting."

"Strange comparison."

"I contain strange comparisons. Part of my whole thing." He stood, already restless. "Actual reason I'm here — Cordelia wants you as neutral judge for the trivia rematch, since I'm apparently 'too invested in psychological warfare to be trusted with scorekeeping.'"

"That's fair, honestly."

"Deeply unfair, but I'll allow it today." Heading for the door, Xander already laughing behind him. "Think about the hot dog thing, though. I really think it could work."

"It's not going to work, Jesse."

"That's what they said about my last four schemes. Two actually worked." He paused at the threshold, grinning back. "History's on my side, Cent. Think about it."

He left before I could argue further.

Millie noticed the car before she noticed anything else was wrong, which was, in hindsight, exactly backward.

Parked on Cypress a full block down, nose-out, the way you'd park if you wanted to leave fast. Same gray sedan, government-plain, three mornings running now. She clocked it from the kitchen window while the coffee finished — automatically, the way she clocked most things these days, an old habit that had never quite worn off no matter how many peaceful years piled on top of it.

They'd bought the house two years ago, after Cent turned fifteen and made it clear, blunt the way she'd inherited from both of them, that she wanted an actual town instead of a compound in the desert. Real neighbors. A real street. A school she could walk to instead of jumping into from some hidden staging point three miles out. Davy had resisted at first. Millie remembered the exact conversation, him pacing the kitchen of the old place with cold coffee in his hand, running through every reason a fixed address was a liability. She let him talk it out, then asked one question — when's the last time anybody actually came looking for us — and watched him not have a good answer.

Four years since the last confirmed activity from anyone connected to Hyacinth's old network. Four years since either of them had needed to check a mirror twice on the way to the grocery store. By the time they signed the papers on Cypress Street, it had felt like maybe they'd earned it. An ordinary house. An ordinary street. A daughter who got an ordinary childhood, or as ordinary as childhood ever got in a family where the father could fold space and the mother had built a career on understanding what that kind of secret does to a person.

Cypress Street didn't feel ordinary this morning.

She didn't say anything at breakfast. Cent was going on about some argument with Willow over email — a hot dog, a sandwich, an argument apparently running a year now across two entirely different realities — and Davy was reading, actually reading, a paperback propped against the syrup bottle, which meant he hadn't looked at the window at all. He read like that most mornings now. Peaceful mornings. Mornings where nothing needed watching.

"You're quiet," Davy said, not looking up, turning a page with his thumb.

"Thinking."

"About?"

"Nothing yet. Give me till lunch."

He looked up at that. Not alarmed. Just looking at her the way he still did after twenty years, like whatever she said next mattered more than the book.

"Cent, you're gonna be late," Millie said, and the moment dissolved into backpack-and-shoes chaos, Cent's usual scramble compressed into the eleven minutes actually remaining before the bus. Davy hugged her at the door, same as every morning. Millie caught herself watching that too, differently than usual — not just the ordinary fondness of watching your husband love your kid, something more clinical underneath it, some old part of her already cataloguing exit routes without deciding to.

Cent left. The house went hushed. Millie stood at the sink with the breakfast dishes, hands in warm water, and let herself finally think about the car properly.

Three mornings. Same car, same spot, gone by nine each time. Not the NSA — she knew NSA parked ugly, knew it in her bones from a decade of watching for it during the worst years. This was newer. Cleaner. A rental, maybe — the kind of vehicle chosen specifically because it disappeared into a residential street instead of standing out.

She thought about Home. About Ilsa's team, the gate technology, the whole careful reconnection that had finally, after twenty years of silence, brought her back into contact with the civilization she'd once belonged to. She thought about the Convergence file, and Fury's own warning at the end of it — classified doesn't mean it stays that way forever — and felt the old cold settle into her stomach.

She dried her hands and found Davy in the workshop, sorting a box of climbing gear he had no plans to use, the way he did most mornings when he wanted his hands occupied while his mind worked something out that had nothing to do with carabiners at all.

"Someone's watching the house," she said, from the doorway.

He didn't ask if she was sure. Twenty years had taught him not to ask that, not with her.

"How long?"

"Three days I've noticed. Could be longer. Wasn't looking before that."

He set the carabiner down carefully, the way a man sets something down when he doesn't trust his hands not to close around it. "Hyacinth's people again?"

"Nothing's come back active. I checked." She had checked, twice. "This feels different. Cleaner."

"Cleaner's worse."

"Cleaner's worse," she agreed.

They stood there a minute. Outside, a lawnmower started somewhere down the block, ordinary Tuesday sound.

"I'll take Cent to school," Davy said finally. "See if it follows."

"And if it does?"

"Then we'll know a lot more than we did an hour ago." He picked the carabiner back up, turned it over once, set it down again. "We knew this might happen eventually. Reconnecting with Home. Sunnydale. The Convergence, the report, all of it becoming a matter of record somewhere. Doors open both ways, Millie. We always knew that."

"I know." Millie leaned against the workbench, the wood cool under her palms. "I just hoped we'd get more than a few good years first."

"We got more than a few good years first," Davy said, and reached for her hand, steady even though she could feel the tightness underneath it. "That's not nothing. Whatever this turns out to be, that part was real."

"When did you get so wise about this stuff?"

"Married a therapist. Some of it rubs off." A tired, real smile. "Go call whoever you need to call. I'll take Cent to school, see what follows. We'll know something concrete by lunch instead of circling the same fear all morning."

She nodded, and neither of them said anything else for a while, just stood there in the sawdust-smelling quiet, hands linked, the lawnmower still going somewhere down the block.

Dad drove me to school, which never happens, because driving takes twenty minutes and jumping takes zero, and the only reason Dad ever does zero-minute things the twenty-minute way is because he's checking something.

"Okay," I said, once we were moving. "What are we checking."

"Nothing. Wanted the drive time."

"We've had eleven years of you not wanting drive time. Not buying it."

He didn't answer right away, which was its own answer. He checked the mirror three times in two blocks — Dad-code for something's actually wrong, not just Dad being Dad. His hands go still on the wheel, exactly ten and two, whenever he's actually paying attention instead of driving on autopilot. I've been cataloguing Dad-tells since I was maybe seven. Private research project. Mom doesn't know the full extent of it. Not sure she'd approve.

"Someone's parked near the house the last few mornings," he said finally. "Mom noticed. We're seeing if it follows."

"Follows us, or follows you specifically, because you're the one who can vanish if it gets weird."

"Would you rather I lied to you?"

"I'd rather you drove faster so we find out." He almost smiled at that — the real one, not the reassuring-parent one. I can tell them apart. The reassuring one's too even, too held, like he's checking a box marked comfort child. The real one crinkles crooked, mostly on the left. I like that one better. Doesn't happen enough, given how objectively funny I am.

We drove the rest of the way mostly quiet, which for us isn't awkward, just efficient. I watched the ordinary Rutherford County houses go by, ordinary lawns, ordinary basketball hoops, and thought about how strange it is, growing up somewhere this normal-looking while also, on weekends, occasionally adjacent to actual interdimensional crisis management. Nobody on this street knows that. Nobody ever will. Rule one, older than any rule Dad's actually written down — you don't tell people things that could get them hurt just for knowing.

Nothing followed. Ordinary Tuesday traffic all the way to school. Dad dropped me off looking like a man who'd wanted a problem to show up so he'd have something to do instead of just living with the not-knowing.

By lunch I'd made zero progress on schoolwork and considerable progress on a running list of everything slightly off about the last week — a car idling too long outside the grocery store, a woman at the library who'd definitely glanced twice, the school WiFi out twenty minutes Monday for no reason. I didn't sleep much that night. Millie noticed, because Millie notices everything, but didn't say anything until morning. One of about six hundred reasons I married her.

"You were doing the thing," she said, over coffee. "The staring-at-the-ceiling thing."

"I was thinking."

"You were doing the thing where you think about Wyoming until you're not thinking anymore, just running the same five minutes over and over."

She wasn't wrong. I'd spent most of the night back in that cell — the water, the drugs, the questions that never stopped being the same questions no matter how many times I answered them. Twenty years, and the file never actually closes. Just gets quieter. Sits further back. Waits.

A session with Millie's old colleague, years back, before Millie herself was who I trusted enough to talk to about it. He'd used the word integration. Hated it at the time — sounded like something you did to a fear, not something a fear did to you. You don't get rid of it, he'd said. You build a life strong enough around it that it stops being the loudest thing in the room. Took me another five years to understand he was right.

"It's probably nothing," I said, a lie, and Millie knew it was a lie, and neither of us said so out loud.

I jumped to the Aerie before breakfast, alone, just to stand somewhere nobody else knew existed. Same rock, same wind, same brutal indifference. Built it back when I had nothing else — no family, no safety net, just the ability to disappear and a desperate need for somewhere that disappearing could actually mean something. Twenty years later it's still the one place my chest fully unclenches. Millie's asked whether that worries her — whether needing a place entirely separate from her and Cent says something about how fully I've healed. Told her the truth: it's not about needing separate from her. It's about knowing the door's there. Makes it easier to choose to stay.

Here's what I knew, standing on that ledge with cold coffee in my hand. Somebody was watching the house. Clean, careful, government-adjacent or trained, three mornings running. Nothing had come back active in years — Millie had checked, and Millie doesn't check halfway. Which meant somebody new had gotten access to information that used to be locked down tight.

I thought about the report. The one Fury classified at the highest level he could grant. My name in it, probably. Millie's. Cent's. No way to explain Sunnydale without touching on how Cent's connection to the Rice family — no, our family now, the whole strange, expanded shape of it — had become the bridge between two realities that never once touched before we got pulled in.

Classified doesn't mean safe. Learned that the hard way, in a cell that smelled like bleach and fear, seventeen years old with no one to call. That lesson never gets old. Just gets reapplied, every time a new organization decides my family's worth the trouble.

I thought about Cent too — seventeen now herself, the exact age I'd been when everything first went wrong for me. Something twisted in my chest at that, though I knew it wasn't really a coincidence. Seventeen's just the age things start mattering to organizations like whichever one this was. Old enough to be useful. Young enough to be leverage. I'd spent every year of her life trying to build her a childhood different enough that this moment would never come. Watching it arrive anyway felt like a wave finding the one gap in a wall you'd spent years building.

I jumped back to the kitchen. Millie had the coffee going again, second pot. Cent's bike was gone from the rack — she'd left for school under her own power, not asking for a jump. She does that more when something's bothering her, not less. Like choosing the slow way is its own small act of control.

"I want to call Giles," I said.

Millie looked up from her mug. "That bad?"

"Don't know how bad yet. That's the problem. Want somebody who actually knows what 'classified at the highest level Fury can grant' means in practice, instead of me guessing off twenty-year-old paranoia that might not even apply here."

"Cent already texted Willow. Word's probably moving."

"Good." I meant it, fully, without the old flinch that admission would've carried five years ago. Twenty years ago I'd have handled this alone — pack a bag, disappear, sort it from a hotel room in some city nobody would think to check. Not that person anymore, mostly. Still costs something, every time, choosing the other way. Telling people. Letting the fear be something we carry together instead of something I carry alone and call protecting everyone else, when really, if I'm honest the way Millie's spent twenty years teaching me to be, it was mostly ever about not trusting anyone enough to let them carry it with me.

"You're doing the thing again," Millie said, gentler this time.

"Which thing."

"The disappearing-into-your-own-head thing. Come back. We're gonna figure this out. Together. Same as everything else."

I sat down across from her, wrapped both hands around my mug more for something to hold than because I wanted the coffee.

"Together," I agreed, and meant it, even though some old, stubborn part of me was already mapping exits, counting distances, doing the math on how fast I could get Cent somewhere safe if it came to that. That part doesn't fully retire. Made a kind of peace with that. Not the loudest voice in the room anymore, most days. That's the win, apparently. A fair fight, mostly won now, with help, in a kitchen that smells like coffee instead of bleach.

Giles called back that evening. Millie put him on speaker at the kitchen table, all three gathered around the phone.

"I've spoken with Director Fury directly," Giles said, formal the way he got when working hard to stay calm for everyone else's benefit. "He confirmed an internal audit flagged unauthorized access to the Convergence file roughly six weeks ago. Couldn't determine the access point immediately — whoever did it was, in his words, 'good enough to worry me.'"

"Define good enough to worry him," Davy said.

"Good enough that Fury's people are still working out precisely how it happened, rather than simply confirming that it did." Pages turning somewhere in the background. "He asked me to pass along that he takes this extremely seriously, and has initiated a full internal review."

"That's not the same as knowing who did it," Millie said.

"No. I wish I had better news. I'd rather give you the accurate picture than a comfortable one."

"We'd rather have the accurate one too," Davy said. "Comfortable's gotten us in trouble before."

Cent had been quiet through most of this, picking at the table edge with one thumbnail.

"Does he know if it's connected to the car outside our house?" she asked.

"He couldn't confirm that. Couldn't rule it out either." Giles' voice softened. "I'm sorry I don't have more certainty for you."

"It's not nothing, though," Davy said. "Knowing it's real. Knowing we're not just spooking each other over a parked car."

"No. Not nothing." A pause. "For what it's worth, Fury's taking this personally. An internal breach of this scale, in an organization he's built considerable trust around — he's not a man who'll let it go unresolved. I've dealt with a great many people in positions of authority, in more universes than I ever expected, and his particular brand of still fury is not one I'd want directed at my own doorstep."

"That's good," Millie said. "I just wish it came with a name attached."

"As do I." He cleared his throat. "He did ask one thing specifically. Wants to know if you'd accept someone assigned, quietly, to keep an eye on the situation from a distance. Not intrusive. Just present, in case anything escalates before his own investigation catches up."

Davy and Millie exchanged a look — twenty years of exactly this look, weighing safety against intrusion.

"Who," Davy said finally.

"Natasha, if she's amenable and available. Already familiar with your situation, given her involvement in Thor's search. Fury thought you'd find her presence less alarming than a stranger's."

Millie let out a breath. "Natasha's fine. Better than fine. Liked her, the little I got to know her during the Convergence work. She struck me as somebody who understood the difference between watching over someone and watching someone. Not a distinction most agencies bother making."

"I'll let him know." A hesitation. "I am sorry this is happening."

"Not your fault," Davy said. "Appreciate you making the call yourself, though. Means something, having somebody explain it properly instead of a form letter."

"Of course. Do keep us informed. All of us here consider your family part of what we're protecting now, not an adjacent concern. Buffy's already asked twice today whether there's anything the rest of us can do."

Cent managed a small real laugh. "Tell her I said thanks. And tell her not to do anything dramatic yet. We don't even know what this is."

"I'll pass that along, though I hold out limited hope it fully takes."

The call ended. The three of them sat a moment in the ordinary quiet, the coffee maker ticking as it cooled. Outside, the neighbor's sprinklers kicked on, right on schedule.

"Well," Cent said, finally. "At least it's not just us being paranoid."

"Small comfort," Millie said.

"I'll take small comfort over none," Cent said, and reached for the last cold slice of pizza on the counter, and for a moment the kitchen just felt like a kitchen again.

"Want a family meeting tomorrow," Davy said, once Cent had gone up to her room. "Proper one. Whatever this turns into, I don't want to run it the old way — deciding alone, informing people after. Cent gets a voice. You too, obviously, but I mean it specifically about Cent. Not a kid I need to protect from information anymore. She's seventeen. Helped stop a reality from tearing apart six months ago. Earned a seat at whatever table we're setting up."

Millie reached across, covered his hand with hers. "Good instinct."

"Learned it from somebody," Davy said, and managed something close to an actual smile.

Natasha showed up two days later, and the first thing she did was walk the entire property perimeter before she even said hello, which I respected more than I respected most people's actual greetings.

She came on foot, not by car — I noticed because I'd been half-watching the road for an hour, restless, homework spread out unread on the porch table. Must've parked down the block and walked the rest, testing the approach. I watched her move along the tree line, slow, methodical, crouching now and then to examine something I couldn't see from the porch — a footprint, maybe, or just the lay of the land.

"You've got a blind spot," she said, finally coming around to the porch. "Northeast corner, past the tree line. I'll want to fix that before I settle in."

"Hi to you too."

That got the ghost of a smile, there and gone. "Hi, Cent. Good to see you again. You've got a blind spot on your northeast corner."

"Better." I closed my notebook. "You're staying, then. Not just a check-in."

"For a while, yeah. Fury wants someone here who already knows the shape of your family's situation. I qualify." She set her bag down on the steps, scanning the yard the whole time, a reflex she'd clearly stopped noticing herself perform. "Your dad around?"

"Aerie, probably. Does that when he's stressed. Goes and stands somewhere quiet."

"Smart habit."

"Drives Mom a little crazy. Works, though. Better than the alternative."

Natasha considered the house a moment — the roofline, maybe, or the distance to the neighbor's fence. "How are you doing with all this? Genuinely asking. Not the polite version."

I thought about lying, the easy reflexive kind you give adults so they stop looking worried, and decided against it. Something about how she'd asked made half-truths feel pointless before I'd even built one.

"I keep doing math I don't want to do," I said. "Six months ago I helped stop reality from literally tearing apart. Thought that meant I'd earned a break. Now there's a car outside my house and an actual spy in our guest room, and I'm doing math on whether the break was ever real or just a good few months between two scary things."

"That's an honest answer."

"You said you wanted the honest version."

"I did." She sat on the step beside me, closer to my level than most adults bothered getting. A thin scar along her jaw I hadn't seen in any of Willow's photos. "For what it's worth — the breaks are real. Even the short ones. I've had a longer career at this than you have, and the good months don't stop counting just because bad ones show up on either side. Took me longer than I'd like to admit to actually let myself enjoy the good stretches instead of waiting for the other shoe."

"That's either really comforting or really depressing. Can't decide."

"Both, usually." Something like real humor crossed her face. "Found the two rarely travel far apart, in this line of work."

"That's basically what Thor said. Almost word for word."

"Long flights together. Some things rub off." Natasha stood, brushing off her jeans, scanning the yard once more out of pure habit now. "Gonna go find your dad. Introduce myself, walk the Aerie's approach routes if he's willing to show me. Want to understand your family's safe spaces before building any new security around them."

"He'll show you. Weirdly proud of that place, given how much effort he put into keeping it secret."

"People usually are proud of the things that kept them alive." Something in her tone made that land heavier than the words alone should have. "You gonna be okay here?"

"I'm always okay here. Everywhere else is the problem lately."

"Fair," Natasha said, and headed off toward the tree line with the easy, economical stride of someone who'd learned never to waste a motion.

I pulled my notebook back out — not for the homework, to write down what she'd said, word for word. Started doing that recently. Keeping a running list of things people said that actually helped. The right sentence, saved, carries you through a surprising amount of bad weather later.

She came back an hour later, Dad trailing behind her, mildly impressed despite himself, already trading sightlines and rotation schedules with the easy shorthand of two professionals who'd recognized the other one knew what they were talking about. I watched from the kitchen window, Mom beside me doing dishes, and felt something in my chest ease slightly — proof, small but real, that whatever was coming, we weren't facing it with just the three of us anymore.

The man who showed up at the hardware store wore a suit that cost more than my truck, which was the first thing that told me this wasn't nothing.

I was buying hinges, replacing a cabinet door that had been sagging for months — the kind of ordinary Saturday errand that had become, the past several days, a small act of defiance in itself. Told Millie I wanted to run it myself, alone, no shadow. She argued five minutes before agreeing, on the condition I texted the second I left and the second I arrived. I'd texted both. Been in the store maybe fifteen minutes, comparing two nearly identical hinges, when I heard him.

The scrape of expensive shoes on a floor that mostly saw work boots. That registered first, before I even turned around. Twenty years of paying attention to exactly this kind of small wrongness had trained the reflex deep enough I didn't have to think about it anymore.

"Mr. Rice." Not a question. He already knew.

I set the hinges down slow, giving myself a second to look at him properly. Mid-forties, clean-shaven, the kind of composed that takes real practice — not natural stillness, cultivated stillness, someone trained out of every visible tell. Good suit, good shoes, nothing that screamed government or corporate or anything else you could name. That absence of a label was itself the most concerning thing about him.

"Do I know you?"

"Not yet. My name's not important right now. What's important is that I represent an organization with a real, professional interest in your family's unique circumstances." Easy, unhurried, like weather, standing a comfortable distance away, hands loose and visible — the posture of a man carefully trained in how to approach someone without triggering a defensive reaction, and who knew exactly why that mattered here. "We'd like to have a conversation. Somewhere more private than a hardware store, ideally."

"Not interested in conversations with people who don't give their names."

"That's fair." He didn't push, didn't crowd, just stood there patient in a way that felt more threatening than an outright threat would have. Patience deployed as a weapon. Something in my chest recognized the shape of it immediately, straight past thought, landing somewhere older. "Let me offer something instead. We're aware of the ability. Aware of the situation involving the reality you've recently reconnected with. We believe there's room for a mutually beneficial arrangement, rather than the kind of unfortunate misunderstanding that's marked your past dealings with less thoughtful organizations."

Hyacinth. Talking about her without saying her name. Dangling the comparison like bait, like I was supposed to be grateful he wasn't that.

"I've had this conversation before," I said, keeping my voice level. "Didn't go well for the people who started it."

"Aware of your history. Like to think we're offering something different." He reached into his jacket, slow, deliberate, wanting me to see every inch of the motion, and produced a plain card — nothing on it but a phone number. No name, no logo, nothing traceable. "Think it over. No pressure, no deadline. We're patient people."

"That is, I think, precisely the correct way to hold it," Thor said, pleased. "Fear does not vanish because allies stand beside you. It merely becomes a good deal more bearable."

We sat together a while, quiet under a sky with far more stars than my usual town-adjacent view offered.

19

The forty-eight hours Natasha had asked for turned into sixty, and I spent most of them doing the single hardest thing anyone had asked of me all week — sitting still.

Millie and I gave statements to Steve and Natasha both, separately and together, going back over twenty years of scattered, half-remembered detail. Names of agents who'd questioned me during the worst of it. Descriptions of facilities I'd only seen once, briefly, before jumping clear. A cousin of Hyacinth's own second-in-command who'd once mentioned, offhand, an "affiliated program" that never made sense to me and still didn't, except Steve's face had gone very still when I mentioned it.

"That's a name I recognize," he said, quiet. "Not confirmed, not something I can act on yet. But a thread worth pulling."

By the second evening, the operations room — our spare bedroom, thoroughly transformed by now into something closer to a command center — had accumulated enough information that Natasha called a second full meeting, focused now instead of merely urgent.

"We've traced the cell," she said, no preamble, pulling up a map on Tony's projection system, a location marked somewhere in rural terrain about ninety minutes out. "Former agricultural research facility, decommissioned fifteen years ago, sold through three shell corporations since, currently registered to an entity that traces back to exactly the funding structure Tony flagged two days ago."

"You're sure," I said.

"As sure as we can be without eyes on the ground. Satellite imagery shows activity — vehicle traffic, structural modifications inconsistent with an abandoned facility. Fits the profile." Grim, focused. "This is where they're operating from. Best lead we're likely to get."

"So we move," Faith said, already halfway out of her seat.

"We move carefully," Steve corrected, not unkindly. "This isn't a smash-and-grab. If HYDRA's running an operation out of this location, there's likely defensive infrastructure we don't understand yet, personnel numbers we haven't confirmed."

"What's the actual plan," Millie asked.

Natasha and Steve exchanged a look, some quick calculation passing between them. "Reconnaissance first. Confirm what we're dealing with before committing to anything larger. Want eyes on that facility within twenty-four hours, hushed, minimal footprint."

"I could get eyes on it faster than that," I said. "Jump close, observe, jump out. No footprint at all."

The room went quiet.

"That's not without risk," Natasha said, careful.

"Less risk than sending a team in blind. Been doing exactly this kind of careful observation my whole adult life. One skill in this entire operation I actually have more practice with than anyone else in this room." I looked around — Avengers, agents, a god, my own family. "Let me do this. Properly, with backup ready if something goes wrong."

Millie's hand found mine under the table, squeezing once — not permission, since that had never been how our marriage worked, but solidarity.

Natasha considered it. "Fine. But not alone. Want a spotter with actual tactical eyes."

"I'll go," Steve said, immediately. "Got the most direct experience identifying HYDRA infrastructure. If there's something recognizable there, I'll know it faster than anyone else in this room."

"And I want a hard extraction protocol," Natasha continued. "If anything goes wrong, anything at all, you jump clear immediately. No hesitation. Confirm?"

"Confirmed," I said, and meant it.

The plan came together over the next several hours — extraction protocols, check-ins, contingencies stacked on contingencies. Cent hovered at the edges of the planning, subdued, clearly wanting to argue for her own involvement and just as clearly understanding this wasn't a fight she'd win.

"Just come back," she said, finding me alone in the kitchen that evening, the reconnaissance scheduled for early the following morning. "That's truly all I'm asking."

"I will." I pulled her into a hug. "I promise you, Cent. Not doing the old thing. Not disappearing into this hoping it resolves itself. Doing this with backup, with a plan, with people who'll notice immediately if something goes wrong. That's the whole difference now."

"I know." She held on a moment longer than usual. "Just wanted to hear you say it out loud anyway."

"Anytime," I said, and held on until she was ready to let go.
