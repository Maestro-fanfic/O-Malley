Chapter 12

The monitor came online on the third day, well ahead of Bruce's own estimate, mostly because Tony had shown up with equipment that made "borrowed" a generous description and refused to sleep until the thing actually worked.

"Okay," Bruce said, stepping back from a setup that had taken over most of the workshop's far wall, wires and borrowed satellite hardware feeding into a display that currently showed nothing more dramatic than a flat, steady line. "In theory, this reads the same resonance signature Marie isolated in the archive data, but live. If the Keepers shift from passive observation to something more active — evaluation, judgment, whatever the actual mechanism turns out to be — we should see it here before anyone tells us directly."

"In theory," Tony repeated. "I love a plan that starts with 'in theory.' Really inspires confidence."

"It's a good plan," Marie said, already cross-checking the calibration against her own model. "The baseline's solid. If it spikes, it spikes for a reason."

They didn't have to wait long.

The line jumped at 2:14 in the morning, sharp and sudden, steep enough that Bruce was out of his chair before the alarm he'd rigged even finished its first tone, the whole household stirring awake within minutes at the sound of it.

"That's not routine observation," Marie said, already pulling up comparison data with hands that weren't quite steady. "That's active. That's — Bruce, that's a pattern shift I don't have a baseline for. This isn't like anything in the archive."

"Meaning what," Davy said, already at the door, already positioned between the monitor and the rest of the house without seeming to have decided to do it consciously.

"Meaning something's actually happening," Bruce said, "somewhere in whatever process the Keepers use to evaluate a case like this. I don't know if that's good or bad. I know it's not nothing."

Ashgrove was awake within the minute, pulling her coat on over sleepwear with the brisk efficiency of someone who'd trained herself, long ago, never to be caught unprepared. She studied the readout for a long moment, something crossing her face that Millie recognized, from their conversation the night before, as plain uncertainty rather than practiced calm.

"I don't know what this specifically indicates," she admitted. "I've never had instruments capable of reading it directly before. But if I had to guess, based on everything I do understand about how these evaluations typically proceed—"

"Guess anyway," Fury said. "We don't have time for you to wait until you're certain."

"I believe a decision is being made. Not the final one, necessarily. But something has moved this from theoretical review to active consideration, faster than I expected, faster than is typical." Ashgrove's jaw tightened. "Which means whatever argument we intend to make on this family's behalf, we may have far less time to make it than I originally believed."

"How much less," Millie asked.

"I don't know. Days, possibly. Perhaps less." Ashgrove looked around the room — the whole assembled roster, gods and scientists and kings and librarians, gathered in the middle of the night around a machine reading signals from a decision none of them could see directly. "I would suggest we stop preparing as though we have the luxury of time we don't actually have."

Thor set down the mug he'd somehow already acquired despite the hour, expression settling into something far more serious than his usual easy warmth.

"Then let us stop preparing," he said, "and start deciding what we actually intend to say, together, when the moment to say it finally arrives."

The line on the monitor held steady at its new, higher baseline, an unfamiliar rhythm nobody in the room yet knew how to read, and the household settled, in the small hours of the morning, into the focused, wide-awake work of building an argument for something none of them could see coming, only feel, already, beginning to arrive.
