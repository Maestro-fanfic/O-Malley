Chapter 7

They came through the gate at exactly the hour they'd promised, which Millie noted quietly to Davy as somehow more unsettling than if they'd been late — Home rarely managed that kind of precision without wanting it noticed.

Four delegates, not one liaison this time. Charlie recognized none of them, though his mother's posture shifted subtly at the sight of the woman leading them, something between old respect and old wariness settling into her shoulders.

"Councilor Ashgrove," Millie said, formal in a way Charlie had rarely heard from her. "Wasn't expecting you personally."

"Wasn't expecting to need to come personally," the woman said. Older than Millie, silver-threaded dark hair pulled back severe and practical, dressed in something between a uniform and ordinary clothes that seemed engineered to communicate authority without quite announcing it. Her eyes swept the assembled room — Avengers and family alike — with the unhurried thoroughness of someone taking inventory rather than being impressed. "You've assembled quite a gathering, Millie Rice. I'd heard rumors. Hadn't quite credited how literally accurate they were."

"Circumstances warranted it."

"So I understand." Ashgrove's gaze found the whiteboard, still covered in Marie's careful math, the trajectory curve plotted out in careful, unhurried handwriting. Something crossed her face — not quite alarm, something closer to true astonishment held carefully in check. "You've confirmed it, then. The trend's honest."

The room went very still.

"You already knew," Davy said, low, guarded in a way Charlie recognized from every conversation that had ever touched too close to this family being watched by people they hadn't chosen. "You knew, and you let us find out on our own from a satellite anomaly instead of telling us directly."

"I suspected," Ashgrove said, sharp enough to cut through the accusation without raising her own voice, "for perhaps eight months. I did not have confirmation, and I did not have the standing to bring this to full Council attention on suspicion alone — not without risking exactly the kind of premature exposure that's now happened anyway, courtesy of a very observant piece of human technology I confess none of us properly accounted for." She let that settle. "I should have found a way to tell you regardless. I judged the risk of being wrong more dangerous than the risk of staying quiet. I see now that judgment cost you time you should have had to prepare for this on your own terms."

Steve stepped forward slightly, the careful, measured motion of a man positioning himself without quite making it look like positioning.

"Perhaps we start," he said, "with what you can actually tell us. Officially. On record."

Ashgrove studied him a long moment — soldier recognizing soldier, Charlie thought, some old professional respect passing between them that didn't need explaining.

"Fairly asked," she said, and took the seat Millie offered her, the other three delegates arranging themselves silent and watchful behind her. "Then let me tell you what Home actually knows, and what we've only ever suspected, and I'll be plain about which is which, because I think you've all earned better than diplomatic hedging today."

She folded her hands.

"The gate technology was not built by Home. We inherited stewardship of a small portion of it, generations ago, under terms none of us alive today negotiated and few of us fully understand. The mission's actual terms — the two conditions, stewardship or collapse — were set by the Keepers, not by us. What I do know is this: the Keepers do not act as one body. There are factions. There have always been factions. Most of what Home has ever dealt with is the moderate center — cautious, procedural, more concerned with maintaining balance across the doors they oversee than with any single Earth's fate."

"And what do the factions think about what we did," Millie asked. "Taking the mission into our own hands instead of just watching."

Ashgrove's expression tightened, weighing the honesty of her answer before giving it.

"That's precisely what's unresolved, and precisely why I came personally rather than sending a routine liaison. There is a faction — cautious, procedural, deeply invested in the mission staying exactly as originally written — who may see unilateral intervention as a violation of the very neutrality the mission depends on, regardless of the outcome it produced. And there is another faction who will see six years of results as the single strongest argument in the mission's entire history that the favorable condition isn't just theoretically possible, but achievable, on a timeline Home never dared hope for." She looked around the room. "I don't yet know which argument wins. I suspect, honestly, that both are about to be made forcefully, by people who've been waiting a very long time for a reason to have this particular fight."

"So we might get punished for succeeding," Cent said, quiet, working it through.

"Or celebrated for it. Possibly both, from different directions, before this resolves." Ashgrove's jaw tightened. "That should concern you as much as it might reassure you. It means there is no single verdict coming. Only an honest disagreement, playing out at a level none of you have ever had to navigate directly before now."

Fury, silent through most of it, finally spoke.

"And what happens," he said, "if the faction that doesn't like what this family did decides to make an example out of them."

Ashgrove met his eyes without flinching.

"Then Home will do everything within our authority to prevent that outcome," she said. "I will not pretend that authority is absolute. I will not pretend I can guarantee it. But I came here myself, today, specifically because I intend to argue this family's case personally, for as long as I am able to matter in the effort." She looked around the room — Avengers, family, the whole strange assembled shape of it — and something almost like relief crossed her face. "And I confess, seeing what's gathered here today, I feel a good deal better about the odds of that argument succeeding than I did walking through that gate an hour ago."
