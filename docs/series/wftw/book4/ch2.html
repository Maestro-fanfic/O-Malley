Chapter 2

The knock at the homestead door came three days later, and Cent knew before she'd even opened it that it wasn't the kind of visit they usually got — too formal, too deliberate, a car parked at the exact respectful distance from the house that suggested someone who understood, at least in theory, that they were arriving somewhere they hadn't been invited.

Fury stood on the porch alone, hands visible, the deliberate stillness of a man who'd learned a long time ago that showing up threatening just made everything take longer.

"Cent Rice," he said. It wasn't a question.

"Depends who's asking." Cent didn't move from the doorway. "You're not local."

"No. Name's Fury. I run an organization that spends a lot of time worrying about things that don't officially exist yet." He held up both hands slowly, produced nothing more threatening than a tablet. "I'm not here to make trouble. I'm here because six years of satellite data says your family's been quietly doing something remarkable, and I'd rather have that conversation with you directly than have it happen without you in the room."

Millie appeared behind her in the doorway before she could answer, wiping flour from her hands onto her apron, and went very still at the sight of the tablet, the careful, deliberate stance of the man holding it, the particular weight of a visit she'd apparently been half-expecting for longer than she'd ever said out loud.

"Millie Rice," Fury said, with a small nod, like confirming something he'd already suspected.

"You have my attention," Millie said, quiet, steady, already doing the fast, practiced math of a woman who'd spent her whole marriage learning to read exactly how much danger walked in with a stranger before deciding whether to let them past the threshold. "Show me what you think you know, and then we'll decide together whether you're someone I want inside this house."

Fury turned the tablet around.

Satellite imagery filled the screen — the Ethiopia site, the spectral anomaly glowing faint and strange across the reclaimed acreage, three other flagged locations pulled up beside it in smaller frames, years of data compressed into a handful of damning, beautiful images.

"Four years of unexplained signatures," Fury said. "All tracking back, eventually, to intervention work nobody's ever publicly claimed credit for. I had a man spend three days confirming there's no government program, no corporate initiative, no academic study that accounts for any of it. Just results. The kind of results that shouldn't be possible, coming from somewhere nobody's been looking."

Millie studied the screen for a long moment, something complicated moving behind her eyes — not quite fear, not quite relief, the look of someone finally setting down a weight she'd been carrying alone for longer than was reasonable.

"How much do you actually know," she said, "and how much is guessing dressed up as data."

"Enough to know it's not natural. Not enough to know what it actually is, where it comes from, or who else might already be looking at the exact same anomalies I am." Fury's voice stayed level, careful. "That last part's the reason I came here quietly, alone, instead of with a full team and a warrant I don't currently have grounds to get. If I found this, somebody else eventually will too. I'd rather you hear that from someone willing to have an honest conversation about it first."

Davy appeared behind Millie, wary, protective, the old careful stillness Charlie recognized from every conversation that ever touched too close to the family's actual history.

"And if we don't want to have that conversation," Davy said.

"Then I walk away, and I hope whoever finds this next is as steady as I'm trying to be." Fury met his eyes without flinching. "But I don't think that's actually the choice in front of you. I think the choice is who you have this conversation with, not whether it happens. And for what it's worth — six years of data, and not one flag I found suggests you've ever used any of this for anything but exactly what it looks like. Helping people. That matters to me. It's why I'm standing here asking instead of standing somewhere else giving orders."

Millie looked at Davy, some silent, decades-practiced conversation passing between them, and then back at Fury, weighing something Cent couldn't fully read from where she stood.

"Come in," she said finally. "But you're explaining exactly how much you actually know, in full, before I explain anything at all. I've spent too many years protecting this family to start trusting a stranger's tablet over my own judgment now."

"Fair," Fury said, and meant it, stepping through the door into a kitchen that smelled like bread and was about to become a great deal more complicated than either of them expected.
