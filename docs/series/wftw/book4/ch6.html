Chapter 6

Charlie's father had the Cessna out at first light, running preflight with the same unhurried thoroughness he brought to everything mechanical, like the airplane deserved the same respect he'd give a person he was about to trust with his life.

"Council meeting's not till tomorrow," Charlie said, holding the fuel sample cup while his father checked it for water and sediment against the pale morning sky. "We got a run to make today too?"

"Two, if the weather holds." His father tilted the cup, satisfied, poured it back. "Davy wants the second strip's grading finished before anybody official shows up here — man's not wrong that it looks better having two working runways than one, if we're about to have Home's whole Council standing around judging how serious we are about this place. And I want the fuel cached at the far strip before we're all too distracted with diplomacy to remember logistics matter regardless of how enormous the week's gotten."

They loaded the plane methodically — fifty-five-gallon drums lashed forward of the CG limit, weight and balance worked out on a scrap of paper his father kept folded in his shirt pocket, the same one he'd used every trip since the strip first went in. "Density altitude's going to bite us if we're not careful," his father said, checking the thermometer nailed to the hangar post. "Eighty-one degrees, five thousand two hundred field elevation — that's pushing seven thousand five hundred effective by the time we're loaded and it's properly warmed up. Cessna's going to want every foot of that runway and then some."

"Still good margins, though."

"Good margins if we respect them. Bad ones if we get casual because we've done this forty times already." His father climbed in, ran the checklist out loud from habit more than necessity at this point. "Fuel selector, both. Mixture, rich for the density altitude — don't lean it till we're established in the climb. Flaps, ten degrees for the short field. Trim set." He glanced over. "You want the right seat today, or you want to sit this one out and keep an eye on the ground crew?"

"Right seat," Charlie said, without hesitation, and climbed in.

The takeoff roll ate more runway than it would have on a cold morning, exactly as his father had predicted, the Cessna hauling itself off the graded dirt with the particular reluctant heaviness of an airplane doing plain work instead of just flying itself around empty. Charlie watched the airspeed indicator climb through the numbers his father called out loud — "Rotate's coming up around fifty-five today, little later than usual, that's the density altitude talking" — and felt the quiet satisfaction of watching a system work exactly the way math said it should, no surprises, no margin eaten by carelessness.

They leveled off at pattern altitude for the short hop to the far strip, grass and empty country rolling out gold beneath them in every direction, and Charlie realized he was thinking, not for the first time, that there was something almost unbearably ordinary about mornings like this — fuel drums and density altitude and a father teaching a son the same careful math he'd learned from his own father — sitting right up against something as enormous as satellite anomalies and interdimensional politics and a Council meeting that might reshape everything both families thought they understood about the door in the barn.

"You thinking about tomorrow," his father said, not really a question.

"Some."

"Good to think about it. Better not to think about nothing else." His father banked the plane gently toward the second strip, already visible as a pale scar through the grass ahead. "Whatever happens at that meeting, this—" he tipped his head, taking in the plane, the cargo, the ordinary work of the morning, "—this doesn't stop mattering. Families still need fuel cached. Strips still need grading. World doesn't pause just because the politics got enormous."

"Feels weird, focusing on runway grading with everything else going on."

"Feels weird because it is weird. Doesn't make it wrong." His father set the plane down clean, the wheels finding packed earth with barely a shudder, taxiing toward the cache point with the same easy confidence he brought to every landing on ground he'd helped build himself. "Your Uncle Max understood that, best I ever heard. Kept watching this land right up until the day he died out there, still waiting on a mission that never did resolve clean while he was alive to see it. Didn't mean the watching didn't matter. Meant he understood what mattered enough to keep at it, even without ever getting the answer himself."

They unloaded in comfortable quiet, drums rolled off and staged under the tarp at the strip's edge, and Charlie noticed he was, mid-task, thinking about his great-uncle in a way he hadn't quite let himself before — not as a name attached to an old grief and an empty canteen, but as a man who'd woken up most mornings and done ordinary, careful work on land he loved, right up until the world he was watching finally took more from him than it gave back.

*

Back at the homestead, the Council meeting logistics had taken over most of the main room, Millie and Fury bent over a rough seating chart while Willow cross-referenced diplomatic protocol against three different fragmentary sources with the dogged determination of someone who refused to walk into any room, interdimensional or otherwise, underprepared.

"Home's sending a full delegation," Millie was saying, when Charlie came in dust-streaked and smelling of avgas. "Not just the usual liaison. That alone tells me this is being treated as more serious than a routine check-in."

"Any word on whether the Keepers are sending anyone," Cent asked, from the corner, where she'd been quietly running the trajectory numbers again, still not quite believing them.

"None. And I don't know if that's reassuring or the opposite." Millie rubbed her eyes, the accumulated exhaustion of the last two days finally showing through the careful composure she'd been holding for everyone else's benefit. "If they're not coming, maybe this stays a manageable conversation with people we actually understand. If they are coming, and just haven't said so—"

"Then we should assume they're already watching whatever we do to prepare," Natasha finished, flat, practical. "Which means the actual meeting isn't tomorrow. It started the moment Marie's numbers confirmed the trajectory was unmistakable."

The room went quiet at that, the kind of quiet that settles over a truth nobody particularly wanted but everybody recognized as accurate the moment it was said out loud.

"Well," Tony said, into the silence, not quite managing to keep the edge of genuine unease out of his usual deflecting brightness, "good thing we're all on our best behavior, then."
