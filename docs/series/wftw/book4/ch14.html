Chapter 14

Shuri came through two days later, unable to stay away once M'Baku's reports back to Wakanda made clear exactly what kind of signal analysis problem had presented itself, and she took one look at Jane's data before either of them had properly finished introductions.

"You said structured," she said, already pulling up her own equipment, far more compact and far more advanced than anything currently occupying Bruce's workshop wall. "Structured like language, or structured like a heartbeat? Because those are very different problems, and I would like to know which one I am solving before I get too excited about solving it."

"Honestly not sure yet," Jane admitted. "There's a rhythm to it, but I can't tell if the rhythm's the message or just the carrier wave for something underneath."

"Then let us find out." Shuri had her equipment interfaced with Bruce's monitor within twenty minutes, working alongside Jane and Marie with the easy, rapid-fire shorthand of three brilliant people who'd each independently decided the fastest way through a problem was simply to stop being polite about interrupting each other's sentences.

"Okay," Shuri said, an hour in, leaning back from the display with an expression that mixed honest excitement and plain unease in roughly equal measure. "It's not a heartbeat. It's closer to a heartbeat that's trying to become a sentence. I don't think whoever's generating this signal has fully decided what they want to say yet. I think we're watching the actual formation of a decision, not just its aftermath."

The room went quiet at that.

"Can we read it," Millie asked. "Once it does decide."

"Perhaps. I do not know your Keepers' language, obviously. But structure this deliberate does not stay unreadable forever, not to people determined enough to keep working at it." Shuri glanced at Jane, something like unmistakable respect passing between them. "I would like to keep working at it. If that is acceptable."

"More than acceptable," Bruce said. "Honestly a relief. I was starting to feel out of my depth."

"You were doing excellent work regardless," Shuri said, generous about it in a way that clearly cost her nothing, a grin already tugging at one corner of her mouth. "I just have better toys."

*

Wanda arrived the following morning, summoned by a call from Steve that had been far more urgent than his usual calm phrasing, Vision a quiet, steady step behind her, and stood in front of the monitor's readout for a long moment before saying anything at all, eyes distant in the particular way that meant she was reading something the instruments couldn't quite capture.

"It's not just data," she said finally. "There's feeling under this. Genuine feeling, not just structure. Whatever's forming this signal — I think it's actually uncertain. Really torn. That's not something a machine generates. That's something alive, wrestling with an actual decision."

"Can you tell which way it's leaning," Ashgrove asked, careful, hopeful despite herself.

Wanda was quiet a long moment, concentration deepening.

"No," she said finally, something almost apologetic in it. "It's too far away, and too foreign, for me to read that clearly. But I can tell you it's not cold. Whatever's deciding this isn't deciding it coldly. That has to count for something."

"It counts for quite a lot, actually," Thor said, quiet, something in his voice carrying old, dearly bought knowledge. "I have stood before powers who judged coldly. This is not that. Cold judgment does not agonize over its own decision."

Vision, who had been quietly studying the readout himself, offered his own careful observation. "I don't have enough to say what this is. But I'd caution against reading too much into the uncertainty itself, one way or the other. My own mind began as something far less settled than what I eventually became. Uncertainty, on its own, tells you very little except that something is still becoming. It doesn't yet tell you what."

"Meaning what, exactly," Millie asked.

"Meaning I don't want to hand you false comfort dressed up as analysis," Vision said. "It would be easy to say the uncertainty means good news is still possible. It would be equally easy to say it means nothing at all. Honestly, I don't know which. I only know that treating uncertainty itself as evidence, in either direction, is a mistake I've watched people make before, and I'd rather not make it here."

Wanda glanced at him, something warm passing between them regardless, the old ease of two minds that had learned, over time, to trust each other's particular way of seeing things — including, especially, each other's honesty about the limits of what either of them actually knew.

Sam, arriving in time to catch the tail end of it, leaned against the doorframe with the easy, grounded skepticism of a man who'd learned to stay useful in rooms a good deal more chaotic than this one.

"So the good news is, whatever's judging you actually cares," he said. "The bad news is, caring and deciding in your favor aren't the same thing, and I've watched plenty of people agonize their way into a decision I really wished they hadn't made."

"Comforting," Davy muttered.

"I'm an honest-comfort guy, not a false-comfort guy. Figured you'd earned honest by now, given the week you've had." Sam pushed off the doorframe, crossing to the coffee pot with the unhurried confidence of someone making himself useful in the most practical way available. "For what it's worth, though — most people, cold or not, respond better to a room full of folks who clearly love each other than to one that's falling apart under pressure. You're doing better than you probably feel like you're doing."

The room gave way to that, small and steadying, one more voice added to a household that had, by now, gotten remarkably good at making space for exactly the kind of help each new arrival actually brought with them.
