Chapter 1

Marie had been staring at the same six years of yield data for three weeks, and the number that finally broke it loose wasn't even a big one.

0.4 percent.

Four-tenths of a percentage point, buried in a column she'd built to catch rounding errors in the Ethiopia reclamation numbers, not revelations. She almost dismissed it as noise the same way she'd dismissed forty other anomalies since she'd started cross-referencing the field data against Home's original baseline archive. Except this one repeated. Same seasonal offset, every single year since the program scaled up, like the land itself had learned a rhythm nobody had actually taught it.

"Cent." She didn't look up from the screen. "Come look at this before I convince myself I'm imagining it."

Cent crossed the workshop in four steps, orbital calculations abandoned mid-equation on the whiteboard behind her — a habit Marie had stopped commenting on months ago, the way half-finished physics just lived on every surface Cent occupied like weather. She leaned over Marie's shoulder, and Marie watched her go very still.

"That's not a measurement artifact," Cent said.

"No."

"That's the land recovering faster than the model predicts it should."

"Faster, and along a curve that matches almost exactly what Home's baseline archive logs as the threshold trajectory — the good one. Stewardship achieved, not collapse averted." Marie sat back, pulling her knees up onto the workshop stool, an old bracing posture Cent recognized from worse nights than this one. "I think what we've been doing out there for six years isn't just good work. I think it's moving the actual needle. Home's own numbers, and we're bending them."

Cent didn't say anything for a moment. Behind her, the whiteboard's equations sat unfinished, deorbit windows and burn calculations for a satellite recovery job that suddenly felt very far away.

"That's good news, though. Isn't it?"

"It should be. I keep waiting for it to feel like good news instead of just—" Marie gestured at the screen, at six years of careful, quiet, deliberately unadvertised work. "Instead of feeling like something that's about to get a lot bigger than the two of us keeping it a secret. Cent, if this trend holds, we're not looking at a decade-long project anymore. We're looking at something that scales. And things that scale get noticed."

*

Two states away, in a workshop that had seen far more late nights than any garage reasonably should, Tony Stark was arguing with a satellite.

"I'm telling you, that's not a sensor artifact," he said, to nobody currently in the room, pulling up the same anomalous imagery for the fourth time that hour — a stretch of reclaimed Ethiopian desert showing a spectral signature that didn't match anything in his materials database, not quite chlorophyll, not quite mineral, sitting there glowing faintly wrong across three separate satellite passes. "FRIDAY, run it again. Compare against every known agricultural tech signature we've got on file."

"Already done, boss. No match. Whatever's out there, it's not on any patent filing, any published research, or any government program I can find a paper trail for."

"That's not comforting."

"It gets less comforting. I cross-referenced the coordinates. Same general region's shown up in three other anomaly flags over the past two years — unexplained yield spikes, soil composition changes that don't track with any known intervention method, and one power signature eighteen months ago that read, briefly, like nothing on Earth."

Tony sat back, the easy deflecting energy draining out of him for a moment, replaced by the particular stillness that meant he'd stopped joking because something had actually gotten his attention.

"Get me Fury," he said. "Tonight, not tomorrow. And pull everything else you've got on unexplained tech signatures over the last five years. I want to know if this is one thing happening in one place, or if I've been ignoring a pattern."

*

Fury took the call standing in a SHIELD situation room that had gone quiet for once, no active crisis on any of the six screens currently glowing around him, which was exactly the kind of calm he'd learned, over a long career, never to fully trust.

"Stark. This better be good, I was almost home."

"Define good. I've got an unexplained tech signature sitting on top of the single most effective agricultural recovery project I've ever seen data on, in a region that had no business producing numbers like this without help nobody's accounting for. And it's not the first flag. It's the fourth."

Fury was already moving toward a terminal before Tony finished the sentence, the stillness of the room evaporating around him.

"Four years," Tony said. "Same general signature pattern, showing up quiet, staying quiet, getting more effective every time it shows up again. Whoever's running this either doesn't know how visible they've become, or doesn't care, and I honestly don't know which possibility worries me more."

"Send me everything," Fury said. "And Stark — keep this off anything that isn't need-to-know for now. If this is what I think it might be, I don't want it getting ahead of us before we understand what we're actually looking at."

"What do you think it might be?"

Fury was quiet for a moment, old instinct working through a shape he hadn't let himself consider seriously in years.

"I think," he said slowly, "we might have just found out somebody's been quietly saving the world for half a decade, and nobody thought to mention it to anybody who could help."
