Chapter 9

Giles worked the way he always worked when a text actually mattered — slowly, methodically, three books open at once and a fourth balanced precariously on his knee, muttering half-formed connections under his breath that nobody else in the room was meant to hear yet.

"The phonetic root," he said, eventually, loud enough to be addressed to the room rather than himself, "matches — not exactly, but closely enough I don't think it's coincidence — a designation that appears exactly twice in the entire Watchers' Council archive. Both references are old. Pre-dating any Slayer line record I've ever cross-checked, which for context means far older than anything else in those archives, and those archives go back a very long way."

"What's it say," Cent asked.

"Frustratingly little in the first instance, and rather more in the second." Giles adjusted his glasses, the old habit Xander had watched a hundred times across a hundred crises. "The first reference is a single line, buried in a section cataloguing what the original scribe calls 'doors not of our making.' It reads, roughly translated: the keepers judge intent as harshly as outcome, and mercy is rarely their instrument. No further elaboration. Unsettling on its own, but not yet useful."

"And the second," Millie said.

"The second is a good deal more relevant." Giles turned the book around, though the text meant nothing to anyone who couldn't read it, the gesture more habit than communication. "A brief account of an incident the scribe describes only as 'the reckoning of the Coelfrith line' — a family, it seems, who maintained some manner of doorway or threshold, not unlike your own, somewhere in what I'd guess was medieval Northumbria given the naming convention. The account states that the family, without sanction, began using access through their door to relieve a famine spreading through the surrounding lands — grain, seed stock, knowledge of storage methods, all drawn from beyond the threshold and given freely. Within a generation, the region's fortunes had truly turned. And within that same generation, 'those who judge such things' arrived to determine whether the family had served the greater design, or simply imposed their own will upon it."

"What'd they decide," Cent asked, quiet.

"That's the frustrating part. The scribe doesn't say, plainly — only that the Coelfrith line 'endured judgment, and were changed by it, though the doorway itself remained theirs to keep.' Which reads, to me, less like punishment and more like scrutiny survived. A test passed, at some cost the record doesn't specify, rather than a family destroyed for their initiative."

The room had gone very quiet.

"That's actually hopeful," Natasha said, careful, testing the shape of the word. "If that's the actual precedent. A family acts without permission, does plain good, gets tested rather than punished, and keeps what they built."

"I'd caution against too much optimism from a single medieval account translated through at least two languages I'm not entirely confident in," Giles said. "But it is, at minimum, evidence that 'endured without sanction' is not automatically synonymous with 'condemned for it.' The Keepers apparently distinguish between intervention that serves their actual aims and intervention that merely resembles it. That distinction is going to matter enormously for how Home's Council frames what's happened here."

"Which means the actual fight," Steve said, working it through out loud, "isn't going to be about whether you broke a rule. It's going to be about whether what you built proves the mission's unmistakable aim, or just imitates it well enough to complicate their judgment."

"That tracks with what Ashgrove said," T'Challa added. "Two factions arguing, not because the facts are in dispute, but because the interpretation is. History suggests that argument can be survived. It does not suggest it will be comfortable."

"Small comfort," Davy muttered, "knowing our best case is 'endured judgment and were changed by it.'"

"I'll take small comfort over none," Millie said, quiet, her hand finding his again.

Bruce hadn't said much, working steadily alongside Willow at a second laptop, but he looked up now with the particular expression of a man who'd found something and wasn't sure yet whether it was good news.

"I think I can build something useful out of this regardless of which way the judgment goes," he said. "Not a weapon — I want to be clear about that going in, because I don't think that's what this situation needs and I don't think it's what any of you would want regardless. A monitor. Something that reads for the same resonance signature Marie's already isolated in the trajectory data, but continuously, in real time, instead of retroactively. If the Keepers are actively evaluating this Earth right now, the way Ashgrove's account suggests, we should be able to tell the difference between routine observation and an actual verdict forming — a spike, a pattern shift, something we can see coming instead of finding out after the fact."

"How long," Fury asked.

"With Marie's baseline model, Giles's translation work, and Stark's equipment once he catches up with us tomorrow—" Bruce did a quick mental accounting, "—three days. Maybe four, if we want it reliable instead of just fast."

"Do it," Millie said, immediately, no hesitation. "Whatever you need from us, take it. I've spent six years not knowing whether the work actually mattered to anyone watching. I'd like to actually see the verdict coming, for once, instead of just waiting on it blind."

Thor, who had been quiet for a stretch uncharacteristic of him, set down the mug he'd been nursing and spoke into the settling silence.

"I have stood before powers who believed their judgment final, their authority beyond question," he said. "I will tell you honestly what experience has taught me about them, because I think this family has earned honesty over comfortable reassurance today." He looked around the room, meeting each face in turn. "Judgment of this kind is rarely as fixed as it presents itself. A power that sends factions to argue rather than simply issuing a verdict is a power still capable of being persuaded. You have already done the one thing that matters most in such a case, simply by having genuine results to show for six years of work, simply by refusing to let this become a quiet, comfortable secret any longer." He raised the mug slightly, an old gesture that needed no translation. "Whatever comes next, you do not face that judgment as one family standing alone at a door. You face it as this."

Nobody said anything for a moment, the weight of it settling easy and solid over a room that had spent the entire day accumulating uncertainty and hope in roughly equal measure.

"Well," Tony said, finally, arriving through the door a beat late with an armful of equipment cases and impeccably terrible timing, "glad I didn't miss the inspirational speech. Somebody catch me up on the whole cosmic-judgment thing while I set up, I work faster with context."
