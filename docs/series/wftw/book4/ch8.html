Chapter 8

"If we're calling this what it actually is," Fury said, once Ashgrove and her delegation had withdrawn to the porch to give the room space to talk freely, "then we're not looking at a family problem anymore. We're looking at a family that just became a test case for an interdimensional institution deciding, in real time, whether unilateral good works get protected or punished. That's not a two-agent problem. That's an all-hands problem, whichever way it breaks."

"You're thinking full roster," Natasha said.

"I'm thinking full roster." Fury was already reaching for his phone. "Thor's off-world, but off-world's exactly the kind of distance that stops mattering when the threat's interdimensional to begin with — man's dealt with more cosmic bureaucracy than the rest of us combined, and honestly, having an actual god in the room tends to focus everyone else's diplomacy wonderfully. Banner's stateside, and if we end up needing to understand technology none of us recognize, there's nobody better. Barton's two hours out. And Wakanda's got more experience defending against exactly this kind of outside-power overreach than anyone else on the planet — T'Challa's going to want to be part of this conversation regardless of whether we ask, so we might as well ask."

"And Giles," Xander said, quiet, the first thing he'd said since Ashgrove's revelation had sunk fully into the room. Everyone looked at him. "I know he's not exactly Avengers-caliber muscle. But he's spent thirty years being the guy who actually reads the old texts nobody else wants to touch, and if there's precedent anywhere — old Watcher records, anything about doors between worlds, anything about how outside powers have judged people like us before — he's the one who'll know where to look. Ashgrove's talking about factions and precedent like it's settled law somewhere. I want somebody on our side who actually knows how to read that kind of law."

"Good instinct," Fury said, already dialing. "Make the call."

Ashgrove, listening with the careful attention of someone cataloguing everything for a report she hadn't written yet, glanced at Millie. "This 'full roster' — I confess Home's own briefings on your world's defenders are somewhat dated. I wasn't aware the original team was still so completely intact."

"We got lucky," Fury said, flat, in the specific tone of a man who didn't examine that luck too closely for fear of jinxing it. "Whatever crisis your world's version of us usually gets tested by, ours never came. No cosmic invasion, no snap, no team torn apart trying to survive it. I don't know why. I've stopped asking. I just make sure we don't waste it."

"A fortunate divergence," Ashgrove said, something thoughtful in her voice.

"The most fortunate one I know of," Fury agreed, and left it there, already moving on to the next call.

*

They arrived over the following six hours in a scattered, mismatched procession that would have looked, from any ordinary vantage point, like the setup for a joke nobody had gotten to the punchline of yet — a god, a scientist, an archer, a king, and a librarian, converging on a homestead barn in rural nowhere, most of them arriving through methods the Rice family's fence posts had never remotely been built to expect.

Thor came through first, and not subtly — a crack of light and ozone at the edge of the strip that had Bessie-Two bolting for the far paddock and Charlie's father muttering something about needing a longer conversation regarding advance warning next time. Thor took in the assembled crowd, the whiteboard, the tension still hanging thick in the room, and set down the strange, elaborately wrapped item he'd been carrying with the easy confidence of a man who understood exactly how odd he looked doing it.

"I brought mead," he announced. "It seemed the sort of occasion that called for it, once Fury explained the shape of things. Also, I have some small experience with bureaucratic factions claiming authority over more than they've earned. I suspect I may be useful for more than the mead."

"We'll take both," Millie said, and meant it.

Bruce arrived quieter, driven up the last stretch by a SHIELD car that had clearly taken the unpaved road slower than strictly necessary, folder of borrowed equipment already under one arm before he'd fully gotten out. He took one look at Marie's trajectory data still glowing on the screen, and was crouched beside her within thirty seconds, the two of them falling into an easy technical shorthand that left most of the room several steps behind within a single exchange.

Clint came in low-key, the unhurried competence of a man who'd learned a long time ago that the loud entrances belonged to other people, nodded once to Natasha, once to Fury, and found a spot near the door that put eyes on every exit without anyone particularly noticing he'd done it.

T'Challa arrived last, through a means far more understated than Thor's — a quiet vehicle, an even quieter escort, and a bearing that filled the room's remaining tension with something that felt, oddly, like reassurance rather than additional weight.

"Councilor," he said to Ashgrove, on the porch, with the careful formal courtesy of one head of state acknowledging another, "Wakanda has some history with outside powers who believe our resources exist for their evaluation rather than our own stewardship, and a long history of arguing that a nation's — or a family's — right to use what they've built as they see fit shouldn't depend on someone else's permission after the fact. I don't say that to claim we understand your Keepers fully. I say it because I suspect this family could use someone in the room who's made that exact argument before, successfully, to people who very much did not want to hear it."

"I would welcome that perspective," Ashgrove said, and meant it plainly enough that even Davy, still tense and watchful in the corner, looked briefly, plainly comforted.

Giles came through the gate nearly two hours after the others, having apparently spent the drive to the airfield and the flight itself buried in three separate texts he'd grabbed off his own shelves the moment Xander's call came through, and walked into the crowded main room already mid-sentence.

"—and if the notion of an outside power judging a mortal family's unsanctioned good deeds sounds at all familiar, which I rather suspect it will once I've had a proper look, there's a reasonable chance the Watchers' own archives touch on something similar, several centuries back, in material nobody's had cause to properly cross-reference before now, because frankly nobody's ever had a case quite like this one to compare it against." He looked up, seeming to register the size of the room for the first time. "Ah. Hello, everyone. Sorry — I do get like this. Where's the data? I should like to see the actual trajectory numbers before I say anything else with false confidence."

Willow handed him the laptop without a word, and he dropped into the nearest chair with the immediate, total focus of a man who'd found, after thirty years, a problem truly worthy of it.

The room had, within a handful of hours, become something Charlie didn't quite have a word for yet — not a war council, not exactly a family gathering, some strange, crowded overlap of both, gods and scientists and kings and librarians and two ordinary families who'd started this whole enormous thing with nothing but a flag on a fence post and a summer that had refused, at every turn, to stay simple.

"Alright," Fury said, once the last of the introductions had wrapped up and the room's attention had gathered, heavy and ready, toward the center of it. "Let's find out exactly what we're dealing with."
