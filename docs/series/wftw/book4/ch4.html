Chapter 4

"Home didn't build the gate," Millie said, and the sentence landed in the room with a weight that made Cent realize, despite a lifetime of hearing bits and pieces of her mother's history, that she'd never actually heard her say it plainly before. "I don't think I ever told any of you that outright, because I didn't think it mattered — Home's who I grew up with, who taught me what I know, who I've spent my whole adult life trying to represent honestly out here. But the gate itself, the actual mechanism — that's older than Home. Home found it. Home's spent generations studying it, maintaining a piece of it, deciding who gets to walk through the piece they control. But they didn't make it, and they didn't write the mission's actual terms. Somebody else did."

"Then who did," Davy said.

"I don't know their name. I only ever knew the word Home uses for them, which isn't really a name so much as a description — the closest translation's something like the keepers, though that flattens a lot of nuance the actual word carries. Home's relationship with them isn't close. It's not hostile, as far as I ever understood growing up, but it's not warm either. More like — a landlord who occasionally shows up to check the property's being maintained to standard, and otherwise leaves you entirely alone to run your section of it. They're the ones who set the two conditions. Stewardship, or collapse. Watch and wait, either way, until one of them's actually met."

"But we didn't wait," Cent said, quiet, working it through out loud. "We didn't watch for stewardship to happen on its own. We went out and built it. That's not the same thing as what the mission actually says to do."

The room went still at that, the particular stillness of a question nobody had quite let themselves ask directly until now.

"So the actual problem," Marie said slowly, "isn't that we did something wrong. It's that we did something nobody ever gave us permission to do, and we don't know if the people who actually wrote the rules are going to see it as a solution or as somebody jumping the fence they were supposed to be watching from a distance."

"That's exactly the problem," Millie said. "I don't know how the Keepers judge intervention versus emergence. I don't know if there's even a meaningful difference to them between an outcome that happens naturally and one that gets pushed into being by six people with a truck and a lot of stubbornness. I never had cause to ask, because nobody in my family ever tried what you two just told me you've actually pulled off."

"So we might be in trouble," Charlie said, "for doing exactly the thing the mission was supposedly hoping somebody would eventually do."

"You might be. Or you might be about to become the first proof in the mission's entire history that the favorable outcome is actually achievable, which is either going to make Home celebrate you as the reason this whole project finally means something, or terrify them that a family acted unilaterally on a decision that was never supposed to be any single group's to make alone." Millie's hand found Davy's, the old, practiced gesture of two people bracing together. "I honestly don't know which reaction we're about to get. I don't think anyone in this room does."

Steve had said little since the meeting started, but he broke his silence now, choosing the pause carefully. "For what it's worth, that's not actually a bad position to be arguing from. You didn't hide anything malicious. You solved a problem the people watching for it apparently couldn't solve themselves. That's a strong case, whatever the reaction turns out to be."

"It's a strong case if anyone's willing to actually hear it that way," Natasha said, practical, already several steps ahead. "Institutions don't always reward the people who solve their problems without asking permission first. Sometimes they punish the precedent instead, regardless of the outcome."

Fury, who'd been listening with the particular stillness of a man weighing exactly how much to say, finally spoke.

"Which is why the timing of this next part matters," he said. "Home reached out to me directly, three days ago. Before any of this conversation happened. Wanted a meeting — Council-level, formal request, no stated agenda beyond 'matters concerning Designated Earth 7-Cascade requiring joint discussion.'" His expression didn't shift, but something in his voice did, the careful weight of a man who'd been sitting on a piece of the puzzle the whole afternoon, waiting for the room to catch up enough to need it. "I was planning to mention it eventually. Didn't expect it to turn out to be quite this relevant, quite this fast. But if Home's asking for a formal Council sit-down the exact week your own data confirms the trajectory's actually bending—"

"That's not a coincidence," Natasha said.

"No," Fury agreed. "I don't believe it is. I think Home's own instruments found the exact same thing yours did, and I think they're every bit as uncertain about what to do with it as you are."
