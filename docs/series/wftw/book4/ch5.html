Chapter 5

They flew back to Sunnydale that night instead of staying, Faith's call more than his — "everybody needs a room that isn't this one for a few hours, Xan, us included" — and Xander hadn't argued, mostly because she wasn't wrong.

The house was quiet when they got in, the particular late-night quiet of a home that had learned, over two years, to hold a lot of noise most days and none at all on the nights that actually needed it. Faith kicked her boots off by the door, dropped onto the couch without turning on more than one lamp, and Xander caught himself standing in his own kitchen for a solid minute before he registered he'd walked there at all.

"You're doing the thing," Faith called, not looking up.

"What thing."

"The thing where something enormous happens and you go make tea like it's a normal Tuesday because your brain needs your hands busy before it'll let the rest of you catch up."

He looked down at the kettle, already filled, already in his hand, and had to admit she had a point.

"In my defense," he said, "it kind of is a normal Tuesday. That's the whole horrifying thing about it. Somebody's been quietly running a planet-scale rescue operation for six years, all by themselves, no backup, no permission slip, and Tony Stark's satellites caught them before Home even fully understood what their own numbers were showing. There wasn't a dramatic reveal moment. There wasn't music. Marie just went 'huh' and then everyone's life got a lot more complicated."

Faith was quiet for a second, the kind of quiet that meant she was choosing her next words instead of just reaching for the first ones.

"You good?" she said, finally.

"Working on it." He set the kettle on the burner, more out of ritual than actual thirst at this point. "Keeps making me think about my own jumping, honestly. Four years I did that alone, nobody knowing, nobody watching, and it never once occurred to me that keeping it quiet might eventually become its own kind of problem — not because I was doing anything wrong, just because eventually somebody notices, and then it's not your secret to keep anymore. Davy's family just found that out the hard way, at a much bigger scale than I ever had to."

"That's a scary kind of found out. Not in trouble, exactly. Just — suddenly not private anymore, and not sure yet whether that's good or bad."

"Yeah." He leaned against the counter, watching her in the low lamp light, the particular stillness she carried on nights when the day had actually gotten to him. "Makes me think about what happens if I ever get found the same way. If somebody with a satellite decides my jumping's worth flagging too."

"You'd have people in your corner already, if that happened. That's kind of the whole point of tonight, right? Nobody's facing this alone anymore. Not them, not you either, if it ever comes to that."

He didn't say anything for a moment, the kettle starting its low building hiss behind him.

"When'd you get so wise," he said, eventually, the old deflection, offered soft instead of sharp.

"Told you. I have my moments." She patted the couch beside her, an invitation more than a command. "C'mere. Kettle can scream at nobody for a few minutes. I want you closer than the kitchen."

He came, because he always did, and the kettle did scream at nobody for a while, and neither of them minded.

*

Cent found her mother on the homestead porch a little after midnight, the rest of the house finally gone quiet, Davy asleep on the couch in the particular collapsed way of a man who'd spent the whole day holding something too heavy to keep holding once nobody needed him upright anymore.

"You should be resting too," Millie said, not turning.

"So should you." Cent sat beside her, the old porch boards creaking the way they always did under the second person's weight. "Keep thinking about what you said. About the Keepers not caring whether the good outcome happens naturally or gets pushed into being. Six years of work, and I never once considered that succeeding might be its own kind of risk."

"I never considered it either. That's the part that scares me more than getting caught, if I'm honest." Millie's voice had gone thin, tired in a way that had nothing to do with the hour. "I spent your whole life teaching you to be careful about being seen, about being found — never once occurred to me the actual danger might come from succeeding too well at the thing we were quietly trying to do all along."

"We didn't do anything wrong, though," Cent said, firm, the same steady certainty she brought to orbital math and family arguments alike. "We helped people. Real people, real land, real results. Whatever the Keepers decide about how we got there, that part doesn't stop being true."

Millie reached over, found her daughter's hand in the dark, and held on.

"When did you get so sure of things," she said, something almost like a laugh underneath it.

"Learned it from you. Don't tell me otherwise. I was there for all of it."

They lingered outside, the compound quiet around them, somewhere off past the strip a coyote calling once and going silent. Cent thought, not for the first time this strange, enormous day, that whatever came next — Council meetings, Keepers, a judgment neither of them could predict yet — this part, at least, the sitting quiet with her mother's hand in hers under a sky that had never asked permission to hold this many stars, wasn't going anywhere. Whatever else the day had cost them, it hadn't cost them this.
