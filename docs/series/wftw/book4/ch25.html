Chapter 25

The disclosure conversation happened three days later, around the same kitchen table that had hosted Council meetings and cosmic monitors and, now, something that felt far more like planning a birth announcement than managing a crisis.

"I keep thinking about how we tell people," Millie said, turning her coffee mug slowly between her hands. "Not whether. I think that decision made itself the moment Tony's satellites found us. But how. I don't want the first thing the world learns about this to be filtered through fear, or through some government's need to control the narrative before anyone else can."

"Then we control it," Jane said, simply. "We tell the story the way it actually happened. A family that spent years quietly trying to help the world, and something new and wonderful that came into being because of it. That's not spin. That's just true, and true is a good story to lead with."

"People are still going to be afraid," Natasha said, practical as always, though something gentler had crept into her usual bluntness these past few days. "Something this big, this unprecedented — fear's a natural first reaction for a lot of people, and I don't think we do anyone favors pretending that won't happen."

"Fear and wonder aren't opposites, though," Wanda said, quiet. "I've felt both, every single day since it first reached toward me. I think most people can hold both at once, if we give them an honest reason to hold the wonder alongside the fear instead of just the fear alone."

Fury, listening with the particular stillness he brought to truly hard decisions, finally spoke.

"I've spent a career controlling disclosures because the alternative usually meant panic, or exploitation, or both," he said. "I don't think this is that kind of disclosure, though. I think this is the rare kind where the truth actually helps you, if you tell it right. A newly-formed presence, born out of six years of a family choosing to help instead of exploit — that's not a threat briefing. That's the best news story anyone's had in years, if we let it be that instead of smothering it in classification the way I'd normally reach for."

"You're suggesting we just... tell people," Davy said, something like disbelief and hope tangled together in his voice.

"I'm suggesting we tell the right people first, carefully, and let the story's own truth do most of the actual work of landing well. Strange's people. Some careful science press, once Shuri and Jane are ready to publish something plain. Governments who've earned enough trust to be brought in as partners instead of just informed as a formality." Fury shrugged. "I've been wrong before about when caution helps and when it just delays the inevitable badly. I don't think this is a moment for maximum caution. I think it's a moment for getting ahead of the story with the actual truth, because the actual truth here is good."

Thor, who'd been quietly listening, set down his mug with the particular decisiveness he brought to moments that mattered.

"I would add this," he said. "I have watched worlds receive news of the miraculous before, across many realms, in many different ways. The worlds that received it best were never the ones most carefully shielded from fear. They were the ones given, alongside the news, an open invitation to actually witness the wonder themselves, rather than simply being told about it secondhand." He looked around the table. "Perhaps, when the time comes, we do not simply announce this. Perhaps we let people see it. Let them feel what we have felt, standing in that open ground. Wonder is far more persuasive experienced than described."

The table went quiet a moment, the shape of something truly hopeful taking form in place of the anxious planning Charlie had half braced for.

"I like that," Millie said, finally, something bright and unguarded breaking through her usual careful composure. "I like that a great deal, actually. Let people meet it properly, the way we did. Let the wonder speak for itself."

"That's going to take unmistakable planning," Natasha said, though her voice had lost most of its usual wariness. "Security, logistics, making sure it's actually safe for everyone involved."

"Then we plan it properly," Steve said. "Same as we'd plan anything worth doing right. Difficult doesn't mean it's not still, underneath all the logistics, a truly good thing to be planning."

The table folded into that, the practical work ahead still genuine, still substantial, but held now inside something that felt, for the first time since the satellite anomaly first surfaced, less like crisis management and more like getting to plan the best possible way to introduce the world to something wonderful.
