Chapter 22

Clara's mother came through Thursday exactly as promised, stepping through the gate barn's door with the brisk authority of a woman who'd seen stranger things than a second family homesteading an alien Earth, and spent the afternoon doing exactly what she'd threatened — walking the strip, inspecting the homestead, grilling me gently but thoroughly on everything the official summary had left out.

"You rode a horse straight at a stampede," she said, for the third time. "At your age. With your mother's blessing, I assume, since I can't imagine Miriam letting either of you do something that reckless without a version of permission attached."

"There wasn't time to ask permission. Cent was three hundred yards out and the herd wasn't waiting on approval."

"No," Sarah agreed, something softening despite her sternness. "Don't imagine it was. Good instincts, though, going for the horses instead of running yourselves. That's taught, not just felt — your father's doing, I'd guess, all those hours drilling flight math into you."

"Some's him. Some's just — you don't get much choice out here but to think fast and right, or you don't get a second chance to think slow."

Sarah studied me, something shifting into an appraising warmth. "Your mother tells me you two are solid," she said, nodding at Clara, who'd gone pink. "I'll tell you what I told her twenty years ago about her own father: the ones who make it aren't the ones who never disagree. They're the ones who ride toward the same stampede together instead of arguing whose job it was to go."

"Mom," Clara said, mortified, and Sarah laughed, moving on to interrogate Millie about crop rotation with the same thoroughness.

It was Marie who found me later that evening, after Sarah had gone back through the gate, and she had the urgency I'd learned to take seriously before she spoke.

"I've been running the historical logs," she said, pulling me aside from the porch. "Going back years further, because once I understood what to look for, I realized I should check whether anything like this had happened before, quietly, without anyone noticing."

"And?"

"Another spike. Older. Buried deep enough nobody ever flagged it, because nobody had reason to connect it to anything." Tablet out, scrolling a much longer timeline. "Eleven years ago. Smaller than the stampede spike, bigger than routine gate traffic. Lasted almost three days. As far as I can tell, nobody at the time figured out what caused it."

"Eleven years ago." I did the math without meaning to. "Before Davy and Millie ever got here. Before Cent existed, unless I'm doing that wrong."

"You're not," Marie said, quiet, careful. "Which means whatever caused that first spike wasn't Cent. Wasn't the Rices at all. Something else, unrelated, tripped this same measurement over a decade before any of us worried about a second door."

The porch light spilled warm across the yard behind her, everybody else still easy, riding the settled feeling of a hard problem solved — and I stood there feeling that solved feeling crack, quietly, right down the middle.

"You haven't told Mom," I said.

"Not yet. Wanted to be sure. Checked it four times, same answer, and I hate that I keep getting the same answer, because it means the story we all just told ourselves — new door, new family, one clean mystery, one clean resolution — isn't finished. Not even close." In the low light her face carried the same fear from the night after the stampede. "Something else has been on this Earth before. Something the watcher measured eleven years ago and never mentioned — not when it spoke, not in anything it said about doors or value. And I don't know if that's because it didn't matter to the conversation we were having, or because it's not finished happening yet."
