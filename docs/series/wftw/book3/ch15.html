Chapter 15

It was Millie who raised the idea first, three days after the stampede, once the shock had settled into ordinary caution and everyone had stopped flinching at Marie's tablet chiming.

"We need a strip," she said, over breakfast, flat and practical. "Real one, at the homestead, graded like yours is here. What happened to Cent — some version happens again, sooner or later, and I don't want our only fast help to be an eight-mile walk and a plane landing on unimproved grass."

Dad, who'd mostly stayed out of the last three days' drama, sat up with the focus he reserved for problems actually his to solve.

"How much flat ground close to the house?"

"Fourteen hundred feet, if you don't mind a slight grade at the north end. More if we clear another cottonwood stand."

"Fourteen hundred's workable, so long as the grade's consistent." Dad was already sketching. "Soil? Sandy, clay, mixed?"

"Mixed. Clay near the river, sandier at the house."

"Clay's a mixed blessing. Compacts hard once properly graded, but murder wet, cracks if the moisture's wrong during compaction. Time it for a dry stretch, and even then do it in stages — rough grade, let it settle, fine grade and compact after."

"How long," Davy asked. "Realistically."

"With the equipment we've got — call it three weeks, weather cooperating, nobody rushing compaction. Rushing that's how you get a strip that fails under the first heavy landing." Dad looked up. "Question's how we get the equipment sixty miles to your place. Not something Cent can jump — not in one piece, probably not disassembled either, not that weight."

"Fly it," Clara said. "Load it on the Cessna, or break it down for two trips."

"Compactor alone pushes the edge of what the Cessna carries safely," Dad said, working the numbers. "Could do it. Two, three trips, good weather each time." He glanced at Davy. "Or a faster way, if your daughter's willing, and we do it smart instead of reckless."

"What do you mean?"

"Break the compactor into component parts — engine, drum, frame, hydraulics — same as your sawmill years back. Series of jumps, each piece well under Cent's limit, proper rest between."

Cent sat up, real interest. "How many pieces?"

"Eight, maybe ten, to stay comfortably under your limit." Dad glanced at Davy for permission, the same respect he'd shown since the stampede. "Your call. Just laying out options."

Davy looked at his daughter, weighing the same protective instinct against her reasonable right to some say — smaller stakes this time, nothing like a stampede with half a second to decide.

"Small jumps," he said finally. "Well under your limit. Proper rest between each, no pushing fatigue just to hurry."

"I can do that," Cent said, real steadiness now.

"Then that's the plan," Mom said. "Dad coordinates disassembly and flight backup. Marie keeps instruments running, same protocol — we stop the second anything trends wrong."

"Once the strip's in," Millie said, "we're not two isolated families. Real transport, both directions. Help's minutes away instead of an hour."

"Something else comes with a real strip," Davy said slowly, an idea taking shape behind his eyes. "Supply runs. Not gold traded through channels I'd rather not discuss. If we've got a strip and gate access, that changes the whole economics. Proper tools. Proper stock. Never let myself think about it, no realistic way to get it there before."

"True," Mom said. "Also means more traffic across a boundary something's apparently watching closely. Not saying no. Saying we go in with eyes open."

"Eyes open," Davy agreed. "Only way any of this works."

Outside, Rick had wandered to Dad's compactor sketch, studying it with the intensity of someone who'd just found an engineering project and was already plotting how to make himself indispensable — and watching him, I thought that whatever this arrangement turned into, something real was already being built here, piece by piece, the patient way everything worth having on this Earth got built.
