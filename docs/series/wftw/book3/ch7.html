Chapter 7

Nobody said anything for a long moment, the silence carrying weight — Davy's jaw tight, Mom's hands flat on the map, Clara paler than I'd ever seen her.

"Show me," Mom said finally.

Marie already had the tablet out, the compound's instrument logs, a column of numbers that meant nothing to me and, going by Mom's sudden focus, meant a great deal to her. She traced a line with one finger.

"Baseline's been flat as long as we've had instruments on it — normal noise, weather, solar activity. Then here." She tapped a point where the line kicked upward. "Six days ago. Started climbing, slow, steady. Not a glitch. A genuine shift in what normal even means for that gate."

"And Cent's first jump onto this Earth," Mom said.

"Same week. Cross-referenced against Mr. Rice's own timeline — six months of homesteading, which puts the first arrival right at the edge of what our logs catch cleanly. Can't prove the exact day. Can prove the week, and can prove nothing else in that window explains a shift this size. No storms. No solar events. Nothing on our end."

Davy had gone still, doing the math on how much of this might be his family's fault. "You're saying my daughter's ability tripped some kind of sensor. Something that's watched this whole planet and never noticed a single footstep in all the years Mrs. Newell and the rest of you have been building and flying and mining here — and Cent walks through once, unaided, and that's what finally gets its attention."

"I'm saying the timing's uncomfortable to call a coincidence," Marie said. "I don't have a mechanism. Don't know what the gate's actual sensing apparatus is, or how it'd distinguish gate traffic from whatever your daughter does. I only have the numbers, and they say something changed the same week she arrived."

"Could be the opposite," I said, and everyone looked at me, which made me wish I'd stayed quiet. But the thought had been sitting there since Marie laid out the timeline. "What if it's not that Cent tripped something new. What if whatever's watching has always been capable of noticing unauthorized traffic, and it just never had anything to notice before, because nobody'd ever gotten onto this Earth except through the one door your family's tended. Maybe the baseline didn't shift because something's suddenly paying attention. Maybe it shifted because there's suddenly a second kind of traffic to measure, and the instrument's just reading the honest truth of that for the first time."

Mom looked at me the way she did when something reorganized her thinking instead of just adding to it.

"That's not a comforting distinction, Charlie," she said, "but it might be the more accurate one." She turned back to Marie. "Still climbing, or settled into a new baseline?"

"Still climbing. Slightly. Not fast. Not flat either."

"So whatever's happening isn't finished."

"No," Marie said. "I don't think it is."

Davy pushed back and stood, walking to the window, looking out at the gate barn and the ordinary-looking door in its wall. When he spoke again his voice had gone quiet enough the whole room leaned in.

"Cent's eighteen," he said. "Didn't ask for any of this. Neither did I — got this ability the way you get a fever, no warning, spent twenty years learning not to kill myself with it before I ever thought what it might mean to something watching from outside this planet. If there's a version where my daughter's existence put a target on this whole Earth, on your family, on mine — I need to know. Plainly. However bad."

"We don't know that," Mom said, careful. "We know a correlation. Not a mechanism. Not an intent. Whatever's watching this planet's been watching a long time without acting, so far as my family's ever determined. A shifted baseline isn't a verdict."

"Might be the beginning of one."

Mom didn't answer right away, and the honesty of her silence said more than reassurance would have.

"I think the responsible thing is getting Millie and Cent here properly," she said finally, "so we're not deciding an eighteen-year-old's future without her or her mother in the room. I think Marie runs these numbers around the clock instead of between chores. And Charlie, Clara — go back out tomorrow, first light, bring them in. Whatever's coming, I'd rather this family meet it standing together than scattered across sixty miles."

"I'll come with you," Davy said immediately.

"I'd rather you didn't," Mom said, not unkindly. "You've been up since before dawn, had one of the stranger days of your life, and if something's genuinely paying closer attention to this Earth, I'd rather my two most experienced hands make the run quiet and quick than escort a tired man who's never flown anything smaller than commercial. Rest. Eat something that isn't jerky. Let Charlie and Clara bring your family home."

Davy looked like he wanted to argue, then visibly didn't, exhaustion finally catching up.

"Home," he repeated, testing the word. "Funny, for a compound I walked into four hours ago."

"It's the word I'd use," Mom said, "for anyone under this roof who needs somewhere safe tonight." She looked around the table — Davy, Marie, Clara, me — something settling into her, the steadiness right before a hard call. "We'll figure out the rest together. That's always been the only way any of this works."
