Chapter 25

Mom went out to the gate barn alone that evening, after the research room emptied, and I found her an hour later at the threshold, arms wrapped against the cooling air.

"You're not asleep either," she said, without turning, the way Marie had said it to me two weeks before.

"Couldn't be. Not after today." I came to stand beside her, looking at the ordinary door, plain wood and iron in the dusk. "You think it'll answer, if you ask something."

"Don't know. It answered once, when the stakes were high enough. Don't know if that's a standing invitation or a single exception." She watched the door a moment. "Want to ask about the gaps shrinking. Need to know, one way or another, if that's something to actually fear."

"Should I stay?"

"Stay," she said, and something in her voice made clear that wasn't really a question either of us would've answered differently.

She spoke to the empty air the way she had two weeks before, out loud, and for a long moment nothing happened, the gate humming its ordinary hum.

Then the door went rigid on its hinges, the padlock seizing warm against the hasp where there should only have been cold iron, the view through it freezing mid-flicker.

Miriam Newell. You have a question you are afraid to ask plainly.

"I do," Mom said. "Marie found a pattern in thirty years of data. Six events. The gaps are shrinking. I need to know if that means something's approaching a threshold — if this Earth, or the people on it, are running out of time before some verdict gets rendered."

The gaps are not shrinking because a verdict approaches. They are shrinking because more people are here to be measured.

Mom went very still. "Explain that."

Thirty years ago, this Earth held one small, careful family, tending a single door, rarely tested past ordinary caution. A crisis worth measuring occurred once every decade or two, because there were few moments where the outcome genuinely hung in doubt. Today this Earth holds two families, a second means of crossing, children growing into responsibilities their parents never carried at the same age, a homestead built from nothing in six years, a strip built from nothing in nine days. More life invites more risk. More risk invites more moments worth measuring. The frequency is not a countdown. It is a consequence of growth.

"So it's not accelerating toward something," Mom said, slow, relief not quite catching up with understanding yet. "It's just more, because there's more of us."

Yes. And more of you, should this continue, means more still. That is not a warning. It is simply what happens when a quiet place stops being quiet.

I felt something loosen in my chest I hadn't realized was clenched. Beside me, Mom let out a long breath, the day's fear draining from her shoulders.

"Max," Mom said, quieter, and I could hear what it cost her to ask plainly. "You measured his death. Did it matter? Did we do right by him, however long it took to find him?"

The door held rigid a moment longer than for anything else, the padlock's warmth deepening, and when the answer arrived it carried something almost gentle.

He was alone at the end. I will not tell you otherwise, because you have spent too many years already being told comfortable things that weren't true, and you deserve better than one more. No one came. No one could have — he told no one where the door was, so no one knew there was anywhere to look. He wrote to you anyway, in what time he had, not because he expected it to reach you, but because writing to someone he loved was the last useful thing left to do. It reached you eventually. That mattered, though it took years, and could not undo what had already happened by the time it did. You have carried the shape of that loss every day since, without letting it make you smaller or more afraid than the man himself would have wanted. That is the entire measurement, Miriam Newell. Not whether the outcome was painless. Whether the people who loved him proved, in the end, worth the risk he took loving them — and whether the ones left behind kept building, rather than closing the door he died trying to protect.

Mom's composure finally broke, quiet tears in the low light, and I put an arm around her shoulders the way she'd done for me more times than I could count. The presence waited with us, wordless, patient.

One further thing, it said, once Mom had steadied. You asked, weeks ago, whether the arrangement required anything further regarding the second door. I told you it required only what you had already extended. I would add now — the two families standing on this Earth together, choosing daily to build rather than merely survive, choosing openness over the easier comfort of secrecy — that is not incidental to what this arrangement has always hoped anyone might become, given enough time and reason. You are not being tested toward some pass or fail. You are simply, finally, becoming the thing this door was always meant to make possible.

The padlock cooled, the door swung free on its hinges again with its familiar faint creak, the flicker resolving to ordinary wildside grass, gone as quietly as it arrived.

"I think," Mom said finally, wiping her eyes, managing something almost a laugh, "I owe Marie a long conversation about not scaring an old woman half to death with unfinished statistics."

"I think she'd say that's exactly why she brought it to you instead of sitting on it," I said. "Even when it was scary. Especially when."

"Yes," Mom agreed, looking back at the compound lights, warm and steady, two families' worth of ordinary life going on inside them. "I suppose that's the whole point we've all just spent a month learning."
