Chapter 12

We spent the morning turning flat ground behind the greenhouse into what Marie insisted on calling "the test range" — four sawhorses, a tape measure, every portable instrument the compound owned under a tarp.

"Baseline first," Marie said, hunched over the gate's remote sensor unit. "Clean ten minutes of nothing happening, so I've got an honest zero to measure against."

"Define nothing happening," Cent said, cross-legged in the grass, restless.

"Nobody jumps. Nobody talks about jumping. Ideally nobody even thinks too hard about it, though I can't enforce that part." Marie didn't look up. "Not superstition. Just need a genuine control period, or I won't know if what I measure afterward is a response to the test or more of the same drift."

Ten quiet minutes that felt considerably longer, Rick fidgeting until Clara's look settled him, Millie and Davy arms-crossed off to one side, watching their daughter with the tension of parents trying not to let their own worry become one more thing she had to manage.

"Baseline's flat," Marie said finally. "Clean." She looked at Cent. "Smallest jump you can do. Well inside your comfort range — just establishing whether the act itself registers, separate from distance or weight."

"Boulder past the paddock fence," Cent said. "Forty yards. I could get there clean."

"Perfect. Whenever you're ready."

Cent closed her eyes a second — a small habit I'd notice every time, like a breath before a diving board — and simply wasn't there. No flash, no sound. Just an empty patch of grass.

"There," she called back, faint, one arm raised beside the boulder. "Confirmed there."

"Reading?" Davy called, looking at Marie's tablet instead of the boulder.

Marie took her time, and her face settled into something careful before she said it.

"Spike. Small, but real, immediate — inside a second of her jumping. Whatever's measuring this, it's fast."

"How big compared to yesterday's spikes?" Mom asked.

"Smaller. Proportional, maybe, to the size of the jump. Forty yards registers a lot less than her first arrival, or whatever produced last night's spike at dinner." Something almost hopeful crossed Marie's face — a scientist watching a pattern resolve into legible instead of noise. "Useful, if it holds. Suggests this isn't a blanket alarm going off every time she exists here. It's tracking actual use — scale, distance, something I haven't isolated. Different picture than 'anything she does draws attention.'"

Cent jogged back, breathing harder than the distance accounted for — the small toll Dad and I had come to recognize.

"So it's not that I exist," she said. "It's that I do things. Kind of what I'd expect, honestly. Everything in my whole life's cost something proportional to the size of the ask."

"Might not be different here," Marie said. "Don't want to overclaim off one data point. Bigger jump next, still inside your comfort range — see if the reading scales the way the theory predicts."

They ran six more tests as the light went gold over the ridge — distances stepping up, a jump carrying weight, two jumps in quick succession to test frequency — and by the end Marie had a graph that meant something even to the rest of us: a clean, climbing relationship between what Cent asked of her ability and whatever answered from somewhere none of us could see.

"It's not random," Marie said, staring at it with the fixed intensity of a pattern finally clicking into place. "Not alarm either, not the way we feared this morning. Reads like measurement. Consistent, proportional, responsive in real time. That's not fear in an instrument. That's attention."

"Attention's not automatically safe," Davy said, low, still watching his daughter. "Being watched carefully isn't the same as being left alone."

"No," Mom agreed. "But it's a good deal more workable than a blind alarm we can't predict. Gives us a shape to work with instead of just fear to react to." She looked at Cent, tired and flushed and unmistakably lit up with having been useful. "One more test tomorrow, then we talk about what a longer-term arrangement looks like. Tonight, everybody eats and rests. We've earned it."

Overhead, the sky slid slow toward evening, and for the first time since the flag went up, I let myself believe — carefully, the way you'd trust ice you hadn't tested all the way across — that this might not end the way Davy's worst fear had painted it that morning.
