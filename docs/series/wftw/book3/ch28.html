Chapter 28

Cent found me in the workshop a few weeks later, alone, hunched over a logbook she clearly hadn't meant to leave out, and the guilty way she moved to close it told me I'd stumbled onto something.

"That's not a homework notebook, is it," I said, catching a column header. "Satellite ID range?"

She hesitated, then slid it back toward me instead of away. "Apex Orbital's job log."

I stared at her. "Apex Orbital. As in — the Apex Orbital. Space Girl."

"Don't call me that," she said, wincing without much heat, the practiced wince of somebody who'd heard the nickname a thousand times too many. "But yes."

I'd seen the videos same as everybody — the viral clip outside the ISS cupola, the Grebenchekov rescue leading every news cycle for a week, the endless speculation about who "Space Girl" actually was. I'd never once connected any of it to the quiet girl who'd sat across a cookfire from me learning about gates and watchers.

"You're Space Girl," I said again, mostly to hear if it landed more sensibly the second time. It didn't. "Cent. You pulled that cosmonaut off the station."

"I did." Plain, no false modesty, something careful underneath. "Dad and I kept my actual name mostly out of the coverage — a leak early on traced back further than we meant, but the DIA's file is heavily redacted even from most people using it. 'Cent Rice, living quietly on a homestead nobody official knows exists' hasn't made the evening news."

"So the job log—"

"Two hundred and some cubesats moved, most standard 1U units, going back to before AOS-Sat One made the tracking catalog." She tapped the log. "Don't run them one at a time. Go up with a batch, a hundred or so, work through the whole load in one trip."

"You've got USSPACECOM on speed dial," I said, remembering the coverage now I had a name to hang it on. "A Sergeant, doing the tracking liaison."

"Technical Sergeant Agatha Mertens," Cent said, fond. "Call her after every deployment. Report ID ranges, mean anomaly, whatever keeps their catalog honest. General Sterling handles anything above her pay grade, which happens more than either of us would like."

I felt the gap between the girl in front of me and the one I'd watched on grainy footage saving a life four hundred kilometers up. "Honestly — hard to reconcile with the last few weeks. Worrying about a stampede and a homestead this whole time, and this other enormous thing sitting underneath it, none of us had any idea."

"Built the homestead the same way," Cent said, plain, not unkind. "Quietly, with the people closest to me, until it's solid enough to survive being seen. Wanted you to know this part, though. Felt different. Important, somehow, even the homestead wasn't, not at first."

"Walk me through the mechanics," I said, setting the log open between us.

She did, and once past the reveal's nerves, the rest came fast, practiced — the fluency of someone who'd explained this to reporters and generals alike. "Thousand dollars a kilogram, flat. KforK, I call it." She tapped a column. "But what makes the business work isn't up-mass pricing. For every kilo I deliver up, I bring three back down — debris, dead hardware. Three-to-one, always. A rocket can't do that economically, because bringing controlled mass down costs nearly as much as putting it up. I don't have that problem. Coming back costs about the same effort as going, so I can weight the ratio hard toward cleanup."

"How do you actually deorbit them? Not carrying propulsion for every unit."

"Don't need to. Part people never expect, even after seeing the footage." Fondness of long practice. "Once I'm up there, I just throw them. Each one, by hand, whatever direction lowers its perigee enough to bring it down eventually. Doesn't take much of a nudge at orbital velocities, though it's not instant — most reenter within a few months, none past six."

"You're throwing satellites," I said, slow, letting the sentence land. "By hand. While the entire United States Space Command waits for your phone call after."

"Shockingly effective," Cent said, unbothered. "Cheaper than propulsion, and works because I'm the one variable that doesn't need fuel to change direction."

"How's the sat phone protocol held?"

"Perfect record. Somebody's always on comms, every trip, no exceptions — rule since the first test, five minutes that felt like five hours to Mom and Dad, no way to know if I was even breathing till I came down and told them myself. Dad fixed that inside a week." She said it plainly, the rule so load-bearing she couldn't imagine operating any other way.

"I'm glad you told me," I said, meaning it. "Most remarkable thing I've heard all month, and this month's had absurd competition for that title."

Cent laughed, closed the logbook. "Want more days like this. Not instead of everything else. Alongside it. More building, less crisis management."

"About the most reasonable thing either of us has wanted in weeks," I said.

She left a while later, and watching her go, I thought that whatever the watcher meant by measuring what people chose to become on the other side of an open door, it was walking back across the yard in the last light — a girl the world already called a hero under a name that wasn't hers, who still felt safest keeping the truest parts of herself quiet until she trusted who she was showing them to.
