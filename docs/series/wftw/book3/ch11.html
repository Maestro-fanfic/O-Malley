Chapter 11

Marie told everyone at breakfast, tablet flat on the table between eggs and coffee, walking through the graph point by point, calm and methodical.

Cent went still partway through. Millie's hand found her daughter's shoulder — an old, practiced reflex neither seemed to decide on.

"So it's me," Cent said, when Marie finished. "Every time I do it — every time I even talk about it — something out there notices a little more."

"We don't know that for certain," Marie said. "Don't want you carrying a certainty I don't have. I know the timing lines up. Don't know what it means."

"Doesn't much matter what it means if the safest move's the same either way," Davy said. Hadn't touched his coffee, both hands flat on the table, doing the same math I'd done on the porch. "If jumping's drawing attention, the answer's simple. She stops."

"Dad—"

"Not forever, Cent. Until we understand what we're dealing with." Real fear underneath the flatness. "I've spent twenty years learning what this ability costs me. Never had to think about what it might cost the entire world you and I are standing on — or whether something out there decides the cost's too high and shuts the whole thing down. Not just for us. For the Newells. For everybody who's built anything here. That's not a risk I weigh casually because you don't want to hear the answer."

"That's not fair," Cent said, voice cracking. "You're the one who taught me this is a gift, not a curse. Mine, and nobody gets to tell me not to use it — and now the second something might be watching, you want me to just stop being who I am?"

"Yes," Davy said, quiet and absolute. "A hundred times yes. I'd rather have a daughter who's safe and frustrated than free and gone. I've buried enough of what I love to know which one I can survive."

The table went silent. Millie's hand tightened on her daughter's shoulder, some old grief passing between them nobody explained.

Mom, who'd said little, finally spoke, her voice carrying the weight she used when something mattered enough to get exactly right.

"I don't think stopping's actually the safest option. I want to explain why, because I understand it feels obvious."

Davy looked at her sharply. "Explain, then."

"Whatever's watching this Earth's been watching for years without acting on anything we've done here. Not because we've been perfect. We haven't. What's kept this arrangement stable isn't perfection. It's transparency — the sense that what happens here happens honestly, without anybody hiding what they are." She looked at Cent. "If your daughter goes quiet now, stops using something fundamentally part of who she is, out of fear rather than genuine restraint — I don't know what that looks like to whatever's measuring. But I know what it looks like from here. It looks like hiding. And hiding's exactly what makes a careful watcher start asking harder questions."

"So your answer's she keeps using it," Davy said. "Draws more attention, on the theory transparency's safer than caution."

"My answer is I don't know the right move any better than you do, and I'd be lying to pretend otherwise. What I know is a decision this size shouldn't get made by two frightened parents at a breakfast table, on a guess." She looked around the table. "We have instruments. We have a girl willing to help us understand her own ability if we ask properly instead of deciding for her. We have some time. I'd rather spend the next several days learning something true than make an irreversible choice tonight and find out in a month we had it backward."

Cent looked at her father, anger shifting into something more like pleading. "Let me help figure it out instead of just stopping. If stopping's the right answer, I'll do it. I promise. But let me be part of finding that out instead of just being told."

Davy took his time, jaw working, weighing a lifetime of protective instinct against a daughter old enough to have earned some say in her own risk.

"One week," he said finally. "Controlled. Small jumps only, nothing that pushes your limits, Marie watching those instruments every hour you're testing. Pattern gets worse instead of clearer — starts looking less like learning and more like alarm — we stop. Immediately. No arguing. Agreed?"

"Agreed," Cent said, real relief in it.

"Agreed," Mom said. "Marie runs the instrument side. Charlie, Clara — you two handle the practical end. Make sure whatever testing happens somewhere controlled, somewhere we can pull back fast."

"What about Home?" Clara asked. "Shouldn't somebody back there know? Isn't there anybody to ask?"

Mom's face did something complicated — not the confident, in-charge mother who always had the next order ready, but a woman genuinely uncertain of the ground under her.

"There isn't anybody to ask," she said. "Max and I left before either of us held a position that'd let us call home and get an answer, even if I knew how to place that call, which I don't. There's me, and whatever we discover, and the choice of what to do with it. My own mother made calls like this alone too, on a world I haven't seen since I was younger than Charlie is now, and I never got the chance to ask her afterward if she'd gotten them right." She looked around the table, two families who'd known each other barely two days already in this together. "We do the best we can with what we actually know. That's the whole job. Always has been, long before it was mine."
