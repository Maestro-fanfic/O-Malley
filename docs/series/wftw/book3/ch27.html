Chapter 27

It was Joey, of all people, who finally said the practical thing out loud, three weeks into what had quietly become a standing arrangement between the two families.

"We need to make this a real thing," he said, over cards one evening, in the tone he used when he'd actually thought something through. "Not just favors back and forth. Real structure. A company, if we're doing gold and lumber and supply runs at any real scale."

"A company," Clara repeated, amused. "For what, selling grazing rights to an empty Earth nobody else can reach?"

"For funding whatever comes next," Joey said, more serious than his usual deflecting humor. "Cent's homestead needs capital same as anything else. We've got the gate, the flying, market access neither side's had a clean way to use. They've got gold, labor, land nobody else can touch. Put that together properly, legally — that's not charity anymore. That's a partnership that survives past whoever's feeling generous this month."

Dad, half listening, set down the engine manual he was pretending to read. "He's not wrong. Goodwill's a fine start. Poor foundation long-term for something this size."

"What would you call it?" Rick asked, elbow-deep in a transmission.

"Wildside Investments," Joey said, immediate enough I suspected he'd been sitting on the name. "Says exactly what it is. Nobody outside this room has to know how literal."

It stuck, and by the end of the month Dad had walked Joey and Davy through the actual mechanics — a legal structure on the tame side, funded by gold Davy brought through in small increments and the growing income Marie pulled flying supply runs for outfits back home who never asked too many questions about where a teenage girl learned to fly a loaded Cessna with that much confidence.

"Not much yet," Davy said, looking over the paperwork with the disbelief of a man who'd measured his whole economy in what he could carry on his back. "But it's real. On paper, in a way none of this has ever been."

"It'll grow," Dad said. "These things always do, once the structure's sound. Give it a few years, more capital moving through than either family imagined needing."

He wasn't wrong, though none of us at that table with cheap coffee and hand-drafted incorporation papers had any sense yet how far it would go — Cent's own restless mind already working on ideas bigger than gold and lumber, though we wouldn't understand the shape of that for years.

✳

The mailbox incident happened the following weekend, and became the kind of story retold so often the exact facts blurred a little more each time — though the core never changed: Rick, driving Joey's truck down the gravel approach for the first time, clipped the compound's mailbox so precisely it ended up embedded, post and all, in the truck bed instead of lying in the ditch like reasonable collateral damage.

"I don't understand the physics of it," Marie said, examining the mailbox wedged in the truck bed an hour later. "You'd need exactly the wrong angle, exactly the wrong speed, for it to end up inside the truck instead of just knocked over."

"I have a gift," Rick said, deadpan, already back under a different hood, like the whole thing had been an unremarkable Tuesday.

"He does," Joey confirmed, grinning. "Doesn't say much. When he does something, it's memorable." He clapped Rick's shoulder, affection under the ribbing. "At least Marie's got an actual excuse for not driving anybody. Girl's got a pilot's license and zero interest in a regular one. Some of us just carry the mailbox incident forever."

Cent, watching from the porch, laughed hard enough to sit down, and I understood, folded into that ordinary absurdity, that whatever else came of this, we'd already built something worth protecting long before any of us thought to ask permission for it.
