Chapter 1

Cows here don't know to be scared of anything. Nothing's ever eaten one, so nothing taught them caution. Bessie-Two had a habit of walking right up to the edge of the caprock chasing grass six inches greener than what she already had.

I'd pulled her back from it twice this month. Ninth today.

"You're going to give me a heart attack," I told her. She kept eating.

Clara's voice came over the ridge before she did. "Charlie! Mom's got the flag up!"

The flag meant the gate. Not a scheduled crossing — I'd have remembered that. Something on the tame side, and Mom needed hands.

I left Bessie-Two to her salad and jogged the ridge. Clara fell in beside me. She'd been doing that for two years now.

"She say what it was?"

"Just the flag."

We came off the ridge into the compound at a run, past the horse paddock and the greenhouse frames, down to the gate barn, the gate itself built into the barn's north wall — heavy wooden door, hinges gone dark with age, a padlock older than I was hanging through the hasp. Mom stood at the open barn door with her arms crossed. Bad sign. Uncle Max had raised that whole structure around the door himself, said a thing worth that much care deserved real walls same as anything else on the ranch, and it still did the job, quiet as anything, an ordinary-looking door most days you'd walk right past without a second look.

"What's going on?"

She didn't turn around. "Nothing's coming through. That's the problem."

"I don't follow."

"The gate's reading traffic. Not ours. Not sanctioned, not scheduled."

Clara went still. "Someone's using the gate."

"Someone's using a gate," Mom said. "The instruments can't tell me if it's ours or something adjacent to it. That distinction matters, because as far as any of us have ever known, this gate is the only way onto this Earth. Not Home — Home's a different business entirely. This wildside. The empty one. Nobody's had a second way in, not in all the years we've been coming out here."

I knew enough about how the gate worked to know why that sentence landed the way it did. The power source sat in its own separate nothing of a universe, so nobody could reverse-engineer it, copy it, sell it. Unique by construction. No exceptions.

"Could be a malfunction," Dad said, in the tone of a man who didn't believe it.

"Could be," Mom agreed, in the tone of a woman who agreed with him completely.

Marie had her arms wrapped around herself, doing the math nobody had asked her to do yet. She did that. Numbers, angles, structural loads — she worked it out quietly while the rest of us talked, then handed you the answer like it had always been obvious.

"How long's it been reading?"

"Six days. I only got confirmation this morning. Whatever's coming through isn't coming through here. Somewhere else on this Earth, far enough that our instruments barely catch the edge of it."

"Somewhere else on the wildside," I said. "Not the tame side."

"Correct."

That was the part that actually scared me. Contamination protocols existed for a reason. This whole enormous unpopulated Earth existed because nothing from the tame side was supposed to wander in and start changing it. Mom's people didn't even let themselves do that carelessly.

If something else was coming through, uninvited, that wasn't a malfunction question. That was a who question.

"We need eyes on it," Mom said. "Real eyes. Whatever's opening, I want someone close enough to see what comes through."

"I'll go," I said, same second Clara said it. We looked at each other. Neither of us was backing down.

Mom studied us a beat too long.

"Dad flies you as close as the terrain allows," she said finally. "Quiet. No fires you don't have to build. No contact if you can avoid it. If it's dangerous, you disengage. You come home. There is nothing on the other side of that gate worth more to me than any of you standing here."

"I know, Mom."

"I know you know. I need you to act like you know, which is historically a separate skill for you."

Clara made a sound that was almost a laugh. Even Mom's mouth twitched.

Up toward the bunkhouse, Rick's voice carried down the slope. "Are we going somewhere?"

"Somebody's got to mind the strip," Mom said, which was as close as she ever came to telling him no. He grumbled about it exactly as much as everyone expected.

For a second, standing in the gate barn's doorway with the gate humming its ordinary hum, it felt like any other morning on the ranch.

It wasn't going to stay that way. I could feel the weather changing before the sky showed it.

Clara caught my hand on the way to the hangar. "Same Earth," she said, low. "That's what's got her rattled. Not just that something's coming through. That it might be close enough to matter."

"Yeah. I caught that too."

✳

We flew out at first light, Dad at the stick of the bush plane, the engine too loud for talk. I had a lot of thinking to do and no real appetite for doing it out loud anyway.

Below us the wildside unrolled the way it always did — grass to every horizon, herds moving across it in slow dark smudges, rivers cutting silver through country that had never felt a plow or a fence post. Mom's people had been watching this place a long time, cataloguing, deciding inch by inch what humanity had actually earned the right to touch.

Somewhere down there, six days running, somebody hadn't asked permission.

Dad banked south, toward coordinates Marie had worked out overnight from six days of instrument noise, and the grass kept rolling out beneath us, empty and, for the first time since I'd come to live on this side of the gate, not entirely honest about what it was.
