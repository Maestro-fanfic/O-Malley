Chapter 1: Chapter 1

Cows here don't know to be scared of anything. Nothing's ever eaten one, so nothing taught them caution. Bessie-Two had a habit of walking right up to the edge of the caprock chasing grass six inches greener than what she already had.

I'd pulled her back from it twice this month. Ninth today.

"You're going to give me a heart attack," I told her. She kept eating.

Clara's voice came over the ridge before she did. "Charlie! Mom's got the flag up!"

The flag meant the gate. Not a scheduled crossing — I'd have remembered that. Something on the tame side, and Mom needed hands.

I left Bessie-Two to her salad and jogged the ridge. Clara fell in beside me. She'd been doing that for two years now.

"She say what it was?"

"Just the flag."

We came off the ridge into the compound at a run, past the horse paddock and the greenhouse frames, down to the gate barn, the gate itself built into the barn's north wall — heavy wooden door, hinges gone dark with age, a padlock older than I was hanging through the hasp. Mom stood at the open barn door with her arms crossed. Bad sign. Uncle Max had raised that whole structure around the door himself, said a thing worth that much care deserved real walls same as anything else on the ranch, and it still did the job, quiet as anything, an ordinary-looking door most days you'd walk right past without a second look.

"What's going on?"

She didn't turn around. "Nothing's coming through. That's the problem."

"I don't follow."

"The gate's reading traffic. Not ours. Not sanctioned, not scheduled."

Clara went still. "Someone's using the gate."

"Someone's using a gate," Mom said. "The instruments can't tell me if it's ours or something adjacent to it. That distinction matters, because as far as any of us have ever known, this gate is the only way onto this Earth. Not Home — Home's a different business entirely. This wildside. The empty one. Nobody's had a second way in, not in all the years we've been coming out here."

I knew enough about how the gate worked to know why that sentence landed the way it did. The power source sat in its own separate nothing of a universe, so nobody could reverse-engineer it, copy it, sell it. Unique by construction. No exceptions.

"Could be a malfunction," Dad said, in the tone of a man who didn't believe it.

"Could be," Mom agreed, in the tone of a woman who agreed with him completely.

Marie had her arms wrapped around herself, doing the math nobody had asked her to do yet. She did that. Numbers, angles, structural loads — she worked it out quietly while the rest of us talked, then handed you the answer like it had always been obvious.

"How long's it been reading?"

"Six days. I only got confirmation this morning. Whatever's coming through isn't coming through here. Somewhere else on this Earth, far enough that our instruments barely catch the edge of it."

"Somewhere else on the wildside," I said. "Not the tame side."

"Correct."

That was the part that actually scared me. Contamination protocols existed for a reason. This whole enormous unpopulated Earth existed because nothing from the tame side was supposed to wander in and start changing it. Mom's people didn't even let themselves do that carelessly.

If something else was coming through, uninvited, that wasn't a malfunction question. That was a who question.

"We need eyes on it," Mom said. "Real eyes. Whatever's opening, I want someone close enough to see what comes through."

"I'll go," I said, same second Clara said it. We looked at each other. Neither of us was backing down.

Mom studied us a beat too long.

"Dad flies you as close as the terrain allows," she said finally. "Quiet. No fires you don't have to build. No contact if you can avoid it. If it's dangerous, you disengage. You come home. There is nothing on the other side of that gate worth more to me than any of you standing here."

"I know, Mom."

"I know you know. I need you to act like you know, which is historically a separate skill for you."

Clara made a sound that was almost a laugh. Even Mom's mouth twitched.

Up toward the bunkhouse, Rick's voice carried down the slope. "Are we going somewhere?"

"Somebody's got to mind the strip," Mom said, which was as close as she ever came to telling him no. He grumbled about it exactly as much as everyone expected.

For a second, standing in the gate barn's doorway with the gate humming its ordinary hum, it felt like any other morning on the ranch.

It wasn't going to stay that way. I could feel the weather changing before the sky showed it.

Clara caught my hand on the way to the hangar. "Same Earth," she said, low. "That's what's got her rattled. Not just that something's coming through. That it might be close enough to matter."

"Yeah. I caught that too."

✳

We flew out at first light, Dad at the stick of the bush plane, the engine too loud for talk. I had a lot of thinking to do and no real appetite for doing it out loud anyway.

Below us the wildside unrolled the way it always did — grass to every horizon, herds moving across it in slow dark smudges, rivers cutting silver through country that had never felt a plow or a fence post. Mom's people had been watching this place a long time, cataloguing, deciding inch by inch what humanity had actually earned the right to touch.

Somewhere down there, six days running, somebody hadn't asked permission.

Dad banked south, toward coordinates Marie had worked out overnight from six days of instrument noise, and the grass kept rolling out beneath us, empty and, for the first time since I'd come to live on this side of the gate, not entirely honest about what it was.

Chapter 2: Chapter 2

Dad put the plane down on flat grassland eight miles out from Marie's coordinates. As close as he'd bring anything with an engine.

"Sound carries different out here," he said, over the dying whine. "No traffic noise to hide it in. Put a plane down any closer and whoever's making that gate reading knows exactly where to look for you instead of the other way around."

"Eight miles on foot," Clara said, already shouldering her pack.

"Eight miles on foot." Dad looked at us both a second, the same look Mom gave us back at the gate barn — measuring whether we were about to do something brave or something stupid. "Radio in every four hours, whether there's anything to report or not. Silence past six, I come looking. Rules or no rules."

"Understood."

He clasped my shoulder, quick and hard, and then Clara and I were walking, the plane fading behind us into the huge quiet of the grass.

Two years, and this world's particular quiet still got to me. Not empty — insects, wind through grass heads, something big and distant shifting somewhere. Just nothing human. No engines, no distant highway hum you don't notice until it's gone.

Clara walked point, reading terrain in a way that still impressed me. She'd grown up half on this side of the gate, longer than any of the rest of us. Picked her line across tall grass without seeming to think about it — avoiding burrows, keeping us near the tree line for cover.

"You're quiet," she said, a couple hours in, not turning around.

"Thinking."

"About the gate."

"About what it means if it's not ours."

She slowed to let me catch up. "Your mom's believed her whole life this gate's the only door of its kind. Not a job passed down to her. Literally where she's from. If that's not true anymore—"

"Then I don't know what the job even is."

"Or the job just got bigger," Clara said. "Not less important. Different."

I didn't have an answer, so we walked another mile in silence that wasn't uncomfortable. Just full.

We made camp that evening in a stand of cottonwoods, no fire, cold rations. Clara took first watch. I lay looking up through the branches at a sky with four times the stars any tame-side sky ever had, and tried to remember what it felt like two years ago, the first time I understood I wasn't in Kansas anymore.

I must have slept, because Clara was shaking my shoulder and it was gray dawn.

"Charlie." Low, the pitch that meant don't ask, just listen. "Smoke."

I sat up fast. She pointed — a thin gray thread two miles off, dead ahead on Marie's line.

"Campfire?"

"Or a burn-off. Or an engine backfiring on something that shouldn't be running out here." Already breaking down her bedroll, quick, no wasted motion. "First actual sign of anything human since we left the plane. We go quiet, go careful, see what we're looking at before we decide what it means."

"Agreed."

We ate walking — cold biscuit and jerky, one hand always near enough to a weapon. Two years ago I'd have called that paranoid. Two years on a world where nothing had learned to fear anything upright had cured me of that opinion.

The smoke got taller as we closed the distance, and by midmorning we came up over a rise and got our first real look.

It wasn't a campfire.

It was a house. Timber-framed, half raised, a second structure's skeleton going up beside it, smoke curling from a cookfire set well clear of both buildings. Fenced paddocks. A garden plot, neat rows, already green. A homestead — the kind of thing that took months, not days, which meant whatever gate reading Mom's instruments picked up six days ago wasn't the beginning of this. Just the first time anybody back home had happened to look the right direction.

Clara went dead still, and her face had gone somewhere between amazed and furious.

"They've been here a while," she said quietly. "Not six days of work. Months."

"Which means the gate's been open a lot longer than the instruments caught."

"Or there's more than one way through." She was already dropping low, angling for brush cover. I followed, heart hammering, and settled beside her with a clear view of the valley — somebody, some family, had quietly built a life on a world that wasn't supposed to have room for uninvited guests.

"What do we do?" I asked.

Clara watched a figure move between house and garden, too far off for details, just a shape doing morning chores.

"We watch," she said finally. "Then figure out how somebody else found their way onto our Earth."

Chapter 3: Chapter 3

We watched two hours, trading field glasses, and what I saw didn't match anything I'd pictured on the walk in.

Poachers, maybe. Somebody running an illegal operation, quick and gone before anyone noticed. Instead: a garden with actual crop rotation, raised beds, a rain catchment system feeding a cistern, a smokehouse standing a deliberate distance from the main structure. These people weren't passing through. They'd sat down and thought about how to live here, the same patient way Dad thought about the bunkhouse.

"That's not a raid," Clara said, low, handing the glasses back. "That's a homestead. Somebody's whole life."

"Which is somehow worse," I said. "Means whoever's got access to this Earth isn't hiding it or selling it. They're just living here. Like it's theirs."

"Isn't it, though?" Clara's voice had an edge. "That's the question our whole side's never had to answer. There's only ever been one door, and we've always held the key. What happens when somebody else finds their own way in?"

I didn't have an answer. The figure I'd clocked doing garden chores straightened, looked straight at our brush, and went still.

"Down," Clara breathed. We dropped flat, my heart doing something unreasonable.

A long minute passed. Then another.

"They made us," Clara whispered.

"Maybe not—"

"Charlie." Her hand gripped my arm. "Somebody's coming."

I lifted my head enough to see a second figure had joined the first, taller, both pointing our direction — then a third, appearing from around the house, moving with the loose readiness of somebody who'd handled a weapon before.

"They're not panicking," I said. "That's—"

"Practiced," Clara finished. "Like they know exactly what an unfriendly approach looks like and have a plan for it."

We had maybe four seconds to decide whether we were people who ran or people who stood. Clara — who'd never once in two years been the kind of person who ran from something she could instead understand — stood up out of the grass, both hands raised.

"We're not here to hurt anybody! We just want to talk!"

I came up beside her, hands raised too, and watched the three figures go very still.

The tallest — a man, weathered, built from actual outdoor work rather than a gym — didn't lower whatever he held. Didn't raise it either. Felt like the most important detail in the world.

"Who are you," he called, "and how in the hell did you get onto our land?"

"Same way you did, probably," Clara called — a guess, but an educated one. It landed on him like a small explosion, readiness draining into something closer to shock.

"That's not possible," he said, quieter now, though the grassland carried it fine. "There's no way through. We checked. For years."

"There's a way through." Clara walked forward, slow, hands open. I matched her pace, every instinct arguing to stay put. "You're standing on it right now. My name's Clara. This is Charlie. Our instruments picked up gate traffic six days running. We needed to know who was making it."

The woman beside him — smaller, dark-haired, coiled — spoke first. "Gate. You said gate. Not—" She stopped, glanced at him, something passing between them too fast to read. "An actual, structural gate. Not what we do."

"What you do?" Clara's steps slowed. "What exactly do you do?"

The younger one — a teenager, close to my own age, hanging back with an expression I recognized from my own face plenty of times — spoke for the first time, relief edging her voice.

"We jump," she said. "Don't need a door. Just — go. Anywhere we've been, or anywhere we can see clearly enough. That's how we got here. Followed my dad's trail six months back and never left."

Clara stopped walking entirely.

Something clicked in my chest, slow and enormous. "You don't have a gate," I said. "Don't need one. You just move."

"That's right," the man said, wary curiosity replacing the flat readiness. "Which means whatever gate you two are talking about — that's new. Not us." He studied us a long beat. "Sounds like we've both been keeping the same secret from opposite directions, and just found out neither of us was actually the only one."

The woman lowered what she'd been holding.

"I think we'd better sit down and talk properly. My name's Millie. My husband, Davy. Our daughter, Cent." Something rueful crossed her face. "If there's a built way onto this Earth as well as whatever our family does — that changes a great deal more than who owns this land."

Clara glanced at me — the same thought landing in both of us. Everything Mom believed about this door being the one unrepeatable thing, standing up against a family who'd never needed permission at all. Who'd just walked here, the way you'd walk into a neighboring room.

"Yeah," I said, steadier than I felt. "I think you're right."

Chapter 4: Chapter 4

His name was Davy Rice, and once we sat around the cookfire with mugs of chicory root instead of coffee — "real coffee's a supply-run luxury," Millie said, "and we're overdue" — the shape of how they'd built this place came out. Better engineering than I'd expected from three people and their eighteen-year-old daughter, no gate, no cargo plane, no way to move anything heavier than a person could carry.

"You didn't fly any of this in," I said, looking at the timber framing — real dimensional lumber, not whatever trees happened to be standing nearby. "Where'd the milled lumber come from?"

"Milled it ourselves, mostly," Davy said. "Portable sawmill, the kind you break down and haul in pieces. Took Cent and me four jumps just for the mill itself." He nodded toward a stand of cottonwood a quarter mile off, thinned in a pattern that told me somebody had thought hard about sustainable cutting. "Every board in that second wall came from trees we felled ourselves, milled ourselves, hauled in by hand. No truck, no dolly, nothing on wheels works on this ground yet."

"How do you jump lumber?" Clara asked. "Thought you needed line of sight, or somewhere you'd already been."

"Somewhere I've been, or somewhere I can see clearly enough through a photo, if I study it long enough first," Davy said. "But there's a weight limit. I top out around three hundred pounds before the effort costs more than it's worth — and that's after twenty years' practice. Cent's still building toward that. So every big job's an exercise in breaking things into three-hundred-pound pieces without losing structural integrity on the other end."

"That's a logistics problem," I said, not hiding how impressed I sounded.

"That's the entire logistics problem," Millie said, dry. "Everything out here started life as a spreadsheet before it started life as a wall."

Clara leaned forward. "Our side runs different. Fixed gate — one location, no line-of-sight requirement, but it doesn't move. All our logistics run through aircraft once we're through it. Dad flew us in and set down eight miles off — as close as he'd risk the noise."

"You've got a working aircraft on this Earth?" Davy sat up straighter. "Fueled how? Don't tell me you jumped aviation gas."

"Flew it through the gate whole," Clara said. "That's the advantage of a fixed structure over a personal jump — doesn't care how big or heavy the thing is, long as it fits through the doorway. We've moved a loaded C-130 through before."

Davy laughed, short and disbelieving. "A C-130. Lord. What I'd have given for a C-130 six years ago, hauling a sawmill through one exhausting piece at a time."

"You'd need a runway first," I said — I'd sat through enough of Mom's planning sessions to know exactly how much that mattered. "A gate doesn't help if you can't land anything heavy on the other side. We've got two thousand feet of graded, packed strip back home, took the better part of a year to build right. Wrong soil compaction and the first heavy landing tears it to ruts."

"How'd you compact it without equipment?" Millie asked — not idle curiosity. Filing the answer away.

"We didn't, without equipment. First big cargo run, before I ever got here — small compactor and grader, flown through in pieces, reassembled on-site. After that it's maintenance. Minimum eight inches of graded base under a packed surface for anything heavier than a light single-engine, and even then you're watching margins close on a hot afternoon, because density altitude eats your effective runway same as a shorter strip would."

"You know all that off the top of your head?" Cent — the daughter — was looking at me somewhere between skeptical and genuinely curious.

"Mom made all of us learn it," I said. "Not just flying. The whole logistics chain, because if the runway fails or the plane goes down out here, there's no calling for help. You're it."

"Density altitude," Davy repeated, tasting the phrase. "Explain that. Thought plenty about weight limits on the jump side. Never had to think about air."

"Hot, thin, or high air's less dense than cold, thick, low air," I said. "Same physical altitude, but a hot afternoon behaves like you're thousands of feet higher — less lift, less engine performance, longer takeoff roll. Doesn't matter how good your runway is if you load past what density altitude supports that afternoon. Dad checks a chart before every single takeoff, no exceptions. Guess wrong on that math and you put an aircraft into the trees at the far end of a strip that looked plenty long on paper."

Millie nodded slowly. "The kind of thing you learn by somebody teaching you properly, or by nearly dying and getting lucky enough to learn from it instead."

"Mom prefers the first option," I said. "Strongly."

That got an actual laugh out of the group, easing something that had been sitting in the air since the standoff that morning, and Davy refilled mugs while the conversation threaded between two very different kinds of hard-won expertise.

"What about the money side," Clara asked eventually. "Building anything this substantial — that's real capital. Where's it coming from, if you're not running supply flights back to civilization?"

Davy and Millie traded a look — the kind that meant they'd had this exact conversation before and had an answer ready.

"Gold," Millie said simply.

"Placer gold in half the streambeds on this Earth," Davy said. "No mining pressure, ever — what's accumulated over a few hundred million years is just sitting in the gravel, waiting on somebody with a pan and the patience. I spend maybe two mornings a week at the river with a sluice box I built myself. Enough, converted through channels I'd rather not get specific about, to fund lumber, tools, seed stock, anything we can't produce ourselves."

"You pan gold," I said, hearing the same note in my voice Clara had had earlier. "By hand."

"Sluice box, mostly — pan's for prospecting a new bar. Once you've located good gravel you build a proper sluice, get your water flow right, riffles set to catch the heavy material without choking on the light stuff. Slow, wet work. One morning in five you come back with nothing but a sunburn." Davy shrugged, easy, like a man describing a hobby rather than his family's entire economic foundation. "But the ratio's good enough over a season we've never gone without anything we truly needed."

Clara looked at me. I knew exactly what she was thinking, because I was thinking it too.

Two completely different kinds of hard-won, bone-deep competence, built from opposite directions to solve the same problem — how do you actually live on an Earth that owed you nothing — and neither side had known the other existed until six days of instrument noise sent two teenagers walking eight miles to find out.

"I think our families have a great deal to talk about," I said.

"I think your mother's going to want a very long conversation with us," Davy said. "And we'll want an equally long one with her." He looked out at the second structure, timber he'd felled and hauled with his own hands. "This is our home now. Whatever comes of this — we're not looking to be managed off our own land by a family that only just found out we existed."

I understood that flavor of fear better than he guessed. There'd been years, before any of this, when I'd watched soldiers stand in my own living room, watched a man I trusted get dragged off at gunpoint over a door that was mine before it was anybody's business. I still checked exits in rooms I'd sat in a hundred times. Still felt my body go tight at an unfamiliar engine on gravel. Nobody had ever fully explained that reflex away, and I didn't imagine Davy's version would go any cleaner than mine had.

"Nobody's talking about that," Clara said, and meant it. "But you should know — Mom's spent her whole life believing this was the one door onto this Earth. Her own people built it, on a world she left before Charlie was born. Finding out there's a second kind of door, one she never built and can't control—" She chose her next words carefully. "That's going to shake something in her that's been solid a long time. I'd rather you hear it from us than get blindsided."

Davy considered that, firelight moving across his face, then nodded, slow and certain.

"Then let's make sure," he said, "that when it comes, we're standing here to meet it together, instead of two families each waiting to see what the other does first."

Chapter 5: Chapter 5

We radioed Dad at the four-hour mark, then again an hour out from the plane, so he had the engine warmed and chocks pulled by the time Clara and I jogged up out of the grass, Davy walking behind at an easier pace — unarmed now, having agreed, after another hour of cautious back-and-forth, to come meet Mom in person.

"Just the one passenger?" Dad asked, sizing Davy up the way he sized up every stranger.

"Just the one, for now," Clara said. "Millie and Cent are staying with the homestead. Davy's coming to talk to Mom."

Dad held out a hand. "Hank Newell."

"Davy Rice." They shook, brief, businesslike. I watched Dad clock something in the grip, the calluses, the way Davy held himself — two men who'd each spent years doing hard physical work recognizing the type in each other without needing to say so.

"You ever flown before?" Dad asked, already turning toward the plane.

"Once or twice, commercial. Nothing like this."

"Hold onto something and don't touch anything with a red label, we'll get along fine." Dad climbed into the pilot's seat and ran his pre-flight out loud, half habit, half teaching. "Fuel selector, both. Mixture, rich. Flaps, ten degrees. Trim neutral. Controls free and correct—" the yoke through its full range, watching the surfaces respond "—clean check across the board."

"You do that every time?" Davy asked, buckling in.

"Every time, rules or no rules. The one time you skip it is the one time something's wrong, and you find out forty feet in the air instead of forty feet on the ground." Dad primed the engine, called clear, brought it over with a cough and a settled roar loud enough to end conversation.

We taxied to the strip's near end, and Dad checked the windsock a moment longer.

"Wind's shifted since yesterday," he called back. "Quartering tailwind — don't love it on a strip this short, but inside margins if we're smart. Full power, hold her down long as I can stand to build airspeed, because tailwind eats short-field distance fast."

The plane surged across the grass, roughness smoothing as speed built. He held it a beat longer than felt comfortable before easing back, and we climbed away clean, banking north over grassland unspooling gold and green in the early afternoon light.

Davy pressed his face near the window the whole climb, watching his own homestead shrink to a smudge behind us. I understood the look without him explaining — the vulnerability of watching everything you'd built get small and out of your control, on a schedule that wasn't yours.

"How do you navigate out here?" he asked, eventually. "No roads. No landmarks I'd recognize twice. What tells you you're actually headed right?"

"Dead reckoning, backed by a compass and a clock," Dad said, not looking away from the instruments. "Heading, true airspeed, rough wind estimate, time since my last confirmed position. Run those together, you get a reliable fix even with nothing on the ground worth calling a landmark."

"No GPS?"

"Handheld backup, and I use it. Don't trust it the way somebody flying the tame side might. Batteries fail. If that thing dies forty miles from anywhere with a name, I still need to get this airplane home on nothing but what's in my head and on the panel. That's the skill I practice, every flight, GPS working or not. It's a convenience. Not the plan."

"That's a lot to hold in your head."

"It's a serious world out here," Dad said. "Nobody's coming to bail you out. Sharpens a man's attention real quick."

Clara caught my eye and gave me a small, private smile — somewhere in the last two hours, Davy Rice had gone from armed stranger to something closer to kindred spirit.

The compound came up forty minutes later, the graded strip a pale scar against the green, and Dad brought us in on a long, easy final, calling numbers out of old habit.

"Sixty-five on final... crossing the fence at fifty... and—" wheels kissed packed dirt with barely a shudder "—down."

He taxied toward the gate barn and cut the engine. In the ringing quiet, Mom was already walking down to meet us, Marie a half step behind, both moving with the urgency of people who'd waited four hours for a radio check that told them almost nothing.

Davy unbuckled and sat a moment, looking at the barn and the plain wooden door built into its wall — the actual gate, the thing his family had never had and never suspected was possible.

"Well," he said, half to himself. "No putting this off any longer."

"No," I said. "There really isn't."

Chapter 6: Chapter 6

Mom didn't say anything at first. Looked at Davy Rice the way she looked at equipment she was deciding whether to trust, then held out her hand.

"Miriam Newell. Everyone calls me Miriam. You've apparently found your own way onto an Earth I've spent my whole life believing only one door led to."

"Davy Rice." He shook her hand, firm and easy. "Feeling's mutual, ma'am. Up until about four hours ago I believed the exact same thing about my family and this place."

Something in that landed with Mom unexpectedly — not suspicion easing, but recognition, one wary custodian sizing up another. "Marie," she said, without looking away from Davy, "get the maps. All of them. And put coffee on, real coffee — I'd guess Mr. Rice hasn't had a proper cup in some time."

"You have no idea," Davy said, with real feeling, and that got the first genuine laugh out of the whole yard.

We ended up around the long table, maps spread out — Mom's own survey work, years of it, herd migration, water sources, the sites we'd deemed stable to build on — and Davy leaning over them with the hunger of a man who'd navigated an unmapped world by memory and dead reckoning, suddenly looking at somebody else's hard-won knowledge laid out for free.

"You've got actual topographic surveys," he said. "Real ones. Elevation lines, water tables—"

"Aerial photography, cross-referenced against instrument flights over fifteen years," Mom said. "Not complete. There's more of this Earth than any of us will ever set foot on. But more than nothing — would've saved you trouble, six years ago, knowing which bars along your stretch of river actually carried gold-bearing gravel."

Davy's jaw tightened. I understood why — Mom testing him, gently but unmistakably, letting him know she already had more on his operation than he was comfortable with.

"You've had eyes on us," he said. Not quite a question.

"I've had eyes on this hemisphere, as routine survey work, longer than you've been alive. Didn't know, until six days ago, that those eyes had missed a family building sixty miles from my own compound. If I had, we'd have had this conversation sooner, with less anxiety on both sides." She held his eyes. "I'm not in the business of managing people off land they've earned. I am in the business of making sure nothing jeopardizes the narrow, fragile arrangement that lets any of us be here at all. That's not a threat. That's the job."

"What arrangement," Davy asked. "Nobody's ever explained it. I assumed — God help me — my daughter and I had found a genuine empty world. No strings. No landlord."

Mom didn't answer right away. I recognized the stillness — calculating how much family history was hers to share with a man she'd known less than an hour.

"There isn't a landlord in the sense you mean," she said finally. "But there's a reason I know what I know, and it isn't inherited duty. It's inheritance in the literal sense." She paused, the stillness that came right before she said something she almost never said aloud. "I wasn't born on this Earth. Neither was my brother Max. We came from the world that built that gate — Home is the only name I ever had for it, the only name my own mother gave it before I was old enough to understand what leaving it would cost either of us." She let that settle. "Whatever's watching this Earth, weighing how anyone uses a world they were never owed — I didn't learn that from files. I learned it the way you learn anything from family: badly, in pieces, mostly too late to ask the questions I needed answered before Max was gone and there was nobody left to ask. Every homestead, every gold pan, every tree felled — it gets weighed, eventually, against a standard I was never allowed to see in full, even growing up inside it. I've spent every year since trying to manage that exposure carefully, because the alternative — a door slammed shut on people careless with the privilege — isn't hypothetical to me. I watched it happen once already, somewhere else, before either of us left."

The room had gone very quiet. Marie, back with the coffee, had stopped moving, mug forgotten halfway to the table.

"You're telling me," Davy said slowly, "my family's homestead — my sluice box, my garden, my daughter learning to jump three hundred pounds of lumber one trip at a time — all of that's being graded. By something. Somewhere."

"I'm telling you everything on this Earth is being graded, whether either family likes it or not, and the best thing you can do is exactly what you've already been doing — building carefully, taking only what you need, leaving the land better than a careless visitor would. Chapter 7: Chapter 7

"So whatever's happening isn't finished."

"No," Marie said. "I don't think it is."

Davy pushed back and stood, walking to the window, looking out at the gate barn and the ordinary-looking door in its wall. When he spoke again his voice had gone quiet enough the whole room leaned in.

"We don't know that," Mom said, careful. "We know a correlation. Not a mechanism. Not an intent. Whatever's watching this planet's been watching a long time without acting, so far as my family's ever determined. A shifted baseline isn't a verdict."

"Might be the beginning of one."

Mom didn't answer right away, and the honesty of her silence said more than reassurance would have.

"I'd rather you didn't," Mom said, not unkindly. Davy looked like he wanted to argue, then visibly didn't, exhaustion finally catching up.

Chapter 8: Chapter 8

Dad had us up before dawn, working weight math on the kitchen table by lamplight while Clara and I ate eggs standing up.

"Four passengers back instead of two, plus whatever gear Millie and the girl want," he said, pencil down a column he'd clearly worked twice already. "Different airplane than yesterday. Swapping to the Cessna — more useful load, but heavier, slower, wants more runway."

"How much runway do we have out there?" Clara asked.

"Call it eleven hundred feet, if nothing's changed since I paced it two days ago. Loaded the way I'm planning — four adults, gear, full fuel — I want minimum nine hundred feet to clear a fifty-foot obstacle with margin I trust, and that's this morning's temperature, not whatever it climbs to by midday." He tapped a chart clipped in a plastic sleeve. "Density altitude again. Cool morning air, real margin. Same load at two in the afternoon on a warm day, might not have enough runway at all, and no amount of wanting it changes the physics."

"So we fly out early, load fast, get back up before the day heats," I said.

"That's the plan, not a flexible one. Not wheels-up by ten, we sit tight till evening rather than gamble a takeoff the numbers don't support."

Davy, slept in the bunkhouse, marginally more rested, leaned over the chart. "You do this math every flight?"

"Every flight, every load," Dad said. "Takeoff distance, landing distance, weight and balance, fuel reserves — none optional out here. No tower to call if I'm wrong, no mechanic on standby, no hospital forty minutes off. Get it wrong on the tame side, you've got options. Get it wrong out here, the options mostly involve walking a very long way home."

We flew out at first light, Dad at the stick of the bigger plane, a deeper, steadier drone filling the cabin. He held a compass heading, checking his watch against a folded chart on one knee, correcting course in increments I only noticed because I was watching for them.

"Wind's stronger aloft than yesterday," he said, mostly to himself, adjusting two degrees. "Crosswind, pushing us right of track if I don't correct. Difference between finding that strip clean on the first pass and burning fuel hunting for it."

"How do you tell you're drifting, no roads to check against?" Davy asked from the back.

"Ground speed versus airspeed. Watching how features track past compared to how they should on a true heading. Takes practice — getting it wrong early, learning what wrong looks like from the air." Dad glanced back. "Not so different from what your family does jumping. Building a mental model, checking it constantly against what you're seeing, correcting the second they stop matching. Different tools. Same discipline."

Davy considered that, something in his face suggesting he liked the comparison.

We found the strip on schedule, Dad easing down a long final with the economy of a man who'd flown this exact grass a hundred times, calling numbers quietly. Millie and Cent were already waiting, packs assembled.

"That didn't take long," Millie said, eyeing the plane.

"Wanted the cool air for the load home," Dad said, already climbing out to redo weight-and-balance with the new passengers.

Cent watched with open interest, leaning over his shoulder at the kneeboard. "You do that math for every passenger?"

"Every one," Dad said. "Weight, gear, fuel, temperature, wind — all of it decides not just whether we get off the ground but how much runway it takes. Not guessing on a strip this short."

"Never had to think about any of that," Cent said, half to herself. "Weight limits I know backward. Runway, wind, temperature — different math I never needed."

"Different tools," Davy said, echoing Dad's own words with a tired smile. "Same discipline. You'll pick it up fast."

Loading took twenty minutes, Dad redoing weight-and-balance twice as gear got stowed, moving a duffel to redistribute toward center of gravity.

"Everybody strapped?" He ran his eyes across all four passengers. "Let's go while the morning's still cooperating."

He took the runway, held brakes against full throttle, released. The Cessna surged forward heavier and more deliberate than the lighter plane, eating the eleven hundred feet with what felt like not nearly enough room — then the wheels lifted clean, almost exactly the margin his numbers promised, and the homestead fell away beneath us, shrinking into the vast green quiet of an Earth that, for the first time in six years, two wholly different families now understood themselves to be sharing.

Chapter 9: Chapter 9

Rick was waiting at the strip's edge before Dad finished rollout, stationed there since breakfast on the theory that if he couldn't come on the rescue, he could at least see the results.

"Is that them? The jump family?" Jogging alongside the taxiing plane, earning a sharp look from Dad he flatly ignored. "Can she really go anywhere? Like anywhere anywhere?"

"Anywhere I've been, or anywhere I can see clearly enough," Cent said, climbing down stiff from two hours folded into an aircraft seat. "Not actually anywhere. There's rules."

"There's always rules," Rick said, aggrieved. "What happens if you jump somewhere you've never seen?"

"You don't," Cent said. "Or you try once, badly, and never again, because it doesn't go well." Light enough, but something flickered underneath that told me there was a real story she wasn't offering somebody she'd known eleven seconds.

Millie was already folded into a hug by Mom, both women sizing each other up over each other's shoulders, deciding fast whether the other was worth trusting.

"Miriam Newell. Everyone calls me Miriam. Your husband's told me a fair amount over dinner, but I'd rather hear the rest from you."

"Millie Rice. Imagine we've both got a fair amount to compare, once everybody's fed and nobody's running on yesterday's adrenaline."

Marie had come down with her tablet under one arm, and made a beeline for Cent almost before introductions finished.

"Can I ask something technical? About the jumping. Sorry — strange thing to lead with, but I've been running numbers on you for two days without meeting you, and I have questions."

Cent blinked, then grinned — the first real, unguarded expression I'd seen from her since the standoff that morning. "Nobody's ever led with the numbers before. Go ahead."

"Does it cost you anything physically? Energy, calories, fatigue — or is it more like flipping a switch, no toll at all?"

"Costs plenty," Cent said, falling into step beside Marie as the group started toward the house, the two of them already a half-step ahead in conversation. "Big jumps, I'm wrecked after — shaky, starving, sometimes sleep for hours. Dad says it eases with practice. Never free, though. Cent hesitated, and I recognized what moved behind her eyes — the cautious hope of a kid who'd spent her whole life believing herself singular, suddenly finding people who might actually help her understand the thing she'd never had anyone but her father to ask about.

"Yeah," she said. "I'd like that. A lot, actually."

Clara caught my eye as we climbed the last stretch to the house — whatever the shifting baseline turned out to mean, something was already happening here that mattered on its own terms. A girl who'd grown up believing herself alone in her own strangeness, walking up a dirt path surrounded by people who found it fascinating instead of frightening.

Inside, Mom had the table cleared and a real meal going — Davy's first real coffee in what he swore was a year, eggs, bread, stew simmering since before dawn — and for a while, sitting around that table with two families who'd started the week believing themselves alone on this Earth, the conversation wasn't about baselines or watchers at all. Gold panning technique. Plane maintenance. Whether horses did better on the wildside than cattle. Rick peppering Cent with questions she answered with more patience than I'd have managed at her age. Clara's hand finding mine under the table.

It didn't last. I understood that even while it was happening. But it lasted long enough, and when Marie finally excused herself to check the instrument readings before dark, I saw her pause at the doorway and look back at the room — two families, eight people, noisy and warm and stubbornly alive — like memorizing exactly what it looked like, in case whatever she found on that screen meant it wouldn't look the same for a while.

Chapter 10: Chapter 10

Chapter 12: Chapter 12

We spent the morning turning flat ground behind the greenhouse into what Marie insisted on calling "the test range" — four sawhorses, a tape measure, every portable instrument the compound owned under a tarp.

"Might not be different here," Marie said. "Don't want to overclaim off one data point. Bigger jump next, still inside your comfort range — see if the reading scales the way the theory predicts."

Overhead, the sky slid slow toward evening, and for the first time since the flag went up, I let myself believe — carefully, the way you'd trust ice you hadn't tested all the way across — that this might not end the way Davy's worst fear had painted it that morning.

Chapter 13: Chapter 13

The next morning's test was supposed to be the last before everybody sat down to talk about what came next. Would've stayed that simple if the wind hadn't shifted an hour before we started and brought the herd with it.

Nobody thought much of it at first. Herds moved across this grassland most days, distant smudges that never came close. Dad had explained early on — something between a bison and something older, heavier through the shoulder, temperament nobody had felt like testing up close — mostly kept its own business, wide and indifferent to four humans and a folding table of instruments.

Marie had Cent lined up for a longer jump, three hundred yards to a dead cottonwood, the biggest distance test yet, and everyone was watching the tablet instead of the horizon when Rick's voice cut the yard, sharp and scared.

"They're not moving away. Look — they're coming this way."

The smudge had resolved, in the last ten minutes nobody noticed, into a genuine herd on the move. Not the slow drift Dad described. A stampede, dust boiling behind it, tracking a line straight across the flat ground where Cent stood, three hundred yards out — past the fence, past anywhere close enough for the rest of us to reach her first.

"Cent!" Davy was already moving, faster than I'd have believed, but three hundred yards was three hundred yards, and the herd was closing it faster than he could close his.

"She has to jump back, now," Millie said, voice tight with panic held down by main force. "You can't outrun that and neither can she—"

"She hasn't studied the return point close enough for a jump this fast," Davy said, already running anyway. "If she jumps blind she could come out anywhere, or nowhere—"

I didn't hear the rest because I was moving too, Dad half a step ahead, both of us cutting toward the horse paddock instead of Cent directly — I'd spent two years around Dad, knew what he was thinking before he said it.

"Can't outrun a stampede on foot," he shouted, vaulting the fence, "but a horse buys us speed to get between her and the front edge — give her something clean enough to jump to instead of jumping blind."

Two horses saddled and moving inside a minute that felt like an hour. I have never ridden anything the way I rode that horse across three hundred yards with a stampede bearing down, wind tearing at my ears, watching Cent's small figure frozen at the base of that cottonwood with a wall of dust closing on her like weather instead of something happening to a person.

"Cent!" Clara's voice, somewhere behind, desperate. "The tree — climb it, you don't need line of sight if you're not jumping, just get up—"

Whether she heard or worked it out herself, by the time Dad and I closed the last hundred yards she'd scrambled into the cottonwood's lower branches, high enough to clear the herd's backs, bare wood groaning under her weight.

The herd split around the tree like water around a stone, thundering past in a wall of dust and noise that swallowed the world for the longest thirty seconds of my life. Dad hauled his horse up fifty yards short — close enough to see, too far to do anything but watch.

It was over almost as fast as it started. Dust settling, hoofbeats fading, and Cent still up there, arms wrapped around the trunk, face gone gray-white.

Davy reached the tree on foot ten seconds after us, and didn't say a word before Cent half-fell out of the branches into his arms. He just held her, both shaking, the rest of the group pounding up behind, breathless and not thinking about instrument readings anymore.

"I'm okay," Cent kept saying into his shoulder. "I'm okay, Dad—"

"You didn't jump," Davy said, pulling back to look at her, hands braced on her shoulders like he needed the contact to believe she was real. "Why?"

"Wasn't clean enough," Cent said, still shaking. "The compound, I know it, but not from three hundred yards with a herd bearing down and half a second to decide — could've come out inside a wall, or underground. Didn't trust it. Climbed instead. Only thing I actually trusted in that second."

Marie, last to arrive, tablet clutched to her chest, was staring at the screen with an expression I couldn't read.

"Marie," Mom said, sharp. "What is it?"

"The reading." Slowly. "Still climbing. Bigger than anything we've seen. And she didn't jump at all."

Chapter 14: Chapter 14

We got everyone back in pieces — Cent riding double behind her father, too shaken to trust her own legs, Rick uncharacteristically silent, Clara's hand finding mine again without either of us commenting on it — and it was a good hour before anybody sat down to actually look at what Marie's instruments caught.

"Walk us through it again," Mom said, once Cent had a blanket and a mug of something hot and had mostly stopped shaking. "Slowly. If it's what I think it means, it changes everything we learned yesterday."

Marie had the graph up, tracing the line with a finger not entirely steady. "This morning's baseline, flat. Herd starts moving — nothing on the instrument, makes sense, nothing to do with Cent. Then here." She tapped a point, steeper than anything from yesterday. "Roughly when the herd's course became unmistakable. Headed straight for her."

"Before she did anything," Davy said. "Before she jumped, before she climbed, before any choice at all."

"Before any of that," Marie confirmed. "The spike starts with the danger, not her response to it. Keeps climbing through the whole stampede, peaks almost exactly when the herd was closest to the tree — then drops off fast, faster than yesterday's spikes decayed, once she was safe."

Nobody spoke for a long moment, everyone working through the same recalculation.

"So it's not tracking jumps at all," Clara said slowly. "Or not only. It's tracking — threat? Risk to her specifically?"

"That's the shape of it, from one data point I very much didn't want to collect this way," Marie said. "Yesterday's tests were controlled, safe. Small spikes proportional to jump size. Nothing like this. Biggest reading we've seen, and it happened during the one moment in two days she was in genuine danger and didn't use her ability at all."

"Doesn't sound like measurement anymore," Davy said, an edge now, the fear curdling into anger. "Sounds like whatever's watching cares, specifically, whether my daughter lives or dies. Most reassuring thing I've heard in two days, or the most terrifying. Can't tell you which."

"Maybe it doesn't have to be either," Mom said, careful, thinking out loud rather than delivering a conclusion. "Maybe it's simpler. If this arrangement's about grading how people treat a world they've been given conditional access to — maybe what's measured isn't Cent's ability at all. Maybe it's whether the people around her responded the way a connected community responds when one of its own is in danger. Charlie and Dad riding out. Clara shouting for her to climb. Davy running toward the herd instead of away. All of us dropping everything the second Rick called out." She looked around the table. "That's not a test of one girl's power. That's a test of what the rest of us do with it."

Cent had gone still, mug forgotten. "You think it was testing all of you. Not me."

"I think I don't actually know, and I'd be doing exactly what I warned against yesterday to pretend otherwise. But it's at least as likely as the alternative, and a great deal more hopeful, and I'd rather hold the version that doesn't require us afraid of an eighteen-year-old's own existence."

Davy let a long moment pass, anger draining into something more like exhausted relief. "Still want that fence line checked. Spotter posted anytime she's out past it, herd or no herd."

"Fair," Mom said. "Good sense, watcher or no watcher."

"What about the testing?" Cent asked, subdued, bracing for the answer. "Do we stop?"

Mom looked at her, then at Davy, some silent negotiation passing between two adults who'd each spent this whole ordeal trying to protect people they loved without fully understanding what they were protecting them from.

"I think we learned something today we couldn't have learned any other way, however much none of us would've chosen it," Mom said finally. "I don't think the answer's stopping. I think it's being smarter — real safety margins, real oversight, nothing that puts you within a hundred miles of another morning like this one. But no. I don't think hiding's the lesson. I think whatever's watching cares more about how we treat each other than what any one of us can do." She reached across, took Cent's free hand in both of hers. "You're not a liability. Whatever else today taught us, it taught us that much."

Outside, the light went gold over the ridge, same as the evening before, giving nothing away — and for the second night running, I found myself hoping, provisionally, that we'd gotten this one right.

Chapter 15: Chapter 15

It was Millie who raised the idea first, three days after the stampede, once the shock had settled into ordinary caution and everyone had stopped flinching at Marie's tablet chiming.

"We need a strip," she said, over breakfast, flat and practical. "Real one, at the homestead, graded like yours is here. What happened to Cent — some version happens again, sooner or later, and I don't want our only fast help to be an eight-mile walk and a plane landing on unimproved grass."

Dad, who'd mostly stayed out of the last three days' drama, sat up with the focus he reserved for problems actually his to solve.

"How much flat ground close to the house?"

"Fourteen hundred feet, if you don't mind a slight grade at the north end. More if we clear another cottonwood stand."

"Fourteen hundred's workable, so long as the grade's consistent." Dad was already sketching. "Soil? Sandy, clay, mixed?"

"Mixed. Clay near the river, sandier at the house."

"Clay's a mixed blessing. Compacts hard once properly graded, but murder wet, cracks if the moisture's wrong during compaction. Time it for a dry stretch, and even then do it in stages — rough grade, let it settle, fine grade and compact after."

"How long," Davy asked. "Realistically."

"With the equipment we've got — call it three weeks, weather cooperating, nobody rushing compaction. Rushing that's how you get a strip that fails under the first heavy landing." Dad looked up. "Question's how we get the equipment sixty miles to your place. Not something Cent can jump — not in one piece, probably not disassembled either, not that weight."

"Fly it," Clara said. "Load it on the Cessna, or break it down for two trips."

"Compactor alone pushes the edge of what the Cessna carries safely," Dad said, working the numbers. "Could do it. Two, three trips, good weather each time." He glanced at Davy. "Or a faster way, if your daughter's willing, and we do it smart instead of reckless."

"What do you mean?"

"Break the compactor into component parts — engine, drum, frame, hydraulics — same as your sawmill years back. Series of jumps, each piece well under Cent's limit, proper rest between."

Cent sat up, real interest. "How many pieces?"

"Eight, maybe ten, to stay comfortably under your limit." Dad glanced at Davy for permission, the same respect he'd shown since the stampede. "Your call. Just laying out options."

Davy looked at his daughter, weighing the same protective instinct against her reasonable right to some say — smaller stakes this time, nothing like a stampede with half a second to decide.

"Small jumps," he said finally. "Well under your limit. Proper rest between each, no pushing fatigue just to hurry."

"I can do that," Cent said, real steadiness now.

"Then that's the plan," Mom said. "Dad coordinates disassembly and flight backup. Marie keeps instruments running, same protocol — we stop the second anything trends wrong."

"Once the strip's in," Millie said, "we're not two isolated families. Real transport, both directions. Help's minutes away instead of an hour."

"Something else comes with a real strip," Davy said slowly, an idea taking shape behind his eyes. "Supply runs. Not gold traded through channels I'd rather not discuss. If we've got a strip and gate access, that changes the whole economics. Proper tools. Proper stock. Never let myself think about it, no realistic way to get it there before."

"True," Mom said. "Also means more traffic across a boundary something's apparently watching closely. Not saying no. Saying we go in with eyes open."

"Eyes open," Davy agreed. "Only way any of this works."

Outside, Rick had wandered to Dad's compactor sketch, studying it with the intensity of someone who'd just found an engineering project and was already plotting how to make himself indispensable — and watching him, I thought that whatever this arrangement turned into, something real was already being built here, piece by piece, the patient way everything worth having on this Earth got built.

Chapter 16: Chapter 16

Dad spent two days with a socket set and a shop manual older than I was, breaking the compactor into what he called "jump-sized units" — a lot more complicated than just unbolting the biggest pieces.

"Can't just pull it apart any old way," he explained, flat on his back under the drum housing while I passed him tools I'd never heard of before that week. "Every piece has to come back together clean — no missing bolts, no cracked seals, no kinked hydraulic lines. Thinking about reassembly, every step. A compactor in ten perfect pieces that won't run once bolted back together hasn't saved anybody a minute."

"How do you know the order?"

"Same way you'd know the order to put it back together. Work backward from the last piece that goes on, forward from the first that comes off, make sure every step depends only on steps already done." He wrestled a stubborn bolt loose. "Not so different from planning a flight route. Think through every leg, what each depends on, what happens if one doesn't go the way you planned."

Nine pieces by the second evening — engine block separate from the frame, drum split from its drive assembly, hydraulic lines drained and capped, each weighed on a rigged bathroom scale, comfortably under Davy's insisted limit.

Cent came out to look them over, crouching beside the engine block to study the padding.

"You really think about all of this," she said. "Never had to think this hard about anything I've jumped. Mostly it's pick it up, picture where it's going, go."

"Because you've mostly jumped things that don't mind getting jostled," Dad said. "An engine block minds. Precision costs effort up front, saves grief on the other end — true whether you're planning a flight or packing cargo."

They started the relay next morning, first light, same cool-air logic Dad insisted on for every flight — except now applied to Cent's own body instead of an airplane.

"Two pieces before a real rest," Davy said, stopwatch in hand despite Mom's gentle observation nobody was racing. "Fifteen minutes minimum between jumps, food and water every third one, and the second you feel a shake starting, you tell me, and we stop for the day."

"Dad, I know my own limits."

"I know you do. Also know you'll push past them if nobody's watching the clock. You get that from me." He held her eyes. "Humor me."

She jumped the first piece — a hydraulic control unit — and came back easy, hardly winded. "Confirmed at the homestead. Millie says it landed clean."

Two more went the same way, Davy's stopwatch ticking off rest, Marie's instruments murmuring small, proportional spikes — nothing like the stampede.

It was the sixth piece, the engine block, heaviest in the relay, that showed the strain.

Cent came back pale, sweating despite the cool morning, and sat down hard in the grass like her legs had simply stopped holding her up.

"Cent." Davy was at her side instantly. "Talk to me."

"Fine," she managed, breathing hard. "That one cost more than I figured. Heavier than the scale suggested, or I'm more tired than I realized." A shaky smile. "Told you I know my limits. This is me telling you I found one."

"That's it. Done for today." Davy didn't ask, didn't negotiate — the same flat call he'd made about stopping the jumping entirely, except nobody argued this time.

"Three pieces left," Dad said, unbothered, gathering tarps. "Finish tomorrow, fresh, or fly them the slow way. Either way, six of nine in one morning's a better pace than I expected."

Cent managed a tired, real laugh. "You planned for this to take longer?"

"I plan for everything to take longer than it needs to," Dad said. "That way going faster's a pleasant surprise, and going slower, I'm not the only one who saw it coming." He hauled the last tarp under one arm, looked at Davy. "Get her fed, get her rested. Finish properly tomorrow."

Davy helped his daughter up, arm firm around her shoulders. Clara watched them with an expression I was probably wearing too — the quiet satisfaction of two very different kinds of hard-won competence, plane math and jump math, finally moving in the same direction, one padded piece at a time.

Chapter 17: Chapter 17

Cent finished the last three pieces the next morning, fresh and steady the way Dad predicted, and by midmorning the compactor sat reassembled on a tarp, nine pieces become one machine, Dad running his hands over every joint before letting anybody near the ignition.

"Hydraulic pressure's holding," he said, squinting at a gauge. "No weeping at the seals. She's clean." He looked up at Cent, watching with visible pride. "Good work. Every piece came through without a scratch. Better record than I get flying cargo through the gate most weeks."

"High bar," Davy said, dry, and Dad huffed something like a laugh.

The grading took four more days, Dad walking the compactor and grader in long, even passes while Davy and Millie staked the centerline with string and pegs, checking grade every twenty feet with a hand level.

"Want it crowned slightly," he explained, sighting down the centerline with Davy. "Couple inches higher in the middle than the edges, so water sheds instead of pooling and softening the surface. Too flat, standing water eats at your compaction from underneath, slow and invisible, until the whole thing's soft where it looks solid."

"How do you build that without survey equipment?"

"String line and a hand level, patience, checking six times more often than feels necessary." Dad sighted again, adjusted a peg an inch. "Out here, patience is most of the equipment anyway. Same as your gold panning — the sluice box doesn't do the work itself. Just gives the work somewhere to happen."

Rick had made himself thoroughly indispensable — running stakes, hauling water for the compactor, pestering Dad with questions Dad answered every one of.

"Why's clay different from sand for this?" Rick asked, third day, watching the drum roll over the darker soil near the low end.

"Clay particles are smaller, flatter, pack tighter once you squeeze the water out right — harder, more stable surface once cured," Dad said. "Trouble's getting moisture content right. Too wet, you're smearing it around, trapping water under a skin that looks solid and isn't. Too dry, won't bond, crumbles under the first heavy roll. Find the window by feel — squeeze a handful, see how it crumbles under your thumb."

"Can I try?"

Dad looked at Davy, who nodded, and handed Rick a fistful of dark soil. Rick squeezed it the way he'd watched Dad do for three days.

"Holds a shape," he reported. "Crumbling a little at the edge when I press it."

"About right for today. Little dry of ideal, but workable. Tomorrow, if that overnight dew comes through, might hit the sweet spot exactly." Dad clapped his shoulder, approving. "Decent hand for this. Most folks squeeze once and declare victory whether they're right or not."

By the fifth day the strip was done — packed, crowned, checked twice more with the hand level — and Davy stood at the north end with an expression I hadn't seen on him before, disbelief and a very specific pride.

"Six years," he said, half to himself. "Never let myself think past what I could carry one trip at a time. Now there's a runway. Ground I've walked past a hundred times, never imagining a plane could land here."

"First flight tomorrow morning, if the weather holds," Dad said. "Light load, nothing to prove. Just confirming she handles the way the numbers say."

"Want to be there when you land," Davy said. "See it."

"You'll see it," Dad said. "You built half of it staking that centerline. Man ought to watch his own work prove out."

That evening, around the homestead's own cookfire for the first time since the week started — Newells and Rices together, chicory and real coffee both going around depending who wanted which — Millie raised her mug in a toast nobody had planned.

"To the strip. And whatever's watching us build it. If you're grading us, I hope you're grading generously, because this is about the best any of us know how to do."

"Hear, hear," Davy said, and the circle drank to it under a sky thick with more stars than either family had grown up believing a sky could hold.

Chapter 18: Chapter 18

Dad brought the Cub in low over the tree line just after seven, light still gold across the grass, and I stood beside Davy watching a man who'd spent six years measuring his world in three-hundred-pound increments watch an airplane come down onto ground he'd staked out himself.

"Wind's light, straight down the strip," Dad's voice crackled over the radio Marie had rigged. "Bringing her in on final."

The plane dropped clean, wheels finding packed clay with a soft double chirp instead of the ragged bounce from our first landing on unimproved grass, rolled out straight, stopped with easily four hundred feet still ahead.

Davy didn't say anything for a second. When Dad cut the engine and climbed down, Davy walked out and shook his hand like trying to convey something a handshake alone couldn't hold.

"Smoothest landing I've had on packed clay in a while," Dad said. "You built her true. Crown's holding, no soft spots, no drift on rollout. Proper strip, Mr. Rice, built by people who'd never built one before. I'd fly it again without a second thought."

"Charlie," Davy said, turning to me, voice thick. "Go get Cent. Want her standing here for the second load. Whatever else this week's cost her, I want her to see plainly what her own hands helped build."

I found her near the garden. By the time we got back, Dad had turned the Cub around, taken off again for the first real supply run — tools, seed stock, a water pump Millie had wanted for two years.

He came in a second time, another clean landing, and this time Cent went silent, watching the plane roll to a stop on ground she'd helped make possible one exhausting jump at a time.

"That's ours," she said, half to herself. "We built that."

"You built that," Millie corrected, arm around her daughter. "Every piece of that compactor came through because of you. Don't shrink it down to something smaller than it was."

Marie had her tablet out, checking readings with the close attention she'd given every test since the stampede.

"Anything?" Mom asked.

"Small spikes on both landings. Proportional, nothing alarming — ordinary gate traffic, based on two weeks of logs. Nothing like the stampede." Real relief crossed her face. "I think this is what normal looks like now. I think we found the new baseline, and it's not a bad one."

"Two weeks," Mom said, looking at the strip, the plane, two families standing on ground that was rough grass a month before. "Two weeks ago I believed my family tended the only door onto this Earth. I don't believe that anymore. Won't again. And I'm not half as frightened by that as I expected."

"What changed your mind?" Davy asked.

Mom considered it, watching Cent and Millie and Rick inspecting the new water pump with the reverence of a boy who'd waited his whole life for exactly this project.

"You did," she said finally. "All of you. I spent my whole life believing the job was controlling access. Then a family who never asked permission showed up on my doorstep, and instead of the disaster I'd have predicted a month ago, two households built something in two weeks neither could have built alone in six months. Maybe the job was never about the door. Maybe it was always about what people do once they're standing on the other side of it — and I only just got to see that proven."

Marie's radio crackled, Dad's voice static-edged but pleased. "Load two down clean, third ready whenever anybody wants to keep going. Strip's holding beautifully. We're in business."

"We're in business," Davy repeated, looking around — plane, strip, garden, second structure nearly finished, two families' kids underfoot, and somewhere above it all, watching, an intelligence none of them had seen and might never understand, but which had, so far, seemed content to let them keep building.

"Bring the third load in," Mom said into the radio, and behind the words was something I hadn't heard from her in two weeks — not just relief. Something closer to hope, unguarded, and for the first time since the flag went up, unafraid.

Chapter 19: Chapter 19

It happened on the ninth day after the strip went in, ordinary right up until it wasn't.

Marie found me in the tack barn checking harness, and she had the controlled urgency I'd learned, over two weeks, to take seriously before she'd even spoken.

"Something's different. Not a spike. Not the usual pattern at all. I need Mom, now."

We found Mom at the house, and by the time the four of us crossed back down to the gate barn, Dad was already there, standing well back from the doorway with an expression I'd never seen on him in two years.

The door had changed.

Not dramatic. No flare, no light. Just gone still in a way it never went still — hanging dead level on its hinges, not so much as a shiver of give when a gust came through the yard behind us, the padlock rigid on its hasp like it had been welded shut instead of merely closed. The view through it had frozen mid-flicker, caught between one channel and the next, neither fully wildside grass nor the barn's own shadowed boards, held there instead of resolving the way it always did in under a second.

"Instruments are dead," Marie said, staring at her tablet. "Not broken. Dead, like whatever they've been measuring stopped registering as measurable. Like it's not being graded anymore. Like it's just — here. Present. Looking back."

Nobody moved. Even Dad, never at a loss for a next practical step, stood rooted at the edge, hands loose, waiting.

"Should we get everyone else?" Clara asked. "Davy, Millie, Cent—"

"Not yet," Mom said, low and careful. "Not until we understand what we're looking at. If this is a response, I don't want six more sets of hopes riding on the next few minutes."

The held flicker lasted less than a minute, and then, without any sound I could point to afterward, words arrived. Not spoken. Not written. Whole and complete, in the same place thoughts arrive — except plainly, unmistakably, not mine.

Miriam Newell. Home-born. You have questions.

Mom's body had gone rigid, but her voice held steadier than I'd have believed.

"I do," she said, into the empty air. "I'd like to know what you are. What you've been measuring these two weeks, and why. And what you intend to do about a girl who reached this Earth by a door my family never built and never knew existed."

The measurement is not new. It has always occurred. Your instruments simply never before had cause to register a second variable.

"That's not an answer to the third part."

No. It is context for it. A pause that felt deliberate. The arrangement was never conditioned on the door remaining singular. It was conditioned on what those who cross it choose to become on the other side. That has not changed since the day you first crossed it, nor in two weeks of a second family's arrival.

Marie had gone completely still, lips moving silently, memorizing.

"The stampede," Mom said. "The spike before any jump. Before any choice. I need to understand what that measured."

Fear is not measured. Response to fear is. A community that moves as one, without hesitation, toward a member in danger — that is data of a different order than any individual ability could provide. It is, in fact, the only data that has ever mattered, across every world this arrangement has extended to. Powers are common. Nearly irrelevant. What a group chooses to do with each other under genuine pressure, when nothing constrains the choice but who they already are — that has always been the entire measurement.

Mom let the silence stretch, and when she spoke again her voice had changed — not less measured, but less afraid.

"My whole life. Me, and my own mother before me, believing the job was managing a door. It was never about the door at all."

The door was never the point. It has only ever been the opportunity.

"The second family," Mom pressed. "Davy, Millie, Cent. Their ability — does it change the arrangement? Separate accounting, separate terms?"

It requires nothing your family has not already, correctly, chosen to extend. Welcome. Patience. Honest cooperation instead of territorial anxiety. You have already demonstrated the correct response, Miriam Newell. That is the entire content of any accounting this arrangement will ever require.

The frozen flicker resolved, settling onto ordinary wildside grass the way it always did, the padlock going loose on its hasp again, Marie's tablet chiming once, readings flickering back.

One further matter. Something in the texture had shifted, gentler now, almost warm. The girl who climbed rather than jumped blind, three hundred yards from a place she did not trust herself to picture clearly under pressure — tell her the caution itself was noted, and valued, exactly as much as either family's courage riding out to meet her. Wisdom under fear is its own form of the thing this arrangement has always been built to recognize.

And then it was simply gone, the door swinging free on its hinges with its ordinary faint creak, the gate settling back into its usual hum, instruments logging nothing but routine baseline noise.

Nobody spoke for a long moment. Dad let out a breath he'd clearly been holding since the door went rigid. Clara's hand found mine, gripping hard. Mom stood at the threshold with an expression I'd never seen on her — a lifetime of half-understood history reorganizing itself into something both smaller and vastly larger than an hour before.

"Well," she said finally, thick with something that might, in anyone else, have been the start of tears. "I think we'd better go tell Davy and Millie. And this time we owe them the whole truth of it. Not managed. All of it. Exactly as it was given to us."

Chapter 20: Chapter 20

We found them at the homestead — Davy up on the roofline setting shingles, Millie in the garden, Cent cross-legged on the porch with a book she wasn't reading — and something in how the four of us walked up together must have shown on our faces, because Davy was down off that roof before Mom said a word.

"What happened," he said, not a question.

"Something spoke to us," Mom said. "At the gate. I'd rather tell you sitting down. You've earned every word of it, not a summary."

They gathered on the half-built porch, and Mom told it plainly — the door gone rigid on its hinges, the padlock welded still, the words that arrived without being spoken, a lifetime of measurement that had never been about the door at all. When she got to the stampede, to fear and response and what a community chooses under pressure, Davy's hand found his daughter's shoulder and held on tight.

"It said something else," Mom continued, watching Cent closely. "Specifically for you. The caution mattered. Climbing instead of jumping blind — noted, valued, exactly as much as anyone riding out to reach you. Wisdom under fear, it called it. Its own form of the thing this whole arrangement exists to recognize."

Cent went still, then, without warning, started to cry — not the frightened tears from the stampede, something looser, relieved. Two weeks of quietly believing her own existence might be the danger everyone was managing, finally hearing, plainly, the opposite was true.

Millie had her arms around her before anyone else moved. Davy crouched in front of both, a hand on each. For a while nobody said anything.

"So it's not about the ability," Davy said eventually, once Cent had steadied, wiping her face and laughing shakily at herself. "Never was. Twenty years I've half believed this was a liability I was passing down. And the whole time, what mattered was—" He stopped, searching for the word.

"What we do with each other," Clara offered. "That's the whole accounting."

"That's it," Davy agreed, wonder plain. "A lifetime of careful management on your side, six years of mine hiding out here terrified of being found, and it was never about controlling access. Just watching whether we'd take care of each other, once we were here."

"I think we've both been carrying more weight than the job required," Mom said. "I managed a door I thought had to stay narrow to stay safe. You hid a gift you thought had to stay secret. And what was being measured was never the door or the gift. Just this." She gestured at the porch, the garden, the strip. "All of it. Exactly this."

Cent, steadier, looked up with wonder of her own. "Does this mean I don't have to be careful anymore?"

"I think it means you're exactly as careful as good sense requires, same as anyone," Mom said. "Not fear-careful. Sensible. Same caution that made you climb that tree instead of jumping blind. Keep it because it's wise, not because something's watching."

"What happens now?" Millie asked. "Practically. Does anything change, or was this just an explanation, and we go back to building the strip and running the garden?"

Mom considered it, looking out at the homestead, the strip cutting its pale line through the grass.

"I think we go back to the life we were building," she said. "Except without the fear underneath it — you, that Cent's existence might get this Earth shut down; me, that a second door might mean I'd failed the one responsibility I've carried since before Charlie was born." A tired smile. "I think we just get to be neighbors now. Properly. Without the weight."

"Neighbors," Davy repeated, testing the word the way he'd tested "home" that first night. "Sixty miles apart, one family flying and one jumping, and somehow, apparently, neighbors."

"The best kind," Dad said, from the porch steps, real warmth in it. "The kind that shows up when the stampede's bearing down, and doesn't wait to be asked."

The evening settled slow over the homestead, both families lingering long past any reason to, and somewhere in the middle of it Rick roped Cent into a half-abandoned engineering project with the water pump, the two bent together with the absorbed seriousness of people who'd found a genuine peer in the strangest circumstances — and watching them, I thought that whatever the watcher's larger purpose turned out to be, we'd already gotten the only answer that had really mattered.

We'd shown up for each other. That had been enough.

Chapter 21: Chapter 21

Word travels strange when part of your family lives on the other side of a gate with no phone line running through it. Took the better part of a week for what happened to reach the tame side, and it was Clara's mother, of all people, who heard it secondhand over the shortwave and immediately insisted on coming through to see for herself.

"You'd think the gate spoke to somebody every other Tuesday," Clara said, watching her mother on the grainy video link, "the way she's treating this like ordinary Thursday business."

"I heard that," her mother said, dry, from the screen. "I am treating it like the single most important development in this family's entire history of tending that door, which it is, and I'd like to see the strip and meet this Rice family properly before I believe any of it happened the way Dad described."

Mom's mouth twitched. "You're welcome anytime, Sarah."

"I'll be through Thursday. Tell Davy and Millie to expect one more for dinner. And tell that boy of yours—" her eyes cut to me through the static "—I want the whole story from his side too, not the official summary. Raised enough kids to know the official summary leaves out the parts that mattered."

The line crackled out. Clara laughed. "She's going to grill you a solid hour. Ask anybody who's ever had actual news."

"I don't mind," I said, and meant it. In the week since the gate went quiet again, I'd found myself wanting to tell the story properly — not the tidy version already calming into local legend, but the actual messy shape of it.

Marie, meanwhile, had thrown herself into a different kind of aftermath — holed up with her instruments and handwritten notes, trying to build something permanent out of two weeks of reactive measurement.

"I want a real baseline," she told me, third evening, notes spread across the table while Rick pretended not to look over her shoulder. "Not just 'normal is whatever it was before the spike.' A model — what a healthy reading looks like across ordinary activity, so if something changes again, we're not starting from scratch."

"You think something will change again?"

"I think something always changes eventually. Not pessimism. The last two weeks taught me the world doesn't stay still just because we understood one piece of it." Chapter 25: Chapter 25

Mom went out to the gate barn alone that evening, after the research room emptied, and I found her an hour later at the threshold, arms wrapped against the cooling air.

Miriam Newell. You have a question you are afraid to ask plainly.

Mom went very still. "Explain that."

Thirty years ago, this Earth held one small, careful family, tending a single door, rarely tested past ordinary caution. A crisis worth measuring occurred once every decade or two, because there were few moments where the outcome genuinely hung in doubt. Today this Earth holds two families, a second means of crossing, children growing into responsibilities their parents never carried at the same age, a homestead built from nothing in six years, a strip built from nothing in nine days. More life invites more risk. More risk invites more moments worth measuring. The frequency is not a countdown. It is a consequence of growth.

"So it's not accelerating toward something," Mom said, slow, relief not quite catching up with understanding yet. "It's just more, because there's more of us."

Yes. And more of you, should this continue, means more still. That is not a warning. It is simply what happens when a quiet place stops being quiet.

I felt something loosen in my chest I hadn't realized was clenched. Beside me, Mom let out a long breath, the day's fear draining from her shoulders.

"Max," Mom said, quieter, and I could hear what it cost her to ask plainly. "You measured his death. Did it matter? Did we do right by him, however long it took to find him?"

The door held rigid a moment longer than for anything else, the padlock's warmth deepening, and when the answer arrived it carried something almost gentle.

He was alone at the end. I will not tell you otherwise, because you have spent too many years already being told comfortable things that weren't true, and you deserve better than one more. No one came. No one could have — he told no one where the door was, so no one knew there was anywhere to look. "I think she'd say that's exactly why she brought it to you instead of sitting on it," I said. "Even when it was scary. Especially when."

"Yes," Mom agreed, looking back at the compound lights, warm and steady, two families' worth of ordinary life going on inside them. "I suppose that's the whole point we've all just spent a month learning."

Chapter 29: Chapter 29

Davy found me the next morning, coffee in hand, watching the horizon at the edge of the strip like it owed him an answer, and I got the sense he'd been waiting on purpose.

"Cent told me she told you," he said, no preamble. "About Apex Orbital. About Space Girl being my daughter, not some stranger on the news."

"She did. Still a little stunned, honestly. Watched the Grebenchekov footage same as everybody — never imagined the person under that visor was sitting across a cookfire from me a few weeks ago."

Davy managed something almost a laugh, proud and rueful at once. "That's Cent. Builds the big things quiet first, with people closest in, till it's solid enough to survive being seen. Did the same with the homestead." He looked out at the strip, settled and clear-eyed. "Millie and I helped where we could — I worked the pricing math, Millie took the first client call because Cent was too nervous. But the shape of it, the ambition — that's hers, completely. We just tried not to get in the way of something that good."

"Are you worried? The scale of what she's doing up there?"

"Every single time," he said plainly. "Doesn't ever fully go away. But I stopped letting the fear make her decisions a while back. Fear's never been a reliable guide to what's worth doing. Learned that the hard way, more than once, out here." A long breath. "Comms protocol stays. Sat phone, someone on the line every second, same as since that first five minutes nearly put Millie and me in an early grave from worry. Beyond that — I'd be a fool to stand in front of something this good just because it scares me."

Millie found us a few minutes later, the same settled clarity Davy had.

"She's going to do remarkable things," Millie said, watching the horizon alongside him. "Always suspected that, underneath the day-to-day of surviving out here. Just didn't expect the rest of you to find out this late."

"She won't need much from any of us," I said. "That's the part that gets me. Already worked out the pricing, the debris matching, without asking a single one of us for anything."

"She'll take advice if it's honest," Davy said. "Won't take charity, not even from people who love her. Never has." Something warm settling under the pride. "My job now isn't to build it for her. Just keep showing up on the other end of that sat phone and trust her with the rest."

"That's most of what any of us can offer anybody," Millie said. "Showing up. The rest, she'll manage."

We stood together at the strip's edge, and up at the compound I could hear Cent's voice, animated, already deep in conversation with Marie about something almost certainly orbital mechanics — two girls dreaming out loud about a future none of us had thought to imagine that large. Watching it unfold, easy and unafraid, I thought whatever the watcher had been measuring this whole strange month, it had gotten its answer plainly enough by now.

Chapter 30: Chapter 30

The weeks that followed settled into something that stopped feeling like an event and started feeling like how life worked now — visits losing their scheduled edges, Cent's sat phone check-ins becoming a familiar rhythm whether she was jumping that week or not, Rick and Cent's engineering obsessions expanding to half of Wildside's aging equipment, Marie and Cent disappearing for hours into conversations ranging from orbital mechanics to whatever else two restlessly brilliant minds found to chew on.

Mom kept her own quiet vigil over the instruments, though the readings had settled into something steady — small, proportional spikes tracking ordinary life instead of crisis, the baseline Marie had hoped to find and finally had.

"Keep waiting for it to feel less strange," Mom told me one evening on the porch, none of the old dread underneath this time. "Knowing something's out there, watching, and not being afraid of what it sees."

"Does it feel less strange?"

"Some days." She considered it honestly. "Other days I still catch myself bracing for the flag to go up again. Don't think that instinct ever fully goes away. You just learn to carry it differently, once you've got proof the answer's more likely kind than not."

Davy had taken to flying out every other week now, comfortable enough that Dad had started actually teaching him instead of just tolerating a passenger, an easy bantering rapport building over shared checklists that reminded me, more than once, of the friendship Uncle Max might have built with him too.

"Your dad tells me I'll solo within the year," Davy said one afternoon, watching Dad run a preflight with the thoroughness he always brought out for an appreciative audience. "Never thought I'd add flying to the list. Figured jumping covered enough ground I'd never need it."

"Different tools," I said. "Same discipline."

"You've stolen that line from your father four times now," Davy said, grinning.

"Good line."

"It is," he agreed. "Doesn't make it less stolen."

Cent kept scaling Apex Orbital — courting an insurance broker willing to underwrite something this unprecedented at volume, three rejections before somebody engaged with the math instead of dismissing the premise; negotiating her biggest client contract yet; running each job with increasingly precise measurements, Marie tracking every point with the same rigor she'd once turned on the watcher's baseline.

"Next batch's scheduled for month's end," Cent told me, vibrating with it, walking the fence line. "Hundred and change dead 1U units, pure down-only — a couple operators pooling decommissioned hardware. I go up, work the load, toss each one onto its own reentry vector, come home. Just volume."

"You nervous?"

"Terrified," she said, cheerfully. "Every test still terrifies me a little. Don't think that ever fully goes away. You just do the thing anyway, scared, because the alternative's never finding out what you're actually capable of."

I thought about Mom saying almost exactly that an hour before, and didn't mention it. Some truths were apparently just true enough everybody found their own way to the same words.

The homestead kept growing too, a third structure that spring — a proper workshop, Davy's answer to the bunkhouse, built with real dimensional lumber now the strip made hauling trivial instead of a six-jump ordeal — and standing at its edge one evening, watching both families' chaos spill warm across the yard, I thought that whatever any of us expected when that first flag went up two months before, none of it prepared me for how completely, how unremarkably, this had simply become home.

Chapter 31: Chapter 31

It came out to the rest of the group by accident, when Marie caught sight of the logbook still on the workshop bench and made the connection herself before anybody told her properly.

"Cent." Standing still, tablet forgotten, staring at a column header the same way I had. "These satellite IDs — same numbering scheme as the tracking catalog release from the Grebenchekov thing."

The room went still. Cent, to her credit, didn't flinch.

"Yeah," she said. "That was me."

"That was—" Marie sat down hard, shock and delight fighting for her face. "You're Space Girl. I watched that footage thirty times. Made Rick watch it. Made Joey watch it, and Joey doesn't watch anything that isn't cards or an engine."

"I did watch it," Joey said from the doorway, grinning. "More than once. Didn't clock the connection though. Very different look in a suit and visor than losing at cards on a Friday."

"We saw all the stuff about your rescue on the news," Rick added, quieter but no less genuine, "before we knew you existed as an actual person. Remember thinking, whoever that is, that's the bravest thing I've ever watched somebody do on live television." Something warm and awed under his usual reserve. "Didn't expect I'd end up teaching her to reset a transmission a few months later."

Cent's composure cracked, bright and embarrassed. "Wasn't that dramatic. Just did the math and went."

"Did the math and went, and pulled a dying man off a space station four hundred kilometers up with a jury-rigged suit and a phone call to General Sterling," Marie said. "You're allowed to let it be dramatic, just this once, for people who genuinely mean it."

Millie, catching the tail end, leaned in the doorway with quiet pride. "She'll never quite let herself sit in it. Comes home from something extraordinary and wants to talk about anything else within the hour."

"That's not true," Cent protested, without conviction.

"Completely true," Davy said, appearing behind Millie. "Had to schedule a conversation to get her to admit she was proud of the Fengyun debris remediation. Made three government agencies formally thank her."

"In writing," Millie added. "Actual letterhead."

Cent groaned into her hands, smiling underneath, and the room dissolved into the easy laughter that had become so ordinary I sometimes forgot how extraordinary the underlying facts actually were.

"For what it's worth," I said, once it settled, "I think Rick's right. Bravest thing I've ever watched somebody do, now that I know who was under the visor. And you get to be proud of it without it costing the quiet parts of your life you love just as much."

"Both things," Cent said, testing it out loud. "Space Girl and just Cent. Without one shrinking the other."

"Both things," Marie agreed. "Rather the whole lesson of this month. Nobody around here's just one thing anymore, if they ever were."

The evening wound on, Joey demanding a full account of deorbit mechanics with the enthusiasm of a man who'd been quietly obsessed with orbital physics since that first viral video and never had anywhere to put it — and watching the ordinary, extraordinary shape of it unfold, I thought that whatever the watcher had been measuring, it had gotten its answer plainly enough in exactly this kind of evening, over and over, long before any of us thought to ask it directly.

Chapter 32: Chapter 32

Clara found me by the horse paddock a few evenings later, the compound quiet behind us, and something in the easy way she settled onto the fence rail told me this wasn't going to be a short conversation.

"I keep thinking about that first morning," she said, watching Bessie-Two graze in the near dark. "The flag going up. Riding out not knowing what we'd find. How much I assumed — poachers, something to handle and put down carefully."

"I remember."

"I think about how wrong I was, and it doesn't embarrass me the way I'd expect. Just humbles me. A little." She turned to look at me properly. "Two months ago I thought I understood your family's whole job. Managing a door. Now I've watched that understanding get taken apart and rebuilt twice over, and somehow it's ended up bigger and kinder than I'd have guessed."

"You held that standoff together better than I did," I said. "Standing up out of the grass, hands raised. Wouldn't have found the nerve half as fast without you going first."

"Wasn't being brave. Was being impatient." She laughed at herself, low. "Never been good at staying still inside uncertainty. Rather walk toward the answer, even a dangerous one, than crouch wondering what it might turn out to be."

"That's its own kind of brave, whatever you call it."

She let a moment pass, voice going softer. "Been thinking about what the watcher said too. About what people choose to become on the other side of an open door." She looked at me properly. "I think about you riding at a stampede without stopping to ask if it was smart. Your mother carrying weight none of us understood for years before setting it down. Davy building a life on nothing but stubbornness. Cent hiding a whole heroic double life because she wasn't sure any of us could hold both halves of her at once."

"What are you getting at?"

"I don't want to spend my life just watching people become extraordinary from the sidelines," Clara said, plain and direct. "I want to be part of whatever this turns into. Not just your girlfriend, tagging along on the interesting parts. Actually part of it. The homestead, the strip, Apex, whatever comes next none of us have thought to imagine yet."

Something in my chest went warm and unsteady, the good kind. "You've been part of it since the day you stood up out of that grass," I said. "Never a version of this where you weren't."

"Know that, logically." She took my hand, easy and sure. "Wanted to say it out loud anyway. Some things matter more once you've actually said them."

We sat at the fence rail a while longer, compound lights warm behind us, Bessie-Two grazing unbothered, living the uncomplicated life of a cow who never needed to understand any of the enormous things happening around her to be content.

"Same time next month," Clara said eventually, echoing the phrase that had already become the whole shape of what this month built between two families.

"Same time next month," I agreed. "And the month after. However long it takes to stop feeling like something we have to say out loud."

The stars came out, four times as many as any tame-side sky, and I sat with Clara's hand in mine, thinking that whatever the watcher had been measuring this whole strange month, it had gotten its clearest answer yet in exactly this — nothing dramatic, just two people choosing each other under a sky that never needed anybody's permission to be this beautiful.

Chapter 33: Chapter 33

By the time the leaves turned that first autumn, "same time next month" had stopped being a phrase anybody said out loud most visits, the way Clara predicted — just the ordinary rhythm of two families running alongside each other now, homestead and compound and Home all three, distinct and connected.

Marie kept her instruments running out of habit more than fear, the baseline steady, small proportional spikes tracking nothing more dramatic than an ordinary crowded life — Rick and Cent's engineering projects, Joey's card games, Apex Orbital's growing quota, Clara underfoot at both compounds often enough Millie kept a second set of riding boots by the door for her.

Mom still went to the gate barn alone some evenings, the dread mostly gone, replaced by something closer to a habit of gratitude.

"Do you think it'll speak again?" I asked her once, walking back from the gate on an ordinary Tuesday.

"I think it'll speak when there's something worth saying," Mom said. "Stopped needing it to, though. That's the real difference this year. Used to need the reassurance. Now I just trust the shape of what we're building, watcher or no watcher."

Davy soloed that October, a clear cold morning, Dad watching from the ground with an expression of naked pride, the whole compound turning out, Cent recording on her phone with the fond exasperation of a daughter watching her father discover a new way to delight himself at forty-some years old.

"Your turn next," Davy told her, climbing down, breathless and grinning. "Fair's fair. You taught me sat phone discipline. I'll teach you the yoke."

"I already fly, Dad. I fly myself, into orbit, regularly."

"That's not flying. That's throwing yourself somewhere and hoping the landing works. I mean an actual airplane, actual controls, actual patience instead of a clear mental picture and a willingness to accept significant personal risk."

"Sounds like the same skill set to me."

"They are not the same skill set," Davy said, mock-offended, a man who'd clearly had this argument before and lost every time. "Fine. We'll find out. Same time next weekend."

The phrase landed easy in the cold morning air, no longer a question either needed to ask twice, and watching them walk back toward the strip, arguing amiably about flight instruction and orbital mechanics in the same breath, I thought about that first morning again — the flag going up, the standoff in the garden that could have gone so many worse directions.

Two months, start to finish, stranger to family. None of us, standing in that ring on the day it started, could have guessed the shape of what we'd build by the time the leaves turned.

Clara found me at the fence rail that evening, same spot, same easy quiet.

"Good day," she said eventually.

"Good day," I agreed.

"Keep waiting for the next enormous thing," she admitted, watching the light go gold over the ridge. "Instead I just keep getting this. Ordinary days. Good ones. Don't know when I stopped expecting that to feel like a letdown instead of the actual point."

"Maybe that's the whole answer," I said. "Maybe the enormous thing was never one event. Maybe it was just this, the whole time — people showing up for each other, ordinary Tuesday after ordinary Tuesday, until it adds up to something worth calling a life."

Clara considered that, stars stacking up thick the way they always did, indifferent and enormous, finally unafraid of anything either family might choose to become underneath them.

"Same time next month," she said, soft, more promise now than question.

"Same time next month," I agreed. "For as long as either of us gets a say in it."

Chapter 34: Chapter 34

I think about that first morning more than I probably should — the flag going up, the standoff in a garden that could have gone so many worse directions. But I think about the after even more, because that's where everybody actually landed, and landing well mattered more than any single dramatic morning.

Mom stopped going to the gate barn out of fear around the third month, though she never stopped going entirely. Most evenings still, sometimes with Dad, occasionally with Millie — the two had become friends who didn't need an occasion for comfortable silence. She told me once, late, she'd finally stopped waiting for the other shoe to drop — carrying Max's death for eleven years without knowing it mattered to something larger than her own grief had been its own weight, and setting it down changed how she carried everything else. She still ran the compound with the same care. Just building toward something now instead of guarding against it.

Dad flew Davy through his instrument rating that winter, bickering happily over checklists, and by spring they were talking about a second strip, out past the ridgeline, "just to see if we can" — Dad with the gleam of a man who'd found, later than he'd expected, an actual peer to build with. He never mentioned Uncle Max without mentioning how much he'd have loved watching this partnership take shape, and somewhere along the way that stopped hurting to say and started feeling true.

Marie finished her baseline model, eventually confident enough to call it real research — the watcher measures response, not risk, community over crisis. She and Cent stayed close the way I'd known from that first card game, two brilliant minds who'd found somebody who never needed an idea explained twice. She still checked the instruments most evenings. Just the way you'd check on an old friend sleeping peacefully now, not a patient you feared might stop breathing.

Rick had had his license for years, same as the rest of the Five — Marie already flying, Charlie having put Rick, Clara, and Joey through the Texas Institute of Aviation at Brenham years before any of this, six days a week for three months, back when the plan was still nothing more ambitious than gold and extinct animals and a ranch nobody outside the five of them knew the truth about. Rick was quietest about it, same as most things that mattered to him, but he was first to take Cent up once she admitted she wanted lessons of her own, and the two came back with the loose grins of people who'd found one more thing to be unshowily excellent at together. Joey still lost at cards more than the odds allowed, still claimed he'd let everyone win on purpose, and had become, somewhere in that strange year, genuinely invested in orbital debris remediation in a way none of us saw coming and all of us found delightful.

Millie kept doing what she'd always done best — holding her family together with a steadiness that never asked for credit, though she got plenty anyway, from Davy most of all. She and Mom built their own friendship, two women who'd each carried more than anybody understood, finally setting some of that weight down together.

Davy never did stop being afraid for Cent, not entirely, not even after a full year of perfect sat-phone records and Sergeant Mertens' steady voice on the other end of every deployment. But he stopped letting the fear make her decisions, same as he'd promised that first morning, and the fear itself changed shape — less something he fought, more something he'd made peace with carrying, the ordinary cost of loving somebody doing something extraordinary and hers alone.

Cent kept building. Apex Orbital doubled its client list by spring, debris numbers climbing the right direction, and she still came home tired and triumphant, still let Millie fuss over her exactly as much as she needed, still sat on the porch with her own quiet notebook, sketching whatever enormous next thing was taking shape before she was ready to say it out loud. She let herself be proud of the Grebenchekov rescue eventually, properly, at a dinner where Marie made everyone toast to it instead of letting her deflect. Never let Space Girl replace Cent, or Cent shrink Space Girl down. Both things, the whole time, exactly as she'd said that first evening in the workshop.

And Clara and I kept doing what we'd been doing since she stood up out of that grass with her hands raised — sure of each other in the way that stops announcing itself and just becomes the quiet architecture underneath everything else. We built things. We showed up. We rode toward whatever the next stampede turned out to be, together, because that had turned out to be the only kind of bravery either of us trusted — not the absence of fear, just the refusal to let it decide things alone.

The watcher never spoke again that whole first year, though the instruments kept their steady hum, small proportional spikes tracking nothing more dramatic than an ordinary, crowded, well-loved life. I don't think any of us needed it to speak again. We'd all finally understood what it had been trying to tell us from the first stampede onward.

It was never about the door.

It was always, only, about what we chose to become on the other side of it — and standing there, a year on, watching two families' worth of ordinary evening spill warm across a strip of packed earth that hadn't existed before any of us thought to ask permission to build it, I finally understood the answer had been sitting right in front of me the entire time, plain and more than enough.

We'd shown up for each other. That had always been the whole and only measurement that mattered.

Chapter 35: Chapter 35

I told Davy about the soldiers eventually. Took a few more weeks of ordinary visits before it felt like something I wanted to say out loud instead of just carry, but I told him, on the half-finished porch one evening while Millie and Mom argued cheerfully inside about whether the workshop needed a second window.

"Watched them put a man of ours in a locked room," I said. "Watched them point a rifle at me from about six feet, close enough to see the safety was off. I was seventeen. Don't know I've ever fully stopped flinching at engines on gravel since."

Davy didn't say anything for a while, just looked out at the strip, the ordinary evening settling gold over ground two families had built from nothing.

"Six feet," he said finally. "Close enough to see a man's eyes. Always figure that's the part that stays with you longest. Not the gun. The eyes behind it, deciding in real time whether today's the day."

"Yeah. About right."

"Don't have a fix for it," he said. "Wish I did. Twenty years on you and mine hasn't gone anywhere either. Just gotten better at not letting it drive, most days. Some days it still takes the wheel, and Millie reminds me I'm allowed to hand it back."

"How do you tell the difference? Between actually being careful and just being scared?"

Davy considered it seriously, the way he considered most things touching Cent's future. "Don't know you always can, in the moment. You notice after, whether the caution served anybody or just made you smaller. Fear that keeps people safe's worth keeping. Fear that just keeps you flinching at nothing — that's the part I try to hand off, when I catch it in time."

I thought about that a long while, the last light going copper over the ridge, Clara's laugh carrying faint from inside where she was clearly losing the window argument.

"Think mine mostly kept me safe," I said slowly. "Careful enough to build this properly instead of rushing. Careful enough to actually listen when Mom talked about the mission instead of wanting it simple." A pause. "But some of it's just flinching too. Don't know I've ever sorted out which parts are which."

"None of us do," Davy said. "Don't think that's a problem you solve once and stop thinking about. Just part of what you carry, alongside everything else worth carrying." He looked over, something steady and easy in his face. "For what it's worth, Charlie — whatever's flinching in you hasn't made you smaller. Watched you build half this place with your own hands, never once seen it slow you down when it actually mattered."

"Thanks," I said, meaning it more than the word probably conveyed.

We sat together a while, compound lights coming on warm behind us, two families' ordinary evening noise drifting into the dark — Millie finally conceding the window argument, Cent and Marie's laughter over something on a laptop, Rick's low steady voice explaining some engineering point to whoever would listen.

"Same time next week?" Davy asked eventually, standing, offering a hand up.

"Same time next week," I agreed, and let him pull me up, and walked back toward the light and noise together, carrying whatever I carried the same easy way everybody out here seemed to have learned to carry theirs — not set down, not fixed, just held, steadily, by people who'd finally stopped expecting to hold it alone.
