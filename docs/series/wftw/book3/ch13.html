Chapter 13

The next morning's test was supposed to be the last before everybody sat down to talk about what came next. Would've stayed that simple if the wind hadn't shifted an hour before we started and brought the herd with it.

Nobody thought much of it at first. Herds moved across this grassland most days, distant smudges that never came close. Dad had explained early on — something between a bison and something older, heavier through the shoulder, temperament nobody had felt like testing up close — mostly kept its own business, wide and indifferent to four humans and a folding table of instruments.

Marie had Cent lined up for a longer jump, three hundred yards to a dead cottonwood, the biggest distance test yet, and everyone was watching the tablet instead of the horizon when Rick's voice cut the yard, sharp and scared.

"They're not moving away. Look — they're coming this way."

The smudge had resolved, in the last ten minutes nobody noticed, into a genuine herd on the move. Not the slow drift Dad described. A stampede, dust boiling behind it, tracking a line straight across the flat ground where Cent stood, three hundred yards out — past the fence, past anywhere close enough for the rest of us to reach her first.

"Cent!" Davy was already moving, faster than I'd have believed, but three hundred yards was three hundred yards, and the herd was closing it faster than he could close his.

"She has to jump back, now," Millie said, voice tight with panic held down by main force. "You can't outrun that and neither can she—"

"She hasn't studied the return point close enough for a jump this fast," Davy said, already running anyway. "If she jumps blind she could come out anywhere, or nowhere—"

I didn't hear the rest because I was moving too, Dad half a step ahead, both of us cutting toward the horse paddock instead of Cent directly — I'd spent two years around Dad, knew what he was thinking before he said it.

"Can't outrun a stampede on foot," he shouted, vaulting the fence, "but a horse buys us speed to get between her and the front edge — give her something clean enough to jump to instead of jumping blind."

Two horses saddled and moving inside a minute that felt like an hour. I have never ridden anything the way I rode that horse across three hundred yards with a stampede bearing down, wind tearing at my ears, watching Cent's small figure frozen at the base of that cottonwood with a wall of dust closing on her like weather instead of something happening to a person.

"Cent!" Clara's voice, somewhere behind, desperate. "The tree — climb it, you don't need line of sight if you're not jumping, just get up—"

Whether she heard or worked it out herself, by the time Dad and I closed the last hundred yards she'd scrambled into the cottonwood's lower branches, high enough to clear the herd's backs, bare wood groaning under her weight.

The herd split around the tree like water around a stone, thundering past in a wall of dust and noise that swallowed the world for the longest thirty seconds of my life. Dad hauled his horse up fifty yards short — close enough to see, too far to do anything but watch.

It was over almost as fast as it started. Dust settling, hoofbeats fading, and Cent still up there, arms wrapped around the trunk, face gone gray-white.

Davy reached the tree on foot ten seconds after us, and didn't say a word before Cent half-fell out of the branches into his arms. He just held her, both shaking, the rest of the group pounding up behind, breathless and not thinking about instrument readings anymore.

"I'm okay," Cent kept saying into his shoulder. "I'm okay, Dad—"

"You didn't jump," Davy said, pulling back to look at her, hands braced on her shoulders like he needed the contact to believe she was real. "Why?"

"Wasn't clean enough," Cent said, still shaking. "The compound, I know it, but not from three hundred yards with a herd bearing down and half a second to decide — could've come out inside a wall, or underground. Didn't trust it. Climbed instead. Only thing I actually trusted in that second."

Marie, last to arrive, tablet clutched to her chest, was staring at the screen with an expression I couldn't read.

"Marie," Mom said, sharp. "What is it?"

"The reading." Slowly. "Still climbing. Bigger than anything we've seen. And she didn't jump at all."
