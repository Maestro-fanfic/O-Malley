Chapter 3

We watched two hours, trading field glasses, and what I saw didn't match anything I'd pictured on the walk in.

Poachers, maybe. Somebody running an illegal operation, quick and gone before anyone noticed. Instead: a garden with actual crop rotation, raised beds, a rain catchment system feeding a cistern, a smokehouse standing a deliberate distance from the main structure. These people weren't passing through. They'd sat down and thought about how to live here, the same patient way Dad thought about the bunkhouse.

"That's not a raid," Clara said, low, handing the glasses back. "That's a homestead. Somebody's whole life."

"Which is somehow worse," I said. "Means whoever's got access to this Earth isn't hiding it or selling it. They're just living here. Like it's theirs."

"Isn't it, though?" Clara's voice had an edge. "That's the question our whole side's never had to answer. There's only ever been one door, and we've always held the key. What happens when somebody else finds their own way in?"

I didn't have an answer. The figure I'd clocked doing garden chores straightened, looked straight at our brush, and went still.

"Down," Clara breathed. We dropped flat, my heart doing something unreasonable.

A long minute passed. Then another.

"They made us," Clara whispered.

"Maybe not—"

"Charlie." Her hand gripped my arm. "Somebody's coming."

I lifted my head enough to see a second figure had joined the first, taller, both pointing our direction — then a third, appearing from around the house, moving with the loose readiness of somebody who'd handled a weapon before.

"They're not panicking," I said. "That's—"

"Practiced," Clara finished. "Like they know exactly what an unfriendly approach looks like and have a plan for it."

We had maybe four seconds to decide whether we were people who ran or people who stood. Clara — who'd never once in two years been the kind of person who ran from something she could instead understand — stood up out of the grass, both hands raised.

"We're not here to hurt anybody! We just want to talk!"

I came up beside her, hands raised too, and watched the three figures go very still.

The tallest — a man, weathered, built from actual outdoor work rather than a gym — didn't lower whatever he held. Didn't raise it either. Felt like the most important detail in the world.

"Who are you," he called, "and how in the hell did you get onto our land?"

"Same way you did, probably," Clara called — a guess, but an educated one. It landed on him like a small explosion, readiness draining into something closer to shock.

"That's not possible," he said, quieter now, though the grassland carried it fine. "There's no way through. We checked. For years."

"There's a way through." Clara walked forward, slow, hands open. I matched her pace, every instinct arguing to stay put. "You're standing on it right now. My name's Clara. This is Charlie. Our instruments picked up gate traffic six days running. We needed to know who was making it."

The woman beside him — smaller, dark-haired, coiled — spoke first. "Gate. You said gate. Not—" She stopped, glanced at him, something passing between them too fast to read. "An actual, structural gate. Not what we do."

"What you do?" Clara's steps slowed. "What exactly do you do?"

The younger one — a teenager, close to my own age, hanging back with an expression I recognized from my own face plenty of times — spoke for the first time, relief edging her voice.

"We jump," she said. "Don't need a door. Just — go. Anywhere we've been, or anywhere we can see clearly enough. That's how we got here. Followed my dad's trail six months back and never left."

Clara stopped walking entirely.

Something clicked in my chest, slow and enormous. "You don't have a gate," I said. "Don't need one. You just move."

"That's right," the man said, wary curiosity replacing the flat readiness. "Which means whatever gate you two are talking about — that's new. Not us." He studied us a long beat. "Sounds like we've both been keeping the same secret from opposite directions, and just found out neither of us was actually the only one."

The woman lowered what she'd been holding.

"I think we'd better sit down and talk properly. My name's Millie. My husband, Davy. Our daughter, Cent." Something rueful crossed her face. "If there's a built way onto this Earth as well as whatever our family does — that changes a great deal more than who owns this land."

Clara glanced at me — the same thought landing in both of us. Everything Mom believed about this door being the one unrepeatable thing, standing up against a family who'd never needed permission at all. Who'd just walked here, the way you'd walk into a neighboring room.

"Yeah," I said, steadier than I felt. "I think you're right."
