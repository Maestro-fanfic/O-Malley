Chapter 4

His name was Davy Rice, and once we sat around the cookfire with mugs of chicory root instead of coffee — "real coffee's a supply-run luxury," Millie said, "and we're overdue" — the shape of how they'd built this place came out. Better engineering than I'd expected from three people and their eighteen-year-old daughter, no gate, no cargo plane, no way to move anything heavier than a person could carry.

"You didn't fly any of this in," I said, looking at the timber framing — real dimensional lumber, not whatever trees happened to be standing nearby. "Where'd the milled lumber come from?"

"Milled it ourselves, mostly," Davy said. "Portable sawmill, the kind you break down and haul in pieces. Took Cent and me four jumps just for the mill itself." He nodded toward a stand of cottonwood a quarter mile off, thinned in a pattern that told me somebody had thought hard about sustainable cutting. "Every board in that second wall came from trees we felled ourselves, milled ourselves, hauled in by hand. No truck, no dolly, nothing on wheels works on this ground yet."

"How do you jump lumber?" Clara asked. "Thought you needed line of sight, or somewhere you'd already been."

"Somewhere I've been, or somewhere I can see clearly enough through a photo, if I study it long enough first," Davy said. "But there's a weight limit. I top out around three hundred pounds before the effort costs more than it's worth — and that's after twenty years' practice. Cent's still building toward that. So every big job's an exercise in breaking things into three-hundred-pound pieces without losing structural integrity on the other end."

"That's a logistics problem," I said, not hiding how impressed I sounded.

"That's the entire logistics problem," Millie said, dry. "Everything out here started life as a spreadsheet before it started life as a wall."

Clara leaned forward. "Our side runs different. Fixed gate — one location, no line-of-sight requirement, but it doesn't move. All our logistics run through aircraft once we're through it. Dad flew us in and set down eight miles off — as close as he'd risk the noise."

"You've got a working aircraft on this Earth?" Davy sat up straighter. "Fueled how? Don't tell me you jumped aviation gas."

"Flew it through the gate whole," Clara said. "That's the advantage of a fixed structure over a personal jump — doesn't care how big or heavy the thing is, long as it fits through the doorway. We've moved a loaded C-130 through before."

Davy laughed, short and disbelieving. "A C-130. Lord. What I'd have given for a C-130 six years ago, hauling a sawmill through one exhausting piece at a time."

"You'd need a runway first," I said — I'd sat through enough of Mom's planning sessions to know exactly how much that mattered. "A gate doesn't help if you can't land anything heavy on the other side. We've got two thousand feet of graded, packed strip back home, took the better part of a year to build right. Wrong soil compaction and the first heavy landing tears it to ruts."

"How'd you compact it without equipment?" Millie asked — not idle curiosity. Filing the answer away.

"We didn't, without equipment. First big cargo run, before I ever got here — small compactor and grader, flown through in pieces, reassembled on-site. After that it's maintenance. Minimum eight inches of graded base under a packed surface for anything heavier than a light single-engine, and even then you're watching margins close on a hot afternoon, because density altitude eats your effective runway same as a shorter strip would."

"You know all that off the top of your head?" Cent — the daughter — was looking at me somewhere between skeptical and genuinely curious.

"Mom made all of us learn it," I said. "Not just flying. The whole logistics chain, because if the runway fails or the plane goes down out here, there's no calling for help. You're it."

"Density altitude," Davy repeated, tasting the phrase. "Explain that. Thought plenty about weight limits on the jump side. Never had to think about air."

"Hot, thin, or high air's less dense than cold, thick, low air," I said. "Same physical altitude, but a hot afternoon behaves like you're thousands of feet higher — less lift, less engine performance, longer takeoff roll. Doesn't matter how good your runway is if you load past what density altitude supports that afternoon. Dad checks a chart before every single takeoff, no exceptions. Guess wrong on that math and you put an aircraft into the trees at the far end of a strip that looked plenty long on paper."

Millie nodded slowly. "The kind of thing you learn by somebody teaching you properly, or by nearly dying and getting lucky enough to learn from it instead."

"Mom prefers the first option," I said. "Strongly."

That got an actual laugh out of the group, easing something that had been sitting in the air since the standoff that morning, and Davy refilled mugs while the conversation threaded between two very different kinds of hard-won expertise.

"What about the money side," Clara asked eventually. "Building anything this substantial — that's real capital. Where's it coming from, if you're not running supply flights back to civilization?"

Davy and Millie traded a look — the kind that meant they'd had this exact conversation before and had an answer ready.

"Gold," Millie said simply.

"Placer gold in half the streambeds on this Earth," Davy said. "No mining pressure, ever — what's accumulated over a few hundred million years is just sitting in the gravel, waiting on somebody with a pan and the patience. I spend maybe two mornings a week at the river with a sluice box I built myself. Enough, converted through channels I'd rather not get specific about, to fund lumber, tools, seed stock, anything we can't produce ourselves."

"You pan gold," I said, hearing the same note in my voice Clara had had earlier. "By hand."

"Sluice box, mostly — pan's for prospecting a new bar. Once you've located good gravel you build a proper sluice, get your water flow right, riffles set to catch the heavy material without choking on the light stuff. Slow, wet work. One morning in five you come back with nothing but a sunburn." Davy shrugged, easy, like a man describing a hobby rather than his family's entire economic foundation. "But the ratio's good enough over a season we've never gone without anything we truly needed."

Clara looked at me. I knew exactly what she was thinking, because I was thinking it too.

Two completely different kinds of hard-won, bone-deep competence, built from opposite directions to solve the same problem — how do you actually live on an Earth that owed you nothing — and neither side had known the other existed until six days of instrument noise sent two teenagers walking eight miles to find out.

"I think our families have a great deal to talk about," I said.

"I think your mother's going to want a very long conversation with us," Davy said. "And we'll want an equally long one with her." He looked out at the second structure, timber he'd felled and hauled with his own hands. "This is our home now. Whatever comes of this — we're not looking to be managed off our own land by a family that only just found out we existed."

I understood that flavor of fear better than he guessed. There'd been years, before any of this, when I'd watched soldiers stand in my own living room, watched a man I trusted get dragged off at gunpoint over a door that was mine before it was anybody's business. I still checked exits in rooms I'd sat in a hundred times. Still felt my body go tight at an unfamiliar engine on gravel. Nobody had ever fully explained that reflex away, and I didn't imagine Davy's version would go any cleaner than mine had.

"Nobody's talking about that," Clara said, and meant it. "But you should know — Mom's spent her whole life believing this was the one door onto this Earth. Her own people built it, on a world she left before Charlie was born. Finding out there's a second kind of door, one she never built and can't control—" She chose her next words carefully. "That's going to shake something in her that's been solid a long time. I'd rather you hear it from us than get blindsided."

Davy considered that, firelight moving across his face, then nodded, slow and certain.

"Then let's make sure," he said, "that when it comes, we're standing here to meet it together, instead of two families each waiting to see what the other does first."
