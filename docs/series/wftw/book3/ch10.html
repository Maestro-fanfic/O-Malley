Chapter 10

Marie found me on the porch before midnight, everybody else long down, the compound quiet except ordinary night sounds — horses shifting, wind through the greenhouse frames.

"You're not asleep," she said, not a question, sitting on the step without waiting to be invited.

"Couldn't. Too much in my head." Something in her shoulders told me she hadn't come for company. "What'd you find?"

"It jumped again." Tablet dimmed low, turned toward me — the same graph, except the line kicked upward again, sharper. "Not gradual this time. All at once, about two hours ago, right when everybody was still at the table."

"Two hours ago." I did the math without meaning to. "That's when Cent was talking to you about the jumping. Whether it costs her anything."

"That's what I thought too." Marie's voice had gone measured. "Don't know it's connected. Coincidence in timing isn't proof, and I told myself that six times walking out here. But I can't think of anything else in that window worth a spike this size, and I don't love that."

"Did you tell Mom?"

"Not yet. She's actually asleep — first time in three days — and I didn't want to wake her over a reading I can't fully explain. Wanted to look longer first. Make sure I wasn't seeing a pattern that isn't really there because I've stared at this graph so long my brain's inventing shapes in the noise."

"Is that a real thing?"

"Pareidolia. Very real, and exactly the mistake I don't want to make here." She scrolled back, showing the whole six-day climb. "But I've checked four times, different ways, and it keeps coming back the same. Direct interaction with Cent's ability — not just her presence, active use of it, or close discussion of it — correlates with jumps in the baseline. No mechanism. Just the pattern, and it's getting harder to argue with."

We stayed quiet with that, horses shifting in the dark below, and I thought about Cent at the table, animated in a way I doubted she got to be often — finally talking about the strangest thing about herself with somebody who found it fascinating instead of frightening — and the uncomfortable thought that the same conversation might have tripped whatever was watching, closer now, twice in two days.

"If you're right," I said slowly, "the safest thing for Cent is never talk about it. Never use it. Stop, and hope whatever's watching loses interest."

"Maybe." Marie didn't sound convinced. "Or that's exactly backward. If something's been watching this Earth for years without acting, and it's only now recalibrating to account for traffic it never had to measure — that could mean it's updating, not alarming. Learning what Cent's ability actually is. Not automatically the same as deciding it's a problem."

"You don't know that, though."

"No. Don't know anything, really, except the numbers keep moving and I don't have data to say confidently which direction." She pulled her knees up, looking small for a second. "Hate this part. Seeing a pattern and not knowing what it means. Mom always says data without interpretation is just noise with better handwriting. That's exactly what I've got."

"So what do we do."

"Watch. Carefully. Don't panic, don't pretend it isn't happening. Tomorrow morning I tell Mom everything, and we figure out together whether Cent needs to stop practicing or whether it means something else." In the low light her face looked older than it was, the way it did when the math got heavy enough to sit on her like weight. "Don't want to be the reason an eighteen-year-old gets told to stop being who she is. Not on a guess. If we're going to ask that of her, I want to be certain, and right now I'm not certain of anything except something out there's paying more attention than it used to."

Overhead, stars stacked four deep in every direction, giving nothing away about whatever might be watching from inside all that quiet dark. We sat a while longer, until the cold finally drove us both back inside.
