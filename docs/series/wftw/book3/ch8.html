Chapter 8 of 35

Chapter 8

Dad had us up before dawn, working weight math on the kitchen table by lamplight while Clara and I ate eggs standing up.

"Four passengers back instead of two, plus whatever gear Millie and the girl want," he said, pencil down a column he'd clearly worked twice already. "Different airplane than yesterday. Swapping to the Cessna — more useful load, but heavier, slower, wants more runway."

"How much runway do we have out there?" Clara asked.

"Call it eleven hundred feet, if nothing's changed since I paced it two days ago. Loaded the way I'm planning — four adults, gear, full fuel — I want minimum nine hundred feet to clear a fifty-foot obstacle with margin I trust, and that's this morning's temperature, not whatever it climbs to by midday." He tapped a chart clipped in a plastic sleeve. "Density altitude again. Cool morning air, real margin. Same load at two in the afternoon on a warm day, might not have enough runway at all, and no amount of wanting it changes the physics."

"So we fly out early, load fast, get back up before the day heats," I said.

"That's the plan, not a flexible one. Not wheels-up by ten, we sit tight till evening rather than gamble a takeoff the numbers don't support."

Davy, slept in the bunkhouse, marginally more rested, leaned over the chart. "You do this math every flight?"

"Every flight, every load," Dad said. "Takeoff distance, landing distance, weight and balance, fuel reserves — none optional out here. No tower to call if I'm wrong, no mechanic on standby, no hospital forty minutes off. Get it wrong on the tame side, you've got options. Get it wrong out here, the options mostly involve walking a very long way home."

We flew out at first light, Dad at the stick of the bigger plane, a deeper, steadier drone filling the cabin. He held a compass heading, checking his watch against a folded chart on one knee, correcting course in increments I only noticed because I was watching for them.

"Wind's stronger aloft than yesterday," he said, mostly to himself, adjusting two degrees. "Crosswind, pushing us right of track if I don't correct. Difference between finding that strip clean on the first pass and burning fuel hunting for it."

"How do you tell you're drifting, no roads to check against?" Davy asked from the back.

"Ground speed versus airspeed. Watching how features track past compared to how they should on a true heading. Takes practice — getting it wrong early, learning what wrong looks like from the air." Dad glanced back. "Not so different from what your family does jumping. Building a mental model, checking it constantly against what you're seeing, correcting the second they stop matching. Different tools. Same discipline."

Davy considered that, something in his face suggesting he liked the comparison.

We found the strip on schedule, Dad easing down a long final with the economy of a man who'd flown this exact grass a hundred times, calling numbers quietly. Millie and Cent were already waiting, packs assembled.

"That didn't take long," Millie said, eyeing the plane.

"Wanted the cool air for the load home," Dad said, already climbing out to redo weight-and-balance with the new passengers.

Cent watched with open interest, leaning over his shoulder at the kneeboard. "You do that math for every passenger?"

"Every one," Dad said. "Weight, gear, fuel, temperature, wind — all of it decides not just whether we get off the ground but how much runway it takes. Not guessing on a strip this short."

"Never had to think about any of that," Cent said, half to herself. "Weight limits I know backward. Runway, wind, temperature — different math I never needed."

"Different tools," Davy said, echoing Dad's own words with a tired smile. "Same discipline. You'll pick it up fast."

Loading took twenty minutes, Dad redoing weight-and-balance twice as gear got stowed, moving a duffel to redistribute toward center of gravity.

"Everybody strapped?" He ran his eyes across all four passengers. "Let's go while the morning's still cooperating."

He took the runway, held brakes against full throttle, released. The Cessna surged forward heavier and more deliberate than the lighter plane, eating the eleven hundred feet with what felt like not nearly enough room — then the wheels lifted clean, almost exactly the margin his numbers promised, and the homestead fell away beneath us, shrinking into the vast green quiet of an Earth that, for the first time in six years, two wholly different families now understood themselves to be sharing.
