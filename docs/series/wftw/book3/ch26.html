Chapter 26

It was Marie's idea, the way most good ideas around the compound tended to be — a proper gathering, nothing crisis-shaped, just the ordinary chaotic evening the Five had had for years, except with three extra chairs never part of the circle before.

"You should come," Marie told Cent, the week after the watcher's second visit, the two of them deep in a running conversation about jump mechanics that had apparently become permanent. "Just a hangout. Rick's making his terrible chili, Joey's going to lose at cards and pretend he let us win, Clara's going to fall asleep on the couch by nine because Charlie ran her ragged on ranch chores all week."

"I heard that," Clara said, not looking up from the fence post she was resetting with Rick.

"I stand by it," Marie said, unbothered, and Cent laughed — real, easy, the kind I'd started noticing more of since her father lowered his rifle in that garden.

Cent came through the gate Friday looking like she'd dressed three different ways before settling on the fourth, hovering behind Davy and Millie with the nervous energy of someone about to walk into a room that already knew each other far better than they knew her.

She needn't have worried. Joey had her at the card table within ten minutes, explaining rules that changed depending on how badly he was losing. Rick — six words in an average evening, each one counting — spent twenty minutes with her on the porch comparing notes on engines, until the two of them had built something suspiciously like friendship out of shared enthusiasm for how things worked.

"He likes her," Clara said, watching from the doorway, warm and a little surprised. "That's not nothing, Charlie. Rick doesn't warm up that fast. Took him most of a year with Marie."

"Maybe she's just easy to like," I said, watching Cent lean over Rick's shoulder at an engine diagram, animated in a way I hadn't seen even at the best of the last two weeks. "God knows she's earned a night where nobody's asking her to explain jump mechanics or grade her family against some invisible standard."

Davy found me by the paddock fence later, mug of something stronger than chicory, watching the bunkhouse windows glow with the gathering's noise.

"Thank you," he said, no preamble. "For this. Didn't fully understand, till tonight, how much I'd let myself believe the three of us were meant to just get by out here, quietly. Watching her in there—" He nodded toward Cent's laugh over Joey's exaggerated groan. "Didn't realize how much she'd been missing until I watched her get it."

"You built her something real out there," I said. "The homestead. That's not nothing either."

"No," Davy agreed. "Not nothing. But not everything, and I needed somebody else to show me the difference." He looked out at the strip, pale in the last light. "Same time next month?"

The phrase landed strange and warm in my chest, half question, half already a promise.

"Same time next month," I said. "And the month after. However long it takes to stop feeling like something we schedule, and just become what we do."

Inside, Marie's laugh rang out over something Joey said too loudly, Clara waved us in from the doorway, and Davy and I walked back toward the light together — two families' ordinary Friday night finally, properly, becoming one.
