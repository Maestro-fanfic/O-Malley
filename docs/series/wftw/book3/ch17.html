Chapter 17

Cent finished the last three pieces the next morning, fresh and steady the way Dad predicted, and by midmorning the compactor sat reassembled on a tarp, nine pieces become one machine, Dad running his hands over every joint before letting anybody near the ignition.

"Hydraulic pressure's holding," he said, squinting at a gauge. "No weeping at the seals. She's clean." He looked up at Cent, watching with visible pride. "Good work. Every piece came through without a scratch. Better record than I get flying cargo through the gate most weeks."

"High bar," Davy said, dry, and Dad huffed something like a laugh.

The grading took four more days, Dad walking the compactor and grader in long, even passes while Davy and Millie staked the centerline with string and pegs, checking grade every twenty feet with a hand level.

"Want it crowned slightly," he explained, sighting down the centerline with Davy. "Couple inches higher in the middle than the edges, so water sheds instead of pooling and softening the surface. Too flat, standing water eats at your compaction from underneath, slow and invisible, until the whole thing's soft where it looks solid."

"How do you build that without survey equipment?"

"String line and a hand level, patience, checking six times more often than feels necessary." Dad sighted again, adjusted a peg an inch. "Out here, patience is most of the equipment anyway. Same as your gold panning — the sluice box doesn't do the work itself. Just gives the work somewhere to happen."

Rick had made himself thoroughly indispensable — running stakes, hauling water for the compactor, pestering Dad with questions Dad answered every one of.

"Why's clay different from sand for this?" Rick asked, third day, watching the drum roll over the darker soil near the low end.

"Clay particles are smaller, flatter, pack tighter once you squeeze the water out right — harder, more stable surface once cured," Dad said. "Trouble's getting moisture content right. Too wet, you're smearing it around, trapping water under a skin that looks solid and isn't. Too dry, won't bond, crumbles under the first heavy roll. Find the window by feel — squeeze a handful, see how it crumbles under your thumb."

"Can I try?"

Dad looked at Davy, who nodded, and handed Rick a fistful of dark soil. Rick squeezed it the way he'd watched Dad do for three days.

"Holds a shape," he reported. "Crumbling a little at the edge when I press it."

"About right for today. Little dry of ideal, but workable. Tomorrow, if that overnight dew comes through, might hit the sweet spot exactly." Dad clapped his shoulder, approving. "Decent hand for this. Most folks squeeze once and declare victory whether they're right or not."

By the fifth day the strip was done — packed, crowned, checked twice more with the hand level — and Davy stood at the north end with an expression I hadn't seen on him before, disbelief and a very specific pride.

"Six years," he said, half to himself. "Never let myself think past what I could carry one trip at a time. Now there's a runway. Ground I've walked past a hundred times, never imagining a plane could land here."

"First flight tomorrow morning, if the weather holds," Dad said. "Light load, nothing to prove. Just confirming she handles the way the numbers say."

"Want to be there when you land," Davy said. "See it."

"You'll see it," Dad said. "You built half of it staking that centerline. Man ought to watch his own work prove out."

That evening, around the homestead's own cookfire for the first time since the week started — Newells and Rices together, chicory and real coffee both going around depending who wanted which — Millie raised her mug in a toast nobody had planned.

"To the strip. And whatever's watching us build it. If you're grading us, I hope you're grading generously, because this is about the best any of us know how to do."

"Hear, hear," Davy said, and the circle drank to it under a sky thick with more stars than either family had grown up believing a sky could hold.
