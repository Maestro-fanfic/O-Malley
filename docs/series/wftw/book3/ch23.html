Chapter 23

Mom took the news the way she took most hard news — quiet, absorbing it fully before reacting — and asked Marie to pull every scrap of archived data the compound had, back as far as the instruments went. The two of them spent most of two days shut up in the small research room off the kitchen.

"Eleven years puts it right around when I inherited the ranch," I said, the second afternoon, staring at the logs spread across the desk. "Before any of us set foot on the wildside. Before there was a bunkhouse, a strip, any of it."

"Could it have been that specifically?" Marie asked. "Not construction — the actual moment the gate changed hands. First time anybody but Max crossed it with real intent to stay."

"That's actually what I think it was," she said, tapping the graph. "It's the very first entry the archive has — nothing before it, because nobody had a working instrument out here yet. This spike is the beginning of the record. Either the first thing worth measuring on this side, or measuring itself started because of whatever this was." She traced the line. "Discrete event, not gradual. Something happened, once, over about three days, right at the start of everything, and then stopped — nobody back then had reason to ask why, because nobody yet understood there was anything to ask about."

Mom went very still, staring at the dates, something crossing her face I hadn't seen since the night the flag first went up.

"Three days," she said slowly. "Eleven years ago, in autumn." She looked up, voice careful in a way that tightened my stomach. "Marie. Exact date range."

Marie checked. "October fourteenth through the seventeenth."

Mom closed her eyes, and when she opened them there was something old and grieved behind her usual composure.

"That's the week your Uncle Max died," she said, the words coming slow.

The room went very still. I'd never met him — too young to remember, just a name at the edges of family stories, Mom's older brother, who'd crossed over from Home with her and never crossed back. Broken his leg badly, less than two kilometers from the gate, some ordinary misstep on ground he must've walked a hundred times, and couldn't get himself home. Lasted a few days alone before the wolves found him. The ranch came to me not long after — his, technically, the one piece of this world he'd ever legally owned, the only reason any of the rest became mine to build on.

"You think that's connected," I said. "The spike. Uncle Max."

"I don't know," Mom said, her voice thickened. "Never had reason to wonder till now. He went missing on the fourteenth. We didn't find him for years — bones and a canteen with a note, addressed to me. He'd have known nobody was coming, not onto a world nobody else knew existed. Three days is just how long he lasted, near as anyone could tell." She pressed her hand flat against the log. "I don't know if it means anything — whether the watcher registers a death out here alone, the way it registered fear during the stampede, or whether it's coincidence I'm building meaning into because grief looks for patterns even where none exist. But I don't think I believe in coincidence anymore. Not after this month."

Dad had come in partway through, drawn by the wrong-feeling silence, and stood in the doorway taking in Mom's face before understanding.

"Max," he said, crossing to put a hand on her shoulder, sharing a grief eleven years old that never fully finished healing. "He'd have loved all this. The Rices. The strip. Cent's gift. Always said this Earth had more in it than any of us could guess." A small, sad smile. "Would've been out there with the compactor himself, arguing with me about grading specs. Never trusted anybody else's soil math."

"I wish he could have been," I said, meaning it, the strange grief of missing someone I'd never known.

"So do I," Dad said. "Every day, if I'm honest." He looked at the log, the dates circled in Marie's neat hand. "If the watcher really did register when we lost him — don't know if that's comforting or terrible. Comforting, that he wasn't as alone as we've always told ourselves. Terrible, if it means whatever's watching's been quietly cataloguing every loss here and never thought to tell us."

"Maybe it's both," Mom said. "Maybe that's just what it is, watching something this large. Comfort and terror tangled together, no clean way to pull them apart."

Marie was still staring at the graph, the reflex she couldn't turn off even now. "If it does register loss, it's not just measuring what we build, or how we respond to danger while it's happening. It's been watching the whole shape of us — good and terrible both — for eleven years at least, probably longer." Something steadier under the grief now. "I want to know how much longer. Go back further, if the archives reach that far. Understand how long this has actually been happening instead of assuming last month was the beginning."

Mom nodded, wiping her eyes, composed again. "Do it. Carefully. And Marie — thank you. I know digging through cold data looking for the shape of Charlie's own uncle's death isn't easy. None of you have to carry that alone."

"I know," Marie said. "I've got all of you." She looked around the small room — Mom, Dad, me — and settled, steadier for having said it out loud. "That's the whole point of everything we've learned this month, isn't it. Nobody's supposed to carry the hard parts by themselves out here."
