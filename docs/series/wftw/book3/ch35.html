Chapter 35

I told Davy about the soldiers eventually. Took a few more weeks of ordinary visits before it felt like something I wanted to say out loud instead of just carry, but I told him, on the half-finished porch one evening while Millie and Mom argued cheerfully inside about whether the workshop needed a second window.

"Watched them put a man of ours in a locked room," I said. "Watched them point a rifle at me from about six feet, close enough to see the safety was off. I was seventeen. Don't know I've ever fully stopped flinching at engines on gravel since."

Davy didn't say anything for a while, just looked out at the strip, the ordinary evening settling gold over ground two families had built from nothing.

"Six feet," he said finally. "Close enough to see a man's eyes. Always figure that's the part that stays with you longest. Not the gun. The eyes behind it, deciding in real time whether today's the day."

"Yeah. About right."

"Don't have a fix for it," he said. "Wish I did. Twenty years on you and mine hasn't gone anywhere either. Just gotten better at not letting it drive, most days. Some days it still takes the wheel, and Millie reminds me I'm allowed to hand it back."

"How do you tell the difference? Between actually being careful and just being scared?"

Davy considered it seriously, the way he considered most things touching Cent's future. "Don't know you always can, in the moment. You notice after, whether the caution served anybody or just made you smaller. Fear that keeps people safe's worth keeping. Fear that just keeps you flinching at nothing — that's the part I try to hand off, when I catch it in time."

I thought about that a long while, the last light going copper over the ridge, Clara's laugh carrying faint from inside where she was clearly losing the window argument.

"Think mine mostly kept me safe," I said slowly. "Careful enough to build this properly instead of rushing. Careful enough to actually listen when Mom talked about the mission instead of wanting it simple." A pause. "But some of it's just flinching too. Don't know I've ever sorted out which parts are which."

"None of us do," Davy said. "Don't think that's a problem you solve once and stop thinking about. Just part of what you carry, alongside everything else worth carrying." He looked over, something steady and easy in his face. "For what it's worth, Charlie — whatever's flinching in you hasn't made you smaller. Watched you build half this place with your own hands, never once seen it slow you down when it actually mattered."

"Thanks," I said, meaning it more than the word probably conveyed.

We sat together a while, compound lights coming on warm behind us, two families' ordinary evening noise drifting into the dark — Millie finally conceding the window argument, Cent and Marie's laughter over something on a laptop, Rick's low steady voice explaining some engineering point to whoever would listen.

"Same time next week?" Davy asked eventually, standing, offering a hand up.

"Same time next week," I agreed, and let him pull me up, and walked back toward the light and noise together, carrying whatever I carried the same easy way everybody out here seemed to have learned to carry theirs — not set down, not fixed, just held, steadily, by people who'd finally stopped expecting to hold it alone.
