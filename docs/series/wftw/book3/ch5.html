Chapter 5

We radioed Dad at the four-hour mark, then again an hour out from the plane, so he had the engine warmed and chocks pulled by the time Clara and I jogged up out of the grass, Davy walking behind at an easier pace — unarmed now, having agreed, after another hour of cautious back-and-forth, to come meet Mom in person.

"Just the one passenger?" Dad asked, sizing Davy up the way he sized up every stranger.

"Just the one, for now," Clara said. "Millie and Cent are staying with the homestead. Davy's coming to talk to Mom."

Dad held out a hand. "Hank Newell."

"Davy Rice." They shook, brief, businesslike. I watched Dad clock something in the grip, the calluses, the way Davy held himself — two men who'd each spent years doing hard physical work recognizing the type in each other without needing to say so.

"You ever flown before?" Dad asked, already turning toward the plane.

"Once or twice, commercial. Nothing like this."

"Hold onto something and don't touch anything with a red label, we'll get along fine." Dad climbed into the pilot's seat and ran his pre-flight out loud, half habit, half teaching. "Fuel selector, both. Mixture, rich. Flaps, ten degrees. Trim neutral. Controls free and correct—" the yoke through its full range, watching the surfaces respond "—clean check across the board."

"You do that every time?" Davy asked, buckling in.

"Every time, rules or no rules. The one time you skip it is the one time something's wrong, and you find out forty feet in the air instead of forty feet on the ground." Dad primed the engine, called clear, brought it over with a cough and a settled roar loud enough to end conversation.

We taxied to the strip's near end, and Dad checked the windsock a moment longer.

"Wind's shifted since yesterday," he called back. "Quartering tailwind — don't love it on a strip this short, but inside margins if we're smart. Full power, hold her down long as I can stand to build airspeed, because tailwind eats short-field distance fast."

The plane surged across the grass, roughness smoothing as speed built. He held it a beat longer than felt comfortable before easing back, and we climbed away clean, banking north over grassland unspooling gold and green in the early afternoon light.

Davy pressed his face near the window the whole climb, watching his own homestead shrink to a smudge behind us. I understood the look without him explaining — the vulnerability of watching everything you'd built get small and out of your control, on a schedule that wasn't yours.

"How do you navigate out here?" he asked, eventually. "No roads. No landmarks I'd recognize twice. What tells you you're actually headed right?"

"Dead reckoning, backed by a compass and a clock," Dad said, not looking away from the instruments. "Heading, true airspeed, rough wind estimate, time since my last confirmed position. Run those together, you get a reliable fix even with nothing on the ground worth calling a landmark."

"No GPS?"

"Handheld backup, and I use it. Don't trust it the way somebody flying the tame side might. Batteries fail. If that thing dies forty miles from anywhere with a name, I still need to get this airplane home on nothing but what's in my head and on the panel. That's the skill I practice, every flight, GPS working or not. It's a convenience. Not the plan."

"That's a lot to hold in your head."

"It's a serious world out here," Dad said. "Nobody's coming to bail you out. Sharpens a man's attention real quick."

Clara caught my eye and gave me a small, private smile — somewhere in the last two hours, Davy Rice had gone from armed stranger to something closer to kindred spirit.

The compound came up forty minutes later, the graded strip a pale scar against the green, and Dad brought us in on a long, easy final, calling numbers out of old habit.

"Sixty-five on final... crossing the fence at fifty... and—" wheels kissed packed dirt with barely a shudder "—down."

He taxied toward the gate barn and cut the engine. In the ringing quiet, Mom was already walking down to meet us, Marie a half step behind, both moving with the urgency of people who'd waited four hours for a radio check that told them almost nothing.

Davy unbuckled and sat a moment, looking at the barn and the plain wooden door built into its wall — the actual gate, the thing his family had never had and never suspected was possible.

"Well," he said, half to himself. "No putting this off any longer."

"No," I said. "There really isn't."
