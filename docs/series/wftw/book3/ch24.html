Chapter 24

Marie went back thirty years into the archive over the next three days, as far as the earliest surviving logs would take her, and by the time she called us back into the research room she had a wall chart instead of a single graph — years of data reduced to a ragged line with six spikes marked in red.

"Six," she said, without preamble, tapping the chart. "Not two. Six, as far back as the records exist. Only caught the first five because I finally knew to look properly instead of assuming last month was where the story started."

"Six," Dad repeated. "In thirty years."

"As far back as we have instruments — not thirty years of the gate's existence, just thirty years of somebody pointing a sensor at it. Could be more, further back, we have no record of." Marie traced the line, pointing out each spike. "Max's death is the most recent before this month's cluster. Before that, twenty-two years back, almost as large, over four days. Before that, smaller, further apart, harder to characterize with the instruments we had running then."

"Do you know what caused the others?" I asked.

"Not with certainty. But I dug the old paper logs too, and the twenty-two-year one lines up with something Mom mentioned once — a bad winter, the worst on record, herds moved wrong, half the compound nearly didn't make it. Nobody died. But it was close, for days, in a way nobody's talked about much since."

Mom had gone very still, listening — this wasn't news to her, just news it had ever been measured.

"So the pattern's not death specifically," Dad said slowly. "Crisis. Times this place, or the people in it, came close enough to a real edge that it mattered."

"That's my working theory," Marie said. "Not perfect. Six events over thirty years is a small sample. But every spike I can explain lines up with something that threatened this place badly enough the outcome genuinely could've gone the other way. Max's death. The bad winter. Now the stampede, and whatever's happened with Cent since."

"That's not entirely comforting," Millie said, she and Davy having come to sit in, Cent beside them. "If it only registers real crisis, every time it's spoken up, something out here actually nearly broke."

"Maybe," Marie said. "Or maybe that's exactly backward, same as the stampede reading turned out to be. Maybe it's not measuring how close things came to breaking. Maybe it's measuring what happened instead of breaking. Every one of these events, far as I can piece together — nobody died alone. Nobody got left to face it alone. The winter, people rode out in weather that should've killed them to reach neighbors worse off. Max—" She glanced at Mom, careful. "Max was found. Eventually. Searched for, by people who never considered giving up."

Nobody rushed to fill the silence.

"And the stampede," Cent said, working it through out loud. "I climbed instead of jumping blind. Charlie and Dad rode out. Everybody moved. Nobody just watched and hoped and did nothing."

"That's the pattern," Marie said. "Not danger. Not death. What people actually do, when it counts, when nothing forces them except who they already are." She looked around the room, two families who'd known each other six days and had already chosen each other without hesitation. "I think that's what it's always measured. Just took this long, and this many false starts, to see it clearly enough to say so."

Davy took a while working something over, and when he spoke his voice had gone rough. "Six years out here, just the three of us, and I never let myself imagine anybody was watching, let alone watching for something like that. I thought the only grading that mattered was whether we survived the winter. Never thought it might matter, in some accounting none of us can fully see, that Millie held me together the year I nearly lost my nerve. Or Cent taught herself geometry off a water-stained textbook because she refused to let this place shrink her future. Thought that was just us, getting by. Never thought it might be the entire point."

"Maybe it's both," Mom said gently. "Getting by, and the entire point. Don't think those have to be different things."

Marie was still tracing the six red marks, something working behind her eyes.

"One more thing," she said slowly. "Almost didn't mention it — not sure what to make of it. But the gap between events is shrinking. Twenty-two years between the winter and Max. Eleven between Max and now. If that pattern holds—"

"Something's coming faster," Dad said. "Not slowing down. Speeding up."

"Don't know that for certain," Marie said. "Two data points isn't a trend I'd stand behind. But didn't want to sit on it, not after this month taught me what happens when we assume we understand a pattern before testing it properly." She looked around the room. "Whatever this means, we should stop treating it as a mystery we've solved. Treat it as one we're still standing inside of."
