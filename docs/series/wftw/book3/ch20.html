Chapter 20

We found them at the homestead — Davy up on the roofline setting shingles, Millie in the garden, Cent cross-legged on the porch with a book she wasn't reading — and something in how the four of us walked up together must have shown on our faces, because Davy was down off that roof before Mom said a word.

"What happened," he said, not a question.

"Something spoke to us," Mom said. "At the gate. I'd rather tell you sitting down. You've earned every word of it, not a summary."

They gathered on the half-built porch, and Mom told it plainly — the door gone rigid on its hinges, the padlock welded still, the words that arrived without being spoken, a lifetime of measurement that had never been about the door at all. When she got to the stampede, to fear and response and what a community chooses under pressure, Davy's hand found his daughter's shoulder and held on tight.

"It said something else," Mom continued, watching Cent closely. "Specifically for you. The caution mattered. Climbing instead of jumping blind — noted, valued, exactly as much as anyone riding out to reach you. Wisdom under fear, it called it. Its own form of the thing this whole arrangement exists to recognize."

Cent went still, then, without warning, started to cry — not the frightened tears from the stampede, something looser, relieved. Two weeks of quietly believing her own existence might be the danger everyone was managing, finally hearing, plainly, the opposite was true.

Millie had her arms around her before anyone else moved. Davy crouched in front of both, a hand on each. For a while nobody said anything.

"So it's not about the ability," Davy said eventually, once Cent had steadied, wiping her face and laughing shakily at herself. "Never was. Twenty years I've half believed this was a liability I was passing down. And the whole time, what mattered was—" He stopped, searching for the word.

"What we do with each other," Clara offered. "That's the whole accounting."

"That's it," Davy agreed, wonder plain. "A lifetime of careful management on your side, six years of mine hiding out here terrified of being found, and it was never about controlling access. Just watching whether we'd take care of each other, once we were here."

"I think we've both been carrying more weight than the job required," Mom said. "I managed a door I thought had to stay narrow to stay safe. You hid a gift you thought had to stay secret. And what was being measured was never the door or the gift. Just this." She gestured at the porch, the garden, the strip. "All of it. Exactly this."

Cent, steadier, looked up with wonder of her own. "Does this mean I don't have to be careful anymore?"

"I think it means you're exactly as careful as good sense requires, same as anyone," Mom said. "Not fear-careful. Sensible. Same caution that made you climb that tree instead of jumping blind. Keep it because it's wise, not because something's watching."

"What happens now?" Millie asked. "Practically. Does anything change, or was this just an explanation, and we go back to building the strip and running the garden?"

Mom considered it, looking out at the homestead, the strip cutting its pale line through the grass.

"I think we go back to the life we were building," she said. "Except without the fear underneath it — you, that Cent's existence might get this Earth shut down; me, that a second door might mean I'd failed the one responsibility I've carried since before Charlie was born." A tired smile. "I think we just get to be neighbors now. Properly. Without the weight."

"Neighbors," Davy repeated, testing the word the way he'd tested "home" that first night. "Sixty miles apart, one family flying and one jumping, and somehow, apparently, neighbors."

"The best kind," Dad said, from the porch steps, real warmth in it. "The kind that shows up when the stampede's bearing down, and doesn't wait to be asked."

The evening settled slow over the homestead, both families lingering long past any reason to, and somewhere in the middle of it Rick roped Cent into a half-abandoned engineering project with the water pump, the two bent together with the absorbed seriousness of people who'd found a genuine peer in the strangest circumstances — and watching them, I thought that whatever the watcher's larger purpose turned out to be, we'd already gotten the only answer that had really mattered.

We'd shown up for each other. That had been enough.
