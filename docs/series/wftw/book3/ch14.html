Chapter 14

We got everyone back in pieces — Cent riding double behind her father, too shaken to trust her own legs, Rick uncharacteristically silent, Clara's hand finding mine again without either of us commenting on it — and it was a good hour before anybody sat down to actually look at what Marie's instruments caught.

"Walk us through it again," Mom said, once Cent had a blanket and a mug of something hot and had mostly stopped shaking. "Slowly. If it's what I think it means, it changes everything we learned yesterday."

Marie had the graph up, tracing the line with a finger not entirely steady. "This morning's baseline, flat. Herd starts moving — nothing on the instrument, makes sense, nothing to do with Cent. Then here." She tapped a point, steeper than anything from yesterday. "Roughly when the herd's course became unmistakable. Headed straight for her."

"Before she did anything," Davy said. "Before she jumped, before she climbed, before any choice at all."

"Before any of that," Marie confirmed. "The spike starts with the danger, not her response to it. Keeps climbing through the whole stampede, peaks almost exactly when the herd was closest to the tree — then drops off fast, faster than yesterday's spikes decayed, once she was safe."

Nobody spoke for a long moment, everyone working through the same recalculation.

"So it's not tracking jumps at all," Clara said slowly. "Or not only. It's tracking — threat? Risk to her specifically?"

"That's the shape of it, from one data point I very much didn't want to collect this way," Marie said. "Yesterday's tests were controlled, safe. Small spikes proportional to jump size. Nothing like this. Biggest reading we've seen, and it happened during the one moment in two days she was in genuine danger and didn't use her ability at all."

"Doesn't sound like measurement anymore," Davy said, an edge now, the fear curdling into anger. "Sounds like whatever's watching cares, specifically, whether my daughter lives or dies. Most reassuring thing I've heard in two days, or the most terrifying. Can't tell you which."

"Maybe it doesn't have to be either," Mom said, careful, thinking out loud rather than delivering a conclusion. "Maybe it's simpler. If this arrangement's about grading how people treat a world they've been given conditional access to — maybe what's measured isn't Cent's ability at all. Maybe it's whether the people around her responded the way a connected community responds when one of its own is in danger. Charlie and Dad riding out. Clara shouting for her to climb. Davy running toward the herd instead of away. All of us dropping everything the second Rick called out." She looked around the table. "That's not a test of one girl's power. That's a test of what the rest of us do with it."

Cent had gone still, mug forgotten. "You think it was testing all of you. Not me."

"I think I don't actually know, and I'd be doing exactly what I warned against yesterday to pretend otherwise. But it's at least as likely as the alternative, and a great deal more hopeful, and I'd rather hold the version that doesn't require us afraid of an eighteen-year-old's own existence."

Davy let a long moment pass, anger draining into something more like exhausted relief. "Still want that fence line checked. Spotter posted anytime she's out past it, herd or no herd."

"Fair," Mom said. "Good sense, watcher or no watcher."

"What about the testing?" Cent asked, subdued, bracing for the answer. "Do we stop?"

Mom looked at her, then at Davy, some silent negotiation passing between two adults who'd each spent this whole ordeal trying to protect people they loved without fully understanding what they were protecting them from.

"I think we learned something today we couldn't have learned any other way, however much none of us would've chosen it," Mom said finally. "I don't think the answer's stopping. I think it's being smarter — real safety margins, real oversight, nothing that puts you within a hundred miles of another morning like this one. But no. I don't think hiding's the lesson. I think whatever's watching cares more about how we treat each other than what any one of us can do." She reached across, took Cent's free hand in both of hers. "You're not a liability. Whatever else today taught us, it taught us that much."

Outside, the light went gold over the ridge, same as the evening before, giving nothing away — and for the second night running, I found myself hoping, provisionally, that we'd gotten this one right.
