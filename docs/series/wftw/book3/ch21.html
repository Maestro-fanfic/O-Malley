Chapter 21

Word travels strange when part of your family lives on the other side of a gate with no phone line running through it. Took the better part of a week for what happened to reach the tame side, and it was Clara's mother, of all people, who heard it secondhand over the shortwave and immediately insisted on coming through to see for herself.

"You'd think the gate spoke to somebody every other Tuesday," Clara said, watching her mother on the grainy video link, "the way she's treating this like ordinary Thursday business."

"I heard that," her mother said, dry, from the screen. "I am treating it like the single most important development in this family's entire history of tending that door, which it is, and I'd like to see the strip and meet this Rice family properly before I believe any of it happened the way Dad described."

Mom's mouth twitched. "You're welcome anytime, Sarah."

"I'll be through Thursday. Tell Davy and Millie to expect one more for dinner. And tell that boy of yours—" her eyes cut to me through the static "—I want the whole story from his side too, not the official summary. Raised enough kids to know the official summary leaves out the parts that mattered."

The line crackled out. Clara laughed. "She's going to grill you a solid hour. Ask anybody who's ever had actual news."

"I don't mind," I said, and meant it. In the week since the gate went quiet again, I'd found myself wanting to tell the story properly — not the tidy version already calming into local legend, but the actual messy shape of it.

Marie, meanwhile, had thrown herself into a different kind of aftermath — holed up with her instruments and handwritten notes, trying to build something permanent out of two weeks of reactive measurement.

"I want a real baseline," she told me, third evening, notes spread across the table while Rick pretended not to look over her shoulder. "Not just 'normal is whatever it was before the spike.' A model — what a healthy reading looks like across ordinary activity, so if something changes again, we're not starting from scratch."

"You think something will change again?"

"I think something always changes eventually. Not pessimism. The last two weeks taught me the world doesn't stay still just because we understood one piece of it." She tapped her pencil against a graph. "If it's really measuring what we do with each other, I want to actually understand that. Not just accept it and move on. Does kindness register differently than mere cooperation? Is there a difference the instruments can see between helping because it's convenient and helping because it costs you something?" Real hunger in her expression. "Not a two-week project, Charlie. Might not even be two years. But I want to start it properly, now, while it's fresh."

Out at the homestead, the second structure had gone up complete, roof shingled, windows set, and Davy had taken to spending evenings on the porch with a running list of everything the strip made possible — real tools, real stock, a proper radio, and, near the top in handwriting pressed hardest into the page, a school.

"Cent's had exactly no formal education in six years," he told me, one evening on that porch, Millie and Clara working supper inside. "Not for lack of trying — Millie's a fine teacher, and Cent reads faster than anybody I've known. But there's a limit to what two parents and borrowed textbooks cover, and I've watched that limit press harder every year." He looked out at the strip, pale in the last light. "With a real airstrip — she could go somewhere for schooling, or bring proper materials in. Don't know exactly what it looks like yet. But it looks like something now."

"Marie'd probably love to help with that," I said. "She's basically run her own private research project the last six years, no formal school either. I think she gets lonely for somebody her own age who wants to talk about the stuff she cares about."

Davy considered that, something warming in his face. "Might be good for both of them. Cent's never had a friend who understood the shape of her own strange life. Sounds like Marie hasn't either."

"Might be good for both," I agreed, and something in the easy shape of that evening felt like the truest proof yet that we'd landed on the right side of whatever the watcher meant by its measurement.
