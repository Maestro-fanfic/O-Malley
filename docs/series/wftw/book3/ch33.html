Chapter 33

By the time the leaves turned that first autumn, "same time next month" had stopped being a phrase anybody said out loud most visits, the way Clara predicted — just the ordinary rhythm of two families running alongside each other now, homestead and compound and Home all three, distinct and connected.

Marie kept her instruments running out of habit more than fear, the baseline steady, small proportional spikes tracking nothing more dramatic than an ordinary crowded life — Rick and Cent's engineering projects, Joey's card games, Apex Orbital's growing quota, Clara underfoot at both compounds often enough Millie kept a second set of riding boots by the door for her.

Mom still went to the gate barn alone some evenings, the dread mostly gone, replaced by something closer to a habit of gratitude.

"Do you think it'll speak again?" I asked her once, walking back from the gate on an ordinary Tuesday.

"I think it'll speak when there's something worth saying," Mom said. "Stopped needing it to, though. That's the real difference this year. Used to need the reassurance. Now I just trust the shape of what we're building, watcher or no watcher."

Davy soloed that October, a clear cold morning, Dad watching from the ground with an expression of naked pride, the whole compound turning out, Cent recording on her phone with the fond exasperation of a daughter watching her father discover a new way to delight himself at forty-some years old.

"Your turn next," Davy told her, climbing down, breathless and grinning. "Fair's fair. You taught me sat phone discipline. I'll teach you the yoke."

"I already fly, Dad. I fly myself, into orbit, regularly."

"That's not flying. That's throwing yourself somewhere and hoping the landing works. I mean an actual airplane, actual controls, actual patience instead of a clear mental picture and a willingness to accept significant personal risk."

"Sounds like the same skill set to me."

"They are not the same skill set," Davy said, mock-offended, a man who'd clearly had this argument before and lost every time. "Fine. We'll find out. Same time next weekend."

The phrase landed easy in the cold morning air, no longer a question either needed to ask twice, and watching them walk back toward the strip, arguing amiably about flight instruction and orbital mechanics in the same breath, I thought about that first morning again — the flag going up, the standoff in the garden that could have gone so many worse directions.

Two months, start to finish, stranger to family. None of us, standing in that ring on the day it started, could have guessed the shape of what we'd build by the time the leaves turned.

Clara found me at the fence rail that evening, same spot, same easy quiet.

"Good day," she said eventually.

"Good day," I agreed.

"Keep waiting for the next enormous thing," she admitted, watching the light go gold over the ridge. "Instead I just keep getting this. Ordinary days. Good ones. Don't know when I stopped expecting that to feel like a letdown instead of the actual point."

"Maybe that's the whole answer," I said. "Maybe the enormous thing was never one event. Maybe it was just this, the whole time — people showing up for each other, ordinary Tuesday after ordinary Tuesday, until it adds up to something worth calling a life."

Clara considered that, stars stacking up thick the way they always did, indifferent and enormous, finally unafraid of anything either family might choose to become underneath them.

"Same time next month," she said, soft, more promise now than question.

"Same time next month," I agreed. "For as long as either of us gets a say in it."
