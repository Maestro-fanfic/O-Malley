Chapter 104

Faith found Thor on the porch steps, alone for once, the rest of the group having drifted inside for food and normalcy after a long evening of impossible conversation. The night had cooled much since sundown, crickets working steadily somewhere in Joyce's hedges, the kind of ordinary Sunnydale evening that felt, after everything discussed inside, almost defiantly unremarkable.

"Didn't peg you for a porch-sitter," she said, mostly to have something to say, settling onto the step below him.

"I find porches remarkably restful. Asgard has balconies. Not quite the same thing." Thor studied her with the same open, unhurried attention he'd given everyone else that night. "You are Faith."

"Guilty."

"You are also the one who could not lift the hammer."

She'd expected that to sting more than it did, hearing it said so plainly, without any cruelty behind the words at all — just fact, delivered the same way he'd probably deliver the weather. "Yeah. That's me. Big fail, right in front of Buffy."

"I would not call it a failure."

"Kinda hard to call it anything else, given the hammer didn't budge an inch and Buffy lifted it like it was nothing."

Thor considered this, unhurried, the way he seemed to consider most things, turning a small stone over in one hand he'd apparently picked up off the porch steps without her noticing. "The hammer's judgment is not always simple. It does not measure strength, or goodness in any tidy sense. It measures readiness — a kind of readiness that shifts and changes across a person's life, sometimes across a single year, sometimes across a single day."

"So I'm just not ready yet."

"Perhaps. Or perhaps the hammer sensed something in you it did not yet have an answer for. Those are different things, Faith, and I would encourage you not to conflate them." He said it simply, without any performance of comfort behind it. "I was not always worthy myself. There was a time the hammer judged me completely unready, and left me without it for what felt, at the time, like an unbearable length of time."

Faith huffed something close to a laugh, mostly at herself. "So I'm cookie dough. Not done baking yet." Buffy's line, actually, stolen fair and square, but it fit too well right now not to use it.

"I do not fully grasp the reference," Thor said, with the particular gravity of a man giving a strange metaphor his complete, genuine consideration anyway, "but I suspect it is accurate."

That got her attention properly, enough that she actually turned to look at him. "You? The actual guy who's supposed to be worthy by default?"

"Nobody is worthy by default. That is rather the entire point of the mechanism." Thor tossed the small stone gently, catching it again without looking. "I was arrogant. Cruel, in ways I am not proud to recall. The hammer knew this before I did, and took itself away until I'd become someone worth trusting with it again."

"What'd you actually do? If you don't mind me asking."

"I started a war I had no business starting, out of pride and boredom rather than any genuine cause." Thor's voice carried no self-pity, just plain, settled fact, the kind of admission that had clearly been worn smooth by many previous tellings. "Cost lives that should never have been risked over something so small as my own wounded ego. I did not become a better person quickly. It took genuine loss, and genuine time, before I understood what had actually been asked of me."

"Damn." Faith went quiet a beat, chewing on it. "So it's not permanent. The no."

"I have never known it to be permanent, no. Only ever a pause, waiting for something to catch up to itself."

"That's either really comforting or really annoying, depending on how patient I'm feeling."

"Most true things are," Thor said, something almost like a smile at the corner of his mouth. "I did not say it to comfort you specifically. I said it because it is true, and because I suspect you would see through comfort offered simply to make you feel better."

"Yeah. I would've." Faith allowed herself something closer to an actual smile. "Appreciate you not doing that, then. Most people can't help themselves."

"I have had considerable practice not doing what most people can't help themselves doing. It is, I have found, one of the more useful skills a very long life eventually teaches you."

"That's either wisdom or you're just old. Hard to tell which with you."

"I contain both in generous supply," Thor said, entirely straight-faced, and Faith actually laughed, short and surprised, the sound cutting cleanly through the evening quiet.

"Okay, that one's actually funny."

"I have my moments."

"That's my brand of comeback, not yours."

"Perhaps it belongs to whoever needs it in the moment," Thor said, echoing something he'd apparently say again, months later, in circumstances neither of them could have predicted from this quiet porch. "I find good lines rarely stay owned by just one person for long."

Faith shook her head, still smiling, and let the moment settle back into something quieter. "I will say this much, Faith — whatever the hammer is waiting for, I do not believe it will wait forever," Thor continued, tone shifting back toward the gravity that had started the conversation. "Nothing worth becoming ever announces exactly when it arrives. It simply arrives, one day, and you look back and realize you've already become it."

She didn't have a ready comeback for that, which had become less rare around people who actually meant what they said to her, and the two of them sat together in the quiet a while longer, the porch settling into comfortable silence, an old, patient god and a girl still catching up to whatever she was becoming, both content, for the moment, to let the quiet hold rather than rush toward an answer neither of them could force into arriving any sooner than it meant to.

"Thanks," Faith said, eventually, quiet. "For not treating it like a big deal. Or, I guess, for treating it like a big deal that's also not a crisis. There's a difference."

"There is," Thor agreed. "I am glad you noticed I was attempting the distinction."

"Hard not to notice, with you. You're pretty obvious, for a god."

"I have been told that before, actually. I've decided to take it as a compliment."

"Good instinct," Faith said, and the crickets kept singing, and neither of them moved to go back inside for a good while longer.

She tried the hammer again that night, same as always, alone in the dark once the house had gone quiet — except this time she found herself almost hoping less for the thing to move than for the trying itself to mean something, the way Thor had made it sound like it might. It still didn't move. She let go slow this time instead of dropping her hand fast the way she usually did, and stayed crouched there a moment longer than she needed to, one palm still resting flat against the head of it, something in her chest loosening instead of sinking for once. She still went to bed after, steadier about it than she'd expected to feel.
