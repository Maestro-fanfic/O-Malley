Chapter 42

Cordelia found the group at their usual table with the determination of someone who'd been saving up gossip all week and had finally found an appropriately captive audience.

"Okay, mountain goats," she said, sliding into a seat nobody had offered her, which had never once stopped her before. "Real, or is Xander exaggerating again? I need to know how much of my day I should spend being impressed."

"Real," Willow confirmed. "I've seen the sketches. Very accurate horn curvature, from what I can tell."

"And he almost got frostbite?"

"Almost is generous," Xander said. "My hands were cold. Cold isn't the same as almost losing fingers."

"That's not what I heard."

"Then you heard wrong, because I was there, and I still have all ten."

"I'm just relaying what I was told. Don't shoot the messenger, shoot whichever idiot actually started the rumor."

"By who?"

"Jesse. Who apparently told three separate people before lunch that you nearly lost a hand to a mountain."

Jesse, caught, had the decency to look at least mildly sheepish. "I may have embellished slightly. For narrative purposes."

"There's no narrative purpose that requires amputee rumors, Jesse. That's just you being dramatic because your own life is boring."

"That's harsh."

"It's accurate. Those aren't mutually exclusive either, apparently, since that seems to be everyone's excuse this week."

Faith, who'd been quiet through most of this, finally looked up from her food. "Can we talk about something that isn't Xander's near-death experiences for five minutes? I've got a personal record to maintain here of not thinking about mountains."

"Fine. New topic." Cordelia considered her options with visible, slightly bored deliberation, examining her nails like the whole table's conversational output had truly disappointed her. "Sandwich debate. Where does it stand currently. And it better be entertaining, because my alternative right now is actual homework."

The table groaned in near-unison.

"It's not resolved," Willow said. "It's never gonna be resolved. It's achieved a kind of permanent stalemate at this point."

"I still think it's a sandwich," Faith said.

"I still think it's not," Jesse said.

"See, this is exactly what I mean," Cordelia said, unimpressed. "Permanent stalemate. I've watched this argument happen at least six times now and nobody's ever budged an inch. It's like watching two rocks argue."

"For the record," Jesse said, "you staying to watch it happen a seventh time is kind of flattering."

"I'm not staying for you, Jesse. I'm staying because the alternative is being alone with my own thoughts, and frankly this is the lesser evil."

"I didn't say you were staying for me. I said it was flattering. Two different statements."

"Everything you say near me is somehow both a compliment to yourself and something I immediately regret hearing."

"That's a skill, actually. Very few people can pull off both at once."

Cordelia gave him a look that could have stripped paint, sharp and totally unamused, and Jesse, entirely undeterred, simply grinned wider, the exact same grin he'd been deploying at her, unsuccessfully, since roughly the ninth grade.

"That's the appeal," Xander said, before Cordelia's expression could escalate further. "If we ever actually resolved it, we'd have to find something else to argue about, and honestly, this one's low stakes and reliably entertaining. I don't want to lose that."

"You're defending an unresolvable argument on the grounds that it's fun. That's honestly the saddest thing I've heard all week, and I sat through Harmony's breakup story this morning."

"I'm defending an unresolvable argument on the grounds that it's the best unresolvable argument we've got, and replacing it feels risky."

Cordelia opened her mouth, closed it, and shook her head slowly, disgusted the way someone gets when they'd hoped for better and, once again, received exactly what they should have expected. "I don't understand any of you, and honestly, I've stopped trying. It's easier this way."

The debate reopened in full a moment later, Jesse's embellished frostbite story getting good-naturedly torn apart from three directions at once, and Cordelia, despite every stated intention of leaving, stayed at the table for the rest of lunch anyway, sharp comments still landing with regularity, apparently unable to resist the material even while insisting she was above it.

42.5. Chapter 42.5

Buffy came along this time, the two of them dropping into a world of towering red rock spires that looked, at first glance, almost too improbable to be natural.

"Okay, this one's dramatic," Buffy said, craning her neck up at formations easily ten stories tall. "Very alien-landscape energy."

"Very alien-landscape energy that's apparently also home to actual wildlife," Xander said, spotting movement along one spire's base. "Look."

Goat-like creatures picked their way along ledges barely wide enough for a single hoof, moving with the same casual, gravity-defying confidence Xander had seen before on actual mountains, except here the terrain seemed determined to make the whole endeavor look even more impossible.

"How do they not just fall," Buffy asked, actually baffled, watching one navigate a gap that looked, from their vantage point, utterly unbridgeable.

"No idea. I've asked myself the same question about basically every mountain creature I've ever met. They just don't. It's very unfair, honestly."

They spent two days working their way through the spire field, cataloguing not just the goats but a whole ecosystem tucked into the shadowed gaps between formations — moss, strange insects, a small stream running clear and cold through a canyon barely wide enough for both of them to walk single file.

"This is genuinely one of the coolest places you've taken me," Buffy said, on the second evening, curled into his side with her back against sun-warmed rock, his arm settled around her shoulders. "I get why you never wanted to stop doing this."

"Yeah. Even after everything else going on lately, this part still feels like the actual point. Just finding stuff nobody's ever seen, getting to be the first." He tipped her chin up and kissed her, unhurried, nobody around for a hundred worlds to interrupt them.

"Good point to have," she said, once she'd caught her breath, settling back against him.

"Yeah. I think so too."

The trip passed without incident, rare enough lately that both of them commented on it more than once, and by the time the jump pulled them home, they had photos of the strangest goats either of them had ever seen and a shared sense that the job had, once again, delivered exactly what it always seemed to when it mattered.

Willow had the door open before they even reached it, already reaching for the camera bag. "Tell me everything. Also, hi. Also, everything."

"Alien rock spires. Goats that defy basic physics." Buffy held up the camera like evidence. "I don't even know how they don't fall."

"I need every photo immediately."

"You've got them," Xander said, already handing it over.

42.6. Show, Don't Just Tell

"I want to see it," Harry said, over dinner, in the same flat, decided tone he probably used for briefing rooms. "Not hear about it secondhand, not read your protocol notes. See it happen. If I'm going to help build contingencies for this, I need to actually understand what I'm building them for."

"That's fair," Xander said. "Fair warning, though — it's not exactly something I can schedule. I don't pick the world. The world picks me."

"Understood. I'm not asking for control over it. I'm asking to be there when it happens." Harry glanced at Helen, some silent conversation passing between them in half a second. "Both of us, if you'll have us."

"Grab hands right when the pull starts, and you're along for the ride. That's really the whole mechanism. People have been coming with me for a while now." Xander shrugged. "Never actually brought parents along before, but I don't see why the rule would be different."

The pull came two evenings later, all three of them waiting it out at the cones together, Harry having insisted on arriving early enough to properly study the setup — the marked square, the rotating security detail, the whole quiet, practiced routine of it — and Xander had just enough presence of mind to grab Helen's hand and shout "NOW" the second he felt it start before the world folded away fully, Harry's hand finding his other wrist a half-second later, quick enough that all three of them landed together, stumbling slightly on ground that hadn't existed for any of them one breath earlier.

Rolling grassland stretched out in every direction, warm and open under an afternoon sun, a tree line breaking the horizon far off to the east.

Harry's reaction was immediate and entirely professional — a full three-hundred-and-sixty-degree visual sweep before he'd even fully found his footing, cataloguing terrain, sightlines, and cover with the practiced speed of someone who'd run exactly this kind of assessment in considerably worse circumstances.

"Clear," he said, mostly to himself, then looked back at Xander with something that might have been genuine respect. "You do this alone. Every time. No backup confirming your read."

"Every time," Xander confirmed. "You get used to it. Or, I don't know, maybe you don't get used to it exactly, but you get efficient at it."

Helen, meanwhile, had already crouched down, running a hand through grass that had never once been touched by anyone, something unguarded crossing her face. "It's real," she said quietly. "I believed you both, obviously, but there's a difference between believing something and standing in it." She looked up at Xander. "Thank you for this. Genuinely. I don't think I understood what my daughter's closest friend's actual life looked like until right now."

They spent the afternoon shadowing Xander's usual routine, Harry asking sharp, economical questions at every step — how he judged terrain before committing to a direction, how he decided when curiosity outweighed caution, what his actual criteria were for approaching an animal versus simply photographing it from a distance — and by the time evening began to settle in, he'd filled several pages of a small notebook he'd apparently had the foresight to bring along.

"Your instincts are better than most junior field agents I've trained," Harry said, watching Xander set up camp with an efficiency that came from four years of repetition. "I mean that as a genuine compliment, not a casual one."

"That might be the best thing anyone's ever said to me. Possibly ever. I'm gonna need a second to fully let that sink in."

"Take your time. You've earned it." Harry looked out at the grassland, gold and endless in the fading light, something almost peaceful settling into his usually watchful posture. "I've spent my whole career in places built for danger. This is the first job I've ever seen that was built completely for wonder instead, and somehow just as demanding. I didn't expect that."

"Yeah," Xander said, following his gaze out toward the tree line. "That's honestly the whole appeal, most days."

They made it home safely the next afternoon, no incidents beyond a curious herd of antelope-adjacent creatures that kept a respectful distance the entire trip, and Helen spent most of the drive back from the football field already composing, out loud, an totally new appreciation for exactly what her daughter's found family had actually built around itself.

"I owe Joyce Summers an even bigger thank-you than I already gave her," she said, mostly to herself. "This whole operation runs on more competence than half the actual government offices I used to work in."

42.7. Faith: The Phone Call

They told me over coffee, the morning after the grassland trip, and I could tell before either of them said a word that it wasn't gonna be the kind of news I wanted to hear, just from the careful way Dad set his cup down first.

"We have to go back," he said. "There's an operation Gib and I have been quietly running point on for the better part of a year, back before any of this, and it just went active in a way that doesn't leave room for anyone else to take it over cleanly. I wouldn't leave if there was any real alternative, and I sure as hell wouldn't leave this soon after finally finding you again, if I had any choice in the timing. I need you to know that."

I felt the old thing try to climb up my throat, the specific, familiar shape of watching people I loved walk out a door with no guarantee about the other side of it, and made myself breathe through it instead of just reacting on instinct the way nine years of practice wanted me to.

"How long."

"I don't know yet. Weeks, probably. Could stretch longer, depending how it unfolds." He reached across the table, steady, not flinching from the question. "This isn't nine years, Faith. It isn't even close to nine years. I need you to understand the difference, because I can see you doing the math against the wrong number right now."

"I know it's not the same." I did know that, actually, somewhere underneath the old reflex. Knowing it and feeling it easy were two different things, same as everything else about this whole strange, complicated month had turned out to be. "I guess some part of me figured you two would just — be around now. Living here. I didn't actually think that through, did I."

"No, and that's on us for not being clearer sooner," Helen said. "We were never going to be able to just relocate to Sunnydale and disappear from our actual work, sweetheart. That was never realistic, however much we both wish it was. What's realistic is this."

"You're never going to lose us to silence again," she went on, cutting in before I could spiral further, her voice carrying the certain flat certainty she used for things she'd already decided weren't up for debate. "That's not a hope, that's a rule we're setting, right now, out loud. You get a call every single week, no exceptions, no missed check-ins without an explanation the same day. You get a number that goes directly to me, not through anyone, not through any office, straight to my actual phone, day or night. If nine years of silence taught me anything, it's that silence is the enemy here, not distance."

"Every week."

"Every week. And if an op ever runs long enough that a week's truly impossible, Gib checks in on my behalf, because Gib's better at remembering logistics than either of us most days anyway." Helen's mouth twitched, something warm cutting through the seriousness. "You are never again going to wonder whether we're alive. I don't care what it costs to guarantee that. It's already decided."

"Gib's staying?"

"Gib's staying a few more days to make sure the handoff's smooth, then he's coming with us," Harry said. "He already tried to argue for staying behind entirely, actually, on the grounds that someone competent should keep an eye on you kids. I told him that's not remotely his call to make, and Giles pointed out, correctly, that this house has survived significantly worse than a temporary Gib absence."

That actually got a laugh out of me, small and real, cutting through some of the tightness still sitting in my chest. "He's gonna miss the sloth stories."

"He will never stop asking about the sloth stories, is what he'll do. You'll get updates. Possibly unsolicited ones, at length, via whatever number Helen gives you." Harry's face softened, some of the careful professional composure finally cracking into something plainer. "I know the timing's brutal, right after we only just got you back. If I could hand this off to someone else and stay, I would, without a second thought. I can't, and I hate that I can't, and I need you to know both of those things are true at once."

"I know." I did know it, actually, watching his face. Nine years of reading people for a living apparently ran in the family, because I could see exactly how much this was costing him to say. "I'm not — I'm not the scared kid who thought you were dead anymore, Dad. I know the difference between you leaving because you have to and you leaving because you're gone. I can hold that. I've got a whole house full of people here who'll help me hold it, if it gets hard."

"That's the best thing I've heard all week," Helen said, something fierce and proud crossing her face. "That right there. That's exactly the thing I needed to hear."

"Every week," I said again, needing to hear it confirmed one more time.

"Every week," Helen repeated. "I promise you that the same way I promised myself I'd find you, and look how that one turned out."

We stayed there a while, coffee going cold between us, and it wasn't the goodbye I'd wanted, not really, not this soon, but it also wasn't the kind of goodbye that used to live in my worst imaginings — no vanishing, no silence, no nine more years of not knowing. Just an ordinary, difficult thing, the kind normal families dealt with all the time, and something in that ordinariness felt, strangely, like its own kind of gift.

They left three days later, Gib's van packed with far more sloth-related merchandise than any grown man reasonably needed, and the house felt quieter for a while after — not empty, never empty, just missing a particular register of noise it had only just gotten used to having around. The calls came every week, exactly as promised, and slowly, over the weeks that followed, that steady rhythm became just one more thread in the whole strange, sturdy weave of a life that had never once stopped finding new ways to hold more people than it had any right to.
