Chapter 17

"You look like you lost a fight with a mudslide," Faith said with a grin, the second Xander walked through the door, duffel bag squelching slightly with every step.

Buffy got to him before Faith finished the sentence, arms around his neck despite the mud, completely unbothered by what it was about to do to her shirt. "You're disgusting," she informed him, not letting go. "Also, hi. Also, you're never doing three days in a rainstorm without me again, I don't care whose weekend it technically was."

"Missed you too," he said into her hair, mud and all. "Also you're gonna smell like a swamp in about ten seconds, just so you know."

"Worth it."

"I lost a fight with a mudslide," Xander said, once Buffy had finally released him, still grinning despite the state of him. "Round one, mudslide. Round two, also mudslide, technically, since I didn't really get a round two so much as a controlled surrender."

"You're bleeding."

"Scraped, not bleeding. There's a difference, and the difference is whether Joyce makes me sit still for antiseptic."

"I'm making you sit still for antiseptic," Joyce called from the kitchen, without looking up from whatever she was cooking, in the tone of a woman who had apparently developed supernatural hearing specifically for the phrase not bleeding.

"See, this is what I get for coming home honest."

He sat still for the antiseptic, mostly, wincing through Joyce's brisk and totally unsympathetic application of it, and told the story of the hillside twice more before dinner — once for Buffy, once for Willow, who wanted the exact sequence of hand placements on the roots for reasons Xander chose not to examine too closely.

"You could've died," Buffy said, somewhere in the second telling, sliding closer to inspect the scrape on his arm herself despite Joyce having already handled it, pressing a kiss to his temple once she'd satisfied herself he was actually fine.

"I did not almost die. I almost had a very embarrassing conversation with a hillside. There's a difference."

"That's the same thing you said about the mudslide."

"Everything out there is a difference from the thing it looks exactly like. It's the whole job."

The zoo delivery happened two days later, once the birds he'd brought back had cleared whatever quarantine process Alan Finch had cobbled together for exactly this purpose, and Xander insisted on coming along to see them settled, mostly because he liked watching the moment a creature that had spent its entire existence assuming it was the last of its kind discovered it very much wasn't.

Alan Finch met them at the zoo's back entrance with a clipboard, genuinely loving his job in a way most civil servants managed to lose within the first year.

"Four new arrivals," he said, checking them off as the crate came through. "Ground-nesting species, previously presumed extinct — the paperwork on this gets more creative every time, by the way, I'm running out of plausible cover stories for where these keep coming from."

"Tell them a very generous anonymous donor with excellent luck," Xander suggested.

"I have used a version of that phrase in four separate official documents this year."

"And it's working?"

"Nobody's asked a follow-up question yet, which either means it's working or means nobody at the state level actually reads these forms closely, and I've decided not to investigate which."

Mayor Wilkins appeared partway through the unloading, drawn by whatever internal radar he apparently had for anything happening on zoo grounds, beaming at the crate like wildlife paperwork brought him more joy than most people got out of an actual vacation.

"Look at that," he said, peering in at the birds. "Aren't they handsome. Alan, make sure they get the good enclosure, not the one with the drainage issue."

"Already on it, sir."

"Wonderful." He turned to Xander with the same warmth, entirely undiminished by the fact that he was, by any reasonable definition, congratulating a teenager on smuggling extinct wildlife across dimensional lines. "And how was the trip, son? You look a bit worse for wear."

"Fell down a hill. Very embarrassing hill. Won the fight eventually."

"Good man. Persistence is the whole game, in my experience." Mayor Wilkins clapped him once on the shoulder, warm and fatherly in a way that always sat slightly strange against everything else Xander knew about him. "You're doing good work here. I mean that. This zoo's become something honestly special because of you."

"Just trying to help the birds out," Xander said with a shrug, a little thrown by the sincerity of it.

"That's exactly the right attitude." The Mayor beamed once more at the crate, then checked his watch. "I've got a council meeting in ten minutes that I am dreading with my whole heart, so I'll leave you to it. Alan — the drainage issue, please."

"On it, sir."

He swept off with the same energy he'd arrived with, and Alan let out a breath once he was out of earshot.

"He means it, for the record," Alan said. "The sincerity. It's real. He's got some actually strange priorities for a man who also runs half the demon economy in this town, but the zoo thing isn't an act."

"Yeah," Xander said, watching the birds settle warily into their new enclosure, already looking less like refugees and more like residents. "I know. It's honestly one of the better things about this whole arrangement."

"Better than the extinct animals?"

"Tied, maybe. Real close tie."
