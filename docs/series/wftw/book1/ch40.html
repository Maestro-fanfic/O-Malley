Chapter 40

"Mountain," Willow said, adding it to the chart, really serious about it. "That's a new biome category. I didn't even have a column for mountain."

"Glad my near-frostbite could expand your spreadsheet," Xander said, leaning over to press a kiss to the top of her head on his way past to grab a chair, the gesture automatic enough that he barely broke stride doing it.

"It's not a spreadsheet, it's a chart."

"It's got columns and everything. That's a spreadsheet."

"The distinction matters to me. Let me have this one thing." Willow tipped her face up for a proper kiss instead, unwilling to let the first one count, which he obliged without complaint.

Faith leaned over to look, stealing a glance at Xander's hands, which had mostly returned to their normal color by now. "You look significantly less frostbitten than I expected, given the way you walked in here."

"I'm a fast healer. Also, threshold for frostbite drama was maybe lower than the actual medical reality. I'm fine. Slightly traumatized by goats, but fine."

"The goats didn't do anything to you."

"The goats made it look easy. That's its own kind of psychological damage."

"That's not a real injury category."

"It should be."

Jesse arrived partway through, dropping into his seat with the timing of someone who'd learned exactly which conversations were worth showing up for. "Did I miss the mountain story? Also did I miss the kissing, because that's twice now I've walked in mid-smooch and frankly I deserve a warning system." He caught sight of one of Faith's patrol contacts leaving as he came in — a girl he'd apparently never seen before, cutting across the yard toward the driveway — and tracked her the entire way to her car with an expression of pure, undisguised appreciation. "Also, unrelated, who was that, and is she single, and is now a bad time to ask."

"Now is an extremely bad time to ask," Faith said, without looking up from her plate. "Also she'd eat you alive, and not in the fun way."

"Noted. Filing that under 'aspirational' rather than 'immediate plan.'"

"We don't run a warning system for that, Jesse."

"You should. Public health and safety. I could walk in on something far more traumatic one of these days."

"You could not possibly be that unlucky."

"I contain surprisingly bad luck specifically around this table's romantic timing. It's a documented pattern."

"You missed the mountain story, but the short version is cold, goats, and Xander complaining about goats specifically."

"The goats were showing off. I stand by the complaint."

"I need the long version," Jesse said. "The short version never has enough suffering in it."

"There was plenty of suffering. My hands hurt for a full day."

"That's not suffering, that's mild discomfort."

"It felt like suffering at the time."

Willow, still absorbed in her chart, added a note in the margin that nobody else could read from across the table, then looked up, clearly pleased with herself for connecting two data points.

"Okay, this is actually interesting," she said. "Cold biomes and hot biomes both produce higher incident rates than temperate ones. It's not just extreme heat that's dangerous, it's extremes generally. That's actually a useful pattern."

"Fascinating," Xander said. "Truly riveting information, delivered approximately six hours after my hands stopped hurting."

"I can't control when the data comes together, Xander, science takes the time it takes."

"I'm just saying, next time, faster science. Preemptive science. Science before the trip, not during my recovery."

"I'll get right on inventing predictive biome science," Willow said with a grin. "Give me a week."

"A week's fair. I appreciate the effort either way."

The table settled into its usual noise after that, Faith stealing fries with her usual lack of shame, Jesse still lobbying for a more detailed retelling of the mountain trip, Willow's chart growing one more careful annotation in handwriting small enough that it would probably need its own key eventually, just to stay readable.

40.3. The Rest of the Family

Harry, Helen, and Gib showed up for dinner that Sunday, official enough that Joyce had actually ironed a tablecloth, and Xander found himself shaking hands with Faith's father for the first time with the distinct sense of being quietly, thoroughly evaluated in the three seconds the handshake lasted.

"Xander," Harry said, in a tone that gave away absolutely nothing about whatever conclusion he'd just reached. "Faith's mentioned you. Good things, mostly."

"Mostly good is honestly better than I expected, given how often I've probably embarrassed myself in front of her."

"That tracks with what she described," Harry said, and there might have been the smallest curve of a smile underneath it, gone before Xander could be sure.

Gib, by contrast, gave up any pretense of subtlety about thirty seconds after sitting down, leaning across the table with open, immediate warmth, like a man who'd apparently decided liking someone was more efficient than working up to it.

"So you're the wildlife guy," Gib said. "The one Faith says brought back an actual sloth. A car-sized sloth. In this yard. Is that a true thing that actually happened or is that Faith messing with her old man for fun?"

"Extremely true. I've got the plaque receipts to prove it."

"I need the whole story. All of it. Don't skip the boring parts, I like the boring parts, the boring parts are usually where the real story's hiding." Gib was already settling in, elbows on the table, treating a good story like its own kind of meal. "Also, I've been in a van outside probably forty different dangerous situations over the years, so trust me, I appreciate a guy whose job is technically 'go somewhere unsupervised and hope for the best.' That's basically my whole career too, minus the sloth."

By the time dinner actually finished, Gib had heard the sloth story twice, the penguin story once, and had started, unprompted, calling Xander "kid" — easy about it, like the designation had already been earned as far as he was concerned.

Helen, meanwhile, had gone very quiet partway through the sloth retelling, something clicking into place behind her eyes.

"Wait." She set her fork down. "Wait, that's — I read about that. Months ago. There was a small piece in a trade newsletter my old office still forwards me out of habit, about an unusually well-documented new species acquisition at some private zoological collection in California. I remember thinking it was strange nobody could explain the provenance." She looked at Xander with something between disbelief and delight. "That was you. That's been you this whole time. I've been idly wondering about a mystery zoo acquisition for months and it turns out my own daughter's — " she paused, glancing at Faith, working out the actual word she wanted, " — the young man in her life was the source the entire time."

"In my defense, it's not really a mystery I was trying to keep. It's just also not something that comes up naturally in conversation with people outside the family."

"No, I imagine not." Helen shook her head, still faintly delighted. "I spent nine years reading intelligence briefings for a living, and I did not see 'my daughter's closest friend is responsible for half the cryptozoology chatter in three states' coming. That's a truly wonderful thing to not have seen coming, for once."

Harry had been quieter through most of it, watching like a man doing careful math, and finally spoke up once the sloth and penguin stories had both been thoroughly exhausted.

"These trips of yours. The random-world jumps." He leaned forward slightly. "I've spent two decades assessing operational risk for a living. I'd like to actually look at how you prepare for one, if you'd let me. Not to take over — I understand this is yours, and I'm not looking to change that. But I've got twenty years of threat assessment built into instincts you might find genuinely useful, and it would mean something to me, being able to offer you that."

Xander considered it, surprised by how little resistance he actually felt to the offer. "Yeah. Actually, yeah, I'd like that. I've been doing this on pure trial and error for four years. Wouldn't hurt to have someone who does this professionally take an actual look at my system."

"I'll be gentle," Harry said, with the dry, level humor Xander was starting to suspect was the closest thing the man had to a default setting. "Mostly."

"Somehow I don't fully believe that."

"Smart," Harry said. "You shouldn't."

40.5. Chapter 40.5

Dr. Marcus Webb, the Mayor's original lead biologist before Elena Vasquez ever took over the role, made his one and only trip that spring, after months of quietly lobbying Alan Finch for the chance.

"I understand the risks," he'd said, in the meeting Xander sat through mostly out of politeness. "I've spent three years processing specimens secondhand. I'd like, just once, to actually see the source firsthand."

The world they landed in was temperate forest, unremarkable at first glance, though Marcus's reaction to it suggested otherwise entirely.

"This is extraordinary," he said, within the first hour, already taking soil samples with a rigor Xander had never quite managed alone. "An entire forest ecosystem, completely undisturbed by human activity, going back who knows how many centuries."

"I mean, yeah, that's kind of the whole deal with these places."

"Understanding it intellectually and witnessing it firsthand are very different experiences, Xander." Marcus knelt to examine a cluster of fungi with genuine reverence. "I want to document everything properly. Soil composition, canopy structure, the works."

They spent three days in careful, methodical documentation, Marcus working with a thoroughness that made Xander's usual notebook look almost embarrassingly casual by comparison, and by the final evening, the older man looked honestly moved by what he'd witnessed.

"Thank you," he said, simply, packing away equipment that had clearly seen much more use than expected. "For letting me finally see it properly. This will inform my work for years."

"Anytime. Genuinely. Feels like more people should probably get this chance."

"Perhaps. Though I suspect the logistics rarely make it simple." Marcus managed a tired, satisfied smile. "Regardless. I'm deeply grateful."

He retired two years later, the demands of the position and his own age catching up with him, and the practice of bringing researchers along quietly lapsed in the transition — not forgotten exactly, just set aside, until a new lead biologist, notably more insistent than her predecessor had been about actually seeing things herself, eventually revived it.

"Dr. Webb went once," Alan Finch told Elena, the day she finally secured clearance for her own trip, years later. "Best research he ever produced, by his own account. Never quite understood why nobody picked the practice back up after he retired."

"Well," Elena said, already packing her own field kit with visible determination. "Consider it picked back up."

40.7. The Prep Session

Harry showed up two days later with a legal pad and an unhurried focus that made it clear he intended to do this properly, and spent the first twenty minutes just watching Xander lay out his usual pre-jump routine without saying a word.

"Walk me through your actual decision tree," he said finally. "Not what you do. Why you do it, in that order."

Xander talked him through it — the pack check, the mental inventory, the four-years-deep habit of listening for wrongness in a landscape before doing anything else — and Harry listened the whole way through without interrupting once, pen moving steadily against the legal pad.

"Okay. Two things." Harry set the pen down. "First, your threat assessment on arrival is actually solid. Better than solid, actually, for someone who built it completely through trial and error. You listen before you move, you catalog escape routes without being told to, and you don't commit to investigating anything until you've confirmed it's not actively hunting you. That's real tradecraft. Nobody taught you that, you just survived your way into it."

"That's oddly the nicest thing anyone's ever said about my process."

"I don't hand out compliments I don't mean. Second thing." Harry tapped the pad. "Your contingency planning's thin. You've got a good in-the-moment read, but almost nothing for 'what happens if the jump timer runs out while I'm mid-crisis.' No fallback protocol, no dead-man's check-in, nothing that triggers if you go quiet for longer than expected on this end."

Xander turned that over a moment. "I've really never had a plan for that. I just — figured if it happened, it happened."

"That's the gap I'd like to help close, if you'll let me. Simple stuff. A check-in with Willow before you go and the second you're back, defined enough that a missed check-in actually means something instead of just being background noise. And a triage framework for the landing itself — since you can't know what you're walking into until you're already standing in it, the useful thing isn't deciding whether to go, it's having a clean set of criteria for what to do in the first sixty seconds once you're there. When to observe only. When it's safe to approach. When the answer is dig in, stay hidden, and just survive until the pull takes you home regardless of what you came for. None of it changes how you operate out there. It just gives you a framework to fall back on when you don't have time to think it through fresh, and gives the people back here an actual protocol instead of just hoping."

"Yeah." Xander nodded slowly, something settling into place that he hadn't realized had been missing until it was named. "Yeah, I'd genuinely like that. Willow's gonna love having an actual system instead of just vibes and a countdown."

"She strikes me as someone who'd appreciate a well-built system," Harry agreed, already sketching a rough check-in framework onto the legal pad, economical and precise, twenty years of field discipline condensed into a few clean lines. "We'll refine it together. You know the actual conditions out there better than I ever will. I just know what it looks like when a plan's missing a piece nobody noticed until it mattered."

"Thanks. I mean it." Xander watched him work for a moment. "This is — I don't know, it's a weirdly nice thing, having someone treat this like it's worth actually protecting properly, instead of just something I got lucky surviving four years of."

Harry looked up at that, something briefly unguarded crossing his face. "You survived four years of it with nothing but instinct and improvised gear. That's not luck, son. That's competence nobody ever bothered to formalize. I'm just here to formalize it."
