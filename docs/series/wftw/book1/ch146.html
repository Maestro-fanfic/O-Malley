Chapter 146

Xander made it out to the fence line the day before, unwilling to just sit at home waiting on secondhand updates, needing to tell Davy and Millie about the Convergence himself before Home's own people ever set foot on this world.

The gate itself opened half a continent away, in Colorado, same as it had for years now — ancient, technically movable, but fixed there by long-standing choice rather than any real limitation, the family having decided, once, that a door this significant deserved a permanent home instead of getting hauled around according to whatever the day's convenience demanded. A real door, heavy and wooden, set into a frame older than either side of it — somebody's hand on the latch, the padlock already worked free, hinges swinging without so much as a creak from decades of careful oiling, the view through it flickering once, barely perceptible, like a TV catching a new channel, wildside grass replacing whatever ordinary Colorado outbuilding sat on the other side; Miriam stepped through first, followed by three strangers from Home, and what came after that was two hours by small plane, the same grassland-and-river route Charlie's father flew on every fuel run, the fence line finally resolving out of the empty distance as a thin dark line against gold-green country.

Xander heard the engine before he saw it, a speck against the sky slowly becoming something real, and felt some of the waiting ease out of his chest at the sight of it. The plane put down in the same stretch of grass Cent had once found a boot print in, which felt, to Xander, like exactly the right kind of poetry for how all of this had started, the forest around the strip going strangely still as it rolled to a stop, birds falling silent as though the woods themselves recognized something momentous had just arrived.

Miriam climbed down first, older than Xander expected somehow, though he couldn't have said why — something in the way she carried herself, perhaps, the weight of a person stepping back into a role she'd believed permanently closed to her — and right behind her came Cent herself — having insisted, apparently, on making the trip alongside her rather than waiting anxiously at home for secondhand updates — followed by three strangers from Home: a woman with sharp, assessing eyes who introduced herself as Ilsa, a younger man who kept glancing at everything with open, barely contained wonder, and a third, quieter, clearly still working through the emotional weight of the crossing itself.

Xander and Cent looked at each other a beat too long before either of them managed to say anything, months of careful notes under rocks and stolen, too-short visits crowding into a single, relieved breath at the sight of her actually here, now, on her own terms instead of whatever narrow window his jump happened to allow.

"Hi," Cent said, first, breaking the moment with a small, crooked smile. "Figured I wasn't sitting this one out."

"Hi," Xander managed back, something warm cutting through the fear currently sitting in his chest. "Glad you're here. Genuinely."

"Xander," Miriam said, extending a hand, giving them both a moment before continuing, her grip firm despite the visible fatigue of the crossing. "Cent's told us so much about you. I'm sorry it took this long to actually meet properly."

"Yeah, this has been a whole journey for everybody, apparently." He shook her hand, glancing at the others. "You must be the team from Home."

"Ilsa Kovač," the sharp-eyed woman confirmed. "This is Petra, Dov." She studied the tree line briefly, then Xander himself, with the same careful, assessing focus Xander had seen in Natasha weeks earlier, her posture suggesting someone who'd walked into more unknown situations than she'd probably ever counted. "Your world feels different than I expected. Louder, somehow, even standing in the middle of the woods."

"I get that a lot from visitors, apparently."

Petra, older and more composed, offered a brief nod of greeting before her attention drifted, almost immediately, to the surrounding vegetation, professional interest visibly warring with the urgency of the moment. Dov, the younger of the two, hung back a moment longer than the others, taking in the tree line, the ordinary suburban rooftops visible through the branches, with an expression closer to wonder than assessment.

"This is the first time I've properly set foot on a tame side we didn't already have our own gate to," he admitted, to no one in particular. "Every other one I've studied was through proper records — its people catalogued and watched over from a distance, each one with its own wildside standing by, quietly, in case they ever needed somewhere to go. This one's just — Miriam's old reports and whatever we can see standing here. Smaller than I expected. And louder, exactly as Ilsa said."

"Welcome to Sunnydale," Xander said. "Small and loud, with occasional demons. You get used to it."

"I intend to document as much of it as I possibly can, given how rare this opportunity actually is," Dov said, already producing a small recording device, delighted like a linguist encountering an totally new dialect for the first time. "I hope that won't prove disruptive."

"Honestly? Least strange thing happening in this town this week."

Getting everyone home turned out to be less complicated than it should have been, and considerably stranger. There was no quick way back through Colorado and onward from there, not on any timeline that made sense against a crisis already measured in weeks — which left exactly one real option, one Xander had never actually tested: wait out the three days until the pull came for him again, and bring everyone through with it.

"I don't actually know if this works with this many people," he admitted, the morning it came due, all five of them gathered loosely in the yard, hands finding wrists and shoulders and each other in whatever configuration let everyone stay in contact at once. "Never tried more than two before."

"Then we're about to find out together," Miriam said, steady, her hand closing around his forearm.

The pull came at its usual hour, and for one held, uncertain second nothing happened at all, five people quietly bracing against a disappointment nobody said out loud — and then the fence line was gone, and Sunnydale's familiar kitchen stood around all of them instead, Joyce halfway through pouring coffee and very nearly dropping the pot at the sudden addition of four new people to her morning.

By the time everyone had settled, the whole household had folded into the same careful rhythm of introduction and debrief that had, by now, become almost routine — Giles walking them through everything, Ilsa listening with an intensity that made even Strange's earlier focus look casual by comparison, occasionally pausing him mid-sentence to clarify a detail with a precision that suggested she was already, mentally, cross-referencing everything against her own instruments.

"Your instruments," Giles said, once introductions settled, "are much more refined than anything we've had access to. I understand you've been tracking this instability independently."

"We have," Ilsa confirmed, already pulling out equipment that looked, to Xander's untrained eye, both ancient and impossibly advanced at once — brass housing wrapped around components that pulsed faintly with an internal light he couldn't begin to explain. "And I'm afraid what we've found is not encouraging."

The room went quiet.

"How not encouraging," Fury asked, having arrived that morning specifically to be present for exactly this exchange.

Ilsa laid out her readings carefully, translating figures Xander couldn't begin to follow into plain, careful language, her instruments displaying graphs that climbed in a curve steep enough to look, even to an untrained eye, actually alarming. "The rate of acceleration isn't linear. It's exponential, and it's been accelerating faster over the past month than in the entire period before it. Whatever this Convergence actually is, it's approaching its critical threshold notably faster than any of our earlier models suggested."

"How much faster," Strange asked, sharp.

"Weeks, not months. Possibly less, if the current rate holds." Ilsa met the room's stunned silence steadily, not flinching from the weight of what she'd just delivered. "I wish I had better news. I don't."

Miriam's face had gone pale, old fear surfacing behind her eyes, her hand finding the edge of the table as though she needed something solid to hold onto. "Twenty million," she said quietly, mostly to herself. "That's what it cost us, last time reality nearly came apart at the seams."

"This isn't the same event," Ilsa said, gently, reaching out to briefly touch Miriam's arm, a small gesture of shared understanding between two people who'd both, in their own ways, carried the weight of that history. "But I understand the fear it's stirring up. I share it."

The room settled into a heavy, focused silence, the full weight of the timeline finally, properly understood by everyone present at once, the abstract urgency of the past weeks suddenly, concretely real.

"Then we don't have the luxury of slow, careful planning anymore," Fury said, finally, voice flat in the way of a man who'd made countless difficult calls before and recognized another one arriving. "We need a working theory of what actually happens at the threshold, and we need a plan to prevent it, and we need both significantly faster than any of us were expecting to produce them."

"Agreed," Strange said. "Which means everyone in this room needs to start working together properly, not just comparing notes politely from separate corners."

Nobody argued the point, and the room shifted, almost visibly, into something more urgent, more focused — four separate parties, four separate reasons for being here, finally united by the same stark, undeniable deadline now sitting over all of them at once, chairs pulled closer together, books and instruments spreading across every available surface, the actual work of survival finally, properly beginning.
