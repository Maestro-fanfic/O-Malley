Chapter 101

The man appeared in the middle of Main Street on a Tuesday afternoon, which under normal Sunnydale circumstances might have drawn notably more panic than it actually did, mostly because he looked less like a threat and more like someone who'd wandered off the set of a Renaissance festival and gotten seriously, spectacularly lost.

He was enormous, for one thing — well over six feet, built like he split logs recreationally, dressed in something that might generously be called armor if armor came in "vaguely medieval cosplay" instead of anything a blacksmith had actually made. He stood in the middle of the street, turning in a slow circle, expression somewhere between confusion and offended dignity, like a man used to being recognized on sight.

"This is not Asgard," he announced, to no one in particular, in a voice that carried significantly further than the empty street should have allowed.

A woman walking her dog gave him a wide, nervous berth and kept walking, muttering something that might have been "renaissance fair got weird this year" under her breath.

"Nor does it appear to be Midgard proper, in any capacity I recognize," he continued, undeterred by the lack of audience. "Curious."

He tried a small storefront next, peering through the glass at a display of discount candles with genuine scholarly interest, and moved on to studying a parked sedan with the same intensity, running one hand along its hood as if expecting it to reveal some hidden mechanism.

He was still standing there, turning slowly, scanning rooftops like a man tracking something specific, when Xander happened to walk by on his way back from the Magic Box, took one look, and stopped dead.

"Okay," Xander said, mostly to himself. "Okay, that's a whole Renaissance-festival situation happening in the middle of the street, and normally I'd just keep walking, but something about that specifically feels like it's gonna become my problem regardless of what I do."

The man turned, spotted him, and brightened far.

"You! Small mortal! I require information."

"I'm being called small by a man who looks like he benches trucks recreationally, but sure, go ahead."

"I seek an object. A hammer, forged in Asgard, sent from my realm some time ago by a trusted friend, for reasons I am still working to fully understand." He said it plainly, expecting the sentence to make perfect sense to whoever heard it. "My senses tell me it rests somewhere in this vicinity. Have you encountered it?"

Xander opened his mouth, closed it, and took a moment to fully process the sentence he'd just heard.

"Okay, weirdly, yes, actually, I think I might have. Big guy, dresses like you, name's Thor?"

The man's whole posture shifted, delight breaking across his face like weather clearing. "You know of me!"

"I know of you from a comic book, mostly, and also apparently now from you standing in the middle of my actual street, which is a truly strange sentence to be saying out loud right now." Xander rubbed his face. "Okay. Yeah. I think I know exactly where your hammer is. Might wanna sit down for the explanation, though. It's a whole thing."

"I do not require sitting. I require my hammer."

"Fair. Very direct. I respect it." Xander gestured vaguely toward the rest of town. "C'mon. Follow me. I know a guy who's gonna have a lot of feelings about this conversation."

Thor fell into step beside him, considerably more graceful than his size should have allowed, still scanning the surrounding buildings with quiet, focused intensity.

"This realm feels smaller than I expected," he observed. "Also much more beige."

"Yeah, that's Sunnydale. Small and beige, with occasional demons. You get used to it."

"Demons," Thor repeated, mildly interested rather than alarmed, like this was a Tuesday rather than a revelation. "I have some experience there."

"Somehow I believe you."

They passed a hardware store, a laundromat, a coffee shop with a hand-lettered sign advertising a seasonal latte, Thor taking in each with the same open, cataloguing curiosity, occasionally pausing to examine something — a fire hydrant, a mailbox, a bicycle chained to a lamppost — determined to understand every strange new piece of this small, unremarkable town.

"What is this vehicle's purpose," he asked, stopping beside the bicycle. "It appears to require considerable personal effort for such modest speed."

"It's a bike. Exercise, mostly, or getting around without a car. It's very normal, please keep walking."

"I find it charming. Inefficient, but charming."

"You're gonna find a lot of stuff here charming-but-inefficient. Just a heads up."

"I look forward to it," Thor said, utterly sincere, and Xander found himself, despite the sheer absurdity of the afternoon, unable to entirely suppress a small, disbelieving laugh.

"You're taking this whole thing remarkably well, by the way. The finding-out-you're-in-the-wrong-reality thing."

"I have found, over considerable years, that alarm rarely improves a difficult situation. Better to gather information calmly and act once the shape of things is clear." Thor glanced at him sideways. "You seem remarkably calm yourself, for someone who's just been informed a god is walking through his town."

"Four years of finding car-sized sloths and getting kidnapped by penguins has given me a pretty generous tolerance for weird, honestly. You're just today's weird. Ask me again in a week, might have a different answer."

"A reasonable coping strategy."

"It's the only one I've got."

They reached the Magic Box a few minutes later, Xander already dreading, with real specificity, exactly how this conversation with Giles was about to go, and paused at the door a moment before pushing it open.

"Fair warning," Xander said. "The guy inside's gonna have a lot of questions, and he's gonna want to write most of your answers down."

"I have no objection to being documented. I find scholars, generally, easier to work with than soldiers."

"That's a good attitude to have here, honestly. We've got a lot of scholars. Fewer soldiers than you'd expect, given everything."

"Then perhaps," Thor said, something almost hopeful in his voice, "this search has finally led me somewhere reasonable."

"Define reasonable," Xander said, and pushed the door open, bell chiming overhead, Giles looking up from his research with an expression that would, within the next thirty seconds, undergo several rapid and dramatic changes.

101.5. Heimdall: What the Eye Sees

Heimdall watched Thor arrive in Sunnydale from the observatory, same as he watched everything eventually, standing where he always stood, golden eyes fixed on a realm most of Asgard would never think to wonder about at all. He permitted himself something close to a smile at the look on the prince's face upon realizing this, of all realms, was where his search had finally led.

Small. Beige, by Thor's own assessment, relayed later and repeated since with visible bemusement. A realm that should, by any reasonable measure, have held nothing worth a god's attention. And yet here it sat, at the exact center of a disturbance vast enough to have reached even his own ancient, patient sight.

He had watched countless realms across countless centuries, had seen empires rise and fall in the time it took other beings to blink, and still, occasionally, a place like this one caught his attention in ways he couldn't fully explain even to himself — small, ordinary, unremarkable by every measure that usually mattered, and yet somehow, quietly, becoming the fulcrum on which something notably larger balanced.

Heimdall had not chosen where the hammer landed, not truly — Mjolnir's own will had carried it there, same as it always did when he set it loose during a crisis, trusting its judgment more than his own in matters of where worthiness might be found. But he had watched, in the intervening time, patiently, the way anyone who understood that worthiness rarely announced itself loudly learned to watch.

He had seen the golden-haired girl lift it without thought, the weight yielding easily to hands that carried burdens Heimdall recognized immediately, having watched significantly more than one champion across considerably more than one realm — a readiness that came not from strength alone but from a settled, steady willingness to carry weight on behalf of others without needing to be asked twice.

He had seen the other girl try, and fail, and the flicker of old hurt crossing her face before she buried it beneath practiced nonchalance — and he had recognized that too, the old ache of measuring your own worth by comparison rather than by any true accounting of what you actually carried. He had watched her walk away from that failure carrying it quietly, the way so many carried such things, unspoken and unresolved, waiting for a moment she didn't yet know was coming.

He had watched, later, when that same girl returned alone, and lifted it after all, and felt something that might have been satisfaction settle in his old, patient chest — proof, once again, that the hammer's judgment ran deeper and slower than most who encountered it ever fully appreciated.

'Interesting,' he thought, watching both moments replay in his memory with the effortless clarity of his sight. 'The hammer made its assessment of one quickly. The other required time to properly become what the hammer was already waiting for.'

He said none of this to Thor, who remained convinced, as princes often did, that his search was purely about retrieval — the simple matter of finding an object and bringing it home. Heimdall had long since learned that princes, even good ones, even ones he loved as he loved Thor, rarely appreciated having their simple errands revealed as something far more layered than they'd assumed.

Heimdall knew better, or suspected he did, watching the small, strange realm continue its small, strange business fully unaware of how closely it was being observed — a town full of ordinary people carrying, without fully realizing it, the weight of something that reached far beyond their own understanding of the stakes.

'Let him learn slowly,' he thought, turning his attention, briefly, to other realms requiring his sight before returning it, as he always eventually did, to the quiet, beige town that had somehow become the center of much more attention than its size would ever suggest it deserved. 'Some lessons land better without being rushed.'

He watched the girl called Faith a while longer, curious despite himself about what shape her eventual readiness might take, and found himself, not for the first time in his long watching, honestly uncertain how the story would resolve — a rare and not unwelcome sensation, after so many centuries of seeing patterns repeat themselves across realm after realm.

He watched a while longer, patient as ever, and said nothing to anyone, keeping his own counsel the way he always did — the same way, he reflected, that Giles the Watcher was currently keeping his, an ocean and a universe apart, both of them equally certain that some truths were better held quietly than spoken too soon, both of them, in their own separate ways, guardians of secrets they'd chosen to carry alone rather than risk mishandling by sharing too early.

Somewhere far beyond even his considerable sight, the disturbance that had first drawn his attention to this small realm continued its slow, patient work, and Heimdall, watching it all unfold from a vantage point no other being in the Nine Realms could claim, found himself hoping, quietly, that whatever came of it would prove worth the long, careful watching he'd already invested.

101.7. Chapter 101.7

Giles took the news of Thor's identity with notably more composure than Xander had expected, mostly by way of removing his glasses, cleaning them with unusual thoroughness, and asking exactly one clarifying question before proceeding as though gods showing up in his shop was simply Tuesday. Buffy, who'd arrived partway through the introduction, had slipped into a chair wide-eyed and quiet, doing careful math on how many impossible things one lifetime could reasonably be expected to hold.

"You said the hammer was sent away during an 'incursion.' Might I ask what that actually entailed?"

Thor considered the question with more gravity than the casual phrasing probably warranted, settling into a chair that creaked audibly under weight it clearly hadn't been designed for. "That is somewhat complicated to explain simply, given even our own scholars argue the particulars."

"I have some tolerance for complicated."

"Some years ago, by Midgardian reckoning, several of my allies undertook an effort involving considerable interference with the flow of time itself. Retrieving certain objects from various points in history, in service of preventing a catastrophe that had already occurred." Thor said this the way a man discusses weather he's long since stopped finding remarkable. "The undertaking succeeded, broadly. It also, by every account I've heard since, fractured something considerably larger than any of us fully understood at the time."

"Fractured how?" Willow asked, leaning in despite herself, notebook already appearing in her hands with the reflexive speed of a habit learned the hard way, across many previous impossible conversations.

"Reality does not enjoy being altered at a single point without consequence. Removing an object, restoring it, moving between moments — each such action apparently sends disturbance outward from the point of change, not merely forward through time but backward as well, weakening whatever separates one reality from its neighbors, at every point along the way simultaneously."

"That doesn't sound like it should be possible," Xander said. "Cause is supposed to come before effect."

"I did not say it was sensible. I said it happened." Thor shrugged, an oddly human gesture on someone his size. "Time, once sufficiently disturbed, appears significantly less linear than any of us would prefer. Our own philosophers have produced entire libraries arguing the mechanics without reaching consensus. I confess I stopped attempting to fully understand it myself some while ago."

"That's either deeply reassuring or the opposite," Buffy said, finally finding her voice. "I actually can't tell which."

"Both, typically," Thor said. "I've found the two rarely travel far apart, in matters this large."

Giles set his glasses back on, very still, rapidly reorganizing several long-held assumptions at once. "You're suggesting this event — whenever it actually occurred — could be responsible for instability reaching far further back than its own point of origin."

"That is my understanding, yes. Imperfect as it is."

"That would explain a great deal," Giles said, quietly, almost to himself, already reaching for a nearby book before catching himself and setting it back down, apparently deciding this conversation deserved his full attention before research could resume.

"Such as?" Thor asked.

Giles glanced at Xander, a silent question passing between them before he answered. "Such as why a boy in this town has been able to reach other realities for the better part of four years, when nothing in any text I've studied suggested such a thing should be possible at all, let alone this consistently."

Thor considered that with genuine interest. "You believe the same fracture responsible for my hammer's journey might also explain his."

"I believe it's the first explanation I've encountered that doesn't require ignoring several inconvenient facts to make sense of the rest," Giles said. "Which, in my experience, tends to mean it's worth taking seriously, however strange the source."

"I thought we already had an answer, though," Willow said slowly, working through it out loud, pen tapping against her notebook. "The Slayer-ritual thing. The scattering. That was supposed to be why Xander could do this in the first place."

Giles was quiet a moment, something uncomfortable crossing his face. "I called it the actual origin, at the time. I remember saying so, with rather more confidence than the evidence probably warranted."

"You weren't wrong, though," Xander said. "Just — not finished being right yet."

"I don't think either explanation replaces the other," Giles said. "The ritual, whatever exactly transpired during it, may well be the mechanism — the reason it manifested in Xander, and presumably in others, as this certain ability, tied to witnessing something ancient and powerful enough to leave a lasting mark." He removed his glasses again, turning them over thoughtfully. "But a mechanism still requires a reality capable of allowing it to function as more than a curious anomaly. If Thor is correct, the fracture may be what made that scattering, ages ago, actually take hold as something real and lasting, rather than fading into nothing the way such things typically would have."

"So the ritual's the how," Xander said slowly, "and the fracture's the why any of it was even possible."

"Approximately, yes. Layered rather than contradictory, if I'm reading this correctly." Giles glanced at Thor. "Assuming your account of the mechanics holds any water at all, which I confess I have no way yet to properly verify."

"Few do," Thor said. "I've long since stopped expecting a tidy answer to satisfy everyone at once. Reality, it seems, prefers its explanations layered rather than simple."

Buffy shifted in her seat. "So what happens now? Now that we've got at least part of an actual explanation?"

"Now," Giles said, "we determine what, if anything, can be done about instability of this scale — assuming anything can be done at all, which I confess remains an open question."

"Strange sources are, in my experience, often the only sources willing to admit how little anyone actually understands," Giles murmured, and something in the easy way Thor had said it made the whole impossible conversation feel, for a moment, almost completely reasonable, four people sitting in a small magic shop quietly reckoning with a truth much larger than any of them had expected to face when the day started.
