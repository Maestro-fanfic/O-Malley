Chapter 100

Joyce's list hit a genuine milestone that Sunday, which she marked by reading the entry out loud with real ceremony: one hundred entries, and the newest one read simply, Xander found another family. A good one. I think this is the happiest I've seen him in years.

"You wrote that?" Xander asked, a little thrown by it.

"I wrote what I saw," Joyce said, passing the potatoes down the table. "You've had a different kind of light in you the last few weeks. I noticed. I write down what I notice."

"That's oddly touching for a list that started with vampire logistics."

"The list contains multitudes," Joyce said, and the table laughed harder than the joke probably deserved, the easy kind of laughter that came from an evening with nothing urgent hanging over it.

"How's Willow's research going?" Buffy asked. "The stuff with Millie."

"Incredible, from what I can tell," Xander said. "She's been buried in notes for a week. I think she's happier than I've seen her in months, honestly."

"I am extremely happy," Willow confirmed, not looking up from the notebook she'd brought to dinner despite everyone's gentle protests. "This is honestly some of the best research access I've ever had. I keep having to remind myself not to get greedy about it."

"You're allowed a little greedy," Faith said. "Given how long you've been chasing mysteries with way less to show for it."

"That's generous of you to say, but I'm trying to hold myself to some kind of standard."

"Your standard's higher than most people's greed," Jesse said. "I think you're fine."

The table settled into its usual comfortable noise after that — Faith stealing fries on principle, Jesse angling for details about Cent that Xander mostly deflected with good humor.

"Come on, is she pretty? I need visuals for my mental filing system."

"That's a weird thing to need, Jesse."

"I have a very organized brain. It requires visuals."

"She's great. That's all you're getting."

"Vague. Suspicious. I'll allow it for now, but I'm filing this under unresolved."

Joyce quietly refilling glasses, long past being surprised by any of it.

"I think that's everything," Buffy said, eventually.

"For now," Joyce echoed, fond as ever, and reached for dessert.

Later, on the porch, just Buffy and Xander, the evening quiet around them, Xander looked out at a street that felt, for the first time in longer than he could easily place, utterly uncomplicated.

"You good?" Buffy asked.

"Really good, actually. Four years of thinking I was the only person who could do this, and now I've got a whole second family who actually gets it from the inside, plus you guys, plus Willow getting to do actual groundbreaking science because of it. Feels like everything just kind of clicked into place at once."

"You earned that. All of it."

"Maybe. Feels more like I got lucky, honestly. Lucky Willow found that photo. Lucky Cent left that marker. Lucky any of it happened the way it did."

"Luck helps," Buffy agreed. "But you're the one who kept going back, four years running, even when it would've been easier not to. That's not luck. That's just you."

He considered that, something settling comfortably into place in his chest. "Yeah. Maybe both, then. Luck and me, working together for once instead of luck just happening to me."

"I'll take that," Buffy said, and leaned over to kiss him, unhurried, the two of them sitting together a while longer afterward, the ordinary Sunnydale night settling in around them, quiet and easy and, for once, entirely unremarkable in the best possible way.

100.5. Cent: "Good, actually."

Dad asked me at dinner, the way he does when he actually wants an answer and not just the easy one.

"How are you doing with all this? Really doing, not the version you give when you're trying not to worry anybody."

I thought about it properly before I answered, because he'd know if I didn't.

"Good, actually. Really good." I turned my fork over, working out how to say the rest of it. "I spent a long time not knowing if there was anybody else out there like us. Now there is, and he's exactly the kind of person I'd have wanted it to be, if I'd gotten to pick."

"You did pick, a little. You're the one who left the marker."

"Fair. I picked to try. Didn't know what I'd get back."

Mom looked up from her book. "And Willow? The research?"

"Also good. Weirdly good, honestly, watching you actually get to talk shop with somebody who's spent months chasing the exact question you could just answer outright. I think you liked it as much as she did."

"I did," Mom admitted. "It's been a long time since anybody outside this family asked good questions about any of this."

"See, that's the part I didn't expect," I said. "Not just that Xander turned out okay. That this whole thing turned out to be good for all of us. Not just me getting somebody to talk to. You two getting something out of it too."

Dad reached over, squeezed my hand once, same gesture he's used my whole life for the things that actually matter. "That's usually how the good ones work. Nobody carries it alone. Everybody gets something."

"Even you? Mr. Still-Watching-The-Dairy-Case?"

"Even me," he admitted. "Though I stand by the dairy case."

"You'll always stand by the dairy case."

"It's actually bad sightlines, Cent."

I laughed, the easy kind, and looked around the table — Mom back in her book, Dad still faintly smiling, the ordinary evening settling in around a family that had, against every odd I used to quietly worry about, actually gotten to have something good land in our laps for once instead of something we had to fight for.

Good, I thought. Actually good. I'm allowed to just let that be true.

100.52. The Sabertooth

Millie came herself this time.

It wasn't something Xander had expected to ask for, and it wasn't something she'd offered lightly — a full day of careful conversation with Davy first, the kind Xander only caught the edges of, before she'd finally said yes, on the condition that she called every part of it, start to finish, no exceptions.

"You've never darted a cat before," she said, checking the rifle over, unhurried, thorough, clearly having done this more times than she probably wanted to count. "Wolves warn you. Cats don't. That's not a design flaw, that's the whole design. So today, you do exactly what I say, exactly when I say it, and if I say run, you run first and ask why later."

"Understood," Xander said, and meant it more completely than he usually managed to mean anything.

Willow had run the dosage numbers three separate times, cross-referenced against everything Millie could tell her about the animal's approximate size, and had still looked faintly ill about the margin for error right up until the morning they jumped. Buffy came for backup, same as always. Faith, hearing about it secondhand, had insisted on coming too, on the grounds that "an actual sabertooth" fell well within her professional expertise as someone who fought things with teeth for a living, an argument nobody at the table had found much room to disagree with.

They found it on the second day, exactly where Millie's read on the territory had said it would be — sunning itself on a rock outcrop overlooking a game trail, tawny and enormous and, even at rest, radiating the certain stillness of something that had never once needed to hurry for anything in its life.

"Okay," Xander breathed, lying flat beside Millie in the grass, camera forgotten in his hand for once. "Okay, that's — that's a lot of cat."

"That's a lot of cat," Millie agreed, fully calm. "Wind's in our favor. Stay low, stay quiet, and let me tell you when."

The wait took most of the afternoon. Xander had never held still that long for anything in his life, not even the sloth, muscles cramping in ways he was fairly sure would still be complaining about it a week later, Faith a silent, coiled presence at his other side, watching the cat with an intensity that had nothing to do with science and everything to do with four years of trained reflex.

"Now," Millie said, quiet and certain, the second the angle opened up clean.

The dart went in high on the shoulder, and the world went loud all at once — a sound Xander felt in his chest before he consciously registered it as a roar, the sabertooth up and moving in a single fluid motion that had every one of them scrambling backward on instinct, Faith already between it and the rest of the group before anyone had decided to move.

"Hold!" Millie's voice cut through the chaos, flat and absolute. "Hold your ground, don't run, don't turn your back — it hasn't decided we're worth the effort yet, and running is the thing that decides it for him."

They held. It felt like the longest thirty seconds of Xander's life, the sabertooth's massive head swinging toward them, weighing something none of them could read, before the dart did its work and its legs started to go, the roar tapering into something lower, confused, and finally, blessedly, silent as it folded down into the grass.

Nobody moved for a long moment after that.

"Okay," Faith said, finally, voice not quite steady. "Okay, I've fought vampires, demons, a literal god's hammer situation. That's up there. That's really up there."

"You're not wrong," Xander agreed, hands shaking as he finally lowered the camera he hadn't realized he was still gripping.

Millie was already moving, checking the animal's breathing, its pulse, with the same unhurried competence she'd brought to everything else. "Dosage held. Good work, all of you." She looked back at Xander, something almost proud crossing her weathered face. "Your friend's numbers were solid. Damn near perfect, actually."

"I'm gonna cry," Willow said, arriving at a dead run from the treeline where she'd been stationed with the radio, skidding to a stop at the sight of it, torn between terror and pure, undiluted scientific joy. "I'm gonna cry, and it's gonna be happy crying, and nobody's allowed to make fun of me for it."

Alan's team, thoroughly briefed this time and equipped with a transport rig that had taken three weeks and a small fortune to properly assemble, arrived within the hour. The zoo's newest, most heavily secured enclosure broke ground the following week.

"Especially the sabertooth," Willow said, catching Xander's eye over the sedated animal, equal parts exhausted and radiant. "Told you it'd be worth doing right."

"Never doubted it," Xander said, which wasn't completely true, but felt like exactly the right thing to say anyway.

100.55. The Ancient One: What Reaches Backward

The Ancient One felt it while meditating in the high chamber of Kamar-Taj, years before the boy from New York would ever darken her doorstep, decades before the name Strange would mean anything to her at all — a disturbance in the deep structure of things, faint but unmistakable, like a single thread pulled loose from a tapestry woven long before any hand now living had touched it.

She had spent centuries learning to distinguish true disturbances from the ordinary background noise of a living, breathing multiverse — the small ripples of everyday magic, the constant low hum of countless realities going about their business, indifferent to her attention. This was different. This had weight to it, gravity, the quality of something that mattered regardless of how far off it might still be.

She did not speak of it immediately. Some disturbances announced themselves as urgent and proved shallow. Others arrived quiet and proved, on closer study, to run notably deeper than their first impression suggested. This one, she suspected within the hour, belonged to the second category.

She held it for three days, meditation stretching into something closer to vigil, before finally drawing on the Eye, tracing the disturbance as far as her considerable skill allowed, and found herself troubled by what she could, and could not, perceive.

The ripple did not originate in her own time. That much was clear immediately — it arrived already old, already rippling outward from some point she could not yet locate, forward and backward through the years in equal measure, the way a stone dropped in still water sends its rings in every direction rather than merely the direction it happened to fall. She traced it as far back as her skill allowed, and further forward too, and found, at both ends, only the same frustrating absence of a clear source — a wound in time's own fabric, felt everywhere at once and yet visible nowhere in particular.

'Something, someday, will disturb time itself,' she thought, turning the sensation over with the patient care she brought to all genuinely difficult questions. 'Not yet. Not for some while yet, by the shape of what I can perceive. But it is coming, and its disturbance reaches backward far enough to touch even this moment, sitting here, long before whatever causes it has occurred.'

Wong found her still sitting there on the fourth morning, having noticed her absence from the usual training sessions. He'd learned to take her silences seriously.

"Something's been keeping you up here," he said, not quite a question.

"I have."

"Should I be concerned?"

She considered the question with real care before answering, the way she considered most things Wong asked her, having long since learned he rarely asked without reason. "Not yet. Perhaps not for a great while. But eventually, yes. Something is coming that none of us currently living will be equipped to fully understand when it arrives."

"That is not especially comforting."

"No," she agreed. "It is not."

She considered warning someone properly, consulting the other Masters at length, or leaving some record for whoever held her position when the disturbance finally, properly arrived. She decided, in the end, against wider disclosure — some knowledge, held too early, did more harm than the ignorance it replaced, and she had long since learned to trust her instinct on which category any given foresight belonged to.

She did permit herself one small indulgence: a private note, sealed and warded, tucked into the deepest archive of Kamar-Taj, addressed to no one in specific, meant only to be found if and when the disturbance ever properly arrived and someone, someday, thought to look.

Something reaches backward through the years, she wrote, the words coming slowly, each one weighed carefully before committing to the page. I cannot yet see its source, only its shape. When it comes, know that it was felt long before it happened, by someone who chose not to interfere with what had not yet occurred. Whether that was wisdom or cowardice, I leave for whoever reads this to judge for themselves.

She sealed it, set it aside, and returned to her meditation, the disturbance fading back into the deep, quiet static of a timeline that had not yet learned it would, eventually, be broken.

Years passed. The disturbance never fully left her awareness, a faint, persistent hum beneath everything else she attended to — present during her training sessions, present during her rare, careful interventions in the affairs of mortals, present in quiet moments between students when her mind wandered toward questions she'd long since stopped expecting to answer in her own lifetime. It surfaced most clearly late at night, in the hours she reserved for her own private study, a reminder that some problems simply outlasted the people who first noticed them.

Right up until the day a young surgeon with broken hands finally found his way to her door, and her attention turned, as it always eventually did, toward more immediate concerns — a life needing shaping, a mind needing opening, the endless, patient work of preparing someone for a role they didn't yet know they'd eventually have to fill.

She died, in time, the way all things eventually did, never learning the disturbance's true shape, never knowing that an Asgardian hammer would tumble sideways into a small, ordinary town on an Earth not quite her own, carried there by the very fracture she'd felt coming decades before anyone thought to look for it, or that a girl half a world away would one day try and fail to lift that same hammer, only to succeed months later, having grown into whatever the hammer had been waiting for all along.

Somewhere, in whatever came after, she might have found some quiet satisfaction in being right. Or perhaps only in having chosen, all those years ago, not to interfere with something that was never truly hers to prevent — a small, private act of restraint that would, decades later, prove to be exactly the gift a stranger's world had needed most.

100.6. Thor: The Search

Thor had visited eleven realms before this one, and none of them had been Sunnydale, which he was beginning to suspect was less a coincidence and more a joke the universe was playing at his expense.

The first had been a world of endless ice, populated by nothing but silence and wind, cold enough that even he had felt it through layers of enchanted armor, the horizon lost somewhere behind curtains of blowing snow that never seemed to fully clear. He'd searched three days before admitting defeat, the hammer's presence nowhere in evidence, nothing but white in every direction he turned.

The second had been ocean, horizon to horizon, no land he could find no matter how far he flew, waves the size of mountains rolling beneath him in patterns that suggested storms of a scale his own realm had never quite produced. He'd nearly lost himself searching that one, exhausted past the point of good judgment before finally admitting the hammer couldn't possibly have landed somewhere with no ground to land on.

The third had held people — actual people, curious and frightened in equal measure, gathering in the streets of a low, sprawling city built from materials he didn't recognize, watching him with expressions that shifted rapidly from wonder to something closer to alarm. He'd felt nothing of the hammer there, and left quickly, mindful of Heimdall's warnings about disturbing realities unprepared for a god's arrival, the memory of those frightened, upturned faces staying with him longer than he'd expected.

The fourth had been jungle so dense he'd had to cut his own path through it, vines thick as his own arm, insects that bit through even his boots. The fifth had been desert, endless and empty, heat shimmer distorting the horizon into something that looked, more than once, deceptively like the hammer's silhouette before resolving into nothing but rock. The sixth through tenth blurred together in memory, a procession of worlds either empty or wrong or simply, maddeningly silent where the hammer's presence should have been.

The eleventh had nearly broken something in him — a realm caught mid-catastrophe, some disaster he never fully understood the shape of, survivors fleeing something Thor couldn't identify and didn't have time to properly investigate before the absence of any hammer-signal sent him onward again, guilt trailing behind him at having left people in genuine need to continue his own search.

"You are certain it went somewhere findable," he'd said, before setting out, standing on the Bifrost's edge, impatient the way he always was when action was called for and waiting was all that was on offer.

"I am certain of very little where the hammer's own will is concerned," Heimdall had replied, infuriatingly calm as always. "I sent it away for its own protection, during the incursion. Where it chose to land afterward was never entirely mine to control."

"You made it sound simpler than this at the time."

"I made it sound urgent, because it was urgent. I never claimed it would be simple to retrieve." Heimdall's golden eyes had held something almost like amusement. "The hammer seeks worthiness, Thor. It does not seek convenience. You, of all people, should understand the distinction."

Thor had understood the distinction considerably less than he'd claimed to, standing here now on his twelfth attempt, in a realm that looked — astonishingly, after eleven failures — almost totally ordinary. Small structures. Modest transportation devices moving along paved paths. People who looked at him with confusion rather than terror, which he'd come to recognize, after considerable trial, as a promising sign rather than a discouraging one.

He'd felt something here immediately, the faintest thread of the hammer's presence, more concrete than any of the previous eleven attempts had offered. Close. Closer than close, if his instincts held, a resonance he hadn't dared fully trust after so many false starts elsewhere.

'Small world,' he thought, taking in the modest scale of the street around him, the buildings no more than a few stories tall, everything built to a scale that felt, after Asgard's soaring architecture, almost deliberately humble. 'Small, but not without its own strangeness. I sense something beneath the surface here. Something significantly older than the buildings suggest.'

He stood there a long moment simply breathing it in, this ordinary street with its ordinary buildings, letting himself, cautiously, begin to hope this search might actually be nearing its end after weeks that had felt, more than once, endless.

He hadn't expected to find a guide so quickly — the young man who'd stopped dead in the street, taken one long look at him, and immediately, correctly, guessed exactly who he was. That, more than anything, told Thor something worth noting about this realm: whatever strangeness lived beneath its ordinary surface, its people were evidently not strangers to strangeness themselves.

'Perhaps this is finally the place,' he allowed himself to think, following his new acquaintance toward whatever destination the young man had in mind, hope sitting cautious and unfamiliar in his chest after eleven realms of disappointment. 'Perhaps the search ends here, at last, in a small, beige town that has clearly seen far stranger things than one displaced god looking for his hammer.'

He hoped so, at least. Twelve realms was, by any reasonable measure, quite enough searching for one lifetime, even a lifetime as long as his — and some quiet, tired part of him, longer buried than he cared to admit, simply wanted to go home, hammer in hand, and rest.

100.65. Thor: The Ping

Thor felt it the moment it happened — a plucked string resonating somewhere behind his sternum, sharp and sudden against the faint, directionless static he'd carried since the day Heimdall sent the hammer away, a background hum he'd grown so accustomed to that its sudden sharpening nearly staggered him mid-stride through the palace's outer hall.

Contact. Someone had touched it. Properly touched it, gripped its weight, engaged with it as an object meant to be lifted.

And it had answered.

The resonance carried a texture he recognized immediately, distant cousin to the feeling he'd known his entire life holding the hammer himself — not identical, nothing was ever quite identical, but close enough in shape that his breath caught before he'd fully processed why. He stopped where he stood, one hand braced against the nearest wall, drawing the sensation in properly, memorizing its exact shape the way a man memorizes a voice he's afraid he might not hear again.

Worthy. Or worthy enough, in whatever measure the hammer used to judge such things, which Thor had never fully understood even after a lifetime of being its chosen bearer.

He nearly called for the Bifrost that same hour, already turning toward the observatory before some older, more dutiful instinct caught him by the collar and held him in place.

He didn't call for it, because Asgard, in the aftermath of the incursion that had forced the hammer's departure in the first place, was still very much a realm requiring its prince's attention. Border skirmishes flared where old wards had thinned, small conflicts erupting along territories that had gone peacefully undisputed for centuries before the incursion's disruption unsettled old boundaries all at once. Three separate delegations demanded audience over territory the incursion had reshaped, each convinced their claim held precedence, each requiring hours of careful diplomacy Thor had never much enjoyed but had learned, over the years, to perform competently regardless. And beneath all of it, quieter than the skirmishes, quieter than the delegations, his father's health sat as a slow, persistent worry nobody spoke of directly but everybody privately measured against the calendar — a decline gradual enough to deny easily, present enough that Thor found himself, more than once, lingering in doorways just to watch Odin sleep, reassuring himself the breathing continued steady.

A god, it turned out, could feel an unmistakable signal and still not be free to chase it, not for months, not while an entire realm needed steadying first. He told himself this daily, some days believing it more than others.

He carried the resonance with him regardless, turning it over in idle moments between council sessions, holding onto the shape of it like a promise he intended to keep the moment circumstances finally allowed. He confided in no one, not even Heimdall, not wanting to voice hope he wasn't yet free to act on, afraid saying it aloud might somehow make the waiting harder to bear.

Circumstances allowed, eventually, though far later than he'd have liked. The border skirmishes settled, slowly, into new treaties both sides could live with. The delegations concluded their business, satisfied or at least resigned. His father's health, for the moment, held steady enough that Thor could leave without the guilt of abandoning a dying man's bedside. By the time Asgard's affairs settled enough for him to be spared, and by the time Midgard's own troubles pulled him briefly elsewhere besides — a skirmish here, a rescue there, the ordinary obligations of an Avenger that never quite paused simply because his own heart was elsewhere — the trail had gone cold enough that he could no longer simply follow it. The resonance's direction, sharp and immediate in the moment it first arrived, had faded back into something closer to general certainty than precise location, months of delay eroding what should have been a clean, findable thread into something much more diffuse.

Which meant a search. A real one, realm by disconnected realm, since travel between branches unconnected by any established path was never so simple as merely willing himself toward a destination — each attempt required searching blind, feeling for any answering trace of the hammer's presence, then moving on when none came, the process slow and uncertain in a way his usual, more direct approach to problems had never quite prepared him for.

He began in earnest once free, finally, properly free of every obligation that had held him back, and found nothing for longer than he'd have liked to admit, the gap between hope and disappointment widening with every failed realm, patience wearing thinner than he cared to acknowledge even to himself.

100.7. The Compound

Natasha noticed first, which surprised exactly no one, given she generally noticed things before anyone else bothered to look.

She'd started keeping a loose mental log of Thor's comings and goings months back, mostly out of habit rather than any conscious decision to monitor him — the same instinct that kept her tracking exit routes in restaurants and counting heads in crowded rooms, applied without much thought to the people she actually trusted. Which was, she'd come to realize, a fairly short list, and Thor sat near the top of it despite three years of insisting to herself that she didn't really do that anymore, the trusting thing.

"Thor's been gone eleven days," she said, sliding into the briefing room where Steve was reviewing reports nobody particularly wanted to be reviewing, the fluorescent light overhead doing nothing kind for either of their moods.

Steve didn't look up right away, still working through a paragraph on supply logistics that had apparently earned his full concentration. "He does that. Space stuff. Family stuff. Sometimes both at once."

"He usually tells somebody. Even if it's just Bruce, muttering something about cosmic errands on his way out the door." She set a tablet down on the table between them, a single line of text glowing on the screen — a timestamp, a location ping that had gone dark, nothing especially alarming on its own. "He didn't tell anybody this time. Just gone."

Steve frowned at that, setting his own folder aside with the careful deliberateness of someone who took interruptions seriously precisely because he didn't get many of them without good reason. "You think something's wrong?"

"I think Thor doesn't usually vanish quietly. Loud entrances, loud exits, that's kind of his whole brand." Natasha crossed her arms, leaning against the table's edge. "Eleven days of quiet doesn't fit the pattern. Doesn't mean something's definitely wrong. Just means I'm paying attention to it."

"Could be nothing. Could be exactly the kind of Asgard business he doesn't think we need looped in on. He's mentioned before, some of what he deals with back home isn't really ours to know."

"Could be." She didn't sound convinced, tapping one finger against the tablet's edge, a habit that had never quite left her from years of waiting on intelligence that wouldn't confirm itself fast enough. "I put out feelers anyway. Contacted a few people who might've heard something, checked whatever satellite data actually tracks something his size moving through atmosphere. Nothing yet. If he's not back inside a week, I think we actually look into it properly, instead of just noting it and moving on."

Bruce wandered in partway through, coffee in hand, catching the tail end of the conversation with the mild, half-present attention of a man who'd been up too many nights running numbers that hadn't cooperated, hair doing something that suggested he'd forgotten it existed sometime around 3 a.m.

"Thor missing?" he asked, settling into the chair across from Steve without quite seeming to register he was joining a conversation already in progress.

"Thor unaccounted for," Natasha corrected. "Slight difference. He's a grown god. He's allowed to go quiet occasionally."

"He usually tells me before he does, though," Bruce said, echoing Steve's earlier point without quite meaning to, rubbing at his eyes. "We've got a standing science thing. Wednesdays, mostly, sometimes he brings actual Asgardian text he wants translated, sometimes we just argue about whether magic and physics are secretly the same discipline wearing different coats. He missed it. Didn't even send word, which — that's actually pretty unlike him, now that I'm properly thinking about it instead of just being annoyed I lost an afternoon."

The room went quiet for a moment, three people independently arriving at the same low-grade unease without quite wanting to name it out loud first, the kind of silence that settled over people who'd each individually decided, without discussing it, that a pattern was worth taking seriously.

"A week," Steve said finally, decisive in the way he always got once he'd actually made up his mind about something. "If he's not back or in contact within a week, we start actually looking. Properly. Not just noting it and hoping it resolves itself."

"A week," Natasha agreed, and made a note on the tablet, the number sitting there glowing quietly, an unremarkable little countdown that none of them yet understood the actual shape of, or where, precisely, it might eventually lead them looking — a small, ordinary Tuesday morning conversation that would turn out, in hindsight, to be the first thread of something considerably larger than any of them currently had reason to suspect.

"For what it's worth," Bruce added, already halfway back into his own thoughts, "if it does turn out to be something, I'd rather know sooner than later. I don't love surprises these days. Had enough of those for one lifetime."

"None of us love surprises," Natasha said. "That's basically the whole job description, though. Loving them was never part of the deal."

Steve managed something close to a tired smile at that, and the three of them settled back into their separate work, the countdown quietly ticking forward in the background of an otherwise unremarkable morning.
