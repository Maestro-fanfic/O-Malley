Chapter 38

Xander left for the next jump on schedule, same as always, and this time Willow handed him a folded page before he vanished, tucked into his jacket pocket like a good luck charm disguised as a spreadsheet.

"What's this?"

"Hazard notes. Based on the chart. Nothing fancy, just — if you land somewhere that looks like X, watch for Y. It's not perfect. It's not even close to perfect. But it's something."

"You made me a cheat sheet."

"I made you a cheat sheet," Willow confirmed, a little defensive about it. "Don't lose it. And don't laugh at the handwriting, I did it fast."

"I would never laugh at your handwriting." Xander tucked it in carefully, then leaned down to kiss her properly before the pull could take him. "This is genuinely really thoughtful, Willow. Thank you."

"Just don't get eaten by anything the chart didn't warn you about. That's all I ask."

"No promises, but I'll do my best."

He vanished on schedule, same flicker of nothing-to-something that never stopped being strange no matter how many hundred times it happened, and the kitchen settled back into its ordinary rhythm without him — Faith already halfway out the door for patrol, notebook of her own tucked under one arm, mural photographs paper-clipped inside; Willow immediately starting a second copy of the hazard sheet, because apparently one wasn't enough insurance against her own nerves; Joyce humming something off-key while she did the dishes, unbothered by any of it after four years of practice.

Buffy caught Faith at the door before she left.

"Anything new on the mural thing?"

"Nothing since Thursday. Giles thinks whatever's building isn't ready yet. Which is either good news or just means it's got more time to get worse before it shows itself." Faith shrugged, stake already in hand. "Either way, I'm watching. Same as always."

"You'll tell me if it gets serious?"

"You'll be the first call. Promise." Faith paused at the door. "For what it's worth — this whole stretch, lately, sloths and seals and ominous murals and Willow's disaster spreadsheet — it's a good stretch. As far as Sunnydale stretches go."

"Yeah," Buffy said with a smile. "It really is."

"Say it quieter next time. This town's got ears."

"That's not a real thing."

"Neither was a car-sized sloth, and here we are."

"Fair," Buffy admitted, and watched Faith head off into the evening, patrol notebook under one arm and a mystery all her own tucked inside it, while somewhere on the other side of existence totally, Xander was three hours into a fresh weekend with a hand-drawn hazard sheet in his pocket and, for once, at least a fighting chance of seeing trouble coming before it found him.

38.5. Vetting the Situation

Harry found Giles at the Magic Box on the third day, no appointment, no warning, just walked in during a lull between customers and set a folder down on the counter with the particular economy of motion of a man who'd done this exact maneuver a thousand times in rooms significantly more hostile than a bookstore.

"Rupert Giles," he said. Not a question. "I did some reading on you. Watchers' Council, former field capacity, current advisor and, from what I've pieced together, informal guardian to a fair number of teenagers who apparently spend their nights fighting vampires."

Giles set down the book he'd been shelving, unhurried, and looked at the folder without touching it. "You've had two days in town and already assembled a file on me."

"Two days and a set of skills I've spent twenty years building. Habit, mostly. Hard to turn off." Harry didn't sit, didn't ask permission, just held Giles' gaze steady. "My daughter's been living under a level of danger I didn't sanction and can't undo, and I've spent nine years unable to do a single thing about any threat to her life. I'd like the actual truth about what she's involved in now, not the version that makes it sound manageable."

"That's a fair request." Giles removed his glasses, cleaning them in the way that meant he was deciding how much honesty the moment could bear. "The truth is far worse than 'manageable,' and considerably better handled than you might expect, given the people around her. Faith is what's called a Slayer. One girl in all the world, called to fight vampires and demons, given strength and instinct to match the task. She did not choose this. Neither did the girl before her, nor will the girl after. It is, without exaggeration, one of the most dangerous callings that exists."

Harry absorbed that with visible stillness, filing the information away rather than reacting to it. "And you're the one responsible for training her for it."

"I'm the one responsible for supporting her through it, which is a distinction I've come to care about a great deal. The Council I formerly answered to treats Slayers as assets to be managed. I no longer operate that way, and I no longer answer to them in any capacity that matters." Giles met his eyes without flinching. "I understand precisely what it looks like, a man my age with unsupervised authority over your daughter's life. I would want the same answers you're asking for, in your position."

"I already ran a check on the Council. Thin file, evasive front, exactly the kind of institutional shell that makes career intelligence people nervous." Harry finally sat, some of the coiled readiness easing out of his shoulders, though not all of it. "I also talked to the Summers woman for twenty minutes this morning. She didn't dress anything up. Told me flatly that your daughter's died once already, doing this job, and came back, and that you were the one who sat with her through the worst of the aftermath."

"That's accurate. I won't pretend otherwise to make myself sound better."

"I appreciate that." Harry studied him a long moment. "I've spent my career reading people who lie professionally for a living. You're not one of them. Which either means you're better at it than anyone I've ever met, or you're exactly what you appear to be, and I've decided to bet on the second one, because the alternative means I've already lost, and I'm not built to operate that way."

"I'd like to earn that trust properly rather than simply receive it," Giles said. "Perhaps you'd allow me to show you the training regimen, introduce you to the resources we actually have, and answer any question you have as thoroughly as I'm able. I suspect you'll find our operation much less reckless than 'unsupervised teenagers fighting monsters' suggests."

"I'd like that. And one more thing—" Harry picked the folder back up, tucking it away rather than leaving it on the counter, some small concession in the gesture. "Whatever resources my organization can quietly offer — equipment, training, intelligence — consider them available. I don't get to be the parent who was there for the last nine years. I can at least be useful for whatever's left."

Two blocks away, in Joyce's kitchen, Helen was learning notably more about her daughter's new life over coffee than any government file could have told her.

"She talks about you," Joyce said, refilling both their cups. "Faith, I mean. Not often, and never with details, but I could tell there was a — a shape missing. Something she'd built her whole guard around."

"I know exactly what that shape looks like from the inside," Helen said quietly. "Nine years of it." She turned the coffee cup slowly between her hands, not quite looking up. "I want to thank you. Genuinely. Whatever this house did for her while we couldn't, I don't have the words for how grateful I am that it existed."

"She did most of it herself, honestly. I just kept the porch light on and made sure there was always enough dinner for one more person." Joyce smiled, warm and a little tired in the way of someone who'd learned to expect the extraordinary at her own table. "Though I'll admit, learning your family's actual line of work has been quite the week, even by this house's standards."

"I imagine you've had a fairly unusual few years yourself, from what Faith's told me." Helen's mouth curved, something sharp and assessing underneath the warmth, the same instinct that had kept her alive through nine years of a life she hadn't asked for either. "I'd like to help, if you'll let me. Not take over. I know how this works when someone barges into a household that's already found its rhythm without them. But I have skills that might be really useful to a house full of teenagers running toward danger instead of away from it, and I'd like the chance to use them for something that actually matters again."

"I think," Joyce said, considering her carefully, "that this house has never once turned away someone who wanted to help for the right reasons. You'll fit right in. Whether that's a comfort or a warning, I truly can't say yet."

"Knowing this family so far," Helen said, "probably both."
