Chapter 98

Millie said yes, with conditions, which Xander relayed back to Willow clearly relieved, since he'd been more nervous asking than he'd let on.

"No names, no locations, nothing that could trace back to them specifically. And she wants to meet you first, before any actual science happens. Her rules, not mine."

"Completely fair. More than fair. I'll bring a list of questions and she can veto whatever she wants."

The meeting happened on neutral ground, a diner two towns over that Xander picked specifically because it wasn't anywhere either family called home, and Willow spent the first ten minutes so carefully polite that Xander nearly laughed watching her restrain herself.

"You can ask your questions," Millie said, finally, amused. "I promise I won't bite. I've had four years less practice than your friend here at being underwhelmed by teenagers with big scientific ambitions, but I manage."

Willow visibly relaxed. "Okay. First question, and feel free to just say pass if it's too much — is the ability itself measurable? Like, is there a physical marker, something in the body that changes, or is it purely — I don't even know what the alternative would be. Purely something else?"

"Good question, and an honest answer is we don't fully know. There are theories. Some kind of neurological pathway that activates under specific conditions, most commonly extreme stress, though that's clearly not universal, given how it's shown up in people who never had that particular trigger."

"Extreme stress being the common trigger, though. That tracks with what Xander's told me about his own start."

"It does. It's the most consistent pattern we've found, for whatever that's worth." Millie studied Willow for a moment, something considering in her expression. "You're asking good questions. Careful ones. I appreciate that more than you probably realize."

"I've had a lot of practice being careful with things that matter," Willow said. "This clearly matters."

They talked for two hours, Willow's careful list of questions slowly giving way to genuine, easy conversation, and by the time they left, Millie had agreed to a second meeting, and Willow had a notebook full of careful, hedged, truly groundbreaking information that she promised, several times, completely unprompted, never to misuse.

"That went well," Xander said, once they were walking back to the car.

"That went incredibly well. Xander, she basically confirmed a theory I've had for months. This is — I don't even have words for how big this actually is."

"I'm glad. Genuinely. You've earned this one."

"We've earned this one," Willow corrected. "You're the one who asked. I just showed up with a notebook."

"A very good notebook."

"The best notebook," Willow agreed, and he kissed her temple as they walked, both of them settling into the easy, satisfied quiet of people who'd both gotten something they badly needed, in completely different ways, from the exact same afternoon.

99. Millie: Settling

Millie found Davy on the porch after Xander's third visit, watching the tree line with an expression she hadn't seen on him in longer than she could easily place — not worry, for once. Something closer to peace.

"You're not doing the face," she said, sitting down beside him.

"I don't have a face."

"You have several faces, and none of them are showing up tonight. It's unsettling."

"Should I manufacture one? For your comfort?"

"Please don't. I've earned this one quiet evening." She watched the same tree line he was watching. "He's good for Cent. You've seen that as clearly as I have."

"I have. Doesn't stop the part of me that's counting exits every time he jumps in."

"That part of you counts exits at the grocery store, Davy. It's not a Xander thing."

"Fair. Though in my defense, the grocery store has genuinely bad sightlines near the dairy case."

"You've said that. Repeatedly. To the manager, once, which I still apologize for on your behalf whenever I run into him."

He almost smiled at that. "He took it well."

"He did not take it well. He gave me a coupon and avoided eye contact for a month." She bumped his shoulder. "I like him too, you know. Xander."

"I know you do. You fed him within twenty minutes. That's your version of a background check."

"It's an excellent background check. Tells you everything about how somebody handles being given something they didn't ask for."

"And?"

"He passed. Obviously. You already know he passed, or you wouldn't be sitting out here looking like this instead of running your usual three-day surveillance protocol."

Davy huffed something that was almost a laugh. "I did consider the surveillance protocol."

"I know you did. I found your notes."

"They were good notes."

"They were unnecessary notes, and I burned them, and you're welcome." She took his hand anyway, same gesture, same meaning it always carried. "I think this one's good, Davy. One you don't have to spend years bracing against."

"I hope so." A beat. "I'll still probably watch the dairy case, though."

"Obviously. I'd worry if you didn't."

They sat there a while longer, no urgency to move, nothing waiting past the tree line for once, and Millie let herself enjoy exactly that — an evening with nothing to brace against, sitting next to a man who'd earned, however grudgingly, the right to just let something be good.

99.5. The Mastodon

It took Alan the better part of three weeks to actually assemble what he needed — a specialized crane rig borrowed from a rescue outfit two states over, a large-mammal veterinarian Millie's old contact had vouched for personally, a trailer reinforced well past anything the zoo had ever owned before, and an enclosure crew working double shifts on foundations that had to go in before anyone dared bring the animal home to them. None of it could actually cross with him — no jump was going to carry a crane truck, and the wildside didn't have so much as a dirt road to drive one in on regardless — so all of it stayed staged right here, waiting on whatever came back through the pull.

"This is the most expensive thing I've ever been involved in," Xander said, watching the crew run a final check on the crane before he and Buffy headed for the shed. "I want that on the record. Mayor Wilkins is gonna need a bigger plaque budget."

"Mayor Wilkins already approved a bigger plaque budget," Alan said, without looking up from his clipboard. "He said, and I quote, 'if the boy's found us a mammoth, he's earned a mammoth-sized plaque.'"

Dr. Adeyemi — the large-mammal vet, sharp-eyed and completely unbothered by the scale of what she'd signed up for — had spent most of the week drilling Xander on the sedation instead of doing it herself, since there was no version of this where she could come along and administer it in person: dosage, injection site, exactly how long to wait before it was safe to get a line on the animal, all of it rehearsed until he could recite it back half-asleep, working off estimates Millie had spent just as long refining against every scrap of data her father's old notes could offer.

They found the herd on the second day, grazing along the river bottom same as Xander had seen once before, and picked the oldest bull — solitary, drifting at the herd's edge the way older males sometimes did, Millie's read on the behavior matching what little data existed almost exactly.

The dart went in clean, and then came the longest, strangest hour of Xander's entire four years, watching a creature that size slowly, ponderously, inevitably lose the fight against sedation, the herd shifting nervously at a distance but never quite committing to intervening, until the old bull's legs finally folded and the ground itself seemed to exhale.

Getting the harness under it and the lines secured after that fell to Xander and Buffy alone, working off nothing but Dr. Adeyemi's drilling and a diagram folded into the pack, every remaining minute before the timer ran out going into it.

"We are never doing that again on this tight a schedule," Xander muttered, cinching the last strap down with barely any margin left before the pull took them.

The mastodon — or mammoth, the debate still technically unresolved even now, pending an actual specialist's final word — came through the pull sedated and secured, straight into a waiting crane sling the second the shed door opened back onto solid, ordinary ground.

"Vitals are good," Dr. Adeyemi called, already moving. "Get the rig locked in, let's get him properly settled before he starts coming out of it."

It took every hand available — Alan's crew, the crane operator, Xander and Buffy still hauling cable even after everything they'd just been through — to get the animal off the harness and into the trailer, the whole operation finishing with real margin to spare now that none of it was racing a jump-timer anymore.

"Worth every bit of the three weeks," Xander said, hoarse from shouting instructions over engine noise for the better part of an hour. "Look at him."

By the time the mastodon woke groggy and confused in a purpose-built enclosure far larger than anything the zoo had built before, it was totally unharmed.

"Mammoth or mastodon?" Willow asked Dr. Adeyemi, notebook ready, the second the animal's classification became a question anyone could actually answer with confidence.

"Give me a week with proper measurements," Dr. Adeyemi said, "and I'll write you an actual paper on it."

"I would like that paper so much."

"You alright?" Buffy asked, hip bumping Xander's as they watched the crew finish securing the enclosure gate, four years of solitary jumps having never once prepared him for logistics on quite this scale.

"Yeah. Just — that was a lot. In a good way." He let out a breath he felt like he'd been holding for three weeks straight. "Two down. One much more terrifying to go."

"The sabertooth," Buffy said, not a question.

"The sabertooth," he agreed. "Willow's already got a countdown going. I've seen it. It has a name."
