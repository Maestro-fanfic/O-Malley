Chapter 147

They worked through the night, and the next, and the one after that, the Magic Box slowly transforming into something closer to a war room than a magic shop — books and instruments and half-finished theories covering every available surface, four separate bodies of knowledge grinding slowly, painfully, toward something like a unified understanding. Joyce kept everyone fed with a steady rotation of sandwiches and coffee that nobody had time to properly thank her for, and Willow's carefully color-coded whiteboards had multiplied into an entire wall's worth of overlapping diagrams that only she and, increasingly, Ilsa seemed able to fully parse.

"The epicenter," Strange said, on the third night, staring at a projection of overlapping reality-signatures that made Xander's head hurt just looking at it, light patterns shifting and rippling across the shop's back wall like something between a star chart and a heat map. "Every reading we've gathered, from every source, points back to roughly the same point."

"Which is where, exactly?" Fury asked.

"Here," Ilsa said, quiet, setting down the instrument she'd been calibrating for the past hour. "Not metaphorically. Literally here. This town. Possibly this exact location."

The room went very still.

"That would explain a great deal," Giles said, slowly, removing his glasses in the gesture Xander had long since learned to read as genuine unease rather than mere thoughtfulness. "Why every disturbance, every anomaly, every arriving party has converged on Sunnydale specifically, rather than simply on this general reality."

"It would," Strange agreed. "Which means whatever's actually happening at the threshold isn't going to happen somewhere abstract, somewhere we can study from a safe distance. It's going to happen here. Possibly very close to where we're currently standing."

Xander felt something cold settle in his stomach, glancing instinctively toward the shop's front window, toward the ordinary street outside that had no idea what was about to potentially unfold around it. "So what do we actually do about it?"

Strange and Ilsa exchanged a long look, the kind that suggested they'd already been circling this exact question separately, arriving now at something they'd each hoped the other might have already solved.

"Containment," Ilsa said finally. "Not prevention — I don't believe prevention is possible at this stage, given how far the acceleration has already progressed. Containment. A localized reinforcement of the boundary, strong enough to hold even as the wider instability continues to build."

"Home's gate technology was built for controlled crossings," Miriam said, quiet, fatigue visible in the set of her shoulders after days of near-continuous work. "Not for reinforcing failing boundaries. I don't know if it can be adapted for this."

"It can, potentially, if paired with something else," Strange said. "Sorcery can reinforce boundaries directly, but not at this scale, not alone. We'd need an anchor. Something with genuine physical presence in multiple senses of the word — mystical, structural, and symbolic all at once."

Silence settled over the room as everyone turned the problem over privately, searching for whatever piece might complete it, the only sound the faint hum of Ilsa's instruments and the distant creak of the shop settling around them.

"The hammer," Giles said, quietly, into the silence.

Every eye in the room turned to him.

"Forged in a realm considerably more attuned to questions of worthiness and structural integrity than either sorcery or gate technology alone," Giles continued, working through the logic even as he spoke it aloud, gathering confidence with each sentence. "An object that already exists, quite literally, as a bridge between realities, having been sent here deliberately during a previous crisis. If anything in this room qualifies as an anchor capable of holding multiple systems together at once, I suspect it's sitting in Buffy's bedroom."

Thor, who'd been quiet through most of the discussion, sat up straighter, something shifting in his expression as the logic fell into place. "The hammer has held realities together before. Not at this scale, admittedly. But the principle is not unfamiliar to it. I have felt it stabilize far smaller tears, back on Asgard, during far lesser crises."

"And it would need someone to actually wield it," Strange said, "during whatever working we ultimately design. Someone the hammer has already judged worthy, ideally, given how little margin for error this leaves us."

Faith, who'd been sitting quietly at the edge of the room through most of the debrief, finally spoke, her voice steady despite the weight of every gaze suddenly turning toward her.

"I can do that."

Every eye turned to her this time.

"I lifted it," she said, simply. "A few weeks back. Didn't tell anybody outside the house, because it felt like mine to sit with first. But I can do it. If that's what this actually needs."

Thor studied her a long moment, something like genuine pride crossing his face, mixed with a father's worry he didn't quite bother concealing. "Then perhaps this crisis has already been preparing its own answer, longer than any of us realized."

"You sure about this?" Buffy asked, quiet, concern plain in her voice.

"No," Faith admitted, honest despite the fear it cost her to say it out loud. "But I'm sure enough to try. That's gotta count for something, given the timeline we're working with."

"It counts for everything," Ilsa said, gently. "Certainty is a luxury none of us have right now. Willingness matters much more."

Willow, quiet through most of this, finally spoke up, notebook already covered in fresh diagrams. "If we're pairing the hammer with the gate technology and Strange's sorcery, we need a working theory of how they actually interface. Three completely different systems, forced to function as one, in real time, under active strain."

"That is, admittedly, the piece I'm least confident about," Strange said. "I've never attempted a working quite like this."

"None of us have," Ilsa said. "That's rather the nature of an unprecedented crisis."

"Comforting," Xander muttered.

"I never promised comfort," Ilsa said, something almost gentle beneath the bluntness. "Only honesty, and the best plan we can actually assemble in the time we've got."

The room settled into a new kind of focus after that — not despair exactly, but the clear-eyed, determined energy of people who'd finally found the shape of an actual plan, however enormous the task ahead of them still remained, papers and instruments shifting once more as the real work of preparation properly began.
