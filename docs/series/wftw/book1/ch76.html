Chapter 76

The jump dropped him into forest, which wasn't unusual by itself — dense, temperate, tall pines mixed with hardwoods he half-recognized from home, undergrowth thick with fern and fallen needles that muffled his footsteps to almost nothing, the kind of terrain that could have belonged to a dozen different worlds he'd visited before. Birdsong somewhere overhead, unfamiliar in its exact notes but recognizable in its shape, the ordinary business of an ordinary morning in an ordinary patch of woods.

What was unusual was the fence.

He nearly walked past it before his brain fully registered what he was looking at — a stretch of wire strung between weathered wooden posts, running in a straight, deliberate line through the trees, the kind of thing that occurred in nature exactly never. He stopped mid-stride, one boot still half-raised, and simply stared at it for a long moment, waiting for his brain to produce some explanation that didn't require rewriting four years of certainty.

It didn't produce one.

"Okay," he said, quietly, feeling his pulse pick up in a way four years of empty worlds had never once required. "Okay, that's not right. That's really, really not right."

He crouched by the nearest post, close enough now to see the details — the wood weathered gray by seasons of sun and rain, grain split in places, a few strands of wire gone slightly rusted where moisture had pooled at old staple points. Not new. Not recent. Whoever had built this had built it some time ago, and had built it to last.

He touched it, finally, one careful finger against the wire itself, half expecting it to somehow not be real, to dissolve at contact the way dreams sometimes did when you pressed too hard against their logic. It didn't dissolve. It was simply wire, cold and solid and undeniably, physically there.

'People,' he thought, the word landing strange and enormous in his head. 'This world's got people. This world isn't empty.' Four years of assuming the rule — no humans, ever, not here, not anywhere he'd been sent — cracked open all at once, and he actually didn't know what to do with the size of that realization, crouched in the dirt with his hand still resting against a fence post that shouldn't have existed anywhere he'd ever been sent.

He stayed there a long time, longer than made any practical sense, just processing, before he finally made himself move — following the fence line at a careful distance, cautious in a way he hadn't needed to be in years, every sense straining for any sign of movement, any sound that wasn't wind or birdsong.

The fence ran for the better part of a mile before the trees finally broke, and what waited beyond them stole whatever breath he had left to lose.

A cleared field, plowed in neat, deliberate rows, crops he half-recognized growing green and orderly against dark turned earth. Further off, past the field's edge, a building — weathered wood siding, a tin roof catching the pale afternoon light, smoke-blackened stones suggesting a chimney somewhere on its far side. A second, smaller structure beside it that might have been a shed, or a barn, or something he didn't have a category for yet.

He didn't approach. Every instinct he'd built over four years of solitary trips screamed at him to observe from a careful distance rather than walk up to a stranger's home unannounced, especially on a world where he had no idea what kind of people lived here, what they'd think of a stranger appearing out of nowhere, or what tools they might have to deal with exactly that kind of intrusion. He found a fallen log at the tree line's edge, settled low behind it, and watched.

An hour passed. Then another. The field stayed empty. Smoke rose, thin and steady, from the chimney he'd guessed at, proof of a fire tended by someone, somewhere, without that someone ever once showing themselves. A dog barked, once, distant and muffled, from somewhere behind the house, and Xander went perfectly still for a full minute afterward, heart hammering, before deciding the sound hadn't been directed at him at all.

He photographed everything he could from his hiding spot — the field, the buildings, the fence line stretching off in both directions — hands steadier now than they'd been at first contact, four years of field discipline reasserting itself even through the sheer scale of what he was actually documenting.

'Willow's gonna lose her whole mind over this,' he thought, backing carefully away from the clearing once the light began to fail, retreating deeper into the trees before making camp well clear of the fence line, further than strictly necessary, close enough only to find his way back if morning brought him the nerve to return. 'This changes everything. Every assumption we've ever made about these worlds, this breaks it.'

He barely slept that first night, mind circling the same questions over and over — who lived there, how long they'd been there, whether they were like him, whether this was the first inhabited world he'd ever found or simply the first one he'd happened to notice signs of. Somewhere well past midnight, a light flickered on in the distant house — small, warm, unmistakably a window catching lamplight — and he lay there watching it for a long time before it finally went dark again, someone's ordinary evening ending in a home he had no right to be watching.

The second day he circled wider, cautious, mapping the fence line's full extent as best he could without ever coming close enough to be seen, and found, near a bend where the wire crossed a shallow creek, boot prints in the mud — recent, purposeful, heading toward the house rather than away from it. Someone had walked this exact stretch within the last day or two. Someone real, with a real, ordinary routine, utterly unaware that anyone else had ever set foot on ground they'd probably assumed, same as Xander once had, belonged to nobody at all.

The boot prints weren't the only tracks he found that day. Farther out from the fence line, where the forest thickened back into something wilder, he crossed a game trail churned up by prints too big and too wrong to be anything he recognized — deep, canine, but larger than any wolf print had any business being, several sets together, moving as a pack. Dire wolves, he'd guess, if he had to put a name to it, though guessing was all he had to go on out here. He photographed those too, hands considerably less steady than they'd been over the boot prints, and gave the trail a wide, deliberate berth for the rest of the afternoon.

The second sighting came an hour before dusk, distant enough across a break in the trees that he almost dismissed it as a trick of the light — a shape moving through a meadow at the valley's far side, gray-brown and simply, impossibly enormous, curved tusks catching the last of the sun before it ambled back into treeline shadow and was gone. Mammoth or mastodon, he really couldn't have said which even if someone had asked; he just knew it was the kind of thing that hadn't walked anywhere on his own Earth in ten thousand years. He stood frozen for a long moment after, camera still raised, uncertain he'd actually seen what he thought he'd seen until he checked the photo and confirmed his own eyes hadn't been lying to him.

The third he only found because he nearly stepped in it — a kill site, a day or two old, what looked like deer bones scattered wide across a small clearing in a way that spoke less of scavenging and more of something big enough to drag a full carcass without much effort, drag marks leading off into brush too dense to follow safely, deep punctures in what was left of the hide that no wolf jaw, dire or otherwise, could have made. He didn't linger there long. Something about the size of those punctures left him deeply uninterested in meeting whatever had made them in person.

'Willow's REALLY gonna lose her whole mind over this,' he thought, backing away from the kill site with significantly more urgency than he'd shown backing away from the farmhouse the night before.

He didn't get close enough to learn anything more before the jump pulled him home on the third evening, the whole trip passing without him ever working up the nerve to actually make contact, and he came back through with photos of a fence, a field, a distant building, a light in a window, boot prints in creek mud, wolf tracks too large to be wolves, something enormous and tusked at the edge of a meadow, and a kill site he still wasn't sure he wanted the full details on — evidence, undeniable and world-shaking, that somewhere out there, on at least one of these countless empty Earths, he was not, and perhaps had never truly been, the only person who'd ever set foot, and that whoever lived there shared their world with far more than farmland.

"You're pale," Buffy said, the second he walked through the door, none of her usual joking making it into her voice at the sight of his face. "What happened?"

"Nothing happened. That's not — nothing bad happened." He set his camera down with hands that weren't quite steady. "I need to call Willow. I need to call Willow right now."

"Xander, what did you find?"

He looked at her, the weight of it still settling, four years of certainty about how these worlds worked cracking open into something he didn't yet have words solid enough to hand her.

"People," he said finally. "I found people, Buffy. Or evidence of them, anyway. There's a fence. There's a farm. There's somebody living out there, and I have no idea who."
