Chapter 132

Buffy and Faith both came this time, all three of them wanting something ordinary before whatever Fury's visit turned out to bring the following week.

The world was prairie, endless grass rolling toward a horizon broken only by distant, scattered trees, wind carrying the smell of earth and growing things.

"Okay, this one's simple," Faith said, taking it in. "Simple's good right about now."

"Simple's great right about now," Buffy agreed. "I could use a few days of nothing complicated."

They spent the first two days working the grassland, cataloguing a herd of long-horned creatures that grazed without much concern for their presence, and found a den of small, burrowing animals on the third morning, popping up and vanishing at the sound of footsteps with a speed that had all three of them laughing within minutes.

"They're so fast," Faith said, delighted despite herself. "Didn't expect to be charmed by burrowing rodents today, but here we are."

"Life's full of surprises," Xander said. "That's basically the whole job description."

They spent the rest of the trip in easy, appreciative quiet, and by the time the jump pulled them home, all three of them felt lighter, steadier, better prepared for whatever the coming week actually held.

Faith caught Xander's hand at one point, the two of them just walking, tentative about it in a way that didn't match her usual easy confidence, glancing at Buffy like she was still checking whether this was actually allowed.

"You can, you know," Buffy said, catching the look, taking Faith's other hand herself without making a thing of it. "We talked about it. This is allowed."

"Still feels new. Gonna take me a minute to stop double-checking."

"Take all the minutes you need," Xander said, squeezing her hand once. "Nobody's in a hurry here."

Joyce was up marking something in her list when they came in, still holding hands, none of them quite ready to let go of it yet. She looked up, taking in all three of them at once — the held hands included — and didn't comment on it. "Good one?"

"Really good one," Buffy confirmed. "Prairie, funny rodents, zero drama. Exactly what we all needed before next week."

"I'm glad," Joyce said, and went back to her list, leaving them the doorway and whatever they wanted to do with it.

133. Cent: "Fury's coming in person?"

Xander told me the news the next time he made it back to the fence line. I could tell how much he was still processing it himself.

"Fury's coming to Sunnydale. Next week. Wants an actual meeting."

I sat down hard. "In person. Not a call, not a report review. Him. There."

"Yeah. Natasha says it's a good sign, actually. Says he's careful, not reckless."

"That's Natasha's read. Doesn't mean it's the right one."

"Fair. I'm choosing to trust her, though. She knows him better than any of us."

I told Dad that same evening. Watched him go very still — the stillness that means he's already running through every worst version of the story before he lets himself hope for a better one.

"In person," he repeated. "That's not standard protocol for a first contact. That's either genuine respect, or it's leverage-gathering dressed up as respect."

"Xander trusts Natasha's read on it."

"Xander hasn't spent thirty years learning what these people are capable of when they decide something's worth controlling instead of just understanding."

"I know, Dad. I'm not arguing that part. I'm just saying maybe this one's different."

He was quiet a long moment, turning it over. "Maybe. I want more information before deciding how worried to actually be."

"What kind of information?"

"Everything Natasha's willing to share about Fury specifically. His history. How he's handled situations like this before, if there's precedent." He met my eyes. "I'm not saying stop it. I'm saying I want us going in with real information, not just one woman's personal endorsement, however much you trust her."

"That's fair. I'll ask."

Mom, quiet through most of this, finally spoke. "Do you want to be there? For the meeting?"

I hadn't actually thought about it until she asked. "I don't know. Feels like it's Xander's thing to navigate, mostly."

"It's also your thing, Cent. You're the reason any of this connected in the first place."

I turned that over. "Maybe. I'll think about it."

"Take your time," Dad said. "Nothing about this needs deciding tonight."

I went to bed that night turning the whole thing over — Fury, the meeting, Dad's careful, earned caution, my own uncertain place in whatever came next. Big week coming. Bigger than any of us fully understood yet, probably.

Hoped we were ready for it. Wasn't fully sure we were.

133.5. Wong: The Sealed Note

Wong found the note by accident, the way most actually important discoveries at Kamar-Taj tended to surface — while searching the deep archive for something else completely, a minor ward-maintenance record from three centuries back, candlelight throwing long shadows across shelves that stretched further into the dark than any ordinary building's architecture should reasonably have allowed, and finding instead a small, warded envelope tucked behind it, sealed in a hand he recognized immediately even after all this time.

He almost set it aside. The archive held decades of the Ancient One's private notes, most of them mundane, catalogued and studied long ago by scholars far more patient than his current schedule allowed for — inventory lists, minor ward adjustments, the accumulated administrative residue of centuries spent maintaining a sanctum most of the world never knew existed.

Something about this one held his attention anyway. The seal, perhaps — heavier than her usual work, layered with wards that felt, even from a distance, much more deliberate than her typical filing habits suggested.

He broke the seal carefully, the wards dissolving at his touch easily, the way protections meant to open for exactly the right person did, rather than resisting everyone indefinitely, and read the single page twice before setting it down, feeling something cold settle in his chest that had nothing to do with the archive's usual chill.

Something reaches backward through the years, it read, her handwriting steady and precise despite whatever uncertainty the words themselves carried. I cannot yet see its source, only its shape. When it comes, know that it was felt long before it happened. Whether that was wisdom or cowardice, I leave for whoever reads this to judge for themselves.

He checked the note's dating against his own records — decades old, written well before the Ancient One's death, well before anyone had reason to suspect the kind of disturbance she was describing, a full generation of scholars passing through Kamar-Taj without ever once suspecting this particular truth sat waiting in the archive beneath their own feet.

'She felt this coming,' he thought, turning the implication over carefully, the candlelight flickering as some draft found its way through the ancient stonework. 'Whatever this is, she sensed it reaching backward through time itself, years before it actually happened.' It was, he had to admit, exactly the kind of foresight he'd come to expect from her, and exactly the kind of restraint too — choosing silence rather than a warning that might have done more harm than the waiting, a judgment call he found himself, even now, unable to fully agree or disagree with.

He brought it to Strange that same evening, finding him deep in his own research, several books open across the sanctum's main table, the Cloak of Levitation draped over the back of his chair like a patient, watchful presence.

"You should see this," Wong said, setting the note down without preamble.

Strange read it twice, the same way Wong had, his expression shifting from mild curiosity to something notably more focused by the second pass, one hand absently rubbing at the old scarring across his knuckles the way he always did when something demanded his full, uninterrupted attention.

"This is dated years before the Snap. Years before the whole time heist." Strange looked up, something sharpening behind his eyes. "You think this is connected to the instability we've been tracking? The strange readings coming off certain realities?"

"I think it would be a remarkable coincidence if it weren't."

"Yeah. I think so too." Strange set the note down carefully, staring at it like it might yield more if he simply looked hard enough, fingers drumming once against the table's edge. "We've had reports — vague, unconfirmed — of anomalous activity. A concentration of it, actually, around one specific reality. Small town. Unremarkable, by most measures, except for the readings."

"Unremarkable towns rarely produce readings worth this much attention."

"No. They don't." Strange met Wong's eyes. "I think it might be time we actually looked into this properly. Directly, rather than just monitoring from a distance. We've been cautious long enough. If she felt this coming decades ago and chose not to act, maybe the lesson isn't caution. Maybe it's that somebody eventually has to."

"I agree." Wong glanced at the note once more, something almost reverent in the gesture. "She felt this coming, decades before any of us understood what it would actually mean. Feels like the least we can do is finally go see what she was trying to warn us about."

"Agreed." Strange was already reaching for his cloak, which lifted itself expectantly off the chair back before he'd even fully extended his hand, something settling into focused purpose behind his eyes. "Let's go find out what's actually happening in this town of theirs."

"You're certain you don't want to gather more information first? Standard protocol would suggest reconnaissance before direct contact."

"Standard protocol also suggested we had years before this became urgent, and apparently we don't." Strange fastened the cloak's clasp, settling into the calm he always wore once a decision had really been made rather than merely considered. "Sometimes the fastest way to gather information is to just walk in and ask. I've got a feeling this is one of those times."

"Fair enough." Wong folded the letter carefully, tucking it into an inner pocket rather than returning it to the archive. "I'm coming with you. Somebody should keep you from asking questions in ways that get us both banned from a reality before we've even finished the introductions."

"That happened one time."

"It happened three times, and I was there for all three."

Strange didn't argue the point, already tracing the first lines of a portal into the air between them, gold light spilling out to fill the room, and the two of them stepped through together, leaving the archive quiet behind them, one more small mystery finally, properly set into motion after decades of patient, careful waiting.

133.6. Mom: The Reconnection

The controller had sat dormant in its hiding place for years, tucked behind a false panel in the barn that Dad had built specifically for it, back when hiding it had still felt necessary rather than just habit. Long enough that Mom had stopped expecting it to ever do anything again — long enough that finding it, these days, felt more like visiting a grave than checking equipment.

The morning it finally chimed with an incoming signal, she was halfway through her second cup of coffee, the house quiet around her, and her hands shook badly enough that she had to set the mug down before she could even reach for the device sitting in her lap.

Home. After all this time, actually Home.

She'd rehearsed this moment more times than she'd ever admitted to anyone, in the quiet years after Max vanished — turning the words over during long drives, during sleepless nights, during the ordinary domestic silences where her mind still, after everything, occasionally drifted back to a mission she'd told herself was over. None of her rehearsals had prepared her for how it would actually feel, sitting here now with the signal truly live for the first time in over a decade — old grief and old duty both surfacing at once, tangled together too tightly to easily separate.

She carried the controller out to the barn before answering, needing the privacy, needing the same walls that had held this secret for so many years to hold this first conversation too.

"This is Miriam," she said, voice steadier than she expected, though her free hand gripped the workbench edge hard enough to whiten her knuckles. "Formerly assigned to the tame-side observation post. Reporting in, considerably later than protocol allows."

The response took a moment, static clearing into a voice she hadn't heard in decades, careful and measured, an older man's voice she half-recognized despite the years. "Miriam. We'd marked your post as lost, following your brother's disappearance. This is... an unexpected relief."

"The gate's active again. My son found it, quite by accident. I'll explain the full circumstances properly, but I wanted to report in first, given what else I've observed since reconnecting."

"Go ahead."

She told them everything — the passenger pigeons, the contamination scare, the whole strange unraveling that had brought her family, finally, fully into the truth of who she actually was. And then, carefully, the readings she'd only recently started properly understanding: anomalous cross-path activity, far beyond anything the old monitoring protocols had ever accounted for, concentrated heavily around one particular corner of this world.

The silence on the other end stretched long enough that she wondered if the connection had dropped.

"Miriam," the voice said finally, careful in a new way now, "how confident are you in these readings?"

"Very. I've cross-referenced them against the old baseline data three times now. This isn't background noise. Something has changed, significantly, and recently."

Another long pause, longer this time, the kind of silence that carried its own weight even across an imperfect connection. "This matches disturbances we've been tracking independently, on our end. We believed them isolated to our own instruments. If you're seeing corresponding activity from the tame side, that suggests the scope is significantly larger than we'd initially assessed."

"I was afraid you'd say that."

"We need eyes on the ground. Directly. Given your history with the post, and given the family connections you've maintained despite the years of silence, you're the most logical liaison, if you're willing."

Mom closed her eyes, feeling the weight of the offer settle over her — the mission again, after all these years of believing it over, reopening at exactly the moment she'd finally, fully let herself belong to an ordinary life instead. Twenty years of careful distance, undone in the space of a single morning conversation.

"I'm willing," she said, "but I want my family with me. Fully informed. No more secrets, not after everything we've already been through because of the ones I kept before. If this is real, they deserve to know exactly what it costs, going in."

"That can be arranged. We'll send a team to coordinate with you directly. Given the scope of what you're describing, I don't think this is something we can afford to investigate lightly."

"Understood. How soon?"

"As soon as we can manage it. This is not a small concern, Miriam. If the readings are accurate, this may be the most significant anomaly we've encountered since the gate itself was first discovered."

The line went quiet after that, the conversation apparently concluded, and Mom held it a long moment in the barn's familiar dimness, the controller heavy in her hands, the weight of an old mission and a new, unprecedented crisis settling over her all at once.

She found Charlie in the kitchen a while later, still working through the same physics assignment he'd been staring at unproductively for the better part of an hour, and pulled out the chair across from him without preamble.

"I need to tell you something. Something big."

He set his pencil down, reading her face with an attention he'd gotten better at over the past year, ever since the truth about her had first come apart at the seams. "Home's back."

"Home's back," she confirmed. "And there's a problem. A real one. Bigger than anything we dealt with before."

"How big?"

"Big enough they're sending an actual team. Big enough I might need to go with them, at least for a while." She turned the controller over once in her hands. "It's the world where Davy's family is. Where you and Clara found the fence line that first summer."

Charlie went very still at that. "Is Cent's family okay?"

"I don't know yet. That's part of what I need to go find out."

He was quiet a moment, processing, and when he spoke again his voice carried more steadiness than she'd expected, or maybe than she deserved after everything. "You're telling me this time. Instead of just disappearing and letting me figure it out after."

"I'm telling you this time. All of it, as it happens, not after the fact." She reached across the table, covering his hand with hers, the same simple gesture she'd used his whole life for the things that actually mattered too much for words to carry properly. "I promised myself that much, at least, once the truth finally came out. You deserve better than secrets, even necessary ones."

"Even scary ones?"

"Especially scary ones. Those are the ones that matter most to actually share."

He managed something close to a smile at that, tired but real. "Okay. So tell me. All of it. Don't leave anything out this time."

She told him everything, start to finish, watching her son absorb news that would have unsettled most people twice his age, and found herself, not for the first time this past year, deeply, quietly proud of exactly who he'd turned out to be.

133.7. Home: The Decision

The Council met within the hour, which in itself told everyone present how seriously Miriam's report had been received — Home's leadership rarely moved quickly on anything, a habit born of decades spent rebuilding with more caution than speed, careful deliberation baked into every institution they'd rebuilt from a population once small enough to fit inside a single settlement. But this, apparently, warranted an exception, chairs still being pulled into place as Director Osei entered the low, unadorned chamber that served, these days, for meetings requiring genuine urgency.

"Twenty million," Director Osei said, without preamble, once the small group had assembled, the number landing with the weight it always carried in that room, spoken aloud rarely and never lightly. "That's what we came from. I don't need to remind anyone in this room what a single unchecked breach in the boundary between realities can cost, if we're careless about how we approach this."

"Nobody's suggesting carelessness," said Ilsa Kovač, their chief instrumentation officer, the one who'd first flagged the anomalous readings weeks earlier, tablet already open on the table in front of her, data scrolling steadily across its surface. "But we can't simply observe from a distance either. Whatever's happening on the tame side, it's accelerating. Every reading we've taken this month shows increased instability compared to the last."

"Which is precisely why I want to move carefully," Osei said. "We have exactly one confirmed asset with any history on that world, and she's been disconnected from us for over a decade. We don't know her current reliability, her family's current disposition, or what condition the tame side is actually in beyond what her single report describes."

"She sounded credible," said Amara, the youngest member present, still finding her footing in these discussions but unwilling to stay silent on this one, hands folded tight on the table in front of her. "Frightened, but credible. I don't think she'd exaggerate something like this. You could hear it in her voice — she wasn't performing urgency. She was carrying it."

"Fear doesn't guarantee accuracy. It guarantees urgency, which isn't the same thing." Osei considered the room a long moment, gaze moving across each face in turn, the weight of decades of careful leadership visible in the deliberate way she took her time before continuing. "That said, I agree we can't simply wait. Ilsa, you'll lead the team. Small. Two others, no more, given our current resource constraints. I want this handled with the absolute minimum footprint we can manage."

"Understood." Ilsa hesitated, glancing down at her tablet before looking back up. "Miriam offered to serve as liaison directly. Given her family's existing foothold there, it would ease the initial contact far."

"Use her. Carefully. She's earned back some trust, reconnecting on her own initiative rather than waiting to be found, but she's still an unknown quantity after this many years apart from the mission." Osei's expression softened, just slightly, something almost sympathetic crossing her otherwise composed features. "We are not the civilization we once were, before the collapse. We cannot afford to treat this lightly, and we cannot afford to overextend ourselves chasing every anomaly with the same resources we once had to spare. Choose your team well. Move carefully. And Ilsa — if this proves as significant as the readings suggest, do not hesitate to request more support. Pride cost us dearly once already. I won't have it cost us again."

"Understood," Ilsa said again, quieter this time, the weight of the assignment settling properly now that the meeting's urgency had given way to its actual stakes.

Amara spoke up once more, hesitant. "What if it's worse than the readings suggest? What if three people isn't enough?"

"Then Ilsa requests more, and we send more," Osei said, simply. "We are cautious. We are not paralyzed. There is a difference, and I intend for this Council to remember it, whatever comes of this mission."

The room settled at that, something like resolve replacing the earlier tension, and Osei rose, signaling the meeting's end with the same economy she brought to everything.

The Council dispersed shortly after, the decision made, the team already beginning preparations for a journey none of them had ever expected to make — back to a world most of Home's current generation knew only through old records and half-remembered stories, now apparently sitting at the center of something none of them yet fully understood, a small, careful delegation preparing to step, for the first time in over a decade, back into a reality their own history had taught them, above all else, to approach with the deepest possible respect.
