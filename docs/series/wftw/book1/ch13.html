Chapter 13

The jump always felt the same way, which Xander had decided a long time ago was either the least or the most reassuring thing about it, depending on his mood — one second standing inside the cone-marked square behind the football field lacing up his boots, the next standing in the exact same spot except the cones were gone, and Sunnydale was gone, and the only sound anywhere was wind moving through grass that had never once been mowed by anybody. Same ten-foot patch of ground the Mayor had marked off years before any of this started, security detail rotating through in shifts nobody talked about openly, everybody at school having long since learned not to ask too many questions about the cones that lived permanently behind the bleachers. Buffy made a point of being there for the return whenever her schedule allowed it, which by now was most weeks — the timing predictable enough, even if the destination never was, that she could plan around it the same way she'd plan around anything else that mattered to her.

"Okay," he said out loud, mostly out of habit, because talking to himself was the closest thing to company he got for the next three days. "Let's see what we've got this time."

This time turned out to be grassland, rolling out flat and gold in every direction, a tree line so far off it looked more like a rumor than an actual landmark. No roads. No fences. No contrails overhead, which still got him every single time — a whole sky with nothing man-made in it, not even the memory of a plane.

He checked the pack out of habit before he did anything else — the Mayor's pack, technically, though four years in it had stopped feeling like borrowed gear and started feeling like just his: bigger inside than any bag had a right to be, deep enough that he'd really stopped being surprised by what turned up in it when he actually needed something specific, like it understood the assignment better than he did some days. Water purification tablets, the machete that had technically started life as Buffy's before quietly becoming his, three days of compressed food, the satellite-phone-shaped brick that didn't actually get signal here but made him feel better to carry anyway, his own personal water reservoir for drinking, the collapsible fish tank Alan had insisted on after the second fish delivery went badly enough that nobody wanted to repeat the experience, the tranquilizer pistol the Mayor had insisted on ever since a very memorable afternoon with a twelve-foot crocodile and an unattended backpack, the bear mace that had been Joyce's one non-negotiable addition after the incident with the bear he still refused to fully describe to anyone at the dinner table, the air horn that had followed a week later once Alan pointed out mace only worked at a distance he'd apparently gotten uncomfortably good at closing, and — folded down small enough to forget it was even in there until he needed it — the collapsible shed, packed flat since it was a solo trip this time and he didn't feel like hauling it out for one guy and a sleeping bag. Everything present. Everything exactly where he'd packed it eighteen hours ago in a bedroom that currently didn't exist as far as he was concerned.

The grass came up to his knees, warm from a sun that looked the same as his own sun, because as far as anyone had ever been able to figure out, this was his own Earth — just a version of it with the "people" setting switched off sometime a very long while back, judging by how thoroughly nature had reclaimed everything. No ruins. No roads under the grass. Whatever this world's history had been, it hadn't included humans doing much of anything to it, which was either a comforting thought or a deeply unsettling one, and Xander had long since stopped trying to decide which.

He walked for two hours before he saw anything worth walking toward — a smudge of trees that turned out, on approach, to be the edge of a much larger woodland than the grassland had let on, and it was in the transition zone between grass and trees that he found the tracks.

"Okay, that's a new one."

The prints were deep, four-toed, each one bigger than his own boot laid flat, and they were fresh enough that the grass hadn't fully sprung back up around the edges. He crouched down, running a finger along the impression without quite touching it, doing the math on stride length the way four years of this had taught him to.

"Big," he said, to nobody. "Big and not in a hurry, based on the spacing." He glanced up at the tree line, suddenly a great deal more interested in what might be standing just inside it, watching him do geometry homework in the dirt. "Cool. Cool cool cool. Big mystery animal, zero backup, day one. Very normal start to the weekend."

He followed the tracks for another twenty minutes, more out of professional curiosity than good judgment, before the trees finally thinned enough to show him what had made them.

It was enormous — bigger than any sloth he'd ever seen in a zoo textbook, easily the size of a small car, moving through the underbrush with a slow, deliberate patience that suggested nothing in this entire empty world had ever once given it a reason to hurry. Shaggy brown fur, claws long enough to tear open a tree trunk like wet cardboard, which it was, in fact, currently doing, peeling bark off a fallen log in search of something underneath.

"Oh, you're real," Xander breathed. "You're actually real. I've been telling people about you for weeks and I wasn't sure you were real."

The sloth — because that was clearly what it was, some giant prehistoric cousin of the little rainforest kind, extinct on his own Earth for ten thousand years and apparently just fine here — didn't so much as glance up at him. It kept working the log, completely unbothered, the same unhurried confidence of something that had never once needed to worry about predators.

He got the camera out with hands that weren't quite steady, snapped four photos before he trusted himself to breathe normally again, and spent a long moment just watching it work, feeling the same quiet awe that never really wore off no matter how many weekends he'd done this.

'Nobody's gonna believe this without the photos,' he thought. 'Not even with the photos, probably. Willow's gonna want measurements. Willow always wants measurements.'

He got those too, pacing off the length of the log it had partially destroyed, estimating height against a nearby sapling, filling half a notebook page with the kind of amateur field notes that would've gotten him laughed out of any actual biology program and would absolutely make Willow's entire week regardless.

The sloth finally looked up around the twenty-minute mark, small dark eyes finding him without any specific alarm, sniffed once in his general direction, and went back to the log, apparently deciding he wasn't worth the effort of caring about.

"Yeah, that's fair," Xander said. "I wouldn't be scared of me either."

He didn't try for it today. Even wanting to — and some small, ambitious part of him absolutely did — a creature that size startled by a dart fired from too far off, or hit with a guess instead of an actual read on its size, was more than one guy alone in an empty world wanted to gamble on, claws that could open a log like wet cardboard doing far worse to a person standing too close when an undersedated animal that big realized something was wrong. Photos and measurements were the safe win today, and the measurements mattered for more than Willow's spreadsheet — no vet on his own Earth had ever dosed anything this size, and guessing wrong in either direction was its own kind of dangerous. He made camp that night close enough to keep an eye on the tree line, and lay awake longer than usual turning the actual math of it over.

'Random jumps,' he thought, the old fact sitting heavier tonight than it usually did. 'No pattern. Willow said it herself, years ago — I don't land in the same place twice. Whatever this world is, wherever it sits in however many worlds are actually out there, I've got no guarantee I ever find it again. Might be tonight's the only shot I ever get.' He turned that over a long while, the sloth's slow, unhurried shape settled somewhere in the dark beyond the firelight, entirely unbothered by his presence the same way everything here always was. 'Small birds, I can grab and go, no thinking required. This is different. This actually needs deciding.'

He spent the next two days doing exactly what his own checklist told him to — getting closer, patient and unhurried the same way the sloth itself moved through the world, letting it get so thoroughly used to him being nearby that its idle, disinterested sniff in his direction stopped happening altogether, like he'd simply become one more unremarkable feature of its territory. It wasn't about getting close enough to touch — it was about getting close enough for a shot he actually trusted, and about the two full days of watching it move, feeding, breathing, stretching that shaggy bulk against the log, that let him talk himself into an actual number for the dart instead of a guess. By the third morning he could sit an arm's length away while it worked a fallen log, close enough to be sure of the shot and sure enough of its size to commit to a dose, and by early afternoon, watching the light shift toward the hour he knew, from four years of practice, meant the pull was close, he made the actual call.

The first dart went in clean, high on the shoulder where the fur was thickest, and got him a startled bellow and a lumbering half-turn that had his heart in his throat for a full ten seconds before the sloth decided, with the same unbothered patience it brought to everything else, that whatever had just happened wasn't worth the effort of investigating. It took a second dart twenty minutes later, his hands much less steady reloading than they'd been the first time, before its movements finally started to slow, blinking heavier, legs folding under it in stages until it eased into the underbrush like something deflating, breathing slow and even and thoroughly, blessedly unconscious.

He gave it another ten minutes before he trusted it enough to actually approach, one hand finally resting flat against its shaggy flank, feeling the slow rhythm of drugged breathing under his palm. Timing was going to have to be exact now — close enough to touch when the pull came, and no margin left to wait it out if he'd read the clock wrong. 'It's not going to panic. It's not going to do anything except keep sleeping, however this goes.' Worst case, still a lot of very confused waking up to do on the other side, and he'd have Buffy and half a house full of people who could handle that notably better than he could handle never finding this place again.

The pull took him without warning, the way it always did, and this time it took the sloth too — one disoriented, half-conscious groan and shifted ground, claws twitching uselessly against grass that had, in the space between one heartbeat and the next, become the cone-marked square behind the football field, and then very much became a problem for several people who had absolutely not been expecting one.

13.5. Faith's Entrance

The Sunnyview Motel sat on the wrong end of the highway, the kind of place that rented rooms by the week to people who didn't want anyone asking questions about why, and Joyce Summers had never once had a reason to know it existed until Giles gave her the address with a carefulness that told her exactly how bad the situation actually was.

"Her Watcher is dead," he'd said, over the phone, voice tight in a way Joyce had learned to take seriously. "Killed three weeks ago. The girl ran. The Council's tracking system finally caught up with her here, and I'm told she's refusing all contact with anyone official."

"And you're telling me this because?"

"Because I don't think official is what she needs right now, Joyce. I think she needs somebody who isn't going to hand her a clipboard and a briefing packet."

Joyce had weighed that a long moment before agreeing to go, mostly because she couldn't think of a single good reason to say no, and several bad ones for saying yes anyway.

The room, when the manager finally let her up, smelled like old carpet and cheap disinfectant, one window with the blinds bent permanently askew, a bed that had clearly seen better decades. The girl on it barely looked up when the door opened — dark hair, sharper cheekbones than a girl her age should have, the kind of stillness that came from someone who'd trained themselves not to react first and ask questions later.

"Room's paid through Thursday," the girl said, flat, not quite a warning. "Whatever you're selling, I'm not buying."

"I'm not selling anything." Joyce stayed in the doorway, deliberately not crowding the space. "My name's Joyce. I know your Watcher passed. I'm sorry."

Something flickered across the girl's face, gone fast. "Everybody's sorry. Sorry doesn't fix dead."

"No. It doesn't." Joyce considered her next words carefully. "I'm not here on behalf of anybody official. I'm here because a friend of mine was worried about you, and because nobody should be doing this alone in a room like this."

"I've done plenty alone. I'm good at it."

"I believe you. Doesn't mean you have to keep proving it to strangers in doorways." Joyce glanced at the room properly for the first time — a single duffel bag, half-unpacked like the girl hadn't decided whether she was staying, a stake resting within easy reach on the nightstand. "What's your name?"

A long pause. "Faith."

"Faith. I have a spare room, Faith. Actual walls that don't have a highway on the other side of them. No strings, no clipboard, no Council paperwork waiting on the kitchen table."

"Why would you do that? You don't know me."

"I know you're seventeen, alone, and grieving in a motel that charges by the week. That's enough to know for now." Joyce kept her voice even, patient. "You don't have to decide tonight. But the offer's there, and it'll still be there tomorrow if you need a day to think about it."

Faith studied her for a long moment, the same flat, assessing look she'd probably learned to survive with. "You're serious."

"Completely."

"People don't just do that. Not for nobody."

"I'm not most people, apparently." Joyce allowed herself a small smile. "Also, for what it's worth, I make excellent chili, and I've been told my spare room has considerably better blinds."

Something that might have been the ghost of a laugh crossed Faith's face before she caught it and put it away again. "This some kinda test?"

"No test. Just an offer, open as long as you need it open."

Faith looked at the duffel bag, half-packed, half a life crammed into it, and then back at Joyce, something calculating and exhausted both at once behind her eyes. "I'll think about it."

"You can think about it in the car." Joyce said it lightly, but there was no give in it at all, the same tone she used on Buffy when curfew truly wasn't negotiable.

Faith's whole posture shifted, wariness sharpening into something closer to alarm. "Excuse me?"

"I'm not leaving you in this room. I've seen the manager downstairs. I've seen this hallway. I'm not driving home and spending the next three days wondering if you're still breathing." Joyce kept her voice even, but she didn't move from the doorway either, and Faith clearly clocked that the exit was, for the moment, not really an exit. "You don't have to unpack anything tonight. You don't have to stay past breakfast if you hate it. But you're not staying in this room another night, and I'd rather you walk out under your own power than make this harder than it needs to be for both of us."

"You don't get to just decide that."

"I'm aware it's not really my call to make for you. I'm making it anyway, because somebody has to, and right now that somebody's me." Joyce didn't raise her voice. She didn't need to. "You can be furious with me the whole drive over. I'll survive it."

"This is insane. You don't even know me."

"I know enough. Grab the bag, Faith."

Faith stood there a long moment, jaw tight, every instinct clearly screaming at her to dig in — and something else, quieter, apparently losing that particular argument, because she grabbed the bag off the bed with more force than it required and shouldered past Joyce into the hallway without another word, radiating fury the entire way down the stairs.

She didn't speak in the car. Joyce didn't push her to. The silence held all the way to the house, Faith staring out the window with her arms crossed, jaw set, looking for all the world like a girl being kidnapped rather than rescued.

"For the record," Joyce said, pulling into the driveway, "I know you're angry. You're allowed to be. I made a decision for you that wasn't mine to make, and I did it anyway, and I'd do it again."

"Then why bother apologizing."

"I'm not apologizing. I'm acknowledging it. Different thing." Joyce cut the engine. "There's a spare room upstairs. Door locks from the inside, if that matters to you. Bathroom's down the hall. I'll be up for a while if you need anything, and I won't be offended if you don't."

Faith didn't answer, just got out of the car, bag over one shoulder, and followed her inside — stiff, furious, clearly still undecided about whether this counted as a defeat.

It took her the better part of a week to stop looking at every closed door like it might lock behind her. It took significantly longer than that to stop being angry about the ride over, though the anger gradually thinned into something closer, eventually, to a grudging, unspoken gratitude she'd never once said out loud, not directly, not even months later.

13.6. The Sloth Situation

Buffy was between him and the sloth before Xander had even finished stumbling from the landing, Slayer reflexes doing what the two security guards stationed at the cones hadn't quite managed yet, both of them still fumbling their radios instead of their rifles, though the sloth itself wasn't going anywhere — a dead weight sprawled half across the marked square, sides rising and falling in the same slow, drugged rhythm it had carried since the second dart, totally unbothered by its sudden change of universe the same way it had been unbothered by everything else in its life.

"Xander." Buffy's voice stayed admirably level, all things considered, stepping carefully around a car-sized animal that had, until several seconds ago, not been part of her Tuesday. "Why is there an unconscious car-sized prehistoric animal on the football field."

"In my defense, it's very sedated," Xander said, already dialing before he'd finished the sentence. "Two darts, should be out for a while yet, but I want Alan's actual guess on the timeline before anybody gets close. Also give me a second, I'm calling Alan."

Alan Finch answered on the second ring, calm and immediate, like a man who had, apparently, spent four years quietly expecting a call exactly like this one.

"How large," he said, no preamble.

"Car-sized. Small car. Two darts of the standard mix, guessed the weight off three days of watching it move — it's down, but I don't actually know how long down."

"Understood. Don't approach it further, don't let anyone corner it if it starts to stir, and give me twenty minutes." A pause, papers shuffling audibly on his end. "Small mercy, son — I've had a protocol drafted for this exact scenario since roughly the second year of this arrangement. Mayor Wilkins insisted. Said your luck couldn't possibly hold indefinitely against something proportionally larger than the birds."

Willow arrived at a dead sprint across the field, having been camped out at the edge of the cones same as most weeks lately, notebook still clutched in one hand, and skidded to a stop at the sight of it with an expression somewhere between alarm and pure, unfiltered scientific joy.

"That's the sloth," she said. "That's THE sloth. You actually brought back the sloth."

"I actually brought back the sloth."

"Why didn't you warn anybody?"

"Because I decided about four seconds before the jump actually happened, and there wasn't exactly a window for a heads-up text." Xander kept his distance same as Alan had said, watching the sloth's ribs rise and fall with zero apparent awareness that it had just crossed into an utterly different universe. "Also I wasn't sure it was gonna work. Random jumps, remember? Wasn't sure I'd ever see that thing again if I didn't just — go for it."

"You could've been hurt. Guessing a dose blind on something nobody's ever darted before."

"Could've. Wasn't, though. Took two days of just watching it before I trusted the number enough to actually pull the trigger. It's honestly the chillest animal I've ever met, which given some of the stuff I've met, is really saying something — didn't even startle much off the first dart."

Alan arrived far faster than twenty minutes, tranquilizer rifle riding in the truck bed same as it had for years now — standard equipment for the job, the same way a first aid kit lived in most people's cars — a transport trailer and two handlers following close behind him, the whole convoy pulling straight onto school grounds through a service gate that had clearly seen this exact routine before. The rifle went unused, Xander's own two darts having already done the job; Alan spent most of the visit checking breathing and reflexes instead, professionally impressed despite himself that the dosage had landed as close to right as it had.

Buffy studied the whole efficient operation with grudging, reluctant awe. "Okay. That's either extremely reassuring or extremely unsettling, that you people had an actual plan ready for this."

"Both," Alan said, cheerfully. "Mostly both."

The eventual transfer became, within the week, the single proudest addition the zoo had ever received, a plaque already being drafted before the enclosure was even finished.

"Four years," Mayor Wilkins said, beaming at the sloth like a man who considered this the highlight of his entire tenure, "and this is, without question, the crown jewel of the whole operation. Well done, son. Genuinely well done. Alan, tell me the contingency protocol worked as smoothly as I'm hearing."

"Smoother, actually. I may have overbuilt it."

"There's no such thing, with this boy's track record."

Xander, watching the sloth settle into an enclosure much larger than anything else the zoo had ever built, felt something warm and satisfied settle into place that had nothing to do with the danger of the day and everything to do with finally, properly finishing a conversation he'd started weeks ago.

"Worth it," he said, to nobody in certain.

"Definitely worth it," Willow agreed, already three pages deep into notes she'd be revising for months.
