Chapter 35

The coastline stretched in both directions further than any of them could see, gray-blue water meeting gray-blue sky at a horizon so clean it looked drawn with a ruler, and for the first few minutes all three of them just stood there, letting the sound of waves replace the sound of Sunnydale in their heads the way it always took a moment to do.

"Okay," Xander said, breathing in air that tasted like salt and nothing else. "This is new. Never had ocean before."

"This is exactly what we needed," Buffy said, already pulling her boots off to feel the sand properly. "After the ants. After the mud. We needed a nice one."

"Don't jinx it," Willow warned, though she was already crouching by the first tide pool, delighted.

They spent the first day walking the shoreline together, cataloguing tide pools with the careful attention Willow brought to everything — strange crustaceans in colors none of them had seen on anything back home, shells in shapes that belonged to no family anyone recognized, a whole ecosystem going about its business in six inches of water without a single human footprint disturbing any of it before theirs. Xander filled a corner of the fish tank with a few of the more vividly colored ones before they moved on, muscle memory from three years of Alan's requests kicking in before he'd even finished deciding to do it.

Buffy walked between them for a stretch, one hand in Xander's and the other looped through Willow's arm, all three of them content to just exist in the quiet together, no urgency to the pace at all.

The seals showed up on day two, hauled out on a rocky outcrop a few hundred yards down the beach, easily twice the size of anything they'd expect to see back home, mottled gray and utterly unbothered by their approach.

"They're enormous," Buffy whispered. "Genuinely enormous. I did not expect enormous today."

One of them lifted its head, regarded all three of them with the same flat, patient curiosity as everything else out here, and went back to sunning itself, apparently deciding none of them were worth the effort of actual alarm.

They watched a long while from the rocks, Buffy settled sideways across Xander's lap with her legs over Willow's, none of them in any hurry to move, the three of them tangled together in the unhurried, unselfconscious way that had stopped needing comment months ago.

"This is going in its own section," Willow said, already sketching furiously despite the awkward angle. "Ocean ecosystem. That's a whole different resource category than anything we've cataloged so far."

"Told you it'd be worth bringing you both this time," Xander said.

They spent the rest of the trip exploring the tide line further out than any of them usually wandered, buoyed by how gentle this certain world seemed to be — no ants, no bees, no hillsides with apparent personal vendettas, just quiet coastline and creatures too unbothered by their presence to cause any trouble.

It was, by the time the jump pulled them home, one of the easiest trips any of them had had in months, all three of them coming back smelling like salt water instead of mud or sweat or antiseptic, a full memory card of seal photos, a fish tank considerably more crowded than it had been three days earlier, and a quiet satisfaction that came from a weekend that hadn't once required anyone to run from anything.

"No stories this time?" Joyce asked, checking all three of them over out of habit the second they walked through the door.

"No stories. Genuinely peaceful," Xander said. "There's an ocean out there, completely untouched, full of seals the size of couches."

"That sounds nice."

"It was extremely nice," Willow said. "I almost don't know what to do with a nice weekend. I think we've all forgotten how they work."

"You'll adjust," Joyce said with a grin. "Give it a few days."

"Please don't say it like that," Buffy said. "That sounded like a threat."

"It wasn't a threat. It was an observation."

"Feels like a threat wearing an observation's clothes," Xander said, and Joyce just smiled, already heading for the kitchen, unbothered by any of it, the way she'd long since learned to be.

35.5. Harry: Nine Years

Gib called at 6:14 in the morning, which told me before he'd said a single word that it wasn't nothing. Gib doesn't call at 6:14 for nothing. Gib barely calls at all anymore, not since the trail went cold the third time and we all quietly stopped pretending we still had momentum.

"Say it fast," I told him, already sitting up, already reaching for the pen I keep on the nightstand for exactly this kind of call.

"Facial match. Ninety-four percent confidence, small-town traffic camera, place called Sunnydale, California. Girl, seventeen, eighteen. Goes by Faith now, apparently, not the name we've been running." A pause, the kind Gib only does when he's bracing me for something. "Harry, it's her. I'd stake nine years of my own credibility on it. It's her."

I didn't say anything for a second. Couldn't, actually. Nine years builds a particular kind of scar tissue over hope, the kind where you learn not to let yourself feel the first jolt of it too hard, because you've felt that jolt collapse under you before, more than once, and each time it collapsed it took a little more of you down with it.

"You're sure," I said anyway, because some habits don't care what your scar tissue's telling you to do.

"As sure as ninety-four percent gets. I've got school enrollment records, Harry. I've got a library card. I've got a girl who's been living an entire ordinary life in an entire ordinary town for the better part of a year, and nobody who's looked for her ever thought to check because nobody expected her to just — settle. Live somewhere. Have a library card."

Helen was already awake beside me by then, some instinct of her own picking up on the shape of the call before I'd said a word to her directly, sitting up slow and careful like she already half-knew and was afraid to let herself know the rest of it.

"Harry."

"It's her," I said, and watched nine years leave her face all at once, replaced by something I hadn't seen on her in so long I'd honestly started to forget what it looked like.

"Where," she said, already moving, already reaching for the go-bag we've both kept packed by the closet door for longer than either of us liked to admit out loud.

"Sunnydale, California. Small town. Nothing about it should mean anything, and I've got a horrible feeling it's gonna mean everything anyway, because that's how our luck's always run."

"I don't care how it runs. I care that we're in the air within the hour." Helen was already dialing before she'd finished the sentence, that specific voice she uses for people who work for us but don't know they should be scared of her yet. "Marcus. Yes. I need the plane fueled and a flight plan filed for California in the next forty minutes, and I don't want to hear a single word about how that's not enough notice."

I called Gib back while she worked. "Anything else. Anything at all. Who's she with, is she safe, is there any read on danger before we walk into whatever this is."

"Working on it. Give me two hours and I'll have you a full picture before you land." A pause, and I could already hear it coming before he said it, same as I always could. "And don't even think about telling me I'm sitting this one out."

"Wasn't gonna say a word."

"Good, because you'd have been wasting your breath. Nine years I've watched you two carry this. I'm not finding out how it ends from a phone call in my kitchen." Something shifted in his voice, dropped the usual bluster down to something quieter underneath it. "She's alive, Harry. Whatever else this turns out to be, she's alive, and she's been alive this whole time. Start there. I'll meet you at the hangar in thirty minutes, and I swear if that plane leaves without me I will never let either of you hear the end of it."

I let myself have that for exactly one full breath, standing in a bedroom I'd shared with Helen through nine years of a hole neither of us had ever managed to properly fill, one full breath of just — alive — before the rest of the machinery kicked back in, because that's what we do, that's what we've always done, feel it for one breath and then move, because moving is the only thing that's ever actually gotten either of us anywhere.

"Pack light," Helen said, already three drawers deep into exactly that. "We can buy whatever else we need once we're there. I am not losing an hour to a suitcase."

"Wouldn't dream of slowing you down."

"You'd better not." She stopped, just for a second, hand braced against the dresser, something raw crossing her face before she visibly, deliberately packed it back down where she could use it later instead of right now. "Nine years, Harry."

"Nine years," I agreed. "And we're done waiting on the tenth."

We were in the air in fifty-eight minutes, Gib making the hangar with four minutes to spare and a travel mug of coffee he hadn't had time to actually drink, dropping into the seat across from us already scrolling through whatever fresh intel had come in on his phone during the drive over. Helen had the pilot doing math on headwinds before we'd even finished the safety briefing, and I sat there running Gib's ninety-four percent over and over in my head, trying to build myself a version of California I could actually walk into steady, instead of the version currently threatening to come apart at the seams the second we touched down.

Nine years. A library card. A whole ordinary life we knew absolutely nothing about.

I'd never wanted anything less like combat prep in my entire career, and never worked harder at holding myself together for one anyway.
