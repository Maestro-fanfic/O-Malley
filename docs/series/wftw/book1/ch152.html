Chapter 152

The departures happened gradually, over the following week, each one carrying its own specific weight of goodbye, the Magic Box slowly emptying of instruments and books and half-finished diagrams as the household eased, day by day, back toward something resembling its ordinary rhythm.

Fury left first, with characteristically little ceremony, his unremarkable sedan waiting exactly where he'd left it a week earlier. "I'll be in touch," he told Giles, in the doorway of the Magic Box, tone giving away nothing and everything at once. "This isn't the kind of thing that stays quiet forever, even contained. But you people handled this better than most professionals I've worked with. That's not nothing."

"High praise, coming from you," Giles said.

"Don't get used to it," Fury said, though something almost warm flickered behind the flat delivery. He paused at the door, something more careful crossing his expression. "One more thing. That report's classified at the highest level I've got access to grant. Doesn't mean it stays that way forever. Keep your guard up, even with this one behind you. I've learned the hard way that the people who shouldn't know things have a way of finding out anyway."

"Understood," Giles said, something in his tone suggesting he'd take the warning seriously rather than as mere parting caution.

Fury nodded once, and left without further sentiment, exactly as he'd arrived.

Strange and Wong lingered a day longer, Strange spending most of it in quiet conversation with Giles about the deeper implications of what they'd all just witnessed, the two of them cross-referencing notes late into their final evening with the easy rhythm of colleagues who'd genuinely enjoyed the collaboration despite its terrifying circumstances.

"The Ancient One would have wanted to know this resolved as well as it did," Strange said, on their last evening. "I intend to make sure her note gets properly annotated, for whoever reads it next. A warning that led, eventually, to an actual answer."

"I'd be glad to contribute what we've learned here," Giles offered. "If it would help future scholars facing something similar."

"It would. Considerably." Strange extended a hand. "You did good work, Giles. All of you did."

"We should come back sometime," Wong added, unexpectedly. "Under significantly less dire circumstances, preferably. I'd be curious to see this shop when it isn't functioning as a war room."

"You're welcome anytime," Giles said, and meant it.

They left through a portal far less startling than their arrival had been, everyone having grown accustomed enough to golden light appearing out of nowhere that barely anyone flinched this time, Willow waving with genuine enthusiasm right up until the light sealed shut behind them.

Home's team stayed longest, Ilsa insisting on thorough final readings before she'd fully trust the containment, days of careful, methodical checking that left her visibly, finally satisfied by the end of it. Miriam lingered even after that, wanting time with Cent that the crisis had, understandably, crowded out, the two of them spending long hours together that Xander mostly gave them privacy for, catching only fragments through open windows — laughter, once, and later, something quieter that sounded like relief, two people who'd found unexpected common ground finally, properly catching up.

"I'm proud of you," she told Cent, the night before Xander took her back through the jump, both of them sitting together on the porch steps, stars just beginning to show overhead. "Of all of you. This could have gone so much worse, and it didn't, largely because people who'd never have found each other otherwise chose to trust one another instead of standing apart."

"You okay? Going back? After everything?"

"I will be. It's strange, reconnecting with Home after so many years believing that part of my life was over. But I think it's a good strange. I think I needed this, in ways I hadn't let myself admit."

"Will you keep in touch? With everyone here?"

"I intend to. This connection matters too much now to let it lapse again." Miriam hugged her tight, holding on a beat longer than usual. "Tell Xander I'm grateful. For all of it. For finding your family in the first place, all those months ago, without which none of this would have come together the way it did."

"I will."

Ilsa and her team left the following morning, the gate closing behind them with a quiet finality that felt, to everyone watching, considerably less dramatic than the crisis that had first brought them here, Petra and Dov both offering warm, genuine goodbyes before stepping through, Dov's recording device still clutched in one hand as though reluctant to stop documenting even in this final moment.

"Reach out if anything changes," Ilsa told Xander, in her last moments before crossing back. "We'll be watching more closely now. Properly, this time, not from decades of disconnected silence."

"I will. Thank you. Genuinely. For coming, for the team, for everything."

"Thank you for being worth coming for," she said, and stepped through, the gate sealing quiet behind her, the woods settling back into ordinary stillness as though nothing extraordinary had ever passed through them at all.

Thor stayed the longest of anyone, though even his own departure, when it finally came, felt less like an ending and more like the natural next step of a story that had found its resting point.

"I must return to my own responsibilities eventually," he told the assembled household, over one last dinner, the table crowded with everyone who'd become, over these strange months, something close to his own found family. "Natasha's already covered for my absence longer than any of us should reasonably expect. But I intend to visit. Often, if you'll have me."

"Always," Joyce said, and meant it completely.

"Good," Thor said, something really moved in his expression. "Because I find, rather unexpectedly, that this strange, small, remarkable town has become something much more than a place I once searched for a lost hammer. It has become, in its own way, a second home."

"We contain multitudes," Xander said, and this time, nobody bothered correcting him, the whole table dissolving into warm, easy laughter instead, one more strange, wonderful chapter closing gently into whatever ordinary, extraordinary life came next.

152.5. Cent: Coming Home

We came back through the jump the morning after that last conversation, Home's team already gone ahead of us, and I slept almost the entire first day home. The kind of sleep that isn't really rest so much as your body finally admitting how much it's been holding. Woke up disoriented twice, not sure which world I was actually in.

Dad was waiting when I woke up for good. Sat on the edge of my bed, didn't say anything at first, just looked at me a long moment. Same look he gets checking gear before a jump. Assessing whether everything's actually intact.

"You scared me," he said finally. "Going through like that. Being right there when it happened."

"I know. I'm sorry."

"Don't be sorry. I'd have done the same thing at your age. Doesn't mean it didn't scare me."

"You'd have done the same thing at any age, Dad. Don't pretend that's a young-person thing."

That got a real laugh out of him, tired but genuine. "Fair. Guess some things don't change with age."

He reached over, squeezed my hand once. Same gesture he's used my whole life for the stuff too big for words. Didn't say anything else. Didn't need to.

Mom came in a while later, sat on the other side of the bed, and for a minute the three of us just sat there together, not saying much, the kind of quiet that only works when everybody in the room actually trusts everybody else.

"Tell me the parts you didn't put in what you told us," Mom said eventually. "The real parts. Not the version you gave us worried about scaring us further."

So I told her. The actual fear, standing at the perimeter, watching Faith hold something none of us fully understood while reality itself tried to come apart. The moment Xander's hand found her shoulder and something shifted, unexplainable, exactly enough. The silence after, more overwhelming than the crisis itself had been.

"That's a lot to carry," Mom said, quiet.

"Yeah. Glad I wasn't carrying it alone, though."

"None of us were, this time," Dad said. "That's new. Worth noticing."

I thought about that — how different this whole thing had felt from every other crisis I'd grown up hearing about, secondhand, from a family that used to face everything in careful, isolated silence. This one, we'd faced together. Out loud. With people who weren't even family a year ago, who were something like family now anyway.

"You think Home's gonna want us more involved going forward?" I asked. "Given everything?"

"Probably," Mom said. "I'm not sure how I feel about that yet. Good thing, mostly, I think. Still getting used to the idea of being reconnected at all, let alone what it's gonna actually mean long-term."

"We'll figure it out," Dad said. "Same way we figure out everything else. Slowly, and mostly by accident, and somehow it works out anyway."

"Comforting life philosophy," I said.

"It's the only one I've got. Seems to keep working, though."

I laughed at that, the real kind, and felt something ease in my chest that had been sitting there since the moment the gate first opened in Colorado and started all of this.

Xander made it back to the fence line the following weekend, checking in properly now that the adrenaline had fully drained out of both of us.

"You doing okay?" he asked.

"Yeah. Slept like a rock most of today. You?"

"Same, actually. Feels good, being able to just rest now. Feels earned, for once."

"It is earned. Don't undersell it."

"Noted." A pause. "Glad you came. For the actual crisis, I mean. Not just — glad you're in my life generally, obviously. But glad you were actually there."

"Me too. Wouldn't have missed it. Even the scary parts."

"Especially the scary parts, probably. Given how it turned out."

"Yeah," I said. "Especially those."

We talked a while longer, nothing urgent, just the easy, ordinary comfort of two people who'd been through something enormous together and come out the other side still finding each other worth talking to about nothing in particular. He told me about Fury's exit line. I told him about Dad's hand squeeze. Small things. The kind that actually stick.

I mentioned Fury's line to Dad in passing the next morning, more as a funny aside than anything else — the whole "people who shouldn't know things have a way of finding out anyway" bit. He didn't laugh. Went quiet a second — the kind of quiet he gets when something old resurfaces without him meaning it to — then just nodded and said "smart man" and changed the subject. Didn't think much of it at the time. Filed it away without really filing it away, if that makes sense.

Big year, I thought, once he'd finally had to go. Turned into a good one, somehow, despite everything. Started with a boot print. Ended with reality itself nearly coming apart, and somehow, all of us still standing on the other side of it.

Wouldn't have traded it. Not even the scary parts.

152.6. Charlie: What Came Back Through

Mom came back through the gate looking older than when she'd left, which didn't make sense given she'd only been gone a handful of days, but grief and relief both age a person in ways that don't always wait for actual time to pass. The gate sealed shut behind her with a low hum, the barn settling back into its ordinary quiet, hay dust drifting in the afternoon light like nothing extraordinary had just happened at all.

I was waiting in the barn when she stepped through, the same wary posture I'd carried around her ever since the whole gate business first came out, though something in my own chest had softened over the past weeks, wariness gradually giving way to something closer to cautious trust.

"It's done," Mom said, before I could even ask. "The immediate crisis. Contained. I'll explain everything properly, but I wanted you to know that first."

"Contained," I repeated. "That's a very careful word for whatever actually happened."

"It's the accurate word. I won't pretend it wasn't close. It was closer than anything I've lived through since we lost almost everything, all those years ago." Mom set down the small case she'd carried through, hands not quite steady. "But it held. People I'd never have expected to find common ground actually found it, and it held."

I took that in for a long moment. "You okay? Really okay, not just saying it?"

"Getting there." She managed a tired smile. "It's strange, being properly reconnected to Home again after so long believing that part of my life was simply over. Strange, but not bad. I think I needed it more than I let myself admit, these past years."

"Are they gonna want you going back? For more of this?"

"Probably, at some point. Not permanently. I made that clear, and they respected it." She looked at me steadily. "This is my home now. You're my home now. Whatever Home needs from me going forward, it's a piece of my life, not the whole of it, the way it once was."

Dad came in partway through, having heard voices from the house, and Mom crossed to him without a word, the two of them holding onto each other a long moment in a way that said more than either of them probably could have managed out loud right then.

"Welcome home," Dad said, finally.

"Good to be home," Mom said, and meant it completely.

We had dinner together that night, the three of us, the ordinary rhythm of the table holding steady around everything that had just happened — not ignoring it, exactly, just letting it settle into the shape of an actual life instead of staying a crisis suspended forever. Dad had made his usual Tuesday chili, unaware or unbothered that this certain Tuesday had nearly seen reality itself come apart at the seams somewhere far outside our own quiet corner of it.

"So there's a whole other family out there now," I said, partway through the meal, spoon pausing halfway to my mouth. "This Xander person. His friends. All of them, connected to us because of some ancient hammer and a fracture in reality itself."

"Apparently," Mom said. "Strange as it sounds."

"Everything about our family's strange, Mom. That one barely cracks the top ten."

That got a real laugh out of both of them, the first one that felt utterly unforced since Mom had left, Dad nearly choking on his own chili trying to laugh and eat at the same time.

"I'd like to meet them properly, someday," I said. "Not through a crisis. Just — meet them. See what it's actually like, having that kind of connection."

"I think that could be arranged," Mom said. "Once things settle further. I'd like that too, honestly."

"I want to bring the Five, when we do. Clara especially wants to meet the girl who lifted a magic hammer. Says that sounds like exactly the kind of person she'd get along with."

"I don't doubt that for a second," Mom said, something warm and amused crossing her face.

We finished dinner slowly, nobody in a hurry to leave the table, the ordinary comfort of being together outweighing, for once, every enormous, complicated thing sitting just underneath the surface of our lives.

Later, lying in bed, I thought about the wildside — the empty grass, the buffalo, the whole vast, untouched world that had started all of this years ago — and found myself wondering, for the first time, what Xander's worlds actually looked like. Whether they held their own quiet wonders, their own strange, undiscovered corners.

Someday, I thought. Someday I'd like to see one myself. Bring Clara along too, probably, once things settled properly. She'd never forgive me if I didn't.

For now, it was enough just knowing they existed, and that whatever thin, strange threads connected our family to his, they'd held, when it mattered most, exactly the way threads like that were supposed to.
