Chapter 89

It happened on an ordinary Tuesday, which Xander would later find almost funny — no dramatic buildup, no sudden clarity, just him sitting at the kitchen table doing homework when he realized, quietly and without fanfare, that he was ready.

"I want to go meet them," he said, to Willow, who was sitting across from him working through her own assignment. "Not next cycle. This one. I think I'm actually ready."

Willow looked up. "You sound sure."

"I am sure. I don't know when that happened, exactly. I think it just kind of arrived while I wasn't looking for it."

"That tracks with how most big things actually work, honestly. You don't usually feel it building. You just wake up one day already there."

He'd left a note on his last trip there, tucked under the same rock — Coming to meet you properly, next time the jump brings me back, if that's still okay — and there'd been nothing to do since but wait and hope the world picked him again. The three days before the jump were worse than any wait he could remember, a state of nervous, restless energy that even Willow's careful patience couldn't fully talk him down from.

"You're allowed to be nervous," Buffy told him, the night before, both of them sitting on the porch steps the way they usually ended up when something needed saying carefully. "This is enormous. Nervous is the correct response to enormous."

"I know. I just wish enormous came with better instructions."

"Nothing enormous ever does. You just kind of have to walk into it and figure out the rest as you go."

"That's not super comforting."

"It's not meant to be comforting. It's meant to be true." She bumped his shoulder with hers. "And I need you to actually hear this part — whatever happens tomorrow, you're not walking into it broken or lost or missing anything. You're walking into it as somebody who's spent four years doing something actually remarkable, alone, and came out the other side of that still kind, still funny, still exactly who you were before any of this started. That's not nothing. That's actually kind of amazing, if you think about it."

He looked at her for a long moment, something in his chest loosening slightly at the words, and kissed her, slow and grateful, the kind of kiss that said more than the actual words had room for.

"Thanks. I needed that more than I probably let on."

"I know. That's why I said it."

He jumped the next morning carrying considerably more than his usual pack — nerves, hope, four years of believing he was the only person alive who'd ever felt this particular pull, all riding on whether this jump, out of however many he had left in him, would actually drop him back at the one fence line he'd once found by accident, ready, finally, to approach it on purpose if it did.

89.5. Cent: "He's coming."

Breakfast, and I nearly choked on cereal getting the words out.

"He's coming. This trip. He said so."

Mom set her mug down carefully. "When?"

"Doesn't say exactly. Soon."

Dad looked at me a long moment. "You ready?"

I thought about four years of somebody else being alone with this, about to not be, because of a piece of carved wood and a stubborn seventeen-year-old who wouldn't let a boot print go unexplained.

"Yeah," I said. "I think I actually am."

89.6. Millie: The Argument

I found Davy in the kitchen, staring at the note Cent had left on the counter — He's coming this trip — like it might rearrange itself into something less alarming if he looked at it long enough.

"You're doing the face," I said.

"I don't have a face."

"You have several faces. This is the one from the anniversary of the thing you won't call by its name. Also known as your only face, most days."

That got the corner of his mouth, briefly, before it went serious again. "A stranger. On our land. That we found out about because Cent went looking for footprints instead of telling us the second she suspected something."

"She told us the second she was sure. That's not the same as hiding it."

"It's close enough that I don't love it."

"I know you don't. I don't fully love it either." I pulled out the chair across from him. "But you're not actually arguing about Cent right now."

"I'm not?"

"You're arguing about Conley's basement."

He went still, the old flinch, quick and small. "That's not — " He stopped. Tried again, quieter. "That's not fair."

"It's completely fair, and you know it, and I'm allowed to say it because I was the one who spent that whole stretch not knowing if you were even alive." I didn't soften it. Didn't need to, not with him. "That's not a scar that heals into nothing."

"So you agree with me."

"I agree the fear's real. I don't agree it gets to make this decision by itself."

"Why not? It's kept us alive this long."

"It's kept us careful. Careful and controlling aren't the same animal, even if they wear the same coat." I reached for his hand, didn't quite get it yet. "If we clamp down on her now, for a reason that's about us and not about him, she learns the caution was never really about safety."

"What if he's exactly the kind of thing the people who took me would send? Patient. Careful. Feels harmless right up until —"

"Then we watch. We have a plan. We trust her read, because she's had a better one than either of us managed at seventeen." I got the hand this time. "You taught her to run. Trust that it took."

"I taught her to run from people who mean her harm. I didn't teach her what it looks like when somebody's just lonely." A beat. "I was that lonely once. Before you. Didn't stop them finding a use for it."

"Maybe that's the actual lesson, then. Not danger versus safety. Lonely versus dangerous. They're not the same thing, and you of all people know the difference better than anybody."

He turned his coffee cup slowly, didn't drink from it. "I still don't love it."

"You don't have to love it. You just can't stand in front of it." I squeezed once. "We'll be close. Not close enough to hover. Close enough to help."

"That's the whole plan? Hope really hard and stand nearby?"

"That's most of parenting, Davy. I don't know why you're only noticing now."

He almost laughed at that, the sound coming out rougher than it should have, and finally nodded, the worry still sitting there under it, the way it always did when Conley's name got this close to the surface. "Okay. Her call. We back her play."

"We back her play," I agreed, and neither of us let go of the other's hand for a while after that.
