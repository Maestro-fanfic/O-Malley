Chapter 22

Willow's notebook had stopped being a notebook sometime around the third species and become, in her own words, "a preliminary field taxonomy," which Giles found charming and Xander found slightly alarming, mostly because she'd started asking increasingly specific questions before he even left for a jump.

"Okay, if you see anything that looks like a big cat," she said, cornering him at his locker Monday morning, "I need you to note ear shape specifically. Rounded versus pointed changes the whole family tree."

"I'll be sure to ask it to hold still for ear measurements."

"I'm serious, Xander. This matters."

"I know it matters. I'm just also aware that most things out there don't love being closely observed by a guy with a notebook, and I'd rather come back with vague ear notes than not come back at all."

"Fair. Vague ear notes accepted." Willow flipped back through her notebook, clearly pleased with herself. "You know this could actually be huge, right? Like, honestly important. An entire parallel ecosystem, isolated from human contact for who knows how long — that's not just cool animal facts, that's actual evolutionary data nobody's ever had access to before."

"I mostly think about it as 'place with weird animals and also apparently bees now.'"

"That's because you're not a scientist."

"I'm an adventurer. Very different skill set. Mine involves not dying, yours involves knowing what almost killed me afterward."

"That's a good division of labor, actually."

"I thought so."

She'd started keeping a second notebook specifically for what she'd begun calling "incident correlation" — cross-referencing which biomes produced which hazards, on the theory that if there was any pattern to which jump landed him where, understanding it might eventually help him prepare better.

"I don't think it's random," she told Giles one afternoon, spreading both notebooks across the research table with a level of organization that made even Giles blink. "Or it might be random, but even random has patterns if you track enough data points. Four years of jumps is actually a pretty solid sample size."

"You believe there's a system to which world he lands in."

"I believe there might be. I haven't found it yet. But I've got desert, grassland, rainforest, and now scrubland, and I keep thinking there's gotta be something underneath the randomness, some kind of actual mechanism instead of just cosmic dice rolls."

"And if there is a system?"

"Then maybe eventually we could predict it. Or at least prep him better for what's coming instead of him finding out the hard way every single time." Willow tapped the incident notebook. "Bee attack, hillside slide, near-miss with something in the dark on night two of the sloth trip — that's three genuine scares in under a month. I don't love that trend line."

"Unrelated theory, while I've got you," she added, flipping to a page with a rough tally scrawled down one side. "It's always three days out there, no matter how long he's actually gone. Doesn't matter if the jump pulls him back after what feels like an afternoon here or a full week — three days, every time, on his end. I think it's not the trips that are inconsistent. I think it's us. Like our world just runs slower than basically everywhere else."

Giles considered that, unhurried. "That is, admittedly, consistent with what he's reported. I'd want considerably more data before calling it established, but it isn't an unreasonable theory."

"Obviously. It's a page-two theory, not a published paper." Willow tapped the tally once more. "But it'd explain a lot, if it holds up."

Giles considered the notebooks for a long moment. "It's admirable work. I'd only caution you against becoming so focused on the pattern that you lose sight of the fact that some risk is simply inherent to what he does. Not every hazard has a predictable cause."

"I know. I just — I don't love not being able to help more than I currently can. Even on the trips I do get to go on, there's still so much he handles solo, just by nature of the job, and all I can do from here the rest of the time is take notes after the fact."

"The notes matter," Giles said with a small smile. "Understanding a system, even partially, is rarely wasted effort. And I suspect he takes considerable comfort in knowing someone's tracking it this closely, even if he teases you about the ear measurements."

Willow managed a small smile at that. "He really does tease me about the ear measurements."

"Extensively, I imagine."

"Constantly. It's very rude, given how useful the ear measurements would actually be."

"Scientists are rarely appreciated in their own time," Giles said, dry enough that it took Willow a second to catch that he was joking, and once she did, she laughed for real, closing the incident notebook with slightly less weight in her shoulders than she'd been carrying it with all afternoon.
