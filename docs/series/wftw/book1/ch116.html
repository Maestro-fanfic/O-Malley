Chapter 116

Buffy came along this time, the two of them landing in a world of red-rock canyons that reminded Xander, briefly and uncomfortably, of the canyon with the handprints, before he registered the actual differences — no paintings here, just endless striated stone and a river cutting far below, silver-bright in the sun.

"Okay, this one's a looker too," Buffy said, peering over the canyon edge with more caution than her tone suggested. "Very grand-canyon energy."

"Very grand-canyon energy, minus the gift shop," Xander agreed. "Watch your footing, though. Loose rock everywhere."

They spent the first day working their way along the rim, cataloguing a flock of pale, crested birds that nested directly into the cliff face itself, diving for insects with a precision that made Buffy audibly gasp the first time she watched one drop nearly a hundred feet in a single controlled fall.

"That's honestly terrifying," she said. "In a cool way. Terrifying-cool."

"Willow's gonna want measurements on the dive angle. She's gotten weirdly specific about aerodynamics lately."

"She's gotten weirdly specific about everything lately. I love it, honestly."

They found a way down into the canyon itself on the second day, a narrow switchback trail worn into the rock by nothing but weather and time, and spent hours at the river's edge, cataloguing fish species neither of them could begin to classify, the water running cold and clear enough to see straight to a rocky bottom. Xander filled the collapsible tank with a few of the more striking ones before he'd even fully thought it through, muscle memory from three years of Alan's increasingly specific requests taking over before his conscious brain caught up.

Buffy waded in up to her knees at one point, jeans rolled up, hair coming loose from its ponytail in the humidity, and Xander caught himself staring for a solid three seconds longer than any actual fish-cataloguing purpose required before he managed to redirect his own brain back toward the notebook in his hands. 'Okay, focus,' he thought, mortified at himself the way only a teenage boy caught mid-stare by his own internal monologue could manage. 'She's talking about fish gill structure. Fish. Gills. Not — whatever your brain's currently doing. Get it together.'

"You're staring," Buffy said, not even looking up from the water.

"I'm cataloguing. There's a difference."

"Cataloguing my knees?"

"...The knees are part of the ecosystem now. Context matters."

"That's the worst save I've ever heard."

"I'm workshopping it. Give it time."

"This is actually one of the best ones," Buffy said, sitting with her feet in the water, watching light play off the canyon walls above them, entirely unbothered by his flustered recovery. "I get why you kept doing this alone for so long, before any of us started coming along. There's something about it. Being somewhere nobody's ever been."

"Yeah. That part never really wore off, even on the bad trips." Xander sat beside her, quiet a moment. "Glad I get to share it now, though. Doesn't make it less special. Just makes it less lonely."

"Good trade."

"Really good trade."

The third day brought trouble, mild but real — a rockslide, distant enough not to threaten them directly, close enough that the sound alone sent both of them scrambling for solid ground, hearts pounding long after the actual danger had passed.

"Okay, that was a lot," Buffy said, once they'd caught their breath. "That was a genuinely a lot of rock, moving very fast, very far away from us specifically, thank god."

"Canyon's earned its keep today. Very dramatic, very much a reminder that we're guests here."

"Noted. Staying further from the walls from now on."

They made camp well clear of the canyon edge after that, and spent their last evening simply watching the sky change color over red rock, comfortable and quiet, the kind of ordinary wonder that had, over four years, become the actual heart of the whole strange job.

By the time the jump pulled them home, they had photos of birds, a tank full of fish nobody back home had ever seen, a story about a rockslide that would get retold at least a dozen times over the following weeks, and the certain, settled satisfaction of two people who'd shared something really beautiful and come home from it together.

Willow was cross-legged on the kitchen counter when they came in, mid-snack, and nearly fell off it sitting up so fast. "Tell me there's fish. Please tell me there's fish."

"There's fish. Also a canyon, some birds, and one truly terrifying rockslide," Buffy said. "Ten out of ten, would recommend."

"I want all the details. All of them. Don't skip the rockslide."

"Wouldn't dream of it," Xander said, already pulling out his notebook, and the three of them slipped into the easy, familiar ritual of cataloguing one more impossible world, primary premise intact, exactly the way it had always been.
