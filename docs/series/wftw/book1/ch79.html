Chapter 79

Boot print. Not Dad's.

I crouched down and checked anyway, out of habit, matching it against the tread I'd memorized without meaning to. Not his. Not Mom's. Not mine.

Fresh. A day old, maybe less.

I followed it a hundred yards. It stopped in open ground. No reason to stop there. No trail out.

Which meant one thing.

I radioed Dad that evening. Kept it short, the way he taught me to keep everything short on the radio, just in case.

"Found tracks. East perimeter. Not ours. They just end."

Pause. Longer than the news should've needed.

"You're sure they're not old?"

"I'm sure."

Another pause, and I could hear something underneath it this time, something I didn't have a name for yet but recognized anyway, because I'd heard it in his voice exactly twice before in my life. "Well." A beat. "That's new."

"That's what I thought too."

"You want the lecture about not jumping to conclusions, or should I skip it?"

"Skip it. I already jumped to it. Sorry."

"Cent."

"I'm serious, though. There's somebody else."

He didn't say anything for a second, and I let him have it. I'd had a whole afternoon to get my head around it. He'd had exactly forty seconds. I knew what forty seconds could do to Dad when something touched the old wound. Growing up, I'd watched him check locks twice on nights he never explained. Watched Mom go quiet the same way, somewhere else in the house, once he told her.

"We always figured it was possible," he said finally. "Statistically. Doesn't mean I expected to actually hear it."

"What do we do?"

"Nothing yet. Watch. Don't approach if they come back. We don't know anything about them."

"They don't know anything about us either."

"Cuts both ways," he agreed. I heard the same carefulness in it he always brought to anything that could circle back around to somebody using one of us against the other. He didn't say Cox's name. He almost never does. Not out of fear, I don't think. More like a debt he never got to finish paying — the man died settling accounts that weren't really his to settle. That kind of math sticks with a person. He didn't have to say the name for me to hear it anyway.

I looked at the print again after we hung up. Somebody had stood exactly there, and then hadn't. No car. No trail. No reason for the ground to just stop explaining itself like that, unless they'd done exactly what I'd have done.

Somebody else could do this.

I didn't sleep great that night, and it wasn't the bad kind of not sleeping. Mostly. Some of it was the bad kind too, the kind that comes from growing up knowing exactly what it can cost, loving somebody who can do what we do.

79.5. Cent: "Does Dad know?"

Mom caught me on the stairs before I'd even put the notebook away.

"You've got your thinking face on."

"Found a boot print out east. Not ours. Told Dad already, over the radio."

She didn't ask if I was sure, which I appreciated more than I said. Just: "Does Dad know what you think it means?"

"I think he's being careful about saying it out loud. I wasn't."

"What'd you say?"

"That there's somebody else."

Mom sat down on the step below me, which put her eye level with my knees, an old trick of hers from back when I was small enough that eye level actually mattered for these conversations. Still worked, honestly.

"How do you feel about that?"

"Scared. Excited. Both at once, which is a stupid combination of things to feel at the same time."

"It's not stupid. It's just big." She reached up, tucked a piece of hair behind my ear, which I would've swatted away from anyone else. "Your father's not being cagey to worry you less. He's being cagey because he doesn't know what to feel yet either, and he processes by going quiet."

"You process by asking a hundred questions."

"I process by asking exactly the right number of questions. There's a difference."

I almost laughed. "That's such a therapist thing to say."

"I am a therapist. Occupational hazard." She was quiet a second, something crossing her face I didn't have a word for yet. Not fear exactly. Just old, and still a little tender when it got poked. "For what it's worth — the government once used me as leverage against your father, back before you were born. Very politely, by their account. Lawyers, a judge, nobody raised their voice. Didn't make it less coercive, being handled politely. I don't want that memory running this conversation before we even know who this person is."

I hadn't expected her to say it plain like that. "You think Dad's scared of that. Specifically that."

"I think it's the shape every fear takes for him now, whether it fits or not. Doesn't mean he's wrong to be careful. Just means we have to be careful not to let one bad memory decide everything in advance."

She stood, hand on my shoulder for a second on her way past. "Keep documenting. Don't chase it. And Cent—it's okay to be excited. You don't have to apologize for that part just because it's also scary."

I sat there a while after she'd gone, notebook back open on my knees, and let myself actually feel the excited part without immediately talking myself out of it.
