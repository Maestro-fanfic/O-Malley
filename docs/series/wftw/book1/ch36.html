Chapter 36

Joyce's list had a new entry by Sunday dinner: seals the size of couches, apparently, and an ocean nobody back home would ever get to see.

"That's the nicest entry you've given me in weeks," she said, passing the salad down the table. "No injuries at all this time?"

"None. Genuinely peaceful trip. I think I forgot how those work."

"Enjoy it while it lasts," Faith said with a grin. "Statistically you're due for something ridiculous next time."

"I already had this argument with Buffy. I'm not litigating it twice in one week."

"Fine, I'll just be right about it quietly, off to the side, and you can deal with that instead." Faith stole a roll off his plate. "Sloth, bees, hillside, mud, ants, and now one peaceful ocean trip. Odds say the universe owes you another disaster."

"The universe doesn't work on a schedule."

"The universe absolutely works on a schedule. It's just a schedule nobody's cracked yet." Faith reached for his fries this time, since the roll was already gone. "Willow's probably got the data somewhere. Right, Red?"

"I'm not building a disaster-prediction model," Willow said, though the slight pause before she said it suggested she'd at least considered it. "That's not what the incident notebook is for."

"You've absolutely considered it."

"I've considered a lot of things. Doesn't mean I'm building all of them."

Joyce shook her head, smiling despite herself, and reached for the bread basket. "I remember when Sunday dinner conversation was about homework and curfew."

"Those were simpler times," Buffy said.

"I don't miss them," Faith said. "A disaster-prediction model's a way better dinner topic than curfew ever was."

"None taken," Buffy said. "Wait, I don't think that made sense."

"It made perfect sense to me."

"That's concerning, actually."

The table dissolved into its usual comfortable noise after that, Jesse arriving fashionably late to catch the tail end of the disaster-model debate and immediately taking Faith's side purely on principle, dropping into the one open chair and stealing a breadstick before he'd even fully sat down.

"What'd I miss?"

"Disaster-prediction model debate," Faith said. "I'm right, everyone else is wrong."

"Sounds accurate. I'm in." Jesse pointed his breadstick at Xander accusingly. "Also, unrelated, you've got Buffy's lip gloss on your neck, so either you two need a signal for when to actually check a mirror before family dinner, or you're doing it on purpose to freak out Joyce."

"I am absolutely not doing it on purpose," Xander said, hand flying to his neck, mortified.

"He is absolutely doing it on purpose," Faith said. "Look at his face. That's a guilty face."

"That's not a guilty face, that's a horrified face, there's a difference—"

Buffy, fully unbothered, reached over and wiped at the spot herself with her thumb, easy and unhurried, the way she'd done it a hundred times before without either of them thinking twice about it in front of company. "There. Crisis averted."

"I hate that this happens regularly enough that you have a technique," Xander muttered.

"I hate that you keep forgetting to check," Buffy said, entirely unrepentant, and kissed his cheek for good measure, right there at the table, apparently just to make his point for him.

Willow quietly sketched what was almost certainly the beginning of an actual chart despite her protests otherwise, Joyce refilling glasses without a word, long since resigned to simply enjoying the noise instead of keeping up with the specifics of any given evening's chaos.

"I think that's everything," Buffy said eventually.

"For now," Joyce said with a tired smile, and reached for dessert, settling in for whatever the following week would inevitably bring to add to an already very long list.

36.5. Faith: The Door

I was drying dishes when the doorbell rang, which is such a stupid, ordinary detail to remember clearly out of the whole night, but I do. Dish towel still in my hand. Water still running behind me. Some part of my brain apparently decided that was the detail worth keeping, out of everything that happened after.

Joyce went to get it. I heard the door open, heard her say something I didn't catch, and then heard her voice do a thing I'd never once heard it do in the months I'd been living under her roof — go careful, go quiet, the way you'd move around something fragile you didn't want to spook.

"Faith," she called back. "Can you come here a second?"

I figured it was Council business. Figured it was Giles, or some new Watcher rep come to make my life difficult, or honestly I didn't figure much of anything, because you don't brace for the actual thing. You can't. There's no bracing for it.

I came around the corner still holding the dish towel, and there was a man standing in the doorway who looked like he'd aged a decade since I'd last seen him and also, somehow, hadn't changed at all, and next to him a woman with her hand pressed flat over her own mouth like she was physically holding something in, and I stood there for what felt like an actual minute just — not processing. Not able to process. Because I knew that face. I knew both those faces, down in whatever part of my brain never stopped keeping them, and the part of my brain that ran the rest of my life had spent years telling me those faces were gone. Actually gone. Not gone like moved away, not gone like lost touch. Gone like a house fire I wasn't in the room for. Gone like a story I'd had to build for myself out of silence, because nobody ever told me different, and eventually silence starts sounding a lot like an answer if you sit in it long enough.

"Dad?" My voice came out smaller than I meant it to, cracked right down the middle.

"Hey, baby girl." Harry's voice cracked too, which undid something in my chest I hadn't braced for at all. "I know. I know this doesn't make any sense right now. I know you thought—"

"I thought you were dead." I heard myself say it flat, too flat, the words arriving before I'd decided to let them out. "I thought you were both dead. I mourned you. I actually — I built a whole life around you being dead, I made peace with it, I—"

"We weren't dead." Helen's hand came off her mouth, reaching for me without quite touching, like she wasn't sure she'd earned that yet. "God, Faith, we weren't dead, we were — we were taken. Held. Almost seven years of it, some of that time not even in the same country as each other, and by the time we got out, by the time we could even start looking—"

"Taken." I said the word like it belonged to somebody else's life. "You were taken. Not dead. Taken."

"Taken," Harry confirmed, and something in his face when he said it told me exactly how much that word was covering, how much of it he wasn't going to say standing in a doorway in front of an audience. "We've been looking for almost two years since we got free. Every lead, every dead end, every false match that turned out to be some other kid who wasn't you. And then three days ago a traffic camera in a town called Sunnydale flagged a face, and I have never in my life moved faster than I moved once I saw that flag come through."

I don't remember deciding to cross the distance between us. I just remember being in my father's arms, the dish towel still somehow in my fist, crying in a way I hadn't let myself cry in years, not since I'd stopped letting myself cry about them at all, because what was the point of crying over dead people, you just had to get up the next day either way. Except they weren't dead. They'd been alive this whole time, somewhere, held by someone, while I'd been building an entire life on the assumption I'd never see either of them again.

"I hated you a little," I admitted into his shoulder, muffled and wet and true. "For dying. I know that's stupid, I know you didn't choose it, but I hated you anyway, for a while, because it felt easier than just being sad."

"That's not stupid," Helen said, close now too, one hand finding my hair the way she used to when I was small enough that gesture still fixed things. "That's just grief. Grief doesn't care what's fair."

There was a third person on the porch I hadn't fully clocked until then, a big guy hanging back with a respectful kind of distance, arms crossed, doing an actually terrible job of pretending he wasn't wiping at his own eyes.

"Gib," I said, because some old, buried part of my memory supplied the name before I'd consciously reached for it.

"Hey, kid." His voice came out rougher than I bet he wanted it to. "Look at you. Look at you, all grown up and apparently living some whole life I know nothing about yet. I want the full download. Every bit of it. But not tonight. Tonight I'm just real glad to see your face."

Buffy had appeared somewhere behind me at some point, close enough to be a steady presence without crowding the actual moment, and Joyce stood a little further back, hand pressed to her own chest, clearly trying not to intrude on something this big while also clearly not about to leave a teenager alone with two strangers claiming to be her resurrected parents, whatever the resemblance said.

"I need — I need everybody to sit down," I said, finally pulling back enough to actually look at both of them properly, taking in the real, physical fact of them standing in Joyce's doorway. "I need the whole thing. I need every single detail, because I have spent years building a version of this where you were just gone, and I don't know how to hold both versions at once yet."

"We'll tell you everything," Harry said. "Everything. Take as long as you need to hear it."

"Okay." I wiped my face with the back of my hand, dish towel finally, absurdly, dropping to the porch floor. "Okay. Come in, I guess. Both of you. All three of you."

Behind me, I heard Joyce quietly telling Buffy to go find more chairs, her voice already shifting into the same practiced calm she always found for exactly this kind of impossible thing, and some small, still-processing part of my brain thought, distantly, that I was about to find out my life could still get stranger, even after everything it had already thrown at me.
