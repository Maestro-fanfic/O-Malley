Chapter 16

The next jump dropped all three of them into rain.

Not a light rain either — a steady, cold downpour that started the second they materialized and showed no signs of planning to stop, turning the ground beneath their boots to something between mud and soup within the first ten minutes of just standing there getting their bearings.

"Great," Xander said, to the rain, which didn't respond, because it was rain. "Love this. Love getting drenched immediately, day one, hour one, in front of witnesses this time."

"In front of witnesses who are equally drenched," Buffy pointed out, wringing out her sleeve to approximately zero effect.

"Small comfort. I'll take it anyway."

They found tree cover within twenty minutes, a dense stand of conifers that cut the worst of it, and spent the better part of an hour watching the rain hammer down beyond the canopy, waiting to see if it would ease. It didn't. If anything it got heavier, the kind of rain that turned every low spot into a stream and every stream into something with actual current behind it.

"This is fine," Willow said, forcing herself to sound optimistic about it. "Rainy weekend. Character building."

"Character building," Xander repeated. "That's one word for it." He was already unstrapping the shed from where it rode flattened against his pack frame, the one piece of gear he never left behind on a multi-person trip anymore, not since the first time three of them had tried to share a tarp built for one and learned better the hard way. "This, on the other hand, is the actual character-saving part."

It took him and Buffy both, working fast in the downpour, to get it staked and upright, canvas snapping taut against the wind before either of them was fully soaked through, and the three of them piled inside a minute later, significantly drier than the alternative would've left them.

"Okay, I take back every joke I've ever made about you hauling this thing around," Willow said, wringing out her sleeves onto the floor rather than the sleeping bags this time, lesson well and truly learned. "This is honestly the best decision anyone's made all day."

They settled in to wait the storm out, canvas walls holding steady against wind that would've flattened a tarp inside the hour.

The second day, the rain finally broke around midday, restlessness after eighteen straight hours crammed into a shed built for cozy, not comfortable — Buffy wedged against Xander's side the whole stretch, his arm looped loose around her shoulders more out of necessity than romance at first, then very much not just necessity somewhere around hour six, Willow's head on his other shoulder while she read old field notes out loud just to fill the silence — and the restlessness got the better of all three of them at once, pushing further than any of them had really planned. The ground was treacherous — soaked through, every slope a potential slide, every root slick enough to turn an ankle if nobody was watching their footing.

They were watching their footing, carefully, right up until the ground simply wasn't there anymore.

The slope gave out under Xander first, mud and loose rock sliding together in a rush that took his feet out from under him, and in the process of grabbing for anything solid, his flailing hand caught Buffy's ankle, which took her down too, which in turn meant Willow — reaching to catch Buffy — went down a half-second after that, all three of them sliding down a hillside none of them had clocked as a hillside until they were already most of the way down it in an undignified, mud-covered tangle of limbs.

"Okay!" Xander shouted, somewhere in the chaos. "Okay, this is a moment, this is a whole moment happening to all of us right now!"

"You grabbed my ANKLE!" Buffy managed, sliding past him.

"I was FALLING, grabbing things is a REFLEX!"

"Why am I involved in this," Willow wailed, arms windmilling uselessly, "I was STANDING PERFECTLY STILL—"

They came to rest in a heap at the bottom, tangled together in mud up to their ears, nobody quite sure whose elbow was in whose ribs, and for a long moment nobody moved, just breathing hard, coated head to toe, profoundly, gratefully, ridiculously alive.

"Ow," Buffy said, into what turned out to be Xander's shoulder. "Ow, but also, everyone still has all their limbs, so, silver lining."

"I lost a fight with a mudslide," Xander announced, flat on his back, staring up at the canopy. "Round one, mudslide. Round two, also mudslide, technically, since I didn't really get a round two so much as a controlled surrender."

"That's not how boxing works," Willow said, spitting mud out of her own mouth with far less grace than the observation deserved.

"It's exactly how boxing works when the other guy's an entire hillside."

They extracted themselves slowly, checking for actual injuries amid the mud — nothing broken, a scrape along Xander's forearm, Buffy favoring one ankle slightly, Willow somehow the least damaged of the three of them despite her protests to the contrary.

"For the record," Willow said, once they'd found drier ground and started scraping the worst of it off, "next time somebody starts sliding, the correct move is NOT to grab the nearest person."

"Noted," Xander said. "Next time I'll just fall with dignity, alone."

"That's not what I said either."

"I don't know what the right answer is anymore. I've been humbled by a hillside."

They made it to actually dry ground before dark, got a fire going with hands that had finally stopped shaking, and Buffy pressed a mud-streaked kiss to Xander's cheek once they'd both stopped shivering, leaving a visible smear behind that Willow refused to let either of them wipe off, insisting it was "documentation." Once the worst of the shivering had burned off and the fire had done its job, they hauled the shed the rest of the way down to drier ground and dragged themselves back inside it for the night rather than trusting three exhausted, mud-caked bodies to a campfire and open sky, and spent what was left of the evening picking mud out of increasingly unlikely places while trading blame for whose reflexes had actually started the chain reaction, a debate that would resurface, unresolved, at lunch tables for weeks afterward.
