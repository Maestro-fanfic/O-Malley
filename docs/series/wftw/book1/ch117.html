Chapter 117

Willow and Faith both came this time, the three of them making an unusual trio that turned out, within the first hour, to work far better than any of them expected — Willow's careful observation balancing nicely against Faith's blunt, practical instincts about which way looked survivable and which didn't.

The world was jungle again, though a different one than any of them had seen before, canopy so dense overhead that the ground stayed in permanent green-tinted twilight even at midday.

"Okay, this is atmospheric," Faith said, peering up at the canopy. "Very haunted-forest energy."

"Very haunted-forest energy that's also apparently home to about six species nobody's ever documented," Willow said, already crouched over something small and iridescent crawling along a fallen branch. "I need to catalogue every single thing in this place before we leave."

"That's gonna take a while."

"I'm aware. I've made peace with it."

Xander left the two of them to it, splitting off toward the tree line to check the wider perimeter the way four years of habit had taught him to on any new world, glancing back every so often with the easy, fond amusement of a man watching two people he loved get along better than he'd necessarily expected them to.

They found the frogs on the second day, dozens of them clustered around a slow-moving stream, skin patterned in colors bright enough to look almost artificial against the muted greens surrounding them.

"Those are definitely poisonous," Faith said, keeping a healthy distance. "Nothing looks that colorful for fun."

Willow had gone very still at first sight of them, the particular stillness of someone actively arguing with an old, deeply irrational reflex. "Okay. They're just frogs. Extremely numerous, extremely bright frogs, and I'm a grown woman with a notebook, and I can absolutely do this."

"You good?" Faith asked, actually looking over now.

"I have a whole thing about frogs. It's very stupid. I'm aware it's stupid. Doesn't make it less real." Willow took a slow breath and crouched down anyway, camera steady despite the faint tremor in her hands, getting her photos from well outside arm's reach. "You're probably right about the poison, for what it's worth. Bright coloring's usually a warning system in nature. Advertise the danger instead of hiding it."

"You're doing the science-brain thing to talk yourself through it."

"Extremely accurate diagnosis. Let me have it, though. It's working."

"Fair enough." Faith drifted a half-step closer anyway, easy, unspoken backup. "See, I know things too. I'm not just muscle."

"Never said you were just muscle."

"You were thinking it."

"I was thinking several things, none of which were that specifically."

They spent the rest of that day working through the underbrush carefully, Faith's patrol-honed instincts catching hazards Willow's scientific focus occasionally missed — a rotten branch that wouldn't hold weight, a nest of something with an aggressive-sounding buzz neither of them wanted to investigate directly.

"This is actually a really good combo," Willow admitted, on the third evening. "You keeping us from dying, me actually knowing what we're looking at."

"Damn right it's a good combo. I should come on more of these."

"You really should. I like having you along."

"Yeah," Faith said, something genuine and pleased under the usual easy confidence. "Yeah, me too, actually."

The pull took them home with a full catalogue of frogs, insects, and one heart-stopping near-miss with what Faith firmly identified as "a spider the size of my actual hand, no thank you," and despite the danger, none of them would have called the trip anything but honestly good, start to finish.

"Nobody's bleeding, so I'm calling that a win already," Joyce said, taking in the state of them at the door.

"Excellent," Willow said, once she'd recovered. "Terrifying spiders, gorgeous frogs, and Faith turns out to be a genuinely great field partner."

"Don't sound so surprised," Faith said, grinning. "I contain multitudes."

"That's still not your line," Xander said, and the whole kitchen dissolved into laughter, one more strange, ordinary evening settling into place around a job that had, four years running, never once stopped finding new ways to be worth doing.
