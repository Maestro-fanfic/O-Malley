Chapter 114

Thor's communication device — some ungainly hybrid Tony had cobbled together specifically so Asgardian tech could actually talk to SHIELD's network, a project Thor still didn't fully understand the mechanics of and Tony still complained about at length whenever it came up — had sat largely ignored since his arrival, given how little use he'd had for it, but chimed for the first time in weeks while he was in the middle of explaining the concept of nachos to Willow with genuine, focused enthusiasm, three separate cheese varieties laid out on the kitchen counter like evidence in some elaborate culinary trial.

"The key," he'd been saying, holding up a chip with real reverence, "is achieving even distribution. Too many toppings pooled in one location, and the remainder suffers for it. This is, I have come to understand, a genuine culinary science, and I intend to master it properly."

"You're overthinking nachos."

"I do not believe one can overthink nachos. I believe one can only under-invest in nachos, and I refuse to be that person."

The device chimed again, insistent, and he frowned at it, surprised, chip still held aloft. "That would be Natasha."

"Should you get that?" Willow asked.

"Probably wise, given how she responds to being ignored." Thor set the chip down with visible reluctance and answered it, and Xander, close enough to catch fragments, watched his expression shift from casual to honestly sheepish within about four seconds, the transformation almost comedic in how completely it overtook him.

"...yes, I am aware it has been some while... no, I did not intend to leave without word... it's rather a long story, Natasha... yes, I understand that 'rather a long story' is not an acceptable excuse for eleven days of silence..."

Willow mouthed 'eleven days?' at Xander, who shrugged, equally curious, both of them now watching the conversation with the rapt attention usually reserved for particularly good television.

"I am well. Genuinely well, in fact. I found the hammer." A pause, Thor visibly holding the phone slightly away from his ear. "Yes. Yes, it required some searching. Twelve realms, as it turned out." Another pause, longer this time, Thor's expression shifting again, something more careful entering it. "That is... considerably more complicated to explain over this connection. Might I call properly, once I've had a chance to gather my thoughts?"

Whatever Natasha said in response made Thor wince slightly, actually pulling the phone an inch further from his ear.

"Yes. I understand. I'll expect the full debrief, and I promise not to make you extract it from me one reluctant sentence at a time." He glanced at Xander, covering the receiver briefly, expression somewhere between amused and actually nervous. "She wants to come see for herself. Apparently my track record for 'I'm fine, just a long story' does not inspire confidence."

"That's... probably fair, given the eleven days thing."

"Entirely fair," Thor agreed, uncovering the receiver. "Yes. Yes, that would be —"

A second voice cut in on Natasha's end before he could finish, sharp enough that Xander and Willow both went still. Tony's, unmistakably, but stripped of every ounce of the easy, talk-show energy either of them would have expected from the name alone — flat, cold, precise in the specific way anger gets when it's actually fear wearing a harder face.

"When, Nat." Not a question. "Because if the answer's anything other than right fucking now, I want you to say that to my face, not down a phone line, and explain to me exactly which part of eleven days of nothing made 'sometime this week' sound like the responsible call."

Somewhere behind him, faint but audible, Steve started to say something that began with "Lang—" and simply stopped there, apparently deciding, correctly, that this was not that moment.

Thor's expression went very still, the last of the sheepishness draining out of it entirely.

"Tony—" Natasha, muffled, clearly trying to reclaim the conversation.

"No. I already did the math once on how many of these end without a phone call at the end of it. I'm not doing it again on this one. I'm coming tonight. Ride with me or meet me there, I don't care which, but I am not spending one more night not knowing for certain." A pause, the edge in his voice cracking just slightly at the end of it. "Just — let me see him. Please."

Nobody in the kitchen said anything. Willow's hand had gone still on her notebook.

A long moment of quiet on the line, and then Natasha again, quieter now herself. "...Tonight, then. Steve and Bruce are apparently coming too, so brace yourself."

"Understood," Thor said, and it came out smaller than anything he'd said yet, something old and grateful and faintly ashamed moving behind his eyes. "I'll let the people here know to expect all of you."

He ended the call and set the phone down with unusual care, not looking at anyone for a moment.

"So," he said, eventually, something quieter under the usual warmth. "Natasha will be coming tonight. Tony as well, and it seems Steve and Bruce along with him. I did not entirely appreciate, until just now, how much that silence actually cost the people who care about me. I owe him considerably more than an apology when he arrives."

Xander didn't say anything right away, just reached over and set a hand briefly on Thor's shoulder, the gesture doing the work words probably wouldn't have done as well.

"You're allowed to have scared people," Xander said finally. "Doesn't make you a bad friend. Just means you're worth being scared for."

Thor managed something close to a real smile at that, quiet and grateful, and the moment eased, gradually, back into something Willow could safely reach past.

"That's still a very reasonable thing for a friend to want to confirm," she said, gentler than her earlier excitement, though it crept back in anyway around the edges. "Also, no offense, but actual Avengers. Plural. Coming here. To Sunnydale."

"I did warn you my associates tend toward the dramatic once really worried."

"I'm not complaining," Willow said. "I'm the opposite of complaining. I have so many questions already forming. Does she actually use the Widow's Bite in the field or is that mostly cinematic embellishment, I've always wondered—"

"I would ask that you keep the questions to a reasonable number," Thor said, weary but fond, clearly having had this exact conversation with enthusiastic strangers more than once before. "Natasha does not always appreciate enthusiasm at first meeting. It tends to grow on her slowly."

"I can be reasonable," Willow said, in a tone that suggested she absolutely could not.

"You always say that," Xander pointed out, "right before you ask somebody four questions about clearance levels in the first five minutes."

"That was different. Those were good questions."

"They were extremely good questions. Also extremely not reasonable, by your own definition."

"I contain multitudes," Willow said, grinning, and both Xander and Thor groaned in unison, the reflex now so well established between the household that it barely required conscious thought anymore.

"That's still not your line," Xander said.

"I'm aware. I'm workshopping alternatives. This one felt right in the moment."

Xander just laughed, already bracing himself, with real fondness, for whatever chaos actual Avengers showing up in Sunnydale were about to add to an already thoroughly unpredictable month, and reached for one of Thor's carefully constructed nachos, earning an immediate, wounded protest about proper cheese distribution ratios that carried them both, laughing, back into the kitchen.

Getting three people and a minivan across the actual boundary between realities turned out to need considerably more hardware than a phone call ever had.

Tony had it half-built already, it turned out — a paranoid, just-in-case project he'd shelved months back and never quite stopped tinkering with in the margins of everything else, and it took him and a borrowed SHIELD storage unit outside the city under four hours to finish it properly, adrenaline apparently doing what deadlines usually did for him. What it amounted to, in the end, was a rough ring of squat black cylinders arranged on bare concrete, cables running to a case not much bigger than a suitcase, the whole rig looking considerably less impressive than the single most expensive interdimensional engineering project any of them would ever personally witness had any right to look.

"This is definitely gonna work," Tony said, not looking up from the tablet in his hands. "Probably. Ninety percent. Ninety-two if I'm feeling generous with myself."

"Those are not confidence-inspiring numbers," Steve said, from the minivan's driver seat, both hands already on the wheel like it might matter yet.

"They're honest numbers, which is different and better. I built a listening device. I did not build a door. What I've got now is that same listening device with delusions of grandeur, wired to enough output that it might, possibly, punch a hole exactly where Thor's own signature's already worn the wall thin for us. Might being the operative word. Try not to dwell on the word might."

"I'm dwelling on the word might extremely hard right now," Bruce said, from the passenger seat, hands folded very deliberately in his lap.

"Don't. Counterproductive." Tony made one last adjustment, and the cylinders hummed up into a low, climbing pitch that raised the hair on the back of Steve's neck. "Whichever of you prays, now's the time."

"I don't," Steve said.

"I sort of do, complicatedly," Bruce said. "Now seems reasonable to start."

The air between the cylinders didn't so much open as it stopped being air — a seam of gold-edged dark, ragged at first, then smoothing, widening, until it stood there clean and impossible, a doorway shaped like nothing that had ever needed a door before.

"There it is." Something fierce and relieved cut through Tony's deadpan for just a second. "Drive slow. Steve, I mean you specifically, I've seen how you take corners."

"That was one time."

"One time is exactly the number of times it needs to happen for me to remember it forever."

Steve eased the minivan forward, and for one held breath the headlights speared straight into gold instead of asphalt, and then the gold was behind them instead of ahead, and the road under the tires had gone quiet and dark and unfamiliar, a sleepy small town's back road standing in for the storage lot they'd just left entirely.

"Well," Bruce said, faint. "That happened."

"That happened," Tony agreed, already grinning, adrenaline burning off into something closer to triumph. "Somebody find me a street sign. I want to know exactly which impossible town we just broke several laws of physics to reach."

✳

Tony showed up that same night anyway, because "talked down" and "actually stayed home" had never once, in the whole history of knowing him, meant the same thing — and he hadn't come alone.

He didn't bring a jet. He brought a rented minivan, sunglasses, zero apology, and, trailing in behind him with the specific weary patience of men who'd tried arguing and lost, Steve and Bruce.

"He wouldn't take no for an answer," Steve said, by way of greeting, before anyone could ask. "We tried. Repeatedly. It didn't take."

"I don't take no for an answer from people who are wrong," Tony said, already walking into the kitchen like he'd been invited weeks ago instead of explicitly asked not to come. "Natasha said don't fly out. I didn't fly. I drove. Very different thing."

"Natasha said not to come at all," Xander said, staring at all three of them.

"Natasha said a billionaire's jet would draw attention. I rented a minivan. Extremely stealth." Tony set a case down on the table and popped it open, revealing something that looked like it had been built out of half a satellite and half a toaster. "Also, I built the thing letting you people talk to a literal Asgardian across a reality boundary, and I wasn't about to leave that completely unsupervised in a town that apparently also has a hellmouth. Non-negotiable. Be annoyed about it later. Steve and Bruce came because I told them it'd be interesting, and because somebody responsible needed to make sure I actually behaved myself. Ongoing project, that one."

"I'm managing expectations," Bruce said, mild, already eyeing the half-satellite-half-toaster device with open scientific curiosity he clearly couldn't fully suppress. "Low expectations. But managing them."

"How does it even work?" Willow asked, already leaning in, notebook forgotten in favor of the actual hardware. "The cross-reality part, I mean. Not the translation layer, I get the translation layer. The part where the signal crosses at all."

"Smartest question anyone's asked me all week." Tony looked, for a second, genuinely pleased. "Short version: I didn't invent a way to punch a signal through the wall between universes, because that's insane and also not how any of this works. What I did notice is that Thor already opens a door every time he moves between realms, and that door doesn't slam shut the instant he's through it — there's residual bleed, a signature, for a while after. I built something that listens for that signature and rides it back, piggybacking a signal on a door somebody else already opened. I'm not bridging realities. I'm eavesdropping on somebody who already can."

"That's—" Willow stared at the device like it had personally impressed her. "That's actually elegant."

"Thank you. Somebody around here appreciates good engineering." Tony glanced at Thor. "You, meanwhile, still owe me eleven days of silence and a much longer explanation than 'long story,' and I'm not leaving until I get it."

Thor sighed, the particular sigh of a man who'd seen this exact standoff coming from a great distance. "I rather suspected as much."

He didn't get away with the short version, and didn't try particularly hard to.

They sat in the Summers' kitchen well past midnight, Joyce working through a pot of coffee that eventually gave way to tea nobody quite finished, while Thor worked through all of it — the hammer's disappearance, the four-year search across twelve realms, Xander, the fracture, the mural, the counter-rite Faith and the others had faced down without ever fully understanding what they were up against. Bruce took notes without seeming to notice he was doing it, the old scientific habit surfacing the moment the conversation touched anything resembling data. Steve listened the way he always did, quiet and total, saving his questions for the moments they'd actually matter. Tony interrupted constantly, which turned out, once Xander adjusted to the rhythm of it, to be its own kind of careful listening — every interruption a specific, sharp question aimed at whichever part of the story didn't yet make sense.

"Back up," Tony said, somewhere past one in the morning, when Thor reached the part about the fracture reaching backward through time as well as forward. "You're telling me the reason reality's got a crack in it traces back to something we did. Us. The team."

"That is my current understanding, yes. Imperfectly held."

"That's — okay, that's a whole different problem than 'my friend's hammer went missing.'" Tony rubbed at his eyes, some of the earlier fury finally burning down into something closer to exhausted focus. "That's a problem I actually need to be in the room for, professionally, not just personally."

"I rather suspected you'd say that," Thor said, something almost fond under the weariness.

"He's not wrong to say it," Steve said, quiet. "If this actually traces back to us, Fury needs the full picture. Today, not eventually."

"I know," Thor admitted. "I have been slow reaching that conclusion, out of some combination of pride and simply wanting to understand it myself before asking anyone else to carry it. I recognize, now, that the delay itself was a mistake."

"Wasn't a mistake," Bruce said, unexpectedly, looking up from whatever calculation he'd been running in the margin of a napkin. "You wanted to bring us something real instead of half-formed panic. I get that instinct. Doesn't mean it was the right call, but it wasn't nothing, either."

Thor considered that a long moment, something in his shoulders easing slightly. "Thank you. I had not thought of it in those terms."

By the time the sky outside the kitchen window started going grey, the four of them had, between them, something closer to an actual working theory than Thor had managed alone in months — messier, more contested, considerably more caffeinated, but real. Tony left with a list of questions he promised to keep to a "reasonable" number once he brought it to Fury, which nobody in the room believed for a second. Steve left with the particular settled look of a man who'd gotten the information he'd actually come for. Bruce left still scribbling, three tangents deep into a theory about the fracture's actual physics he promised, absently, to explain properly once he'd tested it.

"That went considerably better than I feared," Thor said, once the minivan's taillights had disappeared down the street, gold flaring briefly at the edge of sight as they found their way back through wherever Tony had left the doorway standing open for the return trip.

"They love you," Xander said, simply. "Scary amounts, apparently. Kinda nice to see, honestly, given how much of my own life runs on 'never let anyone see you sweat.'"

"They do," Thor agreed, something quiet and grateful settling into his face as the first real light of morning found its way over Sunnydale's rooftops. "I am still learning not to take that for granted."
