Chapter 19 of 29

Ground Stop

Taylor hit the bottom of the chute on one hip, rolled through a mound of damp tablecloths, and struck the concrete wall with her shoulder hard enough to make her right hand go numb.

For several seconds she stayed exactly where gravity had left her. The dark laundry room tipped gently around her. Her lungs kept trying to breathe at the speed she'd been running, though the chase was above her now and there was nothing in front of her except an overturned hamper, a wheeled pressing table, and the pale rectangle of the service door.

Inventory, McClane said.

What?

You. Inventory. Head, neck, hands, feet. Anything broken, anything wet that shouldn't be, anything you can't feel.

Taylor worked through it because his voice gave her something smaller than the whole building to solve. Head sore but clear. Neck moved. Shoulder hurt. Hip hurt worse. Both knees worked. Her right hand prickled back to life one finger at a time.

Nothing broken.

Good. Now the building.

The difference between Taylor's eyes and her swarm had never felt wider. Her eyes saw a basement room under one buzzing fluorescent tube. Her bugs saw MedHall coming apart by sections.

The atrium doors had not opened when the crowd rushed them. The two men stationed there had thrown steel pins into the floor sockets and turned the polished brass handles into decoration. Three guests had gone down in the first crush. Danny and two other men were dragging them clear while people behind them shouted questions nobody answered.

The east exit was shut. The loading entrance was shut. The parking bridge doors on the mezzanine were shut. Fire doors had dropped across two service corridors, dividing the catering staff into small frightened groups with no view of the main room.

Hookwolf stood at the foot of the grand staircase with metal layered over both arms, not fully transformed and not needing to be. Cricket paced the upper landing. Alabaster watched the service doors. The two giant women held opposite ends of the atrium floor. Armed men moved between them collecting handbags and phones into silver serving trays that had been meant for champagne.

The whole building had changed jobs.

An hour ago the doors had been there to welcome people. The corridors had moved food and staff without disturbing the party. The mezzanine had displayed the guests important enough to be seen above everyone else. Now the doors contained, the corridors divided, and the mezzanine gave Cricket a firing position over the hostages below.

That's a takeover, McClane said. Organized, rehearsed, and already underway before the first blade came out. Nobody locks this many doors by improvising.

You said he was reacting to us.

He is. Doesn't mean he built the gun tonight. Means we gave him a reason to take it out of the drawer.

Taylor found Anders through a beetle under the stage.

He was on the atrium floor with everybody else now. Jacket off. Sleeves damp with spilled wine. One hand rested on the shoulder of the councilwoman who had spent half the evening discussing zoning. He spoke quietly to her while one of his own armed men passed six feet away without looking at him.

To anyone watching from the room, Max Anders was another hostage doing his best to keep people calm.

From beneath the stage, Taylor watched two fingers of his free hand tap once against his trouser seam.

Cricket changed direction on the mezzanine.

He gave her an order.

Yeah. McClane's disgust came through cold and clean. And nobody saw it because everybody's watching the guns. He gets to be mastermind and brave civic leader in the same picture.

Danny pushed through the hostages with a folded tablecloth pressed against an older man's bleeding scalp. He was pale. His tie was gone. He kept looking toward the service hall where he had last seen Taylor.

Dad.

Alive. Helping somebody. Keep the categories straight. Alive is the category that matters right now.

Taylor forced herself to pull her attention outward.

The basement had fewer bugs than the warm atrium, but enough. Silverfish in the walls. Flies sleeping near a floor drain. Two spiders behind the electrical panels. She spread them through the service level and began assembling space from touch and vibration.

Laundry occupied the southeast corner. Kitchen storage sat north of it. A mechanical room took up most of the western wall. Between them lay a security office she had missed earlier because its door sealed nearly flush and its windows faced the loading ramp outside.

There were people inside.

Two, both on the floor. One moving. One not.

Taylor was on her feet before McClane asked.

Careful, he said. People on the floor means somebody put them there.

The corridor beyond the laundry room was empty. She opened the service door an inch, then another. The alarm strobes had begun flashing without the horns, red light pulsing silently along white cinderblock. Somewhere in the building, someone had disabled the sound while leaving the visual circuit active. Every third second the corridor became red, then gray, then red again.

She sent bugs ahead at ankle height. No footsteps. No breath. No radio static.

Taylor slipped out.

The security office door had been kicked inward. One MedHall guard lay facedown beside the desk with his wrists cinched behind him using a plastic tie. The second had been shoved beneath the bank of camera monitors. Blood had dried from his temple into the collar of his white shirt.

The first man lifted his head when she entered.

"Kid, get out of here."

"Doors are locked."

"Then hide."

"Already tried that."

She found trauma shears clipped to the unconscious guard's belt. Her hands shook badly enough that the first attempt only pinched the plastic tie. McClane talked her through changing the angle, bracing the guard's wrists against the floor, and cutting away from skin.

The tie snapped.

The guard rolled onto his side and stared at her dress, her bare forearms, and the laundry lint still caught in her hair.

"How did you get down here?"

"Laundry chute. Is he breathing?"

That got him moving. He crawled to his partner, checked an airway, then pressed two fingers to the man's neck.

"Yeah. Shallow. Tom? Tom, wake up."

Tom did not.

Taylor looked at the monitors. Every screen showed the gala as it had looked before the takeover: couples dancing, waiters moving between tables, the quartet playing beneath the chandelier. The same woman in a blue dress crossed the same camera every forty-one seconds.

"They're looped," she said.

The guard followed her eyes. "That's impossible. System's closed."

"It isn't tonight. Where's the outside line?"

He reached for the desk phone. No dial tone. He tried the red handset mounted below the monitors. Nothing. Then the panic switch under the desk.

The red indicator stayed dark.

"Hard line to our monitoring company," he said. "Separate conduit."

Was separate, McClane corrected.

The guard opened the cabinet below the desk. Three cables hung inside with their copper cut cleanly through.

His face changed. Not fear exactly. The collapse of the last explanation that had allowed this to remain a security incident instead of an occupation.

"Who are they?"

Taylor watched Anders comfort his own hostages through the beetle under the stage.

"Empire Eighty-Eight."

The guard looked at her sharply. "How do you know that?"

Metal struck metal somewhere down the service corridor.

McClane heard it through her before she had sorted it from the building noise.

Company. Two, maybe three. Coming from the mechanical room side.

Taylor killed the office light. The pulsing corridor strobe painted the open doorway red every third second.

The guard reached for the empty holster on his belt.

"They took it," he whispered.

Good, McClane said. Means he won't try something brave and get all three of you killed.

"Can you move your partner?" Taylor asked.

"Not fast."

There was nowhere in the office to hide them. The desk left six inches underneath. The cabinet held wires. The exterior windows faced a loading ramp now crossed by flashlight beams.

Taylor felt along the ceiling with two moths and found a return-air grille above the monitor bank. Too small for a person. Large enough for something else.

"When they come in, fall down again. Hands behind you. Don't look at me."

The guard stared. "What are you going to do?"

"Make them look up."

She gathered every basement insect she could reach into the duct. Not many. A few dozen flies, moths, beetles, spiders carried unwillingly on silk. She packed them behind the grille and held them there while the footsteps came closer.

Two men appeared in the corridor, dark suits open now, compact submachine guns visible beneath them. One carried a radio near his mouth.

"Basement east clear. Checking security."

The guard lowered himself beside Tom and put his hands behind his back.

Taylor pressed flat against the wall beside the open door.

The first man stepped through. He saw the guards. Saw the dead monitors. Began turning toward the blind corner.

Taylor released the duct.

The swarm dropped onto both men at once. Not an attack. A black, fluttering sheet falling directly through the red strobe, wings and legs in faces, inside collars, across weapon sights. The first man shouted and brought his gun up toward the ceiling. The second backed into the corridor, swatting with his radio hand.

Taylor went low between them.

She hooked one foot behind the second man's ankle and drove her shoulder into the back of his knee. He fell hard. His radio skipped across the tile. She caught it, rolled, and kept moving while the first man's gun coughed three suppressed shots into the return-air duct.

Plaster dust burst from the ceiling.

The guard surged out of the office after her. He stamped once on the fallen man's wrist until the submachine gun came loose, kicked it through the office door, then dragged the man inside by his collar.

"Go!" he shouted.

Taylor went.

She ran toward the mechanical room because the loading ramp was watched and the laundry room was where they would expect her to return. A bullet struck the wall behind her with a dry crack. Another punched through a metal storage cabinet and sent a stack of baking sheets crashing across the floor.

The radio in her hand came alive.

"Security team, report."

Taylor hit the mechanical-room door, found it locked, and nearly broke her fingers trying the handle twice.

Hinges open toward you, McClane said. Utility cart behind you. Ram the latch side.

She seized the narrow cart stacked with folded aprons, swung it around, and drove its steel corner beside the lock. Once. The door flexed. Twice. The frame split enough to show darkness. On the third impact the latch tore out and the door banged inward.

Taylor hauled the cart through after her and wedged it under the handle.

The mechanical room rose two stories around her, all pipes, valves, and square ventilation trunks. Pumps throbbed beneath the floor. A steel stair climbed to a catwalk and disappeared over an enormous boiler. Heat pressed against her wet skin.

The radio spoke again.

"Security team. Report."

Then a different voice, clipped and impatient: "Cricket is moving to basement east. Nobody fires again unless they have the target. Mr. Anders wants her breathing."

Taylor stopped halfway to the stairs.

Her, she thought.

Yeah, McClane said. We're done pretending they don't know.

The door shook behind her. The cart's wheels squealed across the concrete.

Taylor climbed.

At the top of the catwalk she found a maintenance station bolted to the wall: building diagrams under scratched plastic, valve instructions, emergency numbers, and a gray phone with no dial.

No dial because it called only one place.

She lifted it.

It rang.

Once.

Twice.

"MedHall elevator emergency. State the nature of your emergency."

Taylor nearly laughed. Instead she pressed herself behind a ventilation trunk as the mechanical-room door jumped in its frame below.

"Hostage situation," she whispered. "MedHall gala. Empire Eighty-Eight capes inside. At least two hundred civilians. The security lines are cut and the cameras are looped."

"Ma'am, can you repeat your location?"

"MedHall. Downtown Brockton Bay. Send the PRT, but tell them not to come through the front doors. The host is running it. Max Anders is running it."

The door below burst inward. The cart went over with a crash.

"Ma'am? Who is speaking?"

Taylor saw the first gun barrel clear the doorway.

"Tell them the hostage is the one giving orders."

She dropped the phone and ran along the catwalk.

The first shots struck the railing behind her. Sparks jumped into the dark. Taylor ducked beneath a pipe, hit the far stair too fast, and half slid down it with one hand on the rail. Her swarm found a red wheel valve beside the boiler before her eyes did.

Steam? she asked.

Probably. Also probably hot enough to take skin off. Open it after you pass, not before.

She sent beetles into the wheel's spokes and added her own hand as she went by. The valve resisted, then turned with a shriek.

White vapor punched across the catwalk behind her.

The gunmen swore and stopped firing. One tried to push through, got close enough to feel the heat, and recoiled. Taylor reached the lower door on the far side and pulled it shut behind her.

Only then did she realize the radio was still in her hand.

Voices crowded it now.

"Police units on Grant."

"Two PRT transports behind them."

"No approach yet. Perimeter only."

A pause.

Then the same clipped voice as before.

"Boss says start the clock. Move the hostages away from the glass. Find the girl before the heroes decide to be stupid."

Taylor stood in another dark service corridor, bruised, wet, clutching an enemy radio while sirens rose beyond walls she could not see through.

You got the call out, McClane said.

Part of it.

Part's enough to get them here.

And if they don't believe it?

Outside, a loudspeaker came alive, its first words reduced by concrete to a low mechanical murmur.

Inside, every armed man in Taylor's borrowed map began moving at once.

Then we make them believe it, McClane said.
