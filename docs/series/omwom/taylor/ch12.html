Chapter 12 of 29

Regroup

They gave it ten minutes before either of them said anything worth saying, ten minutes of Taylor sitting still in a basement laundry room that smelled like bleach and warm cotton, half-buried in a bin of used tablecloths at the bottom of the chute, letting her heartbeat come down out of somewhere near her throat before she trusted herself to climb out and decide what came next.

She did not spend those minutes alone.

Footsteps entered the laundry room less than a minute after she landed. Taylor buried herself beneath the tablecloths, drew the nearest insects into stillness, and listened as two men opened cabinets, checked behind the pressing table, and shone a flashlight into the open mouth of the chute.

One beam swept across the cloth over her face. Light turned the weave bright enough for her to see individual fibers.

"Nothing," one man said.

"She came through here."

"Then she kept moving."

The first man kicked the bin. Taylor's shoulder struck its metal side. She locked her jaw before the sound could escape.

They searched for another three minutes. McClane spoke only once.

Breathe when they move. Hold when they stop.

She matched her lungs to their footsteps until the door closed behind them.

Okay. McClane's voice had leveled out, the tactical edge from earlier folded back down into something steadier, more patient. We can't just keep dodging guys in suits all night. Eventually one of them gets lucky, or he stops sending polite ones.

So what, we run? Get my dad and just leave?

Could. Wouldn't blame you for wanting to. No judgment in it, just an honest weighing of the option. But you leave now, with nothing but a hunch and some dead bugs, and he stays exactly what he's been all night. Respected. Untouchable. Free to do this again to somebody else's kid at the next gala he throws, and next time neither of us will be in the room to notice.

That's not fair.

No, McClane agreed. It's not. Doesn't make it less true.

"She's not a cop. She's a scared kid," Gladly said.

"She's also the one there," Fury answered.

The tablecloth bin made a strange kind of confessional, dark and warm and smelling of bleach, and Taylor let herself sit in it a full minute longer than she needed to, just breathing, just being a girl in a basement instead of whatever she'd been upstairs for the last hour. It didn't last. It never seemed to, lately.

She couldn't remain buried in a laundry bin all night, but leaving the basement would throw away the one position the search had already cleared. Taylor climbed out, brushed lint from her torn dress, and moved the rolling carts until they formed a narrow pocket between the wall and the pressing table. From there her bugs could still reach the dance floor while her body stayed out of the room entirely. So what do we do instead.

We stop playing defense. Something settled into his voice, a plan starting to take real shape instead of just reacting to the last five minutes. Right now all we've got is a hallway glance and a guy who's suddenly very interested in finding one anonymous teenager. That's not nothing, but it's not enough to do anything with either. Not enough to tell anybody. Not enough to make a case, if this city's even got anybody left worth making a case to.

You think it doesn't.

I think a city that lets a guy like this throw a charity gala and call it civic pride has bigger problems than one dirty host. Flat, matter-of-fact, no heat in it, just the accumulated weariness of thirty years watching institutions move too slow to matter. So we build something ourselves. Real. Concrete. Something that survives past tonight even if we don't get out of this room clean.

"That's what I recruited him for," O'Malley said, and the gallery went still around the quiet sentence.

Did you just call me a protagonist?

What? No. Didn't say anything. A beat, and she felt McClane go carefully, deliberately still, the same stillness he did right before a room stopped making sense to him either. You hear something?

I don't know. Somebody said "protagonist." It wasn't you. It wasn't me either.

"Quiet," Fury warned the gallery. "Bleedthrough, not a breach. Let it pass."

Taylor shook her head, hard, once, the way she'd shake water out of her ear, and the strange, borrowed sense of being watched by something that wasn't McClane and wasn't one of Anders's men either receded back into the general noise of the party, gone as suddenly as it had arrived.

Build it how? I can send bugs. That's it. That's my whole toolkit.

That's not nothing, kid. That's actually a lot, if you point it right. A pause, and she could feel him laying the shape of it out, piece by piece, the same fast, blunt cataloging he did with rooms, now turned on a problem instead of a space. You can go places a camera can't. You can listen where a microphone would get found. You want evidence on a guy like this, you don't need a smoking gun, you need patterns. Who he talks to. Who he avoids. What rooms he clears before he'll say anything real out loud. Something in his voice eased, just slightly, an idea landing that he clearly liked better than the last twenty minutes of them both standing out in the open. And you don't have to be the one standing there watching it happen. Stay somewhere quiet, somewhere nobody's got a reason to look for a nervous teenager, and let the bugs do the actual work. You're not the one who needs to be brave tonight. They are.

What am I actually looking for.

Whatever doesn't fit. He said it like it was obvious, and maybe, to a man with thirty years of practice, it was. A room full of people is a room full of stories that are all supposed to make sense together. Rich people at a charity gala, staff working an event, a host being a host. You're looking for the piece that's telling a different story than everybody else. Might be a door that gets checked too often. Might be a guy who doesn't quite fit his own uniform. You'll know it when you see it, same as you knew that hallway glance meant something before you could've told me why.

And if we find something?

Then we've got leverage instead of a hunch. Leverage is a different animal. Leverage you can use. Something almost like anticipation crept into the flatness, the energy of a man who'd spent thirty years building cases out of exactly this kind of patient, unglamorous work. We're not trying to beat him tonight. We're trying to make sure tonight isn't the only night this ever mattered.

Taylor watched the room for a long moment. Danny remained near the bar because the event coordinator had asked him to wait there while staff checked the restrooms for her. His drink sat untouched. Every thirty seconds his attention moved from the kitchen doors to the main entrance to the stairs, searching. The men in dark suits remained spread along the walls like furniture nobody had asked for, and Max Anders stood somewhere in the crowd wearing a smile that had stopped, hours ago now, meaning anything at all. It was strange how quickly a room could stop looking like a party and start looking like a battlefield with better lighting, and stranger still how easily she'd made that switch herself, somewhere in the last two hours, without ever quite noticing the exact moment it happened. Two hundred people still moved through it, still laughed, still balanced canapés on little napkins, entirely unaware that the building around them had quietly become something closer to a chessboard, every one of them a piece whose position mattered to somebody, whether they knew it or not.

Taylor found a laundry inventory clipboard and turned the last page over. She drew the atrium first, then the kitchen, service corridor, loading dock, main doors, east doors, and the staircase. McClane corrected proportions when her memory compressed distances. Taylor added the chute and the basement rooms her bugs could reach.

You draw every room you know, he said. Then you mark every room you don't. Unknown space is where plans go to die.

The blank rectangle behind the locked service door became the largest question on the page.

"Gib, you got anything useful, or are you just gonna keep making Whitman jokes," Fury said, dry.

"I contain layers," Gib said. "Some of them are useful. This isn't one of the useful ones."

"Shocking."

Okay, Taylor said, and felt something steady settle into her chest, not confidence exactly, but the shape of it, close enough to work with. Where do we start.

Same place every good case starts. McClane's voice had gone almost warm, the satisfaction of a man handing a rookie their first real assignment. We watch. Careful, quiet, patient. And we let him think he's still the only one in this room who knows what's actually going on.
