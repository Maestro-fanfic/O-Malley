Traffic Control

The mezzanine lounge was still trapped when Taylor felt for it.

A dozen guests, maybe more, the same well-dressed inner circle she'd cataloged from a laundry room an hour ago, backed against the railing with nowhere obvious to go, the main staircase down to the atrium choked solid with the general evacuation and the service stairs on the far side blocked by something Taylor couldn't immediately place, just a straggler's confused, jumbled sense of a hallway gone suddenly impassable.

That's not panic blocking that hallway, McClane said, sharp, already reading it. That's deliberate. Somebody wants that floor sealed.

Why would he seal in his own people?

Maybe he's not sealing them in. Maybe he's sealing everyone else out, so his own crew can work that floor without two hundred witnesses wandering through it. Grim, certain. Either way, that's a dozen civilians about to be very alone with whatever's about to happen up there.

"Not our problem," Fury said, flat. "You've got your own exit to make. Cricket's still out there somewhere."

"You say that," Gib said, dry, "like you actually believe it."

Fury didn't answer that one.

Taylor was already reaching before she'd finished deciding to, threading a thin scouting line of bugs down the blocked service corridor, patient, careful, until she found the actual obstruction: not a person, not a barricade, just an overturned linen cart and a fallen coat rack, knocked sideways in the general chaos and wedged tight enough across the narrow hallway to stop a human body cold.

They can move that.

If they know where to push.

Taylor ran insects over the obstruction. The linen cart's two forward casters had swiveled sideways. One brake was locked. The coat rack lay across its top with a torn garment bag wound around both frames, binding them together.

She gathered the swarm on the locked caster first, packed dark bodies around the small brake pedal, then drew a line up the cart's side to the twisted garment bag.

The barefoot woman reached the blockage and stopped.

"The bugs are on the wheel," someone behind her said.

"I can see that."

She crouched, found the brake beneath the swarm, and kicked it free. The cart shifted an inch. Two men pushed from behind while she pulled the garment bag loose. The coat rack rolled, slid off the cart, and struck the wall. The three of them hauled the cart backward until a shoulder-wide gap opened beside it.

Taylor moved the bugs through the opening.

The barefoot woman understood. She went first.

"She didn't move it," the gallery said. "She showed twelve frightened people how."

Now what, McClane asked, already moving past the win to the next problem. You cleared a path. They don't know it's clear. You gonna walk up there and give a safety briefing?

No. Taylor thought fast, turning the shape of the problem over the way he'd taught her to. But I can make the path obvious. Same way a runway's obvious, if you know what you're looking for.

She worked quickly, sending a thin, visible trail of bugs skittering along the baseboard of the now-clear corridor, deliberate this time, not hiding, a moving line pointed unmistakably toward the service stairs beyond it. It wasn't subtle. It wasn't supposed to be. Twelve terrified people weren't going to follow a hunch. They needed something that looked like an answer.

Lights on a runway, McClane said, and there was real warmth under the observation now, something almost like wonder. You're building yourself an actual flight path. That's not just clever, kid. That's got style.

Someone on the mezzanine screamed at the sight of it, predictably, and then, a beat later, someone else, sharper-eyed or simply more desperate, said the word "look" instead, and pointed, and the small, frightened cluster of guests began, hesitant and disbelieving, to follow a trail of insects toward a door they wouldn't have found on their own in time.

The first man refused to step over them. "Those things are being controlled."

"Obviously," said the woman behind him. She had removed her heels and held one in each hand. "Which means somebody knows where they're going. Do you?"

He did not.

She stepped onto the service corridor.

The others followed because a person moving with confidence was easier to trust than a miracle made of beetles. Taylor adjusted the trail around broken glass, down the narrow hall, and through the door she had cleared. At every turn she gathered the swarm into an arrow before spreading it along the next wall.

Halfway to the stairs, the overhead lights went out.

Red emergency strobes continued pulsing along the walls, bright enough to show fragments and dark enough to ruin depth. The group stopped. Someone began praying.

Taylor gathered the swarm into a thick arrow on the pale wall beneath the next strobe. Red light flashed over it. The arrow vanished into gray, then returned with the next pulse, pointing toward the turn.

At the corner she dissolved it and formed another beneath the following light.

The barefoot woman took the front again.

"Keep a hand on the person ahead of you," she said. "If the bugs turn, we turn."

Twelve strangers became a chain and moved through the dark.

"They're following the bugs," Gladly said.

"They're following the woman who understood them," Fury corrected.

Taylor held the trail steady, patient, careful, feeling strain born not from numbers but from the sustained concentration of holding one clean line of intention through a building full of chaos. She did not let it waver until the last mezzanine guest had cleared the service stairs and vanished from her borrowed sense entirely, safe, or at least safer, headed toward an exit that hadn't existed thirty seconds ago.

She didn't have time to feel good about it. Somewhere behind her, close enough now that she felt it as much as sensed it, something heavy hit the floor of the main hall hard enough to rattle a table three rooms away.

That's not Cricket.

No. McClane's voice had gone very flat, very careful. That's the metal one. Hookwolf. And he's not being subtle about it anymore.

Taylor caught the shape of him through a straggler near the atrium, moving through the wreckage of an abandoned dessert table with the unhurried, deliberate weight of something that had stopped pretending to be in a hurry, silver spines catching the low emergency lighting in long, patient ripples.

He's not chasing one person. He's just... breaking things. Why's he just breaking things.

Because it works. Grim, honest. Because a guy that size walking slow through a room full of overturned furniture doesn't need to run. Everybody in his path clears it for him. That's its own kind of hunting.

He was heading, unmistakably, toward the service corridor Taylor herself needed. Not toward her specifically, not yet, just toward the same geometry of the building she needed to use, and the two facts were about to become the same problem within a matter of seconds.

Options, she said, fast, no time left for anything but the shortest version of the question.

You don't fight that. Full stop, no negotiation on that point. McClane's mind was already three steps ahead, she could feel it, the same fast, blunt cataloging he did with everything, now scaled up to match the size of the actual threat. But he's slow, methodical, working through a room like a man clearing rubble. That means predictable. That means you don't need to beat him. You just need him looking at something else for four seconds.

Like what.

Like every catering cart still standing in that kitchen.

Taylor understood the shape before he'd finished explaining. The kitchen doors stood behind Hookwolf. Beyond them, three loaded service carts waited on the shallow ramp to the corridor, brakes set, abandoned when the takeover began.

No insect could release those brakes or put several hundred pounds of cart into motion.

Taylor could.

She sent the remaining bugs high through the steam, not toward Hookwolf but around his head, a ragged veil that made him raise one bladed arm and turn toward the motion. Taylor used the instant to slip through the opposite kitchen door.

The first cart's brake lever resisted beneath her shoe. She stamped twice. It released with a metal crack.

Taylor shoved.

The cart barely moved.

She put her shoulder against its warm steel side, shoes sliding on wet tile, and drove with both legs. The wheels crossed the ramp's lip. Gravity took the weight away from her.

The loaded cart rolled into the corridor and crashed directly into Hookwolf's path with a sound like the whole kitchen falling down at once.

The first cart struck sideways and spilled racks of glasses beneath him. Hookwolf's next step crushed them into glittering powder.

Taylor stamped the brake on a second cart loaded with coffee urns. A line cook sheltering behind the prep counter saw what she was doing, crossed the kitchen in three strides, and shoved with both hands. Taylor added her shoulder for the first yard. The cart gathered speed down the service ramp, hit the first, and turned it into a wall of steel trays, spilled coffee, and steam.

Hookwolf tore through the collision rather than avoiding it. Metal spines shredded both carts. Steam rolled across the corridor and hid him for two seconds.

Two seconds were more than Taylor needed now.

Hookwolf drove forward through the wreckage, both bladed arms occupied shredding steel out of his path. Taylor went the other direction. She slipped behind the steam, crossed the gap between his turning radius and the wall, and reached the linen-closet side of the corridor before he finished tearing the second cart apart. She passed closer to a monster than she'd ever been in her life and moved too fast, too focused, to feel the full weight of that until she was beyond him.

"Twice in two chapters," the gallery said. "She never fought either cape. She changed what they had to look at."

"That's not a fluke anymore," Fury said, and for once there was no dryness in the observation at all, just plain, weighty fact. "That's a whole toolkit."

You did good up there, McClane said, quiet, urgent underneath it, a man saying it now because he wasn't sure how much longer there'd be breath to spare for saying it at all. Real good. I mean that.

I got lucky twice.

You got smart twice, in two completely different ways, against two completely different problems, inside of maybe ninety seconds. Something almost fierce crept into the words. Luck doesn't repeat like that, kid. Skill does.

Taylor didn't have breath left to argue with him. She made it to the linen closet with her lungs burning and her hands shaking for reasons that had stopped being about cold bugs an hour ago, and didn't let herself stop, didn't let herself think, threw herself into the chute already falling before she'd fully committed to it, dark and cotton and gravity taking her the rest of the way down.
