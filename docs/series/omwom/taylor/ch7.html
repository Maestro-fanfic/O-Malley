Chapter 7 of 29

Holding Pattern

The toast came without warning, which McClane said afterward was exactly the point.

One moment the quartet was halfway through a waltz and waiters were replacing the dinner plates with coffee cups. The next, the musicians stopped on the same beat, the coffee service withdrew, and the lights above the guest tables dimmed while the stage remained bright. The room had been cued without a visible cue.

Taylor caught four people responding before the first guest looked up. The man at the east doors turned his body toward the crowd. The woman near the kitchen shifted closer to the service corridor. Both men on the mezzanine stopped pretending to converse. Max Anders passed his empty glass to a waiter without looking and stepped onto the stage.

Rehearsed, McClane said. Not the speech. The room around it.

The change kept spreading after Anders reached the stage. A woman at the silent-auction table closed the lid of the cash box and slid it beneath her chair. Two MedHall board members near the front turned their name cards outward, suddenly attentive to where the photographer stood. At the back of the atrium, a young man with a reporter's notebook abandoned a conversation in the middle of a sentence and found a clear line of sight between two floral arrangements.

The guests had not merely become an audience. They had arranged themselves into the version of the audience they expected to appear in tomorrow's photographs.

Danny straightened his tie.

"Do I need to stand somewhere?" Taylor asked.

"No. God, no." He glanced toward the stage, then at the board chair beckoning him closer with two fingers. "Apparently I need to stand somewhere. Stay here?"

Taylor caught his sleeve before he could move. She did not know why until she had already done it.

Danny looked down at her hand, then at her face.

"They can see you fine from here," she said.

It was a weak excuse. He knew it. She knew he knew it. But the board chair had already turned away to make room for a councilwoman, and Danny let the moment pass.

"Fine by me," he said. "I didn't come here to decorate the stage."

He stayed beside her.

Good instinct, McClane said.

I didn't have an instinct. I just didn't want him closer to Anders.

That's an instinct. You don't have to like where it came from for it to count.

Someone dimmed the atrium lights by half, and Max Anders climbed two steps onto the small stage where the string quartet had been working all evening, glass in hand, easy and unhurried in the way of a man who'd given this exact speech, or one close enough to it, more times than he could count. Two hundred conversations folded down into one, all at once, the hush a room full of powerful people made when they'd collectively decided somebody else was worth listening to for a minute, waiters going still along the walls, even the string quartet lowering their bows in practiced unison, and for one long beat the whole vast, glittering atrium turned to face a single small man on a stage no bigger than a dinner table.

Here we go, McClane said. Watch him. Not what he says. Where his eyes go while he's saying it.

"I won't keep you from the chocolate fountain long," Anders said, and the room laughed, warm, on cue, the laugh a room always gave a rich man for a joke that wasn't actually funny. "I made that promise to the planning committee in writing, and I have been advised that several attorneys are present to enforce it."

The second laugh came easier. Anders waited exactly long enough to let it crest.

"I just wanted to say, before the night gets away from us, how much it means to have all of you here. This building does real work. The kind that doesn't always make the papers. A child gets an inhaler before an emergency-room visit becomes an overnight stay. A woman who has missed three appointments because the bus route changed gets seen at a neighborhood clinic instead. A dockworker who has spent twenty years being told that pain is part of the job gets a physician who asks a second question. None of those moments belongs on a plaque. All of them are why this place exists."

His eyes found each constituency as he named it. Hospital staff for the child. The council table for the bus route. Danny and the union contingent for the dockworker. It felt spontaneous until Taylor noticed how neatly every example placed gratitude at a different table.

"And it does that work because people like you, and people like Danny Hebert back there, believe a city's worth investing in, even the parts of it that don't always believe in themselves."

He raised his glass toward the silent-auction display. "By tomorrow morning, every pledge and outreach commitment represented here will enter independent state review before becoming part of the public trust. Transparency is not an obstacle to good work. It is how good work earns the right to continue."

That drew the serious applause, board members and city officials responding to the word transparency as if Anders had personally invented it. Taylor saw his gaze touch the loading-corridor entrance before returning to the room.

Danny, three feet from Taylor, went faintly pink, embarrassed and proud at once, the way he always got when someone praised him in public he genuinely admired, and Taylor watched a dozen heads turn his way, polite, brief, attention he'd have hated if it lasted one second longer than it did.

The photographer got the picture anyway. Danny caught mid-blink, one hand still touching the knot of his tie, Taylor beside him with her face arranged into something she hoped would pass for a daughter tolerating her father's public embarrassment.

Anders had made Danny part of the building's moral architecture in twenty words. Tomorrow, anyone reading about the review would see a union man standing in the room when it was announced. Someone honest enough to make the promise look honest merely by having heard it.

He just used Dad as a character reference, Taylor thought.

Without asking him. That's how guys like this get the best references.

At the auction table, the woman with the cash box passed a slim blue folder to one of the board members. Taylor had seen identical folders beside the pledge cards earlier. This one carried a white state seal and a red strip across the corner. The board member opened it only far enough to confirm the contents, then tucked it beneath his program.

Onstage, Anders saw the exchange. His smile never changed.

"Now, I know it's been a strange night for security," Anders went on, and something in his voice shifted, light, almost joking, but Taylor felt McClane go very still inside her, the way a man went still right before he stopped breathing loudly on purpose. "Seems we've got some unwelcome guests. Six-legged ones. My events team assures me it's nothing, probably just the cold driving them in through a door somebody forgot to shut. But if anyone spots one of our little uninvited friends, do let a staff member know. We'd hate for anyone to feel, ah, watched, at their own charity gala."

The laugh that followed was smaller this time, uncertain, the kind of laugh a room gave when it wasn't sure the joke had actually been a joke. Somewhere near the bar, a woman in a green dress murmured something to her husband and got a shrug in return, the universal gesture for a rich man's joke that wasn't worth the energy of actually parsing.

Taylor felt the security staff watching the laugh rather than sharing it. One man near the coat check tracked every guest who looked toward the loading corridor. Another watched the people who did not laugh at all. The woman near the kitchen studied hands instead of faces, looking for a phone disappearing into a purse, a napkin being folded too carefully, fingers tightening around a glass. One of the men on the mezzanine watched the reporter at the back. The other watched the room reflected in the dark glass above the stage.

The toast was not only bait. It was a camera with human eyes, six angles, and a staff trained to notice what embarrassment looked like when someone was trying to call it boredom.

McClane walked Taylor through them as Anders let the laughter fade.

Coat-check guy has the loading corridor. Kitchen woman has hands and phones. Mezzanine left has the press. Right one's using the windows so he can watch without turning his head. East-door guy's watching the people who looked for an exit after the bug line.

And the sixth?

Hasn't moved. That's the one I don't like.

Taylor found him at last beside the sound controls, dressed like a technician, one hand resting on a closed equipment case. He was not watching the guests. He was watching the watchers, checking that every other pair of eyes stayed on task.

Anders had built a chain of observation into the room. Taylor sat at the far end of it, unseen for now, feeling each link pull tight.

Danny leaned toward Taylor. "Little pointed for a bug joke."

She turned to him too quickly. "You noticed?"

"Hard not to. Guy spends all night pretending everything's effortless, then uses a charity toast to complain about insects." Danny lifted one shoulder. "Maybe he's got strong feelings about pest control."

The joke did not hide his unease from her. Taylor wanted to tell him that the insects were hers, that Anders had found them, that the men at the doors were counting reactions. The truth assembled itself behind her teeth and stopped there.

"Probably," she said.

Danny looked at her for another second. Before he could ask the question forming behind his eyes, the applause began and forced them both to face the stage.

"Taylor."

He had not raised his voice. That made it worse.

"What?"

"You've spent half the night watching doors. Now the man giving the toast makes a joke about being watched and you look like somebody read your diary out loud."

She could have told him. Three words would have done it. The bugs are mine. Five more could have made the danger clear. The people here know something.

Then he would look. Not because he was careless, but because he was her father and she had just told him a threat existed in the same room as his daughter. He would look at Anders, the doors, the men pretending to be waiters. Security would see a middle-aged union officer begin assessing exits five seconds after the bait landed.

Taylor made herself glance toward the photographer instead.

"I hate having my picture taken."

Danny followed her gaze. The photographer lowered his camera and moved toward the council table.

"You could've warned me before I dragged you to an event with a hired photographer."

"I did. Repeatedly. I used the phrase 'I'd rather chew glass.'"

"I thought that was about the dress."

"It was a flexible objection."

His mouth twitched. He did not stop being worried, but he accepted the exit she had given him. That, too, hurt.

Everybody's already forgetting he said it, McClane observed. That's the real trick of a room like this. Say something true, wrap it in enough charm, and it evaporates in about four seconds.

Not for us.

No, he agreed. Not for us.

Anders's eyes moved once, slow, deliberate, across the crowd, not lingering anywhere long enough to be obvious, a practiced sweep that could have passed for nothing more than a host checking his audience's mood.

They passed over Taylor for exactly as long as they passed over everyone else.

Don't react, McClane said, fast, low, urgent in a way that cut clean through her own instinct to do exactly the opposite. Whatever you're feeling right now, bury it. Smile at your dad. Look bored. This is the test. This is him fishing, wide, seeing who flinches.

Taylor smiled at her dad. It felt like lifting something heavy.

Danny smiled back, uncertain now. Taylor made herself roll her eyes toward the stage, the universal expression of a teenager trapped at an adult function. His shoulders eased. The movement hurt more than holding still had.

Anders's gaze passed them. It did not pause.

At the east doors, one of his men touched his earpiece and relaxed.

"There it is," the gallery murmured. "The engineered moment. He's not looking for the culprit. He's looking for the flinch."

"I'm flinching," Gladly admitted. "Internally. Constantly."

"You don't have a body, Gladly."

"I flinch on principle."

Anders finished his toast a moment later, warm, gracious, thanking the room again, stepping down off the stage to scattered applause and the low murmur of two hundred conversations picking back up where they'd left off. He didn't look toward Taylor again. He didn't look toward anyone in particular, which was, McClane pointed out grimly, exactly the problem.

He did not vanish into the board members waiting for him. He worked the room.

Anders stopped first at the nurses' table, where he remembered one woman's husband's name and asked another about a grant application that had stalled in committee. He crossed to the city delegation and took a joke from the deputy mayor with the patient smile of a man granting the joke an audience. He touched the reporter's elbow, said something too quiet for Taylor's remaining insects to catch, and left the young man smiling despite the fact that his notebook had somehow ended up closed.

Every stop looked casual. Every stop lasted less than a minute. Every stop left the person he had spoken to facing away from whichever doorway they had been nearest.

Then he came toward Danny.

Don't leave, McClane said.

Taylor had not realized she was shifting her weight until he said it.

Why not?

Because your dad stays if you go, and then you can't hear what Anders asks him. Because walking away from the host coming to thank your table looks weird. Because right now you're not a suspect. You're Danny Hebert's bored kid. Be Danny Hebert's bored kid.

Anders was intercepted twice on the way. A physician wanted thirty seconds. A board member touched the blue audit folder beneath his program and received the smallest possible shake of Anders's head. Not here. Not yet. Then Anders arrived with both hands empty and his attention apparently devoted to Danny alone.

"Danny. Forgive the ambush."

"You could've picked somebody who photographs better."

"I did. She was standing next to you."

Anders turned the smile toward Taylor. It was polished, harmless, and supported by four separate members of his security staff watching the surrounding crowd.

"Taylor, isn't it? Your father tells me you have inherited his patience for formal events."

"He says a lot of things when he's trying to get me into a dress."

Danny made a sound halfway between a cough and a laugh.

Anders laughed too, and Taylor hated that it sounded genuine. "A sensible position. I have spent twenty years trying to make these evenings shorter and somehow only succeeded in making them more expensive."

Up close, there was nothing visibly predatory about him. No unusual stillness. No cold eyes waiting behind the friendly ones. His tuxedo fit perfectly, but not ostentatiously. His cuff links were plain silver. He gave Danny the courtesy of standing at an angle that included Taylor without forcing her into the conversation. If Taylor had met him yesterday, she might have found him reassuring.

That's the package, McClane said. Remember the package isn't the product.

Anders looked back to Danny. "I meant what I said. Community labor made the outreach program possible, and your people caught two design failures before the first clinic opened. The review team should hear that from you, if you're willing."

Danny's expression changed. Praise embarrassed him. Work did not.

"They can hear it. They should also hear the wheelchair ramp at the Mason Street clinic still freezes before the sidewalk does."

"Then I hope you tell them before anyone else does." Anders nodded toward the board member carrying the blue folder. "Nine tomorrow morning. Conference suite on six. No speeches, no cameras, and if anyone tries to turn it into either, you have my permission to leave."

The offer was so exactly shaped for Danny that Taylor could feel the craftsmanship in it. Respect the work. Distrust ceremony. Put the grievance on the record. Anders did not flatter people by telling them what he liked about them. He flattered them by proving he knew what they liked about themselves.

"I'll be there," Danny said.

"Good." Anders offered his hand. Danny took it. "Enjoy the rest of the evening. Both of you. And if our tiny trespassers make another appearance, I promise the building can survive the scandal."

He gave Taylor no extra glance when he said it. No pause, no concealed emphasis. The words were a callback to the toast, available to anyone who heard them.

Taylor still felt the hair rise along both arms.

"We'll keep our eyes open," Danny said.

Dad, no.

Anders smiled. "That's all any of us can do."

Then he moved on.

Taylor held herself still until he had crossed three conversation clusters and the technician beside the sound controls had looked away.

Did he know?

No, McClane said after a moment. He knew your name because your dad's on his program and you came in together. He came over because he just borrowed your dad's reputation in front of a room. Thanking him sells the gesture. The bug line at the end was him keeping the bait in the water, not setting the hook in you.

You're sure.

I'm sure enough to act on it. That's not the same thing as forever.

Danny watched Anders go. "He remembers too much."

Taylor looked at him.

"Names, projects, the ramp. People who are good at that make me nervous." Danny picked up his abandoned drink and frowned when he found the ice melted. "Nobody's that interested in everybody."

For one bright second Taylor thought he had seen all of it.

Then he added, "Still, if he gets the ramp fixed, he can be as unsettling as he wants."

The hope went out quietly. Taylor hated herself for feeling relieved.

He's not looking for you specifically. Not yet. McClane's voice had gone flat, careful, the tone of a man laying out a threat assessment he didn't like the shape of. He's looking for a reaction. Anybody's reaction. That toast wasn't a joke, it was bait, dropped in front of two hundred people to see who bit.

Did anybody?

Not that I could see. Which either means nobody in this room's onto him but us, or it means whoever's actually watching him back is a lot better at holding still than the average party guest. A pause, and something almost like grudging respect crept into the words. Which, for what it's worth, you just proved you can do. He fished. You didn't take it. That's not nothing.

So he doesn't know it's me.

He doesn't know it's you. McClane's certainty had an edge to it now, careful, provisional, the certainty of a man who knew exactly how fast a read like that could go stale. He knows something's off. He doesn't know what, or who, or how much they've actually seen. That's a wide net, kid, and wide nets catch a lot of nothing before they catch the one thing they're actually after.

That's supposed to make me feel better?

It's supposed to make you careful. No warmth in it now, just weight, blunt in the way of a man who'd stopped softening things the moment softening stopped being useful. Wide net now becomes a narrow one fast, the second he gets one real data point to work from. Right now you're a needle in two hundred haystacks. Give him a reason to start counting fewer haystacks, and that math gets a lot worse for you, a lot faster than you want it to.

Taylor looked across the room, once, carefully, and found Max Anders already deep in conversation with a city councilman, laughing at something, easy, warm, a man with nothing on his mind heavier than chocolate fountains and charity math. Behind him, through the tall glass windows overlooking the parking structure, she caught a flash of headlights sweeping across the lot, some latecomer's car pulling in, and for one irrational second her whole body went tight with the certainty that it meant something, before she made herself breathe and let it go. Not everything tonight was a signal. Just most things.

She didn't believe it for a second. Neither, she was fairly sure, did he believe his own performance of it, not entirely, not underneath.

It was a strange thing, watching a man be that good at something so small. Charity math. Chocolate fountains. A whole life's worth of practice spent making a room feel comfortable, and all of it, every warm word and every practiced laugh, sitting on top of whatever he actually was the way a tablecloth sat on top of a table somebody had built to hold considerably more weight than dinner.

"I don't know how I feel about a villain this good at his own toast," the newer voice said. "I almost expect a beautiful woman to step out for a tango."

"That's usually the kind you should worry about most," Fury said. "The ones who never stop performing long enough to slip."

The quartet started up again, something lighter this time, and the party closed back over the moment the way water closed over a stone, smooth, unbroken, as if nothing at all had happened, as if two hundred people hadn't just watched their gracious host cast a net none of them had noticed was a net at all.

Taylor kept smiling. It was, she was starting to realize, going to be a very long night.
