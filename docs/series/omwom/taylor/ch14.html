Chapter 14 of 29

Wind Shear

The music stopped for the second time that night, and this time nobody had to tell Taylor it wasn't an accident.

Max Anders climbed back onto the little stage, and whatever composure the horn player had clawed back over the last hour, he lost it again just watching his host approach, flinching like a man who'd been personally involved in something he'd rather not have Anders think too hard about.

Here we go again, McClane said, already tight, already reading the room a half-second ahead of the words. Watch his hands this time, not just his face.

"Folks, I'm so sorry to interrupt again." Anders's voice carried the same easy warmth as before, but there was something underneath it now, thinner, stretched further than it had been an hour ago. "It seems we've had a small security matter come up, nothing to worry about, but I'm going to ask everyone to stay in the main hall for just a little while longer. My team's going to do a quick walkthrough, purely routine, and then we'll get right back to the evening."

"That's not routine," Gib said, flat, all the earlier dryness gone out of him. "That's a sweep. He's about to have his people walk this room guest by guest."

He can't do that. Taylor's breath had gone thin, careful, even from the safety of the laundry room, hearing it happen one floor up through nothing but a single straggler bug's borrowed sense of the room. Two hundred people. He can't just search two hundred people at his own charity gala.

He's not searching two hundred people. McClane's voice had the flat, fast clarity of a man watching a plan take a shape he recognized. He's watching two hundred people get searched, and cataloging who looks nervous about it. That's not security theater, that's a net closing slow enough that nobody screams about it until it's already closed.

What do I do.

Nothing, yet. You're not in that room. That's the one good thing to come out of you being down here. A beat, weighing it. But your dad is.

Taylor's stomach dropped, cold and total, the laundry room's bleach smell suddenly sharper than it had been a second ago. Danny.

He's not a target. Not specifically. But he's about to get patted down by a guy pretending to be a rent-a-cop, same as everybody else in that room, and he doesn't know what kind of wrong he's standing in. People working from the wrong picture ask dangerous questions at dangerous moments.

Taylor was moving before she'd finished deciding to, up out of the gap between the cart and the wall, toward the chute, toward the stairs, toward a room she'd worked so hard to disappear from an hour ago.

Slow down. Not a command this time, something closer to a hand on her shoulder. You go up there panicking, you undo everything the last hour bought you. Think. What do you actually need to happen.

I need my dad to be fine.

He will be, if you let the sweep do what it's built to do, which is find nothing on a union rep who showed up with an invitation and a clean conscience. McClane's voice had gone steady, deliberate, walking her back from the edge of something neither of them wanted to name outright. What you need is eyes in that room without your body in it. You've still got bugs up there. Use them. Watch him get searched from a safe distance instead of walking back into the one room where showing up flustered actually gets you noticed.

Taylor stopped at the base of the chute, one hand on cold metal, and made herself breathe, slow, the way she'd been making herself breathe all night. Her whole body still wanted to climb, every instinct she had screaming at her to be in the same room as her father right now, close enough to actually do something if something went wrong instead of watching it happen through a bug's flat, distant sense of the world.

I know, McClane said, quiet, like he'd heard the want in it even without her saying anything. I know exactly what that feels like. Doesn't make it the wrong call to stay put.

Okay. She closed her eyes, reached, found the handful of stragglers still drifting near the bar, and settled them, carefully, into a loose ring around where Danny stood, close enough to warn her, not close enough to be noticed by anyone looking for exactly that kind of thing. Okay. I'm watching.

The sweep took eleven minutes, unhurried, thorough, six men in dark suits working the room in loose, overlapping patterns that never quite looked like a search unless you already knew what a search looked like. Taylor watched it happen across a dozen borrowed vantage points at once, a mosaic of small moments stitched together into one long, unbearable eleven minutes: a woman in a red dress laughing off a search of her clutch, an elderly man patting his own pockets helpfully before he was even asked, the horn player himself still visibly rattled from earlier, going stiff and pale the second one of the suited men approached his little stage. Two hundred people, one at a time, each of them a small, separate held breath she couldn't do anything about except watch. They started at the edges and worked inward, polite, apologetic, reciting a practiced script about insurance requirements and unfortunate necessity. The performance would have satisfied Taylor completely an hour ago. Now, through borrowed bug-eyes, it read as rehearsed as everything else tonight.

They were not looking for a weapon. Taylor understood that after the fourth handbag.

Weapons received a glance. Phones received attention. Each man checked recent calls, camera screens, unsent messages, and the warmth of the device before returning it. At the silent-auction table, one of them opened a donor's text conversation and scrolled far enough to earn an offended protest. He apologized, blamed a signal-interference concern, and watched the protester rather than the phone.

They were looking for communication leaving the building.

One guest refused.

He was a defense attorney Taylor recognized from television, silver-haired and loud enough that people made space for his voice by habit. When the suited man asked for his phone, he held it against his chest and demanded to know under whose authority a private security contractor intended to search privileged communications.

Conversation died around him in widening rings.

Anders stepped down from the stage before the confrontation could become a scene. "Gerald, you're absolutely right," he said, warm and apologetic. "This has been handled poorly. Nobody is entitled to your private messages."

Relief moved through the nearby guests. The attorney lowered the phone.

Anders held out his hand. "Let me keep it secure until the walkthrough is finished. Personally. Then there can be no question of anyone accessing it."

The attorney surrendered the device to the host he trusted.

Anders passed it behind his back without looking. The suited man carried it away.

He just won the argument and got the phone, Taylor thought.

That's why he's the guy on stage and the others are carrying guns under their jackets.

The search expanded into the service levels while the guest sweep continued. A pair of men checked the kitchen, dry storage, and staff lockers. Another descended the basement stairs.

Taylor felt him through insects in the cinderblock wall.

He's coming back to laundry.

Then we make laundry look searched, McClane said.

She left the bin overturned from the first visit. She scattered tablecloths toward the opposite door, opened a supply cabinet, and sent roaches moving down the corridor beyond it. Then she climbed onto the pressing table, pushed a ceiling tile aside, and folded herself into the narrow space above it.

The man entered thirty seconds later.

He followed the false trail through the laundry room, saw the open cabinet and disturbed cloth, and continued toward the mechanical corridor. Taylor lay across two steel supports above his head, dust against her face, one hand clamped over her mouth while the ceiling tile bowed beneath her hip.

He stopped in the doorway.

Turned.

Looked up.

A spider crossed the light fixture. Taylor sent it down on a thread past his face.

He swore, batted it away, and left.

Taylor remained above the ceiling until her arms began to tremble.

Danny stood where the coordinator had repeatedly returned him, no longer patient and not remotely confused. He submitted to the light, professional pat-down because refusing would cost time he wanted spent finding Taylor. He even made a joke about hoping they'd at least buy him dinner first, an automatic line deployed without humor, and the man's professional smile didn't crack, not even a little, which told Taylor more about him than anything he'd actually done with his hands.

"Phone," the man said.

Danny handed it over.

The screen showed three missed calls, all from the same number. Their home phone. Taylor had not made them.

Danny frowned. "That's odd."

The suited man's thumb stopped above the call log. "Expecting someone?"

"My daughter came with me. Nobody should be home."

Taylor felt the trap open beneath a sentence Danny had no reason not to say.

"Where is your daughter now?"

Danny looked around the room. His gaze found the coordinator, the main doors, and the kitchen in a sequence Taylor had already watched him repeat six times.

"Bathroom, probably." He took the phone back before the man offered it. "She's fifteen. Keeping me informed isn't high on the list."

The joke sounded better than the first one because Danny wanted it believed. He knew Taylor was not in either bathroom. He had been asking staff to find her for nearly an hour. The lie came without preparation, offered to a stranger who had become too interested in where his daughter was.

The suited man touched his earpiece as he moved away.

They made those calls, Taylor thought.

Yeah. Wanted to see who noticed, who looked for somebody missing, who gave them a relationship they could trace.

And Dad just gave them me.

No. They already suspected. Your dad just told them he doesn't trust the question. He lied for you without knowing why. Keep that distinction. It matters.

Outside, through the tall windows, Taylor caught the slow, deliberate sweep of a flashlight crossing the parking structure again, and this time a second beam joined it, the two of them converging near the loading dock before the transmission crackled once, brief and low, and both lights winked out at the same moment, satisfied with whatever they'd found. Or hadn't.

He's fine, McClane said, once the man moved on to the next guest. Told you.

This time.

This time, McClane agreed, no false comfort in it. Which means we're out of this room's worth of patience. Whatever we're building, kid, we're building it faster now. Not because I want to rush you. Because he's not gonna give us a slow night twice.

"There it is," the gallery said, all lightness gone. "The clock was always running. Now everybody in the room knows it."
