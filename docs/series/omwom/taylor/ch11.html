Chapter 11 of 29

No-Fly Zone

Whatever quiet satisfaction the ruined horn note had bought her lasted exactly as long as it took Max Anders to stop smiling at the mayor's wife.

Taylor caught it from across the room, a flicker, brief and total, the practiced warmth dropping off his face for less than a second while his eyes swept the crowd once, hard, methodical, before the smile reassembled itself like nothing had happened at all.

He knows. McClane's voice had gone very quiet. Not you. Not yet. But he knows something just moved through his room that shouldn't have, and he knows two of his own people just lost whoever they were chasing.

How do you know that's what happened.

Because that's the face of a man whose plan just failed and who's already three steps into the next one. No hesitation in it, no doubt, the flat certainty of someone who'd worn that exact expression himself, more than once, in rooms a lot less comfortable than this one. Watch. He's not gonna panic. He's gonna adjust.

Anders adjusted.

The first person to test the change was an elderly donor in a tuxedo who reached the main doors with his coat already over one arm. The coat-check man stepped in front of him smiling, listened to a complaint about an early flight, and produced an event coordinator who promised the valet line was temporarily backed up. The donor accepted another drink while he waited.

A couple tried the east doors next. One of the false waiters apologized for a spill in the vestibule and guided them toward the silent auction instead. Neither noticed the steel pin already seated in the floor.

The third attempt came from a kitchen employee carrying two trash bags toward the loading dock. He returned thirty seconds later without the bags, face pale, accompanied by a man Taylor had counted twice among Anders's crew.

Nobody shouted. Nobody drew a weapon. Three exits had been refused in three different polite ways, and the party continued.

Then Danny tested the main doors.

He did not have his coat over his arm. He had the claim ticket and the expression he used when a union meeting had exhausted every graceful way to end. Taylor felt him place the ticket on the counter through a fly clinging beneath its edge.

"Two coats," he said. "Hebert."

The attendant who had surrendered her stool reached automatically for the numbered rack. The man occupying her place put one hand over the ticket first.

"It may be a few minutes, sir. We had to move several racks while the entry carpet was cleaned."

"Then show me where they went."

"I'm afraid guests aren't permitted in the storage area."

"I'm not asking to tour it. I'm asking for my coat."

The event coordinator appeared before his voice rose. She apologized, used Danny's name without being given it, and promised both coats would be brought directly to him. Danny listened with his jaw set, then looked over his shoulder for Taylor.

The search crossed the crowd once, returned to the auction display, and sharpened.

"My daughter was right there."

"I'm sure she hasn't gone far," the coordinator said.

Danny took the claim ticket back from beneath the man's hand. "Find the coats. I'll find my daughter."

He left the counter before they could offer another drink.

The deadline ran out, Taylor thought.

Yeah.

He's looking for me.

And the two guys looking for you are watching him do it. You walk straight to him now, they get both of you in one place.

Danny started with the dessert table. Taylor stayed where she was, separated from him by thirty feet of guests and a search that had learned to use him as a compass.

He's testing the lock, McClane said. Making sure his people can turn folks around without anybody realizing they're being contained.

What happens when someone doesn't turn around?

Then the polite part ends for that person. Everybody else keeps drinking.

It happened slow enough that no single moment felt alarming, which was, McClane pointed out, exactly the point. Two more men in dark suits appeared near the atrium's east exit, unhurried, unremarkable, their presence reading as security only if you already knew to look for it. A third drifted toward the main doors. A fourth, then a fifth, spreading out along the room's perimeter in a pattern that had nothing to do with serving drinks or clearing plates. Somewhere beyond the tall glass windows, Taylor caught the slow arc of a flashlight beam sweeping the parking structure, then another, two men working the lot in a pattern too deliberate to be anything but a perimeter check, front doors and back doors and every door in between, the whole building being quietly, methodically wrapped in a net nobody outside it would ever notice tightening.

Taylor watched a waiter get quietly redirected away from the east exit by one of the new men, a brief exchange, a polite nod, the waiter changing course without ever seeming to register he'd been told to. It was, she thought, a small, ugly kind of elegance, the way none of it looked like anything at all unless you already knew exactly what you were watching for.

"That's a lot of very serious men for a charity gala," Gib observed, dry, none of his earlier lightness left in the voice at all.

"He's not looking for one girl anymore," Fury said. "He's locking the room. Nobody gets to leave until he's satisfied, whatever satisfied looks like to a guy like this."

Taylor felt it too, before McClane had finished saying it, a shift in the air itself, subtle and total, a room that had quietly, without announcement, become a place nobody was getting out of easily.

Is he actually going to lock two hundred people in a building?

Not obviously. Not all at once. McClane's voice had the careful, weighing quality of a man building a threat assessment out of a dozen small, ugly pieces. Guy like this doesn't need a lockdown. He just needs enough eyes on enough doors that leaving looks suspicious, and staying looks normal. Works the same either way. Either the person he's looking for gets nervous and bolts, which flags them immediately, or they stay, which means he's got all night to keep narrowing the field.

And if I try to leave?

Taylor was already moving before he'd finished the question, one step, then another, angled toward the bar, toward Danny, toward some half-formed idea about a headache and an early night and a coat check that suddenly felt like the only sane plan left in the building.

Stop. Not loud. Flat, immediate, a word that had clearly stopped better people than her cold in worse rooms than this one. Right now, this second, you're the one guest who suddenly needs fresh air the second security tightened up. You'd be handing him exactly the read he's fishing for. That door was open five minutes ago. Walking to it now, on your own two feet, in front of everybody, gets you nothing.

So what do I do instead.

Same play as the horn note. Bigger. Something sharpened in him, fast, already three steps ahead of the sentence. Last time you needed thirty seconds and one man's attention. This time you need the whole room looking everywhere at once, so hard that nobody's actually looking at you specifically. You don't walk out the front door. You disappear in the middle of it and let two hundred people do your covering for you.

Bigger how.

You tell me. You've got a lot more than four bugs to work with now.

She did. Taylor let the thought settle, cold and clear, and reached for every warmed-up straggler still drifting the edges of the room, a dozen small commotions instead of one, scattered wide, timed close enough together that no single one of them would read as anything but bad luck. A moth against a candle two tables over. Something skittering across a white tablecloth near the bar. A soft, startled shriek from someone by the dessert table who'd felt a wing brush the back of her neck. A man near the mezzanine stairs swatting at his own collar. Another near the coat check stamping his foot at nothing. Nothing dramatic on its own. All of it at once, everywhere, impossible to track.

The room didn't panic. It just turned, all at once, in a dozen directions that weren't Taylor's, a hundred small distractions blooming across the crowd like the room itself had developed a nervous tic. In the half-second of scattered attention that bought her, Taylor didn't just slip sideways into the crowd. Some quiet accounting she'd been keeping without meaning to all night already knew exactly where to go: a linen closet near the kitchen doors, a laundry chute she'd never have found on her own, but her bugs had been living in the walls around it for the better part of an hour, drawn to the dark and the fabric and the warm, undisturbed dust. She reached the closet and started folding herself over the chute's metal lip before the last commotion had finished blooming. It was a route that didn't exist on any floor plan a man in a dark suit would think to watch. If she could get inside it, she would not be harder to spot. She would be gone.

One of the men saw the closet door move.

Taylor felt him through a roach beneath the buffet as he abandoned the false disturbance and cut toward the kitchen. She was inside the closet but not yet inside the chute, one leg over the metal lip, dress caught on a protruding screw.

He's coming, McClane said.

She pulled. The fabric held.

Footsteps struck the service tile outside.

Taylor reached back, found the snag by touch, and tore the hem rather than trying to save it. The closet handle turned as she folded both arms over her head and let herself drop.

Light cut across the chute above her. A man's hand caught empty air where her ankle had been.

Then the metal shaft turned, gravity took her around the bend, and the hand disappeared.

"Bigger, messier, and this time she vanishes instead of getting lucky," the gallery said. "That's growth."

She landed in a bin of tablecloths at the bottom of the chute, breath knocked half out of her, buried in warm cotton and the smell of bleach, safe, hidden, and utterly alone for the first time in an hour.

She didn't look for Danny again, not yet, not until she'd put real distance between herself and the spot the men in dark suits had last seen her standing. Somewhere above her, faint under a dozen small overlapping commotions, she thought she heard Max Anders' voice go flat for just a second before smoothing back over, a man doing math he very much did not like the answer to.

It'll be a nice night out, Taylor thought, into a pile of somebody's used napkins, breathless and half-laughing despite herself, in a cadence that wasn't quite hers, borrowed off a man who'd apparently said some version of this to himself in a much worse duct, a much worse night, a long time before tonight. Get out of the house, they said. Meet some new people, they said. She spit out a mouthful of somebody's linen. God, I hate this job.

"Now we're locked in a building with a guy who might kill people," Gladly said.

"That's the job," Fury answered. "Funny right up until it isn't."

She didn't need her own eyes for the rest of it. A straggler still drifting near the loading dock corridor caught a sixth man taking up position there, the same corridor she'd sent three cold bugs under, hours ago now, in a night that had felt, at the time, like it might still turn out to be nothing.

McClane.

Yeah, kid.

He's not going to stop until he finds someone. Is he.

No. Careful, honest, no comfort offered because there wasn't any real comfort to offer. He's not. Which means the only question left is whether he finds you before you find whatever it is he's actually hiding.

She found Danny at last through a bug perched near the bar, not her own eyes, laughing at something, easy and unaware, safe for now on the other side of a door she'd just slipped through. Somewhere behind that borrowed sense of him, Taylor heard, for the first time all night, the quiet click of a door being locked, real or imagined, she genuinely couldn't tell anymore, and found she no longer cared which.
