Chapter 26 of 29

Danny

Three nights after MedHall, Danny put two mugs of coffee on the back porch and asked Taylor to begin with the bugs.

He did not ask whether there were bugs. He had followed a moving trail through a collapsing building, watched an elevator close on a man who healed every few seconds, and seen his daughter look toward empty corners whenever the voice called John said something useful.

Denial had been available before the chandelier fell. It had not survived the loading dock.

Taylor sat beside him on the top step. The porch light hummed overhead. Moths circled it, ordinary except for the fact that she knew each position without looking.

"How long?" Danny asked.

"Since January."

His hands tightened around the mug. "January."

The month connected to too many things. The hospital. The school saying Taylor had been found ill. The clothes the hospital disposed of before Danny arrived. Blackwell's careful voice explaining that the responsible students had not been identified.

Danny placed his mug on the porch beside him.

"The locker."

Taylor looked down at her bandaged feet.

"That's when it happened."

"You said you didn't remember."

"I remembered. I couldn't tell the doctors without telling them about the bugs."

"What was in it?"

This was the part she had spent months refusing to describe even to herself in complete sentences. The porch gave her no alarms to pull, no injured stranger to prioritize, no corridor to map instead.

McClane stayed silent.

Taylor told Danny.

She did not give him every smell. She gave him enough. The soaked trash. The sealed vents. The laughter outside. The length of time before someone opened the door. The moment every insect in the school arrived inside her awareness at once while she still could not make her own hands reach the latch.

Danny left the step.

For one frightened second Taylor thought he was walking away. He reached the porch rail, gripped it with both hands, and bent his head.

"Who?"

Taylor said nothing.

"Taylor. Who did that?"

"If I give you names right now, what will you do?"

His answer came too slowly.

"I don't know."

"That's why I'm not giving them tonight."

Danny turned. Anger had made his face unfamiliar, but it was not pointed at her.

"You don't get to protect them."

"I'm protecting you."

"From teenage girls?"

"From doing something that lets their parents and the school turn this into you being dangerous instead of them."

He opened his mouth and stopped.

Taylor had spent two years learning exactly how blame moved at Winslow. Danny had spent three nights learning that his daughter understood hostile systems better than he did.

He came back to the step.

"Tomorrow," he said. "We discuss the names tomorrow. With a plan."

"Okay."

It was the first rule they made.

"I didn't know how to tell you."

"Were you going to?"

Taylor looked into the yard. "I kept deciding I would after I understood it better. Then after I had rules. Then after I knew I could keep you out of it."

"You decided that from a hospital bed?"

"I decided not to make you look at me like everyone else would."

"How would everyone else look?"

Taylor moved the moths from the rail. They covered her forearm from wrist to elbow, wings folded, dozens of small bodies making a living sleeve.

Danny did not recoil.

That hurt in a different direction.

"And did you? Understand it?"

She lifted one hand.

Every moth around the porch light landed along the railing in a straight line.

Danny stared.

Taylor spread them into a circle. Two beetles emerged from the grass and took positions at either end. A spider descended from the porch roof until it hung between them, turning slowly on its thread.

"I control them," she said. "All of them inside a few blocks. More if I'm moving. I know where they are. I can use their senses if I concentrate. I can make them work together."

"Can you hear through them?"

"Not like ears. I get vibration, air movement, contact. Enough insects together can build more."

"See?"

"Light and dark. Shapes sometimes. Flies are better than ants."

"Every bug in a few blocks. All the time?"

"They're there whether I pay attention or not. Like knowing where your hands are. If I focus, I can separate them."

Danny looked toward the neighboring houses. "Can you feel inside those?"

"Yes."

"People's bedrooms? Bathrooms?"

"If bugs are there. I don't look."

"But you could."

"Yes."

The answer frightened him more than the straight line of moths had. Power was impressive when it moved. Its quiet reach was harder to fit inside a father's idea of his daughter.

"Does it turn off when you sleep?"

"No. I don't always remember what happens. Sudden movement wakes me sometimes."

Danny glanced toward the back door. "That's how you always knew when I checked on you."

Taylor nodded.

He absorbed one more ordinary memory becoming evidence of something he had missed.

"Can they hurt people?"

"Yes."

The honesty hurt him. She saw it land.

"Have you?"

"Yes. Not at MedHall. Not badly there. Before."

Danny set down the coffee before his hands could spill it.

He deserves the rest, McClane said.

I know.

Taylor told him about the costume she had made in the basement while pretending the long hours down there were homework and sewing practice. She told him the armor came from spider silk, that the lenses were scavenged and cut by hand, and that every seam had been designed around the possibility of someone trying to stab or shoot her.

Danny stopped her there.

"Shoot you."

"It hasn't happened."

"That is not the reassuring sentence you think it is."

"I didn't think it was reassuring."

She told him about her first night out. The gang members surrounding a man in the yard. Lung changing while he fought. Taylor attacking because she believed children were going to be killed. The Undersiders arriving. Armsmaster meeting her afterward and telling her exactly how badly she had misread parts of the situation.

Danny listened without touching his coffee.

"Lung," he said when she finished that portion.

"Yes."

"Your first night."

"Yes."

"The man who turns into a dragon."

"Eventually."

Danny stood again. This time he walked the length of the porch, reached the steps, came back, and remained standing because sitting required more stillness than he possessed.

"And Armsmaster knew."

"He knew Skitter. He didn't know who I was."

"Skitter."

Taylor had expected embarrassment when she gave her father the name. What arrived instead was the strange distance of hearing him place two versions of her beside each other.

"That's me."

"I have read about you in the paper."

"Not much."

"I remember every cape story involving a teenager, Taylor. I remember thinking somebody's parents were going to see it and lose their minds."

He pressed his palm against his forehead.

"Apparently I was optimistic about the parents seeing it."

Taylor told him about the Undersiders. She told him what she had intended to do, stay near them, learn who funded them, pass the information to the heroes once she had enough. She did not present the plan as noble. Said aloud on a porch, with Danny listening and McClane refusing to help her polish it, it sounded exactly like a lonely fifteen-year-old inventing an undercover operation because asking an adult for help had stopped feeling possible.

"Do they think you're their friend?" Danny asked.

Taylor looked at the moths.

"Some of them might."

"Are they yours?"

"I don't know."

That answer hurt all three people in the conversation.

She finished with every patrol, every meeting, every lie she could remember telling to get out of the house. She did not turn the account into confession or defense. She gave him facts in order, the way McClane had taught her to build a room: entrances first, then obstacles, then every exit she had tried.

Danny listened until she finished.

"I was in the house," he said. "All those nights, I was in the house."

"I know."

"You walked past my bedroom."

"Usually."

"I heard you in the basement."

"Sometimes I was there."

"And the other times?"

"I left the recorder running."

Danny stared at her.

"Your mother's old cassette recorder."

Taylor wished he had shouted. The quiet made each small piece of preparation sound worse.

"Yes."

"You planned what I would hear."

"I planned not to get caught."

"By me."

"By anyone."

"I am not anyone."

Taylor flinched.

Danny saw it and regretted the volume immediately, which did not make the sentence less true.

"No," she said. "You're not. I didn't know how to make you the exception without making you part of it."

"Usually."

There were versions of her father who shouted here. Taylor had rehearsed them. There were versions who demanded the costume, grounded her forever, called the PRT, or broke beneath the accumulated proof that he had not known his daughter for months.

Danny only looked tired.

"I knew school was bad," he said. "I knew you were unhappy. Every time I asked, you said it was handled, and I let wanting that answer count as believing it."

"You tried."

"I made phone calls. I accepted meetings where Blackwell told me there wasn't enough evidence. I let the absence of paperwork become the absence of a problem."

He rubbed both hands over his face.

"Then you ended up in the hospital and I let them tell me nobody knew why."

"You didn't let them. They lied. I lied too."

"You're the child."

"I'm not five."

"No. You're fifteen, which is old enough to make choices and young enough that I was supposed to notice when every available choice had become terrible."

Taylor had no practiced answer for a parent taking responsibility without using it to take hers away.

Danny looked toward the dark kitchen window. Their reflections sat beside each other in the glass, both altered by bandages.

"Your mother would have noticed."

The old sentence entered the space between them. Danny used it against himself often enough that Taylor recognized the shape before he finished speaking.

"Maybe," she said. "She also would have tried to fix everything in one afternoon and terrified the school secretary."

A breath escaped him that might have been a laugh.

"She was very good at terrifying administrators."

"I remember."

They let Annette sit with them for a moment without turning her into either accusation or solution.

"John," he said. "How long has he been there?"

"Since the gala."

"Not January."

"No."

"Did you choose him?"

"No. A man named O'Malley chose him. John agreed before he arrived. I didn't get asked."

Danny's expression hardened again. "Someone put an adult stranger in my daughter's head without asking her."

Yeah, McClane said. Tell him that's on the list of things we're gonna discuss with O'Malley if I ever get him in a room with a door.

Taylor relayed it.

"Good," Danny said. "Put my name on the list."

Some tension left Danny's shoulders. Not much. Enough to show that he had feared the stranger inside his daughter's head had been there through every earlier secret too.

"Can he control you?"

"No. Never unless I give permission, and I can take it back. I haven't given it."

"Can he hear me now?"

Every word, McClane said.

"Yes."

"Can he see through you?"

Taylor considered how to explain a boundary she experienced more than understood.

"He gets what I'm paying attention to. He doesn't sit behind my eyes like a camera. He can tell when I'm hurt. He hears what I hear. Thoughts if I direct them at him, sometimes thoughts I don't mean to direct when they're loud enough."

"Can you make him stop listening?"

Yes, McClane answered immediately. Tell him you can close the door. Not perfect when we're under pressure, but good enough for privacy. And tell him I knock.

Taylor repeated it.

Danny looked toward the yard. "Do you? Knock?"

Every time I know there's a door there.

"He says every time he knows there's a door."

"That's a very police-officer answer."

He's not wrong.

"John agrees."

Danny leaned forward, elbows on his knees. "Then I would like to speak to him."

Taylor had imagined this too. None of the imagined versions accounted for having to serve as both sides of the conversation.

"He can hear you, but you can't hear him. I have to repeat it."

"Fine." Danny looked toward the yard rather than at Taylor, perhaps because speaking to open air felt less strange than speaking to his daughter's face. "John McClane."

That's me.

"He says that's him."

"Thank you for bringing her out."

McClane took longer to answer.

Tell him she brought me out too.

Taylor swallowed. "He says I brought him out too."

Danny nodded once. "I don't know what that means."

Neither do I, completely. Tell him I was in trouble somewhere else. Tell him helping you stopped me from becoming the only thing in the room I could see.

Taylor translated.

Danny listened with the same grave concentration he gave a contract negotiation.

"Are you a police officer?"

NYPD detective. Depending on what year anybody's asking from.

"NYPD detective. The year is complicated."

"Are you an adult?"

Last I checked.

"Yes."

"Where were you before the gala?"

McClane gave Taylor the answer in short pieces. Dulles. A cargo system beneath an airport. Men trying to rescue a prisoner responsible for a country's worth of murder. A knife in his side. O'Malley's offer arriving before the fight reached its final movement.

Taylor translated without describing the waiting room yet. That part belonged to McClane and O'Malley unless McClane chose otherwise.

Danny's gaze moved to Taylor's bandages. "So you came here from your own emergency."

Yeah.

"And when you leave?"

I go back to the instant I left. That's what he promised.

"He goes back to the same instant."

"Still injured."

Still fighting.

Taylor repeated it.

Danny took that in. "Then every day you spend here costs your family nothing."

Clock time? Nothing. Me? We'll see.

Taylor hesitated, then translated exactly.

"Fair answer," Danny said.

Your dad likes answers nobody else likes.

"John says you like answers nobody else likes."

"Occupational hazard."

Danny finally looked directly at Taylor again. "Has he asked you for control?"

"Once. At MedHall. To show me how to fall through the laundry chute without breaking my neck. I said no. He talked me through it."

And she did it better than I would've, McClane added.

Taylor included that too.

"If she says no?" Danny asked the yard.

Then it's no. I'm a passenger, not the wheel. I have kids. I know the difference between helping and deciding for somebody, even if I've screwed it up often enough to learn it the expensive way.

Danny waited for Taylor's translation, then nodded.

"You're not her father."

No. She has one. I'm not auditioning.

Taylor's throat tightened unexpectedly when she relayed it.

Danny looked at her, not offended and not relieved. Measuring whether the boundary had been spoken because it was real or because he wanted to hear it.

"Then what are you?"

McClane was quiet long enough for a moth to strike the porch light twice.

Backup, he said at last. The kind that can't carry a gun, call a squad car, or fill out paperwork. Mostly I tell her where the exits are and complain when she ignores the good ones.

"Backup," Taylor said. "Very opinionated backup."

Kid.

Danny's mouth almost moved. "I can work with backup."

Danny turned to Taylor. "Then why was my fifteen-year-old daughter the one pulling an armed trooper down three flights of stairs?"

Because everybody with a badge was outside and everybody with a gun was between her and the door. McClane's answer came flat, with no attempt to make himself sound better. Because I couldn't move her body and wouldn't have if I could. Because she decided a man was going to die if she waited. And because she was right.

Taylor repeated it without softening.

Danny closed his eyes.

"I don't like that answer."

Neither do I.

"Neither does he."

"But he let you do it."

"He told me the cost. I chose."

"You're fifteen."

"The trooper was still hanging."

Danny stood and walked to the porch railing. The moths moved away from his hands without Taylor consciously directing them.

"That sentence is going to keep happening, isn't it?" he asked. "You're fifteen, and somebody is still hanging. You're fifteen, and the building is still locked. You're fifteen, and if you don't do it, nobody else will get there in time."

Taylor did not answer.

"That's what scares me," Danny said. "Not the bugs. Not even John, though I'm reserving judgment on the voice in my daughter's head. It's that every time you tell me why, the reason is going to be good enough."

He's got you there, McClane said.

"John says you've got me there."

"Glad somebody appreciates it."

Danny returned to the step. "I can't make this unhappen. I can't keep you home by pretending the rest of the city stops existing outside our door. But no more disappearing without somebody knowing. No more deciding a plan is safe because you're the only person you told it to. If you're going out, we build rules together."

Taylor's first instinct was refusal. Rules became control. Control became permission, and permission could be withdrawn.

Then she remembered a safety line holding a PRT trooper above a stairwell.

Rules aren't the same as chains, McClane said. Good ones tell the people coming after you where the line broke.

"Okay," Taylor said.

Danny looked surprised.

"Not every rule," she added. "We negotiate."

His mouth twitched. "You are definitely my daughter."

He went inside for paper. Taylor expected a legal pad. He returned with the unopened MedHall medical-bill envelope, a pen, the sealed Tower handset, and her neglected crutches.

"Use these or I call the doctor before we negotiate anything."

"That's coercion."

"That's parenting."

He's got you there again, McClane said.

Taylor put the crutches within reach.

Danny wrote CONTACT across the envelope.

"If you go out in costume, I know before you leave."

"You don't get the location if knowing it makes you a target."

"Then I get a sealed location. Note, scheduled message, something I can open if you miss contact."

"Two copies. One hidden outside the house in case someone comes here first."

Danny paused, then wrote it down.

They set a check-in interval. Danny began at fifteen minutes. Taylor countered with two hours. They settled on forty-five minutes during an active patrol, ninety if she was stationary and had warned him first.

"If you miss one?" he asked.

"You call the Tower line."

"How long before I call?"

"Fifteen minutes."

"Five."

"Ten."

He wrote ten.

"If you answer under pressure?"

Taylor thought of the false call from Blackwell's office and the way a correct name had nearly made the lie work.

"Challenge phrase," she said. "You ask whether I fed the dog."

"We don't have a dog."

"Exactly. Safe answer is that he ate twice. Any other answer means someone can hear me or I'm being forced."

Danny wrote DOG ATE TWICE beneath the contact times.

"And if someone makes you say it?"

McClane supplied the answer. Words plus action. Have her tap the transmitter twice before speaking. Anyone can steal a phrase. Harder to steal procedure they don't know exists.

Taylor relayed it. Danny added two circles beside the phrase.

The second heading was BACKUP.

Taylor would carry the Tower handset until the PRT provided something smaller. Danny would keep dispatch numbers beside every telephone. If she identified an imminent threat, she called before intervening whenever a call was physically possible. If she could not call, she left a bug marker on the house's rear window in one of three shapes: circle for delayed, line for danger, X for call everyone.

"Everyone means PRT and police," Danny said.

"And the Dockworkers."

"The Dockworkers are not a cape-response team."

"They're a hundred people who know the docks better than the PRT. Information only. Nobody confronts capes."

Danny wrote INFORMATION ONLY twice and underlined it.

The third heading was NO SOLO.

That argument lasted twenty minutes.

Danny meant Taylor never entered danger without an adult physically beside her. Taylor pointed out that most available adults could not follow her, would not believe her, or had spent MedHall outside obeying the wrong camera. Danny said this did not make a fifteen-year-old the default substitute. Taylor said a rule that could never survive contact with reality was only a promise to lie next time.

The words sat between them.

Danny crossed out NO SOLO.

He wrote NO ONE ELSE KNOWS IS NOT A PLAN.

Taylor read it twice.

"I can agree to that."

"At least one person knows what you're doing. Me, John, PRT dispatch, someone on your team. Preferably two."

"John always knows."

"John cannot call an ambulance."

Also fair, McClane said.

"One person outside my head," Taylor agreed.

The back of the MedHall envelope filled faster than either of them expected. Danny turned it sideways to fit the questions that could not be settled tonight.

Curfew became midnight on weekends, ten on school nights, automatically extended during declared cape emergencies only if Taylor made contact. Danny wanted the costume locked in his room. Taylor refused before he finished asking. They tabled storage after agreeing the mask could not be the only copy and the costume needed an emergency identification card sealed inside it.

Teams stayed unresolved. Danny did not want her returning to the Undersiders. Taylor would not promise to abandon people she had already placed in danger by joining them. McClane refused to break the tie.

That's not a porch decision, he said. That's a whole separate bad idea with names and moving parts. Sleep first.

They wrote TEAM TOMORROW.

The PRT received the same treatment. Danny wanted a formal meeting. Taylor wanted Miss Militia present and Armsmaster not solely in charge. Both wanted every promise written. The heading became PRT, WITH WITNESS.

When the page filled, he set down the pen.

"One more question for John."

Go ahead.

"Does he have family?"

McClane's answer arrived without hesitation.

Wife. Two kids. Tell him I'm getting back to them. I just don't know when.

Taylor repeated it.

Danny looked at her for a long moment, then toward the empty yard.

"Then we'll keep her alive until you do."

Deal, McClane said.

"He says deal."

Danny picked up his coffee. It had gone cold.

Taylor leaned against his shoulder, the way she had not done in years. Above them, the moths returned to the porch light one at a time.

Nothing about the night was ordinary anymore.

The quiet still was.
