Chapter 10 of 29

Go Around

The third attempt didn't ask.

Taylor felt it before she saw it, a shift in the crowd's texture near the edge of the atrium, two of the catering-uniformed men closing in from opposite angles, unhurried, professional, converging on a spot that happened to be exactly where she was standing. No clipboard this time. No pretense of a phone call.

The wall clock had not yet consumed Danny's final three minutes.

He was making his way toward her from the bar, one hand already inside his jacket for the coat-check ticket, when the first man changed course. He did not move between Taylor and Danny. That would have looked deliberate. He intercepted a server carrying coffee, took two cups, and turned with the tray at precisely the angle that forced Danny to step around a knot of departing board members.

The second man entered from Taylor's right.

Neither looked at her. Both altered the room around her.

Move. McClane's voice cracked through her like a slap, all the careful patience from the last two attempts gone. Now. Toward the crowd, not away from it. Don't run, don't look like you're running, just get somewhere thick with people they can't work around without making a scene.

She moved. Not fast, not obvious, a girl weaving toward the dessert table the way any bored teenager might, and felt the two men adjust behind her, recalculating, still closing, still patient, something in that patience making her skin crawl. They didn't hurry. That was the worst part. Two men who could have closed the distance in three strides instead paced themselves to match the room's own unhurried rhythm, professionals who'd clearly done this enough times to know that speed was the one thing that would actually get somebody's attention in a crowd like this.

Danny cleared the board members and looked toward the place Taylor had been.

She could call to him. The first syllable would bring his head around. It would also tell both men that she understood the approach and needed rescue from it. After two polite failures, that might be all the confirmation they required.

Taylor kept moving.

He'll see I'm gone.

That's better than him seeing why. McClane's answer came instantly. Keep him out of their hands first. Explain it when you're not surrounded.

The man on her left reached the dessert table first and stopped to examine a row of pastries. The second drifted behind her. Neither blocked her path. Together they left only one comfortable route, a gap between the chocolate fountain and a decorative partition leading toward the service hall.

They were herding her.

Taylor slowed by half a step and let the man behind her believe the lane had worked. Beyond the partition, a security guard waited beside the service-hall door with his face turned toward the stage. He held no tray, no clipboard, no reason to stand there. The comfortable route ended with three men and no witnesses.

Taylor changed direction toward the dance floor.

The man beside the pastries abandoned an untouched tart. A real server emerged from the kitchen with a tray of water glasses just as he turned. The false waiter clipped the tray with his elbow.

Glass struck glass. Water sheeted across the server's sleeve and splashed onto the carpet. The server stopped in shock, then began apologizing to the nearest guests even though nothing about the collision had been his fault. The spill closed Taylor's route back toward Danny and drew a loose semicircle of people around it.

The men had built a temporary wall out of manners.

The man behind her placed two fingers against her elbow.

"Miss Hebert," he said pleasantly. "Careful. Crowded floor."

The grip was gentle enough that nobody watching would have called it a grip. His thumb rested over the nerve inside her arm. When she tried to continue, pressure flashed down to her hand.

Don't pull, McClane said. Pulling starts a contest and tells him you know you're in one. Turn toward him. Make him move his feet.

Taylor pivoted as if responding to the warning. The man had to release her elbow or twist his own wrist. She smiled, stepped around a couple entering the dance floor, and put six moving bodies between them.

The couple were older and moving carefully through turns they had probably learned before Taylor was born. Taylor matched their rotation for three steps, using the man's shoulder and the woman's raised arm as cover. The grip man circled clockwise. His partner circled the other way. They did not shove through the dancers. They waited for the music to deliver Taylor back to the edge.

It would take less than one verse.

That was a grab.

That was him finding out whether you'd let it become one. McClane's anger remained controlled, which made it feel larger. Next one won't ask.

Her hands wanted to shake. She didn't let them, mostly because McClane's voice in her head had exactly the texture of a man who'd never once let his own hands shake at a moment like this, and some petty, stubborn part of her didn't want to be the first one to break the streak.

They're not gonna grab you in front of two hundred witnesses. That's not the play. The play is corner you somewhere the crowd thins out, make it look natural, walk you somewhere quieter before anybody notices you're gone.

So what do I do? I can't out-walk them forever.

No. A beat, and she felt him doing the thing he did, fast and blunt, cataloging not just the room now but the whole shape of the problem, angles, exits, a crowd that could be used as cover or as a trap depending entirely on who was moving through it. You need a diversion. Something that pulls attention somewhere else, buys you a clean thirty seconds to actually get lost in this room instead of just delaying the inevitable.

I don't have thirty seconds worth of anything.

You've got a few dozen bugs already warmed up from earlier, and a room full of people who'll all look the same direction the second something interesting happens. Something sharpened in his voice, fast, tactical, the exact texture of a man building a plan out of nothing because nothing was what he had to work with. Chandelier. Big one, over the dance floor. You said you can get things moving without anybody clocking the how. Give me a distraction up there, loud enough to turn every head in the building, and you use that half second to get lost in the crowd.

I can't move a chandelier.

Forget the chandelier. Chandelier's a maybe. This is a definitely. Something sharpened further, fast, certain, a plan snapping into its final shape. You've got a wasp in that batch. Don't waste it making shapes in the dark where nobody can see it clearly. Send it down. Right onto whoever's playing that horn. Nobody looks at a hallway when a live musician screws up a note that hard, in front of everybody, and can't for the life of him figure out why.

Taylor found the wasp clinging to a warm lighting fixture above the quartet. It was sluggish, one hind leg damaged, its body working harder than it should have had to in the winter air moving down from the glass ceiling.

It might not make it.

Then shorten the route. Wait until the dancers turn you toward the stage. Bring it straight down.

Taylor let the older couple carry her through another half turn. The grip man appeared between shoulders, four yards away. His partner waited at the edge of the floor. Danny stood beyond both of them, searching now, coat ticket in his hand.

The horn player lifted his instrument.

Taylor sent the wasp.

It took less than the thirty seconds he'd promised her. Three bugs went up into the dark above the quartet, cover, motion, nothing that mattered. The fourth, the wasp, dropped low and straight, landing on the horn player's hand a half second before he needed it, and stung him right between two knuckles just as he drew breath for the next phrase.

The note that came out wasn't a note at all, a blaring, cracked bleat that cut through the whole quartet at once, and the horn player flinched hard enough to nearly drop the instrument, shaking his hand out, red-faced, mouthing something that was very clearly not an apology fit for a charity gala. The rest of the quartet stumbled half a bar trying to cover for him before giving up entirely. The whole room's attention snapped toward the little stage, immediate, total, the exact pull a professional musician visibly losing his composure mid-performance always had in a crowded room, a pull no amount of scrabbling in a chandelier could have matched.

The two men in catering black-and-white hesitated, just barely, just long enough.

Taylor was already moving, already three tables deeper into the crowd, already turning a corner around a cluster of city councilmen who'd all craned their necks toward the little stage like the horn player's ruined evening owed them an explanation. She cut left around a server balancing a full tray, ducked under the arm of a man mid-toast with someone she didn't recognize, wove through a knot of women comparing earrings loudly enough to make actual conversation impossible for anyone standing near them, using the party's own careless, oblivious mass the way McClane had told her to, a river she could disappear into as long as she kept moving with its current instead of against it.

She did not run toward Danny. Both men expected that. One recovered from the distraction and drove directly toward him, eyes cutting across the faces between them. The other went to the main doors, betting that a frightened girl with a waiting father would choose the exit over cover.

Taylor turned away from both.

She slipped behind the silent-auction display, crossed through the photographer's abandoned line of sight, then reversed direction along the inner edge of the dance floor. The move bought her a view of Danny through three separate insects. He reached the coat check alone, set his ticket on the counter, and looked back over the room.

Taylor saw the instant concern replaced confusion on his face.

I'm making him think I left him.

You're making sure he doesn't watch two grown men put hands on you. Keep moving.

Behind her, distantly, she heard one of the two men say something sharp into a lapel she was fairly sure hid a radio, a short, clipped transmission that had nothing polite left in it at all.

"There it is," the gallery said. "That's the engine: never beat the men chasing her when she can make the whole room look somewhere else."

"The horn player flubs the note," someone said, pleased. "And yes, I was part of that chase."

Nice work, kid. Real warmth in it now, unmistakable, a man who'd built a hundred plans out of nothing over thirty years and recognized one landing clean when he saw it. That's exactly the kind of thing I meant. You didn't have the toolkit for that an hour ago. Now you do.

Taylor felt something loosen in her chest that had nothing to do with the two men she'd just lost in the crowd, something closer to pride, sharp and unfamiliar and a little bit frightening in how good it felt. She'd built things before, science fair volcanoes and a robotics club project nobody else had wanted a part of, and none of it had ever felt like this, like a piece of herself clicking into a shape she hadn't known was missing until it filled in.

"Is this the moment?" Gladly asked.

"No line yet," Fury said. "Rules are rules."

Taylor, half-hidden behind a pillar now, catching her breath, felt the shape of the last thirty seconds settle into something she hadn't expected to feel this early into a night like this one. Not fear. Not even really relief. Something closer to the electric satisfaction of a plan working exactly the way it was supposed to.

You good? McClane asked, quieter now.

Yeah. She almost smiled, and let herself, just this once, just for a second, safely out of sight behind a pillar nobody was looking at. Yeah, I think I actually am.

Good. Because I gotta be honest, kid, watching those two idiots stand there looking up at nothing for a full three seconds while you walked right past them... Something in his voice cracked, unexpectedly, into real delight, the first unguarded laugh she'd heard out of him all night. Yippie Kay Yay, Mother Fluffer.

There was a beat of total, ringing silence from the gallery.

"THAT'S the moment!" Gladly shrieked. "That's it, that's it, pay up, pay up, everybody pay up!"

"...Goddammit," Fury said.
