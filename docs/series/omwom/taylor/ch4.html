Chapter 4 of 29

Baggage Check

Your host. McClane's attention had gone somewhere past her, tracking a man in a good suit working his way toward the bar with the easy, unhurried confidence of somebody who owned the room and had never once had to raise his voice to prove it. Anders, you said his name was.

Max Anders. Taylor kept her eyes on the councilwoman, nodding at a sentence she hadn't heard the end of. He's, like, actually important. He funds half this hospital's outreach program. My dad thinks he's too slick too.

Yeah, I bet he does. Something in the voice had gone flat, professional, all the dry humor from a minute ago folded away someplace McClane clearly kept for exactly this purpose. Watch him for me. Not the smile. The eyes, when nobody's looking at them.

She didn't want to. Wanting to was almost worse than actually doing it, some traitor part of her already turning to find Max Anders in the crowd before she'd finished deciding whether a total stranger riding around in her skull deserved that kind of trust.

You don't have to believe a word I say, McClane added, like he'd caught the hesitation without her needing to say it out loud. Just watch. Costs you nothing to look at a guy for five seconds.

And if I don't want to.

Then you don't, and we sit here drinking bad soda until your dad's ready to go home, and I never bring it up again. No pressure in it, which was somehow worse than pressure would have been. Your call, kid. Always gonna be your call.

She looked anyway.

McClane did not tell her what to find. That surprised her. He asked her to pick three people who belonged in the room and watch how they moved through it first.

Taylor chose a waiter, the woman at the silent-auction table, and Danny.

The waiter looked toward faces and empty glasses. His route changed whenever a hand rose or a tray came back full. The auction volunteer looked toward clipboards, bid sheets, and the clock above the bar. Danny looked toward whoever was speaking to him, then searched for Taylor whenever the conversation broke.

Baseline, McClane said. Everybody looks somewhere. The question is whether they're looking where their job says they should.

Max Anders's job was to host.

He watched the room like it was something he owned and something he expected to resist ownership. His eyes touched the main doors whenever they opened, the mezzanine whenever someone leaned over its rail, and the service corridor whenever one of the men in dark catering uniforms passed through. He did not stare. He did not need to. Each glance landed once and moved on, another item checked.

A waiter approaching from behind changed direction before reaching him. Anders had not turned or spoken. His left hand had dropped beside his jacket with two fingers extended, the gesture small enough to pass as nothing. The waiter carried his tray toward the east wall instead and stopped beside a man wearing an earpiece.

Did he just redirect that waiter? Taylor asked.

He redirected somebody. McClane's attention tightened. Question is whether that guy was ever a waiter.

The man with the tray did not offer a drink for the next four minutes.

He was refilling a woman's glass near the bar, laughing at something she'd said, head tipped back just enough to sell it. And then, for less than a second, so fast Taylor would have missed it completely if she hadn't been told exactly where to look, his gaze cut sideways toward the service corridor, flat and assessing, the exact same read she gave a room when she was counting exits.

It was gone before she could be sure she'd seen it. He was laughing again, warm, easy, already turning to greet somebody else, a hand finding a passing councilman's shoulder with the same practiced ease he'd used on Danny's, the same warmth turned on and off like a switch nobody else in the room seemed to notice had a switch at all.

He moved through the crowd like water finding the lowest point, never rushed, never idle, always exactly where the next conversation needed him to be a half second before it started. Taylor watched him work a full circuit of the room in the time it took her to finish a single sentence with the councilwoman, touching down with a state senator near the ice sculpture, a hospital administrator by the silent auction table, a woman in red who might have been a judge, each conversation lasting exactly as long as it needed to and not a second longer, a hundred small transactions of warmth handed out like currency he never seemed to run low on. Taylor had watched people work rooms before, teachers doing it badly at school assemblies, her own father doing it worse, stiff and earnest and visibly uncomfortable the whole time. She'd never watched anyone do it like this, effortless in a way that made effortless look like the wrong word for it, too easy to be natural and too natural to be only practice.

The route brought him past every public entrance without ever appearing to inspect one. At the main doors, he greeted a late-arriving couple while his gaze checked the coat room behind them. Near the staircase, he stopped to admire a donor's necklace while taking in the mezzanine landing over her shoulder. At the kitchen doors, he bent to retrieve a napkin before a server could reach it and came upright facing the service corridor.

Each act was gracious. Together they formed a patrol.

Taylor felt the pattern settle into her awareness beside the map of insects warming in the walls. She had spent years noticing danger by watching for the person who looked directly at her. McClane noticed the person who had arranged never to need to.

There. McClane's voice had an edge to it now, the satisfaction of a man who'd just watched a hunch confirm itself. You catch that?

Catch what? He looked at a hallway.

"'Catch what,' she says," the gallery said, delighted. "Get a load of this girl!"

He looked at a hallway the way a cop looks at a hallway. Or a guy who used to run something that needed exits covered. A pause, and she felt him doing the thing again, that fast, blunt cataloging that didn't match her own instincts at all, sharper in some places, cruder in others, built for a completely different kind of danger than the one she'd trained herself to watch for. Guy like that, hosting his own party, in his own building? He doesn't check exits. He's got people for that. Unless he doesn't trust the people, or the exits are the kind of thing he never learned to stop checking himself.

"Or he just doesn't like crowds," said the same bored, anonymous voice from earlier, unconvinced as ever. "Some people check exits because a fire actually happened to them once. Not everybody's secretly evil, you know."

"Statistically," the first voice allowed, "you're not wrong. Dramatically, you're about to be very wrong."

This is insane. Taylor kept her voice down to something that barely qualified as breath, angled at her ginger ale like she'd developed a sudden fascination with ice cubes. You've been here fifteen minutes. You looked at a guy for one second. You don't know anything about him.

I know enough. No heat in it, just certainty, flat and immovable, something that made her chest go tight for reasons she didn't fully understand yet. I've spent thirty years reading rooms full of people who didn't want to be read. You get good at it or you get dead. That guy's running something under all this charity-gala varnish, and I'd bet my pension, if I've still got one, that not one person in this building's clocked it, because he's good, and because nobody here's actually looking.

"I told you we needed Clockblocker here," Gladly said.

People don't just know things like that. Not from a glance.

Sure they do. Ask any beat cop who's still alive to complain about it. You spend enough years watching people lie to your face, you stop needing the whole sentence. You just need the first word and the way somebody's shoulders move while they say it. A beat, and something almost self-deprecating crept into the certainty. Doesn't mean I'm right. Means I've been right often enough that I stopped being able to afford ignoring the feeling.

You don't know that.

Kid, I watched him check a hallway like a man clearing a room, and then I watched two hundred people keep right on drinking champagne like nothing happened. Something almost gentle crept into the flatness, underneath the certainty, the patience of a man who'd had this exact argument before, with other people, in other rooms, and lost most of them. That's not a guess. That's just what I saw. You can decide what to do with it. I'm not gonna make you believe me.

Taylor didn't answer right away. Across the room, Max Anders had circled back toward Danny, one hand landing warm and easy on her father's shoulder, saying something that made Danny laugh, actually laugh, the forced one, not the one he saved for people he liked.

"Everyone in this city got issued blinders at birth," the gallery muttered, and the phrasing landed hard enough that Taylor nearly missed McClane saying almost the same thing a half-beat later.

"Bleedthrough," Gladly said, alarmed. "She's not supposed to hear us!"

Everybody in this town's got the same pair of blinders on, McClane said, watching Anders work the room with the weary contempt of a man who'd met a hundred versions of him under a hundred different names. And I gotta be honest with you, kid, that scares me a lot more than the guy does.

"Nobody's writing anything down," Fury said. "This isn't a seminar."

"It could be. I'd attend."

Taylor watched her father laugh again. The same forced laugh as the first time. And felt something small and cold settle into her stomach that had nothing to do with the ginger ale.

So what do we do.

Nothing. Not yet. McClane's certainty had a shape to it now, something almost like a plan, or the outline of one. You don't move on a read this thin. Not in a room full of his own people, at his own party, with your dad standing three feet from him. You watch. You let me watch, through you, since apparently that's how this works now. And if I'm wrong, you never have to think about any of this again, and we can both go back to whatever we were doing before some jackass decided to reroute my whole night.

And if you're right?

Then we've got a problem a lot bigger than one bad hallway glance, and we're gonna need a better plan than standing here drinking a soda you don't even like.

What kind of plan.

Don't know yet. Haven't earned one. He said it simply, no frustration in it, the flat patience of a man who'd built enough plans from nothing to know better than to rush the part where you actually had something to build with. Right now all we've got is a feeling and a hallway. You don't build a plan on a feeling and a hallway. You build a plan on facts, and facts take time, and time's the one thing neither of us gets to rush without paying for it later.

That's not much of a pep talk.

Wasn't trying to give you one. Trying to give you the truth instead. Figured you'd rather have that.

She would, and hated a little that he'd guessed it correctly on the first try.

Taylor set the ginger ale down on a passing tray, mostly full, and didn't reach for another one.

You feel that? McClane asked, and there was something almost gentle underneath the flatness, the tone of a man checking on a rookie after their first real scene. Setting the drink down. That's not nothing. That's a decision.

It's a drink.

It's never just a drink. No lecture in it, just an observation, offered plainly. You put it down because some part of you already knows you're not going back to being a girl who drinks ginger ale and watches a string quartet tonight. Whatever's coming, you already agreed to it. Your body just beat your mouth to the announcement.

Taylor didn't have an answer for that, mostly because she suspected he was right, and admitting it out loud felt like signing something she hadn't finished reading yet.

Across the room, Max Anders excused himself from Danny with a warm word and a hand on the shoulder, and turned, unhurried, toward the service corridor Taylor had clocked on her very first lap of the room, the one she was fairly sure, now, he'd checked without meaning to be seen doing it.

"Dammit," Fury said. "What a time to be missing a character."

She found Danny with her eyes before she let herself follow Anders any further, an old reflex now on top of an old reflex, and found him fine, laughing at something a hospital board member had said, three feet and a whole world away from whatever his daughter had just quietly agreed to.

Somewhere above them both, the chandelier over the dance floor caught the light and threw it back down across two hundred unbothered faces, and Taylor found herself doing that math again, tonnage and cable tension, without quite knowing yet why some part of her had already started filing it away as useful.

She watched Anders go, and for the first time all night, the watching wasn't just habit.
