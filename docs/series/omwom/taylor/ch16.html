Chapter 16 of 29

Blades

Max Anders never made it all the way to Danny.

Taylor watched it happen through three separate bugs, three separate angles, and even with all of them stitched together the moment still didn't quite make sense to her at first: Anders slowing, then stopping, then turning, unhurried, toward the small stage where the quartet had gone quiet again, like a man who'd just remembered a more urgent appointment.

He's not going for your dad. McClane's voice had gone tight, fast, recalculating in real time. He's stepping back. Why's he stepping back.

"Because he doesn't need to do it himself," Gib said, and for the first time all night there was no dryness in it at all, nothing but flat, cold recognition. "Watch the doors."

The men in dark suits moved.

Not all at once, not obviously, but in a pattern that made Taylor's stomach drop even before she understood what she was looking at: six men, then eight, then more still filtering in from the service corridors and the mezzanine stairs, converging on the main hall from a dozen different points at once, and as they moved, something about the way they moved changed too, the careful, unremarkable stillness they'd worn all night sloughing off like a coat nobody needed anymore. It was, she thought distantly, the exact opposite of everything she'd watched Anders do all evening, no warmth, no performance, just cold, practiced coordination, a machine built for a very different kind of party revealing itself all at once.

The nearest one's hands weren't hands anymore.

What is that. Taylor's voice had gone thin, disbelieving, watching metal ripple and reshape itself across a man's fingers, a spine of jagged silver blooming where knuckles should have been. McClane, what am I looking at.

I don't know. Flat, honest, the first time all night she'd heard him admit it outright. That's not anything I've got a file for. That's not anything I've got a category for. But whatever it is, it's not a rent-a-cop anymore, and I need you to trust me when I tell you that changes everything about the next five minutes.

"Hookwolf," the gallery said, hushed. "And Cricket by the east doors. From her stance, she's already clocked every exit in the room better than Taylor ever could."

Another of the suited men had gone very still near the coat check, a stillness that had nothing relaxed in it at all, head cocked at an angle that suggested he was listening to something none of the ordinary guests could hear, a long, curved blade unfolding from somewhere at his hip like it had always been there, waiting.

A third, closer to the bar, went perfectly white all at once, skin and hair and even the fabric of his suit bleaching out to the same flat, colorless pallor, and Taylor felt McClane's attention snag hard on that one specifically, some old cop instinct clocking a threat display even without a name to put on it.

That one's not even trying to look human anymore. Something in his voice had gone very careful, very controlled, a man doing math on a threat board that kept adding new columns faster than he could total them. Whatever the rules are in your world, I don't think he's planning on following polite society's version of them tonight.

Nearby, two enormous women who hadn't been part of the earlier count of suits at all simply stood up from a table, guests apparently, until they weren't, height stretching impossibly as evening gowns tore at the seams, and the low murmur of confused party chatter finally, belatedly, started curdling into something closer to actual panic.

Taylor's swarm, without her quite telling it to, pulled back from the room in a single flinching ripple, old animal instinct recognizing a predator's silhouette faster than her conscious mind could catch up to it.

Somewhere in the crowd a woman screamed, short and sharp and immediately swallowed, and that seemed to be the signal the rest of the room had been waiting for without knowing it, two hundred people remembering all at once that a charity gala wasn't supposed to look like this, glasses hitting the floor, chairs scraping back, a slow, ugly current of bodies starting to push toward exits that Taylor already knew, with cold certainty, were no longer actually open.

The chandelier over the dance floor swayed, just slightly, as the current of fleeing bodies crashed against the tables beneath it, crystal chiming against crystal in a bright, discordant music that had nothing to do with the quartet, and for one wild, useless second Taylor found herself doing the math again, tonnage and cable tension, wondering whether tonight was when the calculation finally mattered.

Danny, she thought, and the word came out smaller and more frightened than anything she'd let herself feel all night. Where's my dad. I can't see him. I can't find him in this.

He's fine. He's exactly where he was. McClane's voice, impossibly, had gone steadier instead of less, an anchor thrown deliberately into the panic. A stampede's actually good for him right now, kid. Nobody's got time to focus on a single union rep when two hundred rich people are trying to get through the same door at once. Ugly as that sounds, it's cover. Use it.

They're parahumans. It wasn't a question. Saying it out loud, even silently, made it realer, worse, a fact she couldn't unknow now that she'd named it. He's not just some guy with a security detail. He's got capes working for him.

Capes. McClane turned the word over like he was checking it for a blade of its own. That the word for it in your world?

That's the word.

Then yeah. He's got capes. Question is how many, and I'm not liking the math I'm doing so far.

The quartet's stage lights cut out.

It happened all at once, no flicker, no warning, the whole small platform going dark except for the pale gleam of the horn player's abandoned instrument, and in the sudden half-dark that followed, Taylor felt, more than saw, the room's mood shift, two hundred guests going from confused to afraid in the space of a single held breath.

"Ladies and gentlemen." Max Anders's voice, still warm, still perfectly pitched to carry, and underneath it now, unmistakable, something colder than anything he'd let show all night. "I'm so sorry for the disruption. It seems we do have an actual security concern after all, and I'm going to need everyone to remain exactly where they are while my people handle it."

The main doors locked with two heavy impacts.

At the east exit, a man drove the waiting steel pin through its floor socket. Another rolled the silent-auction display across the kitchen corridor. The route remained technically open, but only as a shoulder-wide gap beneath Alabaster's colorless stare.

The men who had spent the evening carrying trays opened their jackets and produced compact weapons from harnesses beneath them. The transformation was less dramatic than Hookwolf's blades and more frightening for being ordinary. Safety levers moved. Stocks unfolded. Muzzles selected sections of the crowd.

"Phones on the floor," the man near the sound board ordered. His voice came through the atrium speakers, stripped flat by an electronic filter. "Hands visible. Move toward the center aisle and sit down. Anyone approaching an exit will be treated as a threat to the other guests."

Nobody obeyed immediately.

Then Hookwolf put one bladed arm through a banquet table.

Wood, china, and white linen collapsed beneath the strike. The sound ended the argument the room had not yet begun having. Guests lowered themselves among broken glasses and overturned chairs. A few kept hold of their phones until armed men began collecting them into champagne buckets.

Anders stepped down from the stage with both palms raised. One of the gunmen seized his shoulder and pushed him into the first row of hostages.

The movement looked rough. Taylor, watching through a fly on the man's sleeve, felt the grip loosen before it landed. Anders went to one knee without losing balance and turned immediately to help the councilwoman beside him sit.

Host and hostage in the same motion.

He's inside the crowd now, McClane said. Every camera sees a victim. Every gunman sees the boss.

Danny did not sit.

An older man had fallen near the bar when the doors locked. Danny pushed through two rows of chairs, crouched beside him, and pressed a napkin against the cut above his eye.

"Sir," one of the gunmen said.

"He's bleeding."

"Sit down."

Danny looked at the weapon, then at the injured man. "Help me move him and I will."

For one suspended second, Taylor watched her father negotiate with a gun the same way he negotiated with shipping managers: as though the other side remained a person who could be embarrassed into behaving correctly.

Anders raised his voice from the floor. "Let him help. Panic will do more damage than cooperation."

The gunman looked toward the sound-board operator. The operator looked at Anders. Anders's hand rested flat against the floor, two fingers bent beneath the palm.

"Move him," the operator said.

Danny did, unaware that the man granting permission sat among the hostages wearing Max Anders's face.

Taylor sent a moth to the back of Danny's collar. She could not speak through it. She could not make him understand. But the small living point gave her his position inside the crowd, steady and exact.

It was all she could give him without giving herself away.

"There it is," the gallery said, quiet, grim. "No more pretending. He's not performing host anymore. That's the mask coming off, right there, in real time."

Taylor. McClane's voice cut through the rising noise in her own head, sharp, urgent, an anchor. I need you to hear me. You are not fighting whatever that metal one is. Not tonight. Not with what you've got. This isn't the fight, this is the moment before the fight, and the entire job right now is making sure there's a later for you to actually have it in.

Danny's still in there.

I know. No comfort in it, none available to give. And getting to him means getting through people who can probably kill you in about four seconds if you do this wrong. So we're not doing this wrong. We're doing this smart, or we're not doing it at all.

Across the darkened hall, the one Gib had called Cricket turned, slow, deliberate, her head tracking something Taylor couldn't see, and for one long, terrible second, Taylor was certain the woman was looking directly at the exact spot where a straggler bug sat perched on a pillar, watching.

She can't actually see the bug, McClane said, fast, though his own certainty had a thin edge to it now. Can she?

I don't know. I don't know what her power does. I don't know what any of their powers do.

Then we don't test it. Pull back. All of it. Right now.

Taylor called her whole scattered net home in a single convulsive pulse, no patience left for careful, no time left for subtle, bugs abandoning windowsills and curtain rods and the undersides of tables in a ragged, unmistakable wave that any trained eye in that room would read instantly for exactly what it was.

Cricket's head snapped toward the movement.

"Oh no," Gladly said, small and scared in a way none of them had heard from him all night. "Oh no, she saw that. She definitely saw that."

Move. McClane's voice had lost every trace of calm it had been holding together with. Now. Out of laundry and away from the chute. If she reads which direction that swarm came from, you do not stay at the end of the line.

Taylor was already on her feet. She took the clipboard map, shoved it beneath the rolling cart, and left the pocket between the pressing table and the wall for the first time since the search.

The basement corridor offered two routes. Left reached the chute landing, then the stairs Anders's men had already searched twice. Right ran through mechanical storage and climbed by a narrow staff stair to the service level behind the kitchen.

Taylor went right.

She sent the returning swarm past her rather than drawing it home, a ragged stream continuing toward the loading dock before splitting in four directions. Cricket might read motion. Taylor gave her four false answers and kept none near her own body.

Behind her, the laundry-room door opened hard enough to strike the wall.

Not Cricket. One of the suited men, reporting into his collar as he saw the abandoned hiding place.

"Lower service. She was here."

Taylor reached the mechanical-room door. Locked.

Map showed another way, McClane said.

She reversed, cut through dry storage, and climbed over a stack of folded banquet tables to reach the second door. Voices entered the corridor behind her. A flashlight swept across the cinderblock wall and caught the edge of her torn green hem as she pulled it clear.

"There!"

Taylor shoved through the door, crossed a boiler room hot enough to steal her breath, and found the narrow staff stair. Kitchen workers were already coming down it, fleeing the armed takeover above. She flattened herself against the rail while a dishwasher and two line cooks squeezed past, then climbed against their flow.

You're going up, McClane said.

Laundry is burned. The loading dock is watched. The other basement stair puts me under their search team.

Good. Keep going.

At the top, the service corridor had become a collision of catering staff trying to escape and armed men driving them back toward the kitchen. Taylor entered between those currents with her head down and her torn skirt gathered in one hand. She was no longer hiding in a room Cricket could locate by following insects. She was moving through a floor full of people dressed differently, running for different reasons, all of them equally inconvenient to track.

The last image she kept from the atrium before dispersing the observation net was Hookwolf's silver spine catching the light from an emergency exit sign, patient and unhurried, like a predator that already knew it had all the time in the world.
