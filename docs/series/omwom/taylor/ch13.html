Chapter 13 of 29

Radar Contact

The laundry room made a decent office, once she stopped thinking of it as somewhere she'd fallen and started thinking of it as somewhere nobody would think to look for her twice.

Taylor settled into the gap between a rolling cart of folded napkins and the wall, knees pulled up, and let the real work start. Not the dramatic kind. Not diversions, not stings, not anything McClane would have recognized from thirty years of doorway standoffs. Just patience, and a few dozen bugs spread thin across a building that had, over the course of one very long night, stopped feeling unfamiliar to her at all.

Patience did not come as easily to Danny.

Through insects above the atrium, Taylor watched him wait the five minutes the event coordinator requested. At six, he crossed to the women's restroom and asked a nurse leaving it whether a tall teenage girl in a green dress was inside. At eight, he returned to the coat check and demanded both coats again. At ten, he found the coordinator and made her stop walking while he spoke.

"She's not in either restroom."

"We have staff checking the second floor, Mr. Hebert. Teenagers do wander at these events."

"Taylor doesn't."

The coordinator softened her face. "I understand you're worried."

"Then stop understanding and find her."

Two men near the main doors watched the exchange without appearing to. The coordinator touched Danny's forearm and guided him back toward the bar, away from the exits and the service corridor. She promised an announcement if Taylor had not returned in another ten minutes.

I should tell him where I am.

How? McClane asked. No phone. No clean messenger. Any bug trick you use gets reported straight to Anders, and a note means putting your body back where they're hunting it.

He's scared.

Yeah. And right now scared has him standing under lights with two hundred witnesses. We can hate the cost without pretending the alternative is safer.

Taylor marked Danny on the back of the laundry clipboard anyway. Not because she could forget where he stood. Because every plan from here forward had to include the route between them.

Slow, McClane said, settling into something closer to a coach's voice than a cop's. You're not looking for one big thing. You're looking for a shape. Patterns don't announce themselves. They just show up, over and over, until you notice you've seen them before.

So what am I actually watching for.

Who he talks to without smiling first. That's rule one. Everybody at this party gets the smile. Not everybody gets the version underneath it.

She worked outward in rings, the way she'd learned to build a perimeter, starting close and widening slowly: the atrium first, then the service corridors, then the kitchen and the loading dock and, eventually, careful and patient, a scattering of bugs finding their way up the grand staircase to the mezzanine level itself, into a lounge where the party's more important guests had been quietly siphoned off for a second, smaller gathering all night. It took the better part of forty minutes, spread thin and careful across the whole ground floor and half the second, before the shape started to show itself. A man near the coat check, the same build as the two who'd tried the clipboard trick, getting a private nod from Anders that lasted exactly as long as it needed to and not one second longer. A woman near the kitchen doors, sharp-eyed, unsmiling, who Anders crossed the room twice to speak with briefly, low, out of anyone's earshot but a bug's. A locked door off the service corridor that three different men had checked, separately, in the last half hour, unhurried each time, the check of men confirming a perimeter rather than looking for a bathroom.

He's got at least six people here who aren't caterers and aren't guests, Taylor thought, cataloging it the way McClane had taught her to without quite noticing she'd started doing it his way. Maybe more. All of them checking in with him separately. All of them acting normal everywhere else.

That's not security for a charity gala. McClane's voice had gone flat again, certain, the weight of a man watching a threat assessment solidify into something he could actually use. That's a crew. Organized. Disciplined. The kind of discipline that doesn't come from a rent-a-cop agency, it comes from people who've worked together before, under worse conditions than a chocolate fountain.

Worse how.

I don't know yet. That's the next question.

There were smaller things too, details that wouldn't have meant anything on their own, that only added up to a shape once she had enough of them stacked together. The way certain guests, three of them, four, got a slightly warmer welcome from Anders than their apparent importance seemed to justify, men and women who didn't run companies or sit on hospital boards, as far as she could tell, but who Anders greeted like colleagues rather than donors. The way the bartender, of all people, kept glancing toward the locked door every few minutes, quick and involuntary, like a man checking a clock he wasn't supposed to be watching. None of it proved anything. All of it fit.

The mezzanine lounge, once she got a bug close enough to actually listen, turned out to be its own small ecosystem entirely, a dozen well-dressed men and women who never quite mingled with the party below, conversations pitched low, body language stiff not from formalwear but from the effort of looking off duty. She counted faces twice, cross-referenced against the ones circulating downstairs, and came up with at least four who never appeared in both places at once, a rotation, careful and deliberate, someone always watching from above even while the party below carried on as if nothing up there existed at all.

You're building a case, McClane said, something like approval threaded through the observation. Real one. Not evidence a court would take, not yet, but the kind of thing an investigator builds a case out of before the evidence ever shows up. You're doing this right.

I don't feel like I'm doing anything. I'm just sitting in a laundry room.

That's most of the job, actually. Nobody tells you that part in the movies.

The locked door off the service corridor was the one that mattered most, and Taylor spent ten careful minutes working a single bug under the gap at its base, patient, slow, past a draft that smelled like cold concrete and old paper. Voices, muffled, two of them. Anders's, unmistakable now after a night of hearing it work a room. A second, rougher, unfamiliar, calling him something that wasn't his name and wasn't quite a title either.

The insect could not see, but sight was only one way to construct a room.

Its feet found sealed concrete instead of carpet. The temperature was lower than the corridor. Air carried machine oil, cut foam, metal, and a sharp chemical odor McClane recognized before Taylor did.

Explosives, he said.

You can smell explosives through a fly?

I can remember what a room full of demolition gear smelled like, and you're giving me enough pieces to hate the resemblance.

Taylor moved the fly beneath a shelving unit. Large cases stood against the far wall, their weight bowing the lowest shelf. Coiled wire hung above them. A man crossed the floor carrying something rectangular in both hands and set it into foam with careful precision.

Behind the cases stood two fireproof document cabinets on low steel dollies. Their manufacturer plates had been covered with black tape. A portable machine sat open on top of one, cables arranged beside a row of empty drive cases. Taylor did not know what it copied. She knew it had been positioned to move.

Explosives and office equipment, McClane said. That's two different jobs sharing one locked room.

This was not a private office. It was equipment storage.

Prepared equipment storage.

Boss.

That's it. That's what he called him. Taylor kept her breathing even, careful, the same discipline she'd been using all night to keep from talking to herself out loud in a room full of people. Not Mr. Anders. Not sir. Boss.

The rougher voice said something else after that, too low and too fast for the single bug to catch cleanly, and then a second voice, higher, tighter, clearly reporting rather than conversing, the cadence of someone giving an update to a man who didn't like being kept waiting for one. Taylor caught fragments only. Handled. Contained. A word that might have been reporter, might have been something else entirely, gone before she could be sure.

"Archive credential arrives through the dock," the higher voice said. "Transfer team can begin as soon as the room clears."

"It will arrive," Anders said. "No transfer until the observer is identified."

"Exterior teams are set," the higher voice said more clearly. "Door crews can close in ninety seconds."

"Not until we identify the surveillance," Anders answered.

"We can proceed without it."

The silence that followed lasted long enough to feel punitive.

"No," Anders said. "We cannot. An unknown observer turns a controlled operation into evidence. Find the observer. Then we proceed."

Taylor pressed both hands against the laundry-room floor.

The party had not become dangerous because she and McClane noticed Anders. Whatever waited inside those cases had been placed before Danny's borrowed sedan reached the valet stand.

They had not created the plan.

They had become the one piece Kaiser could not account for inside it.

"There it is," the gallery said. "A respectable charity host does not get called Boss beside demolition gear."

Good. McClane's voice had gone very quiet, very careful, a man handling something he didn't want to spook. That's exactly the kind of thing I meant. Not a confession. Not a smoking gun. Just one word, in the wrong room, from the wrong guy, that doesn't fit the story he's telling everybody else tonight.

It's not enough, though. Is it.

No. Honest, the way he'd been honest all night, no comfort dressed up as reassurance. One word's a data point. You need more than one before it's a pattern, and more than a pattern before it's something you can actually use. But it's a start, kid. A real one. First one you built yourself, start to finish, without me telling you where to look.

Taylor let the bug ease back from the gap under the door, slow, careful, and called the rest of her scattered net home in the same unhurried rhythm, gathering pieces of a night that had started as a party and was quietly, steadily, becoming something else entirely.

She sat with it for a moment, alone in the dark with a dozen small, disconnected facts and the growing certainty that they were all going to add up to something she wasn't going to like. Somewhere above her, muffled through a ceiling and a chute and however many feet of building, the string quartet started up again, bright and oblivious, playing for a room that had no idea it was sitting on top of any of this.

"I love this chapter," Gladly said.

"You say that every chapter," Fury answered.
