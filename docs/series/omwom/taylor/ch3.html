Now Arriving

They nearly turned around at the curb.

Danny had borrowed Alan Barnes's sedan because the truck made a noise above forty-five that he did not trust on the highway, and the sedan chose the moment a valet opened Taylor's door to display an orange CHECK ENGINE light across the dashboard. Danny stared at it. The valet stared politely past it. Taylor stood in the cold wearing a dark green dress bought for Emma's cousin's wedding two years earlier and altered at the waist by a tailor who had asked too many questions about why it fit differently now.

"We could go home," Danny said.

He tried to make it a joke. The invitation in his inside pocket had spent three weeks on the kitchen counter while he invented reasons not to answer it, then another week beneath a magnet on the refrigerator after Taylor told him he should go. He had agreed only after she agreed to come.

"We could," Taylor said.

For one second they both meant it.

Then the valet held out a claim ticket, Danny took it, and the choice closed behind them with the sedan door.

MedHall's entrance rose above the drive in glass and pale stone, its name cut into a steel canopy that reflected the rotating blue light from a decorative fountain. Guests moved through revolving doors in pairs, coats surrendered before the cold had finished leaving them. A photographer waited beyond the vestibule with a wall of sponsor logos. Taylor and Danny stepped around him at the same time without discussing it.

Security occupied the next bottleneck. Two men checked invitations against a printed list while a woman in a MedHall blazer directed handbags through a portable scanner. It was discreet enough to reassure donors and visible enough to remind everyone else whose room they were entering.

Danny gave his name.

The man found it immediately. "Mr. Hebert. Dockworkers Association. Welcome."

Danny's shoulders eased by less than an inch. Taylor saw it because she had spent years learning the movements he thought nobody noticed.

The scanner swallowed her small purse. She felt three half-dormant flies clinging to the underside of a planter near the outer doors, a moth tucked behind the sponsor wall, and nothing else within easy reach. November had stripped the city down to whatever insects could find heat and wait. Her power felt like an enormous empty room with four quiet occupants.

Then the inner doors opened, warm air rolled over her, and hundreds of small lives lit at the edge of her awareness from kitchens, flower arrangements, wall cavities, coat hems, and loading crates.

The room became crowded before she stepped into it.

MedHall did charity galas the way it did everything else, which was to say competently, expensively, and with just enough clinical white in the color scheme that Taylor couldn't decide if the building wanted to look like a hospital or was just embarrassed to admit it already did. Chrome railings. Frosted glass panels lit from behind in a soft blue that was probably supposed to read as calming and instead made everyone's champagne look like it belonged in a specimen cup. A string quartet worked through something she didn't recognize in the atrium, competing politely with the low roar of two hundred conversations she wasn't part of.

The atrium alone could have swallowed her school's entire gymnasium and had room left over for the parking lot, three full stories of glass curtain wall rising up toward a ceiling strung with lights arranged to look, from below, like a slow-motion snowfall that never quite finished landing. A grand staircase swept up one side of the room toward a mezzanine where a second, smaller party seemed to be happening entirely for its own sake, donors important enough to merit their own altitude. Somewhere overhead, a chandelier the size of a small car hung suspended over the dance floor, and Taylor found herself doing the math on it before she'd even meant to, tonnage and cable tension and how many people would need to be under it for that to matter, old habits reaching for new numbers.

She'd counted the exits before she'd finished her first lap of the room. Three that mattered, two fire doors flanking the kitchen and the main entrance behind the coat check, and a service corridor she'd clocked mostly by the staff traffic moving in and out of it, not because anyone had told her it was there. She hadn't decided to do that. It was just what her eyes did now, lately, without asking her first.

"You're doing the thing," Danny said, low, not looking at her directly, the way he did most things that were actually about her.

"Dammit, I said that was my thing!" Fury said. "I think I need to start making sure my fly's up. Everybody's walking off with my material tonight."

"What thing."

"The thing where you case a room like you're planning to rob it." He said it fondly, which somehow made it worse. "Nobody else here's doing that. Look around. Everybody else is just standing here being rich at each other."

He wasn't wrong. The gala's actual guests moved through the space unhurried, at ease in a way that came from never once in their lives needing to know where the exits were, because nothing bad had ever happened to them in a room this nice. Taylor recognized maybe a third of the faces from the newspaper, city council members and hospital board chairs and men who ran shipping concerns Danny had spent twenty years fighting for a fair contract against, all of them here tonight in the same room, pretending for one evening that they liked each other.

Somewhere behind her a waiter navigated the crowd with a tray of something wrapped in prosciutto, and Taylor caught herself doing the math on him too, out of pure habit, before she remembered he was actually supposed to be there. That was the trouble with a room like this. Everyone in it looked, at a glance, like they belonged, which meant belonging stopped being useful information the second you actually needed it.

"I'm allowed to be nervous," she said. "This isn't really my crowd."

"It's not mine either." Danny adjusted a cuff that didn't need adjusting, a tell she'd learned to read years before she'd learned to read anything else about him. "I keep waiting for somebody to check the invitation and realize there's been a mistake."

"There it is," said a dry voice from the gallery. "The 'surely they'll realize I don't belong here' beat. Every gala movie has one. I'd set my watch by it, if I had a wrist."

Danny Hebert belonged here more than he'd ever admit to himself. The Dockworkers Union carried real weight in this city, whatever he privately thought about his own standing, and the invitation sitting in his coat pocket hadn't been a clerical error. He just wasn't built to believe that about himself, not out loud, not even to his own daughter at a party neither of them particularly wanted to be at.

"Danny! Glad you could make it."

Max Anders arrived the way he did everything, smoothly, at exactly the right moment to be noticed without looking like he'd been working for the effect. Good suit, better haircut, a handshake that lasted precisely as long as a handshake was supposed to and not one second more. He had a politician's gift for remembering names, warm and practiced, the kind of attention that made you feel, briefly, like the only person in the room, right up until he moved on to make the next person feel exactly the same way.

"Mr. Anders." Danny's grip was firm, a little stiff, a workingman's handshake in a room full of men who'd never worked with their hands a day in their lives.

"Max, please. And this must be your daughter." Anders turned the warmth on Taylor without missing a beat, easy, practiced, entirely unthreatening. "Welcome. I hope Danny's told you what a difference the Union's made keeping this city's docks running, because half this room wouldn't have a supply chain to complain about without him."

Taylor said something polite. She didn't remember what, later, because it didn't matter, because Anders had already moved on to the next handshake, the next name recalled from some internal ledger, the whole performance so smooth it barely registered as one.

"Give it a rest," said someone else, anonymous, bored. "Sometimes a rich guy's just a rich guy."

"Sometimes," the first voice agreed, entirely unconvinced.

Taylor drifted toward the edge of the room once Anders had moved on, the way she always did at things like this, close enough to Danny that leaving wouldn't look like leaving, far enough that she could watch the room instead of being watched by it. Old habit. Older than the bugs, even, older than any of it. She'd been doing this at school for years before her power ever gave her a better reason to.

The councilwoman had a laugh like a hinge that needed oiling, sharp and a little too frequent, deployed at points in the conversation that didn't obviously call for it. Taylor filed that away too, the way she filed everything, without deciding to, without quite knowing yet what any of it would end up being for.

She was three sips into a ginger ale she didn't want, half-listening to the woman explain something about zoning to a man who clearly didn't care, when the voice arrived.

It didn't knock. It didn't ease in at the edges the way her own thoughts usually did, drifting up from somewhere she couldn't always name the source of. It just arrived, mid-thought, complete and unmistakably not hers, disoriented and sharp all at once, like someone had shoved a stranger through a door into the middle of a conversation they hadn't been part of thirty seconds ago.

For one full second, the whole room tilted. Not physically, nothing anyone standing near her would have seen, but everywhere at once inside her own skull, a vertigo with no direction to fall in, two sets of instincts trying to parse the same room through the same eyes at the same time and mostly just tripping over each other in the doorway. The string quartet's melody smeared, briefly, into something almost unrecognizable, familiar notes arriving a half-beat out of step with themselves, and the whole bright, glittering atrium seemed, for that one second, very far away and much too close all at once.

Okay. Okay, this is new.

Taylor's glass didn't quite make it back to her mouth. She set it down instead, carefully, both hands, over-controlled in a way she hoped read as manners and not as a girl trying very hard not to look like she'd just heard a man's voice inside her own skull at a party full of Brockton Bay's most respectable liars.

Who, she thought, as loud and as flat as she could make a thought, are you.

Lady, I was gonna ask you the same thing. A beat, and she could feel him taking inventory the way she took inventory, except faster, harder, a lifetime of habit doing in half a second what had taken her months to learn to do at all. You're, what, sixteen? Fifteen? And I'm apparently riding shotgun in your head at a party. This somebody's idea of a joke, or should I be more worried than I already am?

I don't know what's happening. Her heart was going hard enough that she was half convinced the councilwoman would hear it over the string quartet. I don't know who you are. I don't know how you're in my head, I don't

Hey. Hey, slow down. Something in the voice shifted, dropped half a register, the same tone she imagined a cop used on someone standing too close to a ledge. Panicking's fine. Panicking's normal. Just do it quiet, because we are apparently standing in the middle of two hundred people who are gonna notice if you start talking to yourself out loud.

She hadn't realized her lips had moved until he said it. She pressed them together, hard, and forced her eyes back onto the councilwoman's face, nodding at something about parking variances she hadn't heard a word of.

Good. That's good. Keep doing that. A pause, and she felt him doing something she didn't have a name for yet, some quick, practiced survey of the room through her own eyes, cataloging in a rhythm that didn't match hers at all, faster, blunter, built for a different kind of danger than the one she'd trained herself to watch for. Okay. Talk to me. Where are we, what is this, and why do half these people look like they'd rather be anywhere else.

It's a charity gala. She kept her voice down to something that barely qualified as a whisper, angled toward her ginger ale like she was talking to the ice. My dad's here. I'm here because he's here.

Somebody make you come, or you volunteer for this?

Neither. It's complicated.

You got a name, or should I just keep calling you 'kid' in my head, because I gotta tell you, that's gonna get old for both of us fast.

Taylor. She hesitated, half a second, the reflex of a girl who'd learned the hard way not to hand things out for free, even a name, even to a voice she had no way to verify wasn't some new, worse kind of trouble. You?

McClane. John McClane. Something dry crept into the words, a weary humor that had clearly gotten plenty of practice, a man who'd introduced himself under stranger circumstances than this one and lived to make a joke about it. NYPD. Or I was, about ten minutes ago, before somebody decided to reroute my whole evening without asking.

A cop. Taylor felt something in her chest tighten, an old, familiar reflex, the one that had nothing to do with tonight and everything to do with every conversation she'd never had with an adult who might have actually listened. Great. That's great. That's exactly what I need right now.

She closed her right hand.

It closed.

She opened it, lifted one finger, then another. McClane followed the movements without causing them. She could feel his attention settle on her hand, but no pressure joined hers, no second set of muscles competing beneath the skin.

What are you doing?

Checking whether this is still my body.

The answer silenced him for half a beat.

It is, he said. Guy who sent me made that very clear. I don't get a vote on your hands unless you hand me one, and you can take it back whenever you want.

The guy who sent you.

Long story. Bad coffee. Weird paperwork. We can do it after we're not pretending to listen to a zoning speech.

Taylor moved her swarm. Every insect within range shifted toward the outside walls at once.

McClane reacted like the floor had dropped beneath him.

What the hell was that?

She stopped the movement. What was what?

The room got bigger. No, not the room. You. There were... points. Hundreds of them.

His confusion was real. Taylor felt it without wanting to, accompanied by an involuntary flash of another place: concrete, a moving belt, blood warm beneath a torn shirt, seven airplanes turning circles somewhere overhead. The impression vanished before she could separate memory from sensation.

McClane felt her catch it.

They both went quiet.

Bugs, Taylor said finally. I control bugs.

Of course you do.

What does that mean?

Means I finally get dropped into a teenager's head, and she's got the one superpower guaranteed to make every ventilation duct in the city feel personal.

Despite herself, a short breath escaped Taylor that might have become a laugh under better circumstances. The councilwoman glanced toward her. Taylor covered it by lifting the ginger ale.

You believe me, she thought.

Kid, I'm currently deadheading behind your eyes while you feel a moth on the other side of the room. Tonight's way past my usual standard of proof.

Relax. I'm not here to write anybody a ticket. A pause, and she felt him settle, some part of him going quiet and focused, nothing to do with calm and everything to do with a man deciding, deliberately, to stop panicking because panicking wasn't going to fix anything. I don't know what you are, or what just happened to either of us, and near as I can tell we're both stuck figuring that out together, whether we like it or not. So how about this. You keep smiling at Councilwoman Whoever, and I'll do what I'm actually good at.

Which is?

You're not gonna believe me if I just say it.

Try me. Tonight's already been weirder than most of my week.

Fair. Something that might have been the ghost of a laugh. Reading a room. Something in the words sharpened, and for just a second, underneath the confusion and the dry humor, she felt the edge of something that might have been useful, might have been dangerous, might, she'd learn later, have been both at once. Because I gotta tell you, kid, thirty seconds in, and I already don't like something about the guy who just walked off with your dad's hand still half-shook.

Taylor's stomach dropped, quiet and cold, underneath a smile she didn't remember choosing to hold onto.

What guy.

"And there it is, ladies and gentlemen! The villain is clocked!" the gallery said, delighted.

"Yeah, we should have Clockblocker here," Gladly said.

"Shut up, you," Fury said.
