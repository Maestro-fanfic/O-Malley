Chapter 27 of 29

Something We Keep

A week after the porch, on a slow Tuesday that felt almost aggressively ordinary, Taylor caught herself saying "yippie kay yay" under her breath while she reorganized her locker between classes, and didn't even notice she'd done it until the girl at the next locker over gave her a strange look.

Ordinary required more work than it used to.

Her right ankle remained inside a rigid brace. The crutches had become one crutch after the follow-up appointment, then a folding cane Danny bought at a pharmacy and insisted she carry even when she did not use it. Bandages pulled beneath her shirt when she reached for the upper locker shelf. The hearing in her left ear still faded at the end of crowded days, turning the hallway roar into something distant and underwater.

MedHall played on phones between classes.

Students replayed the chandelier drop beside the drinking fountain. Someone had edited the oxygen cylinder into a bowling clip with Kaiser as the last pin. A sophomore Taylor did not know claimed his uncle had been in the atrium and that Tower was a forty-year-old PRT intelligence officer who controlled rats. Three competing rumors identified Armsmaster, Miss Militia, or an unnamed Case Fifty-Three as the insect cape.

No one looking at Taylor saw farther than Danny Hebert's injured daughter, one of two hundred people unlucky enough to attend.

That protection did not feel permanent.

The defense attorney's description had reached Winslow before Taylor returned. Tall teenage girl. Dark hair. Green dress. At least six students knew she had worn green to the gala. Blackwell had called the house to discuss "media-management concerns" and found Danny, a Dockworkers attorney, and a PRT liaison already on the line.

The second call had been shorter.

At school, Taylor's locker had been moved to a corridor covered by a working camera. Emma, Sophia, and Madison had received a temporary no-contact order while the investigation into January reopened. None had been suspended. Blackwell called that neutrality. Danny called it preserving the school's options.

Taylor called it Tuesday.

You're doing it again, McClane said, amused.

Doing what.

Talking like me. You've been doing it for days. Little things. The flat delivery when you're annoyed. That thing where you call something "a hell of a time" for the wrong thing to happen. Something almost fond in it, underneath the observation. Didn't figure you'd notice on your own before I said something.

Yesterday I told Mr. Quinlan his homework policy had a hostage-negotiation problem.

It did.

He asked whether I was feeling all right.

Reasonable response.

Taylor shifted her history book to the lower shelf. Danny says I've started saying “pal” when I'm angry.

Could be worse. Could've picked up my taste in undershirts.

I don't know what that means.

Then O'Malley has spared you one thing, at least.

Taylor closed her locker, slower than she needed to, turning the thought over. Is that bad?

No. No hesitation in it at all. It's just what happens, I think, when two people spend enough time sharing the same space. You pick things up. I've probably picked up a few of yours too, if I'm honest, and I haven't even got a body to notice it in yet.

Like what?

McClane considered.

I count smaller now.

What does that mean?

Used to walk into a room and get people, doors, weapons. Big shapes. Now I catch the moth in the light fixture. Ant trail under the vending machine. Which guy keeps scratching one sleeve because there's something moving under it.

Taylor glanced down the hallway. A boy near the stairwell rubbed his left cuff for the third time. A tiny beetle had become trapped between fabric and skin.

She guided it out.

The boy stopped scratching without ever knowing why.

Useful? she asked.

Annoyingly.

You do this thing. Cataloging exits before you catalog how you feel about a room. A beat, something almost self-deprecating in it. I used to think that was just me. Cop thing. Turns out it's just a scared-kid thing that never really goes away, whether you're a cop or a teenager with a swarm of bugs and a father helping write her check-in rules.

Taylor didn't have an answer for that right away, and let the hallway noise fill the silence instead, lockers slamming, the low roar of a school day nobody else in the building knew was anything but ordinary. She thought about the last month, cataloging exits at pep rallies, cataloging exits in the cafeteria, cataloging exits at her own kitchen table, and wondered, not for the first time, how much of that had actually started with him and how much of it had just been waiting inside her all along for someone to finally recognize it out loud.

A month ago, before any of this, she'd have called that same habit anxiety and left it there, one more small, embarrassing thing about herself she didn't examine too closely. It was strange, having a name for it now that didn't sound like a diagnosis. Strange, and a little bit like being handed a tool she hadn't known she'd been missing her whole life.

Tools could still be used badly.

Emma waited at the end of the corridor with Sophia and Madison, technically more than the required twenty feet from Taylor's locker. Madison watched the camera. Sophia watched Taylor's injured leg. Emma watched Taylor's face.

The arrangement would have frightened her two weeks ago. It still did. The difference was that fear arrived with information now.

Camera behind Taylor, working. Faculty-room door open thirty feet left. Stairwell right, empty except for one student descending. Sophia's weight forward on the balls of her feet. Madison holding a phone but not recording yet. Emma expecting Taylor either to retreat or cross the distance first.

You don't have to win the hallway, McClane said.

They're violating the order.

Then make that the problem. Don't let them trade it for whatever they want you to say next.

Taylor took the Tower handset from her bag. It looked like an ugly pager with one recessed button.

Madison lowered her phone.

Sophia shifted her weight back.

Emma smiled. "Really? You're calling the heroes because we stood in a hallway?"

Taylor did not press the emergency button. She held the handset beside the visible camera and read the time from the clock above Emma's shoulder.

"Documenting contact at ten forty-three. North second-floor corridor. All three subjects inside the restricted distance."

There was no recording light. Emma did not know that.

"We didn't speak to you," Madison said.

"You just did."

The faculty-room door opened. Mr. Quinlan stepped into the hall carrying a coffee mug and stopped when he saw the four of them.

Sophia moved first. She turned and walked toward the stairs. Madison followed. Emma remained for one second longer, smile fixed in place while the situation refused to become the one she had arranged.

Then she left too.

Taylor's hands began shaking after they turned the corner.

You didn't threaten them, McClane said. Didn't bluff past what you had. Put time, place, and witnesses on the record. That's how you make the next conversation start with what they did instead of how you reacted.

I was terrified.

Yeah. Competence doesn't cure that. Mostly it gives your hands something useful to do while you're terrified.

Mr. Quinlan approached carefully. "Do you need the office?"

Taylor considered the routes. Considered the old instinct to say she was fine and disappear before help could become another problem.

"I need you to write down what you saw," she said.

He looked toward the stairwell, then nodded. "All right."

That answer felt like a victory too small for television and large enough to keep.

Which one is it, do you think, she asked. Something you gave me, or something you just noticed I already had?

Honestly? Bit of both, probably. No certainty pretended where none existed. I didn't put anything in you that wasn't already there. I just know what to call it, and knowing what to call a thing changes how loud it gets to talk.

Can I ask you something, she said, finally, once she'd found a quiet stretch of hallway to walk without anyone close enough to overhear a girl apparently talking to nobody.

Always.

Holly. Your wife. She felt him go still at the name, not guarded exactly, just careful, the way a man got when a wound hadn't finished healing all the way. You don't talk about her much.

No, he agreed. I don't.

Why not?

He didn't answer right away, and when he did, the dry humor had gone out of it entirely, replaced by something quieter, harder-won. Because I spent twelve years being real good at my job and real bad at coming home from it. Not bad like I didn't love her. Bad like I never figured out how to leave the job at the door when I walked through it. Brought it inside with me every time. The watching. The cataloging. Never really turned it off, not for her, not for anybody.

That sounds lonely.

It was. No self-pity in it, just plain fact, offered flat. For both of us, probably, though she never once said so out loud, because she wasn't the kind of woman who made a man feel smaller than he already felt about it.

What's she like. If you don't mind me asking.

A pause, and when he answered, something in the flatness had softened into something closer to reverence. Sharp. Sharper than me, most days, though she'd never say it that plainly herself. Funny in a way that snuck up on you. Stubborn enough to leave New York for a job in California that actually paid her what she was worth, even knowing what it'd cost us. I used to give her grief about that. Took me longer than I'm proud of to figure out she was right to go.

I'm telling you this because I don't want you making my mistake, kid. McClane's voice had gone careful now, deliberate, a man handing over something he clearly didn't hand over easily. You've got a gift most people never get. You can read a room, work a plan, keep your dad safe from things he doesn't even know exist. That's real, and it matters, and I'm not gonna tell you to stop being good at it. But there's a difference between carrying the job and letting the job carry you clean out of your own life. I didn't learn that difference until it cost me almost everything. I'd like it if you learned it a little cheaper than I did.

What would you have done differently?

The question stopped him longer than asking about Holly had.

Come home before I had an answer, he said. That's the first thing. I kept thinking I had to solve the case, fix the problem, become somebody who deserved to walk through the door. Meanwhile Holly was on the other side of it living with whatever version of me bothered showing up.

Danny wants details that could put him in danger.

Then tell him that. Give him the choice with the danger attached. Don't make the choice for him and call it protection.

Taylor thought of every patrol route she had hidden, every lie about homework, every time she had decided Danny was safer because he did not know enough to object.

What if he says no?

Then you argue. Maybe you lose. Maybe he does. Family isn't a command structure, no matter how convenient it'd be if everybody saluted and followed the plan.

You were a police officer telling your wife what was dangerous.

Yeah. I was also a husband who mistook being right about the danger for being right about everything that followed. Don't copy that part.

Taylor walked the rest of the hallway in silence, letting that settle, feeling the shape of it land somewhere she hadn't expected advice to reach.

You still love her, she said, eventually. Not a question.

Every day. Simple, unadorned, the truest thing he'd said all week. Some jobs you clock out of. That one, you don't. Wouldn't want to, even if I could.

Do you think about going back? To her. To your life.

A long pause, longer than she was used to from him, and when he answered, there was something almost gentle underneath the honesty. Every day. O'Malley said I go back to the instant I left. He neglected to say when that happens from our side, which is the sort of detail he probably finds funny. What she felt from him was not doubt that he would return, but the strain of having no date to count toward. I know I'm here now, with you, and I'd rather spend the time actually being here than turn every day into a waiting room for somewhere else. Something that might have been a smile crept into the words. Besides. Somebody's gotta keep teaching you to read a room properly. Can't exactly do that from three thousand miles and one impossible universe away.

Taylor smiled, actually smiled, the first easy one she'd let herself have in days, and pushed through the doors into the parking lot, into ordinary autumn light, into a life that looked, from the outside, exactly like it had a month ago.

Her bus did not arrive for twelve minutes.

Taylor sat on the low brick wall beside the student lot and took out the Tower handset. The ordinary call function reached home through a number the PRT had routed without putting either Hebert name on the account.

Danny answered on the third ring.

Taylor tapped the transmit button twice before speaking.

"Did you feed the dog?" he asked.

"He ate twice."

The ritual sounded ridiculous in daylight. That was part of why it worked. No one listening casually would hear anything but a father checking on an animal they did not own.

"How was school?"

Taylor looked back at the second-floor windows. "Contact violation at ten forty-three. Quinlan witnessed it. I documented the location. No physical contact."

Danny went silent for one breath. "Are you safe now?"

"Yes."

"Good. We handle the report when you get home."

Not I'll handle it. Not forget it. We.

Taylor tightened her grip on the handset.

"I'm meeting the Undersiders."

The parking lot continued around her. Engines started. A basketball struck asphalt near the gym. Somewhere across the street, her swarm found two men unloading bread from a truck.

"Today?" Danny asked.

"This afternoon. No job. No patrol. I need to tell them circumstances changed before they hear it from someone else."

"Where?"

Taylor gave him the address. The old instinct objected so sharply that the words almost stopped. She said them anyway.

"Check-in?"

"Five-fifteen. Escalate at five-twenty-five. If I use the dog phrase without two taps, call Tower dispatch immediately."

"John with you?"

Tell him I'm insulted he has to ask, McClane said.

"Yes. He's insulted you asked."

Danny exhaled through his nose. "Good. Tell your opinionated backup I remain reserving judgment."

Fair.

"He says fair."

"Five-fifteen, Taylor."

"Five-fifteen."

She ended the call.

Nothing about giving Danny the address made her less capable of surviving the meeting. Nothing about his knowing placed him automatically in the room. The information existed outside her head now, attached to a clock and a person who would notice if the line broke.

It felt less like surrender than she had expected.

"Alright," Gladly said, brightening. "Since we're doing sincere moments, can I say something too?"

"No," Fury said.

"I wasn't done asking."

"The answer's still no."

"I contain multitudes, Fury. Let me multitude at her for one second."

Taylor laughed out loud this time, quick and surprised, and got a strange look from a passing freshman that she didn't have it in her to care about.

You good, kid? McClane asked, quieter now, just for her.

Yeah, she said, and meant it, really meant it, for the first time in what felt like a very long stretch of careful, guarded days. Yeah. I think I actually am.

Somewhere behind that thought, distant and steady, she felt him settle too, two strangers who'd been thrown together on the worst night of one very strange October, finding, against every reasonable expectation either of them had started with, something that was starting, cautiously, to feel less like an accident and more like a partnership worth keeping.

Yippie kay yay, she thought, quiet, almost private, a joke just for the two of them now, worn smooth from use, warm in a way it hadn't been that first terrified night in a cargo hold four thousand miles and one impossible bridge away.

Mother Fluffer, McClane finished, and she could hear the smile in it even without a mouth to make one with, and somewhere in the anonymous rows, she was almost certain, though she'd never actually admit to hearing it, somebody started quietly taking bets on which word came next time.
