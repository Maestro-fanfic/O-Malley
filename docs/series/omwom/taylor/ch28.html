The Waiting Room

She fell asleep on the couch that night, homework unfinished, television murmuring something she'd stopped following twenty minutes earlier, and found herself, without any sense of transition, standing in a room that hummed with the low electrical hum of every ops center she'd never actually visited, radar scope pulsing its slow green sweep against one wall.

The transition removed the couch but not the body that had fallen asleep on it.

Taylor's ankle brace remained beneath the cuff of her jeans. The stitches across her back pulled when she straightened. Her left ear carried the faint cotton-filled distance that arrived after long days. Even the ink mark on her palm, a reminder to call Danny at five-fifteen, had followed her into the room.

This was not a dream offering escape from consequences.

It was a place where consequences had chairs.

She knew this room. She'd never seen it, not with her own eyes, but she knew it the way you knew a place you'd lived in without ever quite touching, borrowed and familiar all at once.

Consoles formed two curved rows facing the radar display. Paper flight strips waited in plastic racks. A headset rested beside a coffee cup with a brown ring dried around its rim. Beyond the nearest bank of equipment stood a wall of dark windows that reflected the room without showing anything outside it.

Taylor knew which chair McClane had occupied during O'Malley's offer. She knew where he had tested the floor before trusting it. She knew the coffee had been burned hours before either of them arrived.

The memories were his.

Recognizing them with her own eyes made them briefly theirs.

And across from her, solid, real, unmistakably present in a way that made her breath catch, stood a man.

Mid-forties, tired around the eyes with the wear of more than one bad night, undershirt still faintly bloodstained from a fight that had happened, for him, what felt like only hours ago. He looked as disoriented as she felt, turning a slow circle, taking in the consoles and the burnt coffee smell and the wall clock ticking its steady, unhurried hours.

His right hand searched for a weapon before he remembered none had followed him. His left protected the knife wound automatically. Taylor had felt both instincts from inside. Watching muscles perform them made the voice in her head abruptly, uncomfortably human.

"McClane?"

He turned fast, cop-fast, and then went very still, staring at her the way a man stared at something he'd spent weeks knowing only as a voice.

"You're..." He stopped, started over, something working hard behind his eyes. "You're a kid. I mean, I knew that. I knew the number. Didn't stop it from being a whole different thing, seeing it."

His gaze moved over the ankle brace, the careful way she held one shoulder, and the healing cut visible above her collar.

"Jesus, kid."

"You knew about those too."

"Knowing and seeing. Whole different thing. Apparently that's tonight's theme."

"Yeah." Taylor felt suddenly, strangely shy, aware of herself in a way she hadn't been in weeks of talking to a voice with no face attached. "You're taller than I pictured."

"Everybody says that." A ghost of the old dry humor, warm underneath the disorientation. "Guy in the movies was shorter. Long story."

They stood six feet apart with a month's worth of intimacy and no idea what ordinary greeting fit it.

McClane offered his hand.

Taylor took it.

His palm was callused. His grip remained careful until hers tightened, then answered. The contact carried no thoughts, no shared balance, no borrowed instinct. For the first time since MedHall, McClane was entirely outside her.

The absence inside her head lasted only as long as the handshake. It still frightened her.

He saw that too.

"Still here," he said aloud.

His voice also answered from within.

Taylor released a breath. "Good."

"Gentlemen. And lady." A third voice, unhurried, amused, and Taylor turned to find a man in a DULLES OPS windbreaker seated at the console, watching them both with the satisfaction of someone who'd been looking forward to this moment for a while. "I do apologize for the informal venue. I find reunions go better somewhere familiar."

"O'Malley." McClane didn't relax exactly, but something in his stance shifted, recognition settling into wary respect. "You do this a lot? Pull people out of their own heads for a chat?"

"Rarely," O'Malley said. "Only when the partnership's actually earned it. Which, I should say, this one has, rather more thoroughly than most."

Up close, his borrowed face carried the same almost-correct quality McClane remembered. The operations chief from Dulles, but assembled from memory by something that understood every measurement and missed one human fraction. His smile began at the right time and reached his eyes a heartbeat late.

Taylor had promised Danny a conversation.

"You didn't ask me."

O'Malley's smile disappeared.

McClane turned from Taylor to him. "There it is."

"Yes," O'Malley said.

"You asked John," Taylor continued. "You explained the rules to him. You put him in my head while I was standing in a crowded room and didn't give me a choice."

O'Malley rose from the console. The satisfaction left his posture with the smile. "I did."

"Why?"

"Because I believed the moment required speed, and because the design of the connection made refusal after contact possible. I treated the ability to end an imposition as equivalent to consent before it. It is not."

Taylor had prepared for a joke, a cosmic explanation, or a statement that everything had worked out.

The admission left her without the next line.

McClane found one. "You do that often?"

"No answer improves what I did here."

"Wasn't asking for improvement."

"More often than I should have. Less often than I once did."

The wall clock ticked through the silence.

O'Malley faced Taylor. "The connection can be ended here. Entirely. John returns to the instant he left. You retain your own memories and whatever you have learned. No penalty, no erasure, no replacement passenger. If either of you asks, I will do it."

McClane looked at Taylor. Not through her, not from behind her thoughts. Across the room, giving the decision physical space.

"Your call, kid."

For one instant Taylor imagined silence inside her head. Complete privacy. No dry comment when she missed an exit, no second perspective finding the part of a room she overlooked, no witness to the thoughts she could not say aloud.

The idea hurt.

That did not make the decision automatic. Keeping someone because losing him hurt was not the same as choosing the arrangement that placed him there.

Taylor looked at McClane. "Do you want to go?"

He gave the question its full weight.

"I want to get back to my family. O'Malley says that costs them no time. I also want to stay until you don't need backup every time the room gets bad. Maybe longer, if you'll have me and the rules stay the rules. Both things are true."

Taylor turned to O'Malley.

"Then I choose to keep the connection. Now. Not retroactively. You were still wrong."

"Yes," O'Malley said. "I was."

"And if either of us changes our mind?"

"This room remains available. The first clear request ends it."

McClane stepped beside Taylor, not in front of her. "Put that in whatever passes for writing around here."

O'Malley took a blank flight strip from the rack. Words appeared beneath his pen as he wrote them. He turned the strip around.

EITHER PARTY. FIRST CLEAR REQUEST. IMMEDIATE RETURN. NO PENALTY.

Taylor read it. McClane read it. O'Malley placed it beneath the glass top of the central console where none of them could pretend it had not been said.

"Danny wants his name on the list of people unhappy with you," Taylor added.

O'Malley's almost-correct smile returned by a cautious degree. "That seems fair."

Taylor looked back at McClane, really looked, cataloging without meaning to, the same way she suspected he was cataloging her: the set of his jaw, the tired kindness in his eyes, a man who'd spent a month of her life as nothing but a voice and was, in the flesh, exactly as solid and exactly as tired as she'd always imagined. There was a scar along one forearm she'd never known about, pale and old, and a newer line of stitches at his ribs that made her chest ache just looking at it, evidence of a fight she'd only ever felt secondhand, filtered through a voice that had never once let the pain slow down what it needed to say.

"That's from the knife," she said, before she could stop herself, nodding at his side. "Isn't it. That first night."

"Yeah." He glanced down at it like he'd half forgotten it was there. "Guess some things follow you into the dream whether you want them to or not."

"You never told me how bad it was."

"Was busy."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the one I've got."

Taylor folded her arms.

McClane stared at the gesture, then laughed. "Okay. That's your dad. You got that whole thing from him."

"He says you talk with your hands when you think you're losing an argument."

"Your father and I need less access to each other's observations."

"He said thank you. Properly."

The humor settled.

"You told me."

"I repeated the words. That's different from seeing you hear them."

McClane nodded. "Then I heard him. Properly. Tell him again when you wake up."

"He also wrote six headings of safety rules on a medical bill."

"Six?"

"Contact. Backup. No one else knows is not a plan. Curfew. Team tomorrow. PRT with witness."

McClane counted them on his fingers. "That's a good list. Curfew's gonna be a problem."

"For me or him?"

"Yes."

Taylor told him about the school hallway, Mr. Quinlan's witness statement, and the call before her Undersiders meeting. McClane had experienced the events from within her, but telling him face to face changed their shape. He interrupted only to ask whether Danny had filed the contact violation and whether the five-fifteen check-in had happened.

"On time," Taylor said.

"Good."

"Lisa knew something had changed before I sat down."

"Also not exactly shocking."

"I told them my father knows I cape. I didn't tell them about you."

McClane glanced toward O'Malley. "Some things stay need-to-know."

"That's what I decided."

"Your decision," he emphasized.

The flight strip beneath the console glass made the words heavier.

"Ortega asked about you," Taylor added. "He sent a message through Miss Militia. It said Tower still owes him a stun charge and one functional knee."

"Tell him the knee was defective when issued."

"I already did."

McClane looked delighted. "See? Talking like me."

"I wanted to say thank you," she said, before she lost her nerve. "Properly. Not just in my head at two in the morning when I'm too tired to say it right."

"Kid, you don't owe me anything..." He stopped himself, huffed something that was almost a laugh. "Force of habit. You're allowed to say thank you. I'm allowed to hear it. Go ahead."

"Thank you." She said it plainly, and meant every word of it. "For not making me be brave alone. For teaching me the difference between a plan and a hope. For never once trying to take the wheel, even when it would've been so much easier for you if you had."

Something moved across his face, complicated, moved. "You're welcome." A beat. "And thank you, right back. For trusting a stranger with the inside of your own head. For being smarter and steadier than most rookies twice your age. For giving a washed-up cop stuck between his own bad night and God knows what else a reason to feel useful again."

"You weren't washed up."

"Felt like it, some nights." He said it without self-pity, just plain fact. "You gave that back to me. Didn't ask me to. Did it anyway."

Taylor stepped forward.

McClane did not assume. He waited until she put both arms around him, then returned the embrace carefully, one hand between her shoulders and the other avoiding the bandaged line beneath her shirt. He smelled like stale airport air, blood, and the soap from a bathroom he had used hours before entering a month of her life.

It lasted only a few seconds.

It was not paternal, romantic, or anything else requiring a name they had not chosen. It was proof that the person who had occupied every frightened silence was real enough to hold once, and disciplined enough to let go when she did.

When Taylor stepped back, McClane cleared his throat.

"All right. That's enough emotional competence for one night."

"You started it."

"I distinctly remember you saying thank you first."

O'Malley, tactfully, occupied himself with a strip of paper that didn't need his attention, giving them room.

"Is Holly okay?" Taylor asked, quieter now. "Do you know? Wherever you actually are right now?"

Something soft and complicated crossed his face. "She's on a plane over Dulles with fuel running out and men on the ground trying to make sure nobody lands. So no. Not okay yet." He looked toward the motionless wall clock. "But O'Malley puts me back in the second I left. Whatever is happening there hasn't moved while I've been here. I go back, and I finish it."

"You sound certain."

"I have to be. Also, I'm very hard to kill. Ask anybody."

Taylor looked at the blood on his undershirt.

"Not evidence in your favor."

"Still standing."

He looked at her, steady. "She'd like you. For what it's worth. She always did have a soft spot for stubborn people who didn't know when to quit."

"Tell her I said thank you too," Taylor said, surprising herself with how much she meant it. "For sharing you for a month. Even if she doesn't know she did."

Something flickered across his face, gratitude and grief tangled too close together to fully separate. "I'll tell her. Someday. However that works, wherever this all actually goes." A beat. "She'd probably ask if you're eating enough vegetables, knowing her."

"I'm not that stubborn."

"Kid, you triggered a fire alarm to out-think a supervillain instead of running. You're exactly that stubborn."

Taylor felt herself smile, unexpectedly, warm all the way through.

"For what it's worth," McClane said, and his voice had gone careful now, deliberate, a man choosing his words the way he'd chosen them all month, "I meant what I said about your dad. About not letting the job carry you clean out of your own life. I know I'm not... I know I don't get to be anybody's father here. That's not what this is. But if a guy who's spent a month riding shotgun in your head gets one piece of advice, it's that. Keep somebody like Danny close. Whatever else this life ends up asking of you."

"I will." She meant it. "I promise."

"That's all I needed to hear."

The room's edges had begun, faintly, to soften, the give of a dream sensing its own ending, and Taylor found she wasn't afraid of it, not the way she might have expected to be.

"This a one-time thing?" she asked, echoing a question she didn't know had ever been asked in this room before, though something in O'Malley's expression suggested it had.

"I find," O'Malley said, mild, not looking up from his paperwork, "that certain doors, once properly opened, have a habit of staying that way. Should you ever need to find your way back here, I suspect you'll manage it well enough."

"Good," Taylor said. "I'd like that."

"So would I," McClane agreed.

Somewhere behind them, in rows that had never once been visible from inside the dream itself, the gallery rose quietly, the way an audience did when a story had earned its ending the hard way. Nobody riffed on it. Some things, even for them, earned the silence instead.

The room dissolved slowly, consoles and radar scope and borrowed face all fading like morning mist burning off a runway, and in the last, soft edges of it, Taylor heard his voice, warm, unmistakably certain, following her back toward waking.

See you tomorrow, kid, McClane said. Same as always. Not goodbye. Just until next time.

Yeah, she thought, feeling the couch cushions solidify back under her, the television still murmuring, her own ordinary living room waiting on the other side of sleep. Yeah. Until next time.
