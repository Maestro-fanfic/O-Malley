Chapter 5 of 29

Diverted

Okay, McClane said, once Anders had disappeared past the frosted glass doors at the end of the service corridor. Small test. Nothing that gets you noticed. You said you can send bugs places without anybody clocking it, that still true at a party like this?

It's true anywhere. Taylor kept her back to the corridor, eyes on the room, working a slow circuit toward the coat check the way a person did when they were bored, not hunting. Bringing them in is the hard part. This place has been swept for pests. Probably twice, given who's paying for the catering.

Then don't bring the whole army. Just enough to listen.

She found three that had ridden in on somebody's coat, half-frozen and sluggish from the cold outside, and walked them the long way around the room, along the baseboard, behind a curtain, under the gap beneath the service door. It took longer than she wanted it to. Cold insects moved like cold insects, no matter how badly she wanted them to move like something else.

You could warm them up faster, McClane offered, watching her work through the effort of it. Right? You said something about that earlier.

Not without moving them somewhere warm first, and the only warm places near that baseboard are people's ankles. I'm not doing that tonight. Not for a test run.

Fair. Didn't mean to backseat drive your own power.

You kind of are, though.

Yeah, he admitted, and there was something almost rueful in it. Occupational hazard. I'll work on it.

"This is the least cinematic surveillance sequence this reviewer has ever witnessed," the gallery observed, unimpressed. "No suction cup on a window. No guy in a van with headphones. Just three sad, chilly bugs."

"I know who you are, Gib," Fury said. "But now's not the time."

"Give her a break," said another voice, newer, warmer, overeager in the exact way of a substitute teacher trying to get the whole class excited about a pop quiz. "This is great tradecraft, actually! Working with what you have! I'd give this a solid B-plus!"

"Nobody asked you to grade it, Gladly."

"I grade things unprompted. It's part of my whole thing."

The bugs made it as far as the gap under the door before the trail went cold, literally, a draft of outside air rolling under the service corridor's far end that told her, more than anything she could actually hear, that the door at the other end was open to the loading dock. Voices, muffled, two of them, one she was fairly sure was Anders, the other lower, harder to place. No words she could make out. Just cadence, back and forth, unhurried.

Taylor pushed one fly farther into the gap and built the corridor from fragments. Painted cinderblock held the day's warmth. A rubber mat ran down the center. Air moved steadily from the loading dock, carrying diesel exhaust, wet pavement, and the sour remains of produce boxes broken down after the kitchen delivery.

Two pairs of shoes stood beyond the turn. Anders wore polished black oxfords. Taylor recognized them because she had watched them cross the atrium for half an hour. The second pair were heavy, square-toed, planted shoulder-width apart and never shifting through the whole conversation.

Something passed between them. Paper brushed cloth. A metal clip clicked shut.

The heavy shoes moved away toward the loading dock. Anders remained for three seconds, no more, then returned toward the party.

Taylor pulled the fly behind a pipe before the frosted door opened. From the atrium, she watched Anders emerge alone, adjusting one cuff. Nobody greeted him. Nobody appeared to notice he had been gone.

Exchange, McClane said.

You don't know that.

No. I know paper moved from one man to another where the cameras couldn't see it. Exchange is the short word until we learn the long one.

That's it. That's all I've got.

It's something. McClane didn't sound satisfied, exactly, but there was an edge of something like approval underneath the disappointment. Loading dock conversation, private, away from his own party. Guy hosting two hundred people doesn't step out to take a call unless the call matters more than the party does.

Or he stepped out for a cigarette.

Anders doesn't smoke. You'd have smelled it on him already, standing that close all night.

That was true, and Taylor hated that it was true, hated that a stranger she'd known for less than an hour had already built a more detailed profile of Max Anders than she had after years of watching him shake her father's hand at every Union function that mattered.

So what do we do with "something."

We find someone who'll actually listen to it.

That was where it fell apart.

She tried the closest thing MedHall had to security, a broad-shouldered man in an earpiece stationed near the coat check, wide as a doorway and twice as unmovable, and got exactly as far as "I think there's something weird happening near the loading dock" before his expression did the practiced thing adult faces did when a teenager said something they'd already decided not to take seriously. He told her, kindly, that the loading dock was for deliveries, that Mr. Anders sometimes stepped out to take calls his event planner would rather he didn't take at all, and that she should go enjoy the dessert table, there was a chocolate fountain, had she seen it.

"Someone met him there," Taylor said.

The guard's smile remained in place. His eyes changed, not alarmed, just more attentive to her than before. "A lot of people meet Mr. Anders. He's hosting the event."

"They passed something."

"Did you see what?"

"No."

"Did you see either person carrying a weapon?"

"No."

"Did you hear a threat? An argument? Someone asking for help?"

Each no made the next question easier for him and harder for her.

The guard touched two fingers to his earpiece, listened, and glanced toward the service doors. "Loading supervisor says Mr. Anders approved a late floral invoice. Paperwork, okay? Nothing dangerous."

That was fast, McClane said.

Taylor looked at the guard. "You checked?"

"That's what we're here for." His smile warmed, reassured by his own answer. "Everything's fine."

McClane's attention stayed on the earpiece. He didn't call anybody. Somebody was already listening and fed him the explanation.

Security listens to security.

Then why'd they need an explanation ready for a conversation nobody expected you to have?

Taylor stepped away before the guard could ask her name.

She almost tried again with a different word for it, something that might land better, weird replaced with suspicious, suspicious replaced with something more specific, and gave up somewhere in the middle of composing the sentence, because she could already hear how it would go. There wasn't a version of "a rich man looked at a hallway funny" that sounded like anything but a bored teenager inventing a mystery to feel important at her father's work party.

She thought, briefly and uselessly, about all the other adults in that atrium who might listen if she framed it right, the state senator, the hospital administrators, the woman who might have been a judge, two hundred people with badges and titles and the kind of institutional weight that could actually move something, and understood, with a clarity that tasted like defeat, that every single one of them would hear the exact same thing the guard had: a nervous kid, a rich man, a hallway. Money didn't just buy charm. It bought the benefit of the doubt from a whole room at once, and Max Anders had apparently been buying it for years.

She tried once more anyway.

A hospital administrator stood near the auction table, silver name badge identifying her as Marianne Holt, Vice President of Community Relations. Taylor waited until the woman finished speaking to a donor and delivered the account without saying bugs, voices, or the man inside her head. Mr. Anders had met someone at the loading dock. Something had changed hands. Security had supplied an explanation before checking with her.

Holt listened. That almost made it worse.

"Max handles half a dozen private pledges at every event," she said when Taylor finished. "Some donors don't want their names announced. Some vendors need signatures. I appreciate you paying attention, but discretion can look strange from the outside when you don't have the context."

"What context would make security know I was asking before they called anyone?"

Holt's expression tightened by a fraction. "I'm sure they followed procedure. Now, are you here with someone?"

There it was. Not what did you see. Who is responsible for you.

Taylor pointed toward Danny before the woman could summon him by name.

"Then I think you should enjoy the evening with your father," Holt said. "These events can be overwhelming the first time."

Taylor thanked her because failing to thank her would become the only part of the conversation anyone remembered.

"To be fair," Gladly offered, "'weird stuff by the loading dock' is not, strictly, a police report."

"You're supposed to be on our side, Gladly."

"I contain multitudes."

McClane's frustration came through clean and sharp, no dry humor left in it at all. That's the whole problem with a read like this. It's real. I'd stake my badge on it, if I still had one that meant anything tonight. But it's not evidence, it's not a crime in progress, and nobody in this building's gonna move on a hunch from a fifteen-year-old and a voice in her own head that she can't exactly cite as a source.

So we just watch. Same as before.

Same as before. A beat, and something in him settled, not calm, exactly, but controlled, a man deciding, deliberately, that a closed door was information too. We just watch harder.

She was three steps back toward the main room, already turning the problem over, already half-formed on some idea about the caterers, about whether the loading dock had a window she could put a bug through instead of under, when a hand closed gently around her elbow.

"Hey." Danny's voice, low, not sharp, but with an edge underneath the gentleness that told her he'd been watching her for longer than she'd realized. "Where are you headed?"

"Just looking around."

"You've been looking around by that service door for five minutes." He wasn't angry. That was almost worse. He had the look he got when he was worried about her in a way he didn't know how to say out loud, the same look from every parent-teacher conference where somebody had used the word "isolated" like it was a diagnosis. "You feeling okay? You want to head out early?"

"I'm fine, Dad."

"You don't look fine. You look like you're about to go do something." He said it lightly, almost like a joke, but his grip on her elbow didn't loosen, and his eyes had gone to the same service corridor she'd been circling for the better part of ten minutes, not with suspicion exactly, just a father's blunt, uncomplicated instinct that something near that door was pulling his daughter toward it, and that whatever it was, he didn't like it.

"Do what, exactly?"

"I don't know. That's what worries me." He almost smiled, the tired, fond kind he saved for moments when he knew he was being overprotective and had decided not to care. "You've had this look since we got here. Like you're waiting for something to go wrong so you can finally relax."

It was, Taylor thought, an uncomfortably accurate description of most of her life, and she hated that he'd noticed it standing three feet away from a man he genuinely believed was one of the good ones.

"I just wanted some air."

"There's a balcony for that. Nice one, too. I saw it on the way in." He steered her, gently, unmistakably, back toward the main room, back toward the string quartet and the chocolate fountain and two hundred people who had no idea anything in this building was wrong. "Come on. Stay where I can see you for a while, huh? Humor your old man."

"And there's the wall's other half," the gallery said, quiet now. "Nobody has to believe you're wrong. Somebody just has to love you enough to stop you before you find out."

Taylor let herself be steered. There wasn't a version of this where she explained it to him, not here, not now, maybe not ever, and the unfairness of that, of watching her own father's protective instincts work perfectly against the exact thing they were supposed to protect him from, sat in her chest like something swallowed wrong.

He's not wrong to stop you, McClane said, quieter than she'd heard him yet. For what it's worth. He doesn't know what he's stopping. Doesn't make the instinct bad.

Doesn't make it right either.

No, McClane agreed. It doesn't. Welcome to the job, kid. Half of it's exactly this.

She let Danny walk her back into the light and the music and the small talk, and didn't look at the service corridor again, though she could feel, distantly, three cold, sluggish bugs still sitting under a door that had already told her everything it was going to tonight.

For what it's worth, McClane said, once Danny had drifted off to refill his own drink, satisfied she was staying put, you handled that well. Didn't argue. Didn't push. Just let him think he'd won.

He did win.

Tonight, sure. Something almost like a smile in the words, tired and a little grim. Tomorrow's a different question. But tonight, letting him win was the smart play, and you found it without me telling you to. That's worth something.

She didn't answer, mostly because she wasn't sure yet whether being told she was good at lying to her own father was a compliment or something closer to a warning.
