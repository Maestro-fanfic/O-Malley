Chapter 15 of 29

Center of Gravity

Taylor almost missed him entirely, one more suit in a room full of them, if it hadn't been for the notebook.

She half-recognized him once she looked twice. Marcus Thorne, city desk, the byline under half the breathless crime stories the Brockton Bay Bulletin ran on a slow news week.

Nobody carried an actual paper notebook to a charity gala anymore, not unless they wanted people to notice they were working, and this man wanted exactly that, weaving through the crowd with the hungry energy of somebody circling a story he hadn't quite landed yet. She'd clocked him twice already tonight without registering it, once near the bar, once hovering at the edge of Anders's toast, always close enough to overhear, never quite close enough to be part of the conversation.

Reporter, McClane said, flat, the second she thought to ask. Local paper, probably. The notebook's a tell. Everybody else in that room's got a phone in their pocket. He wants people to see him writing something down.

Is that bad?

Depends what he's writing.

He's been talking to caterers all night. Kitchen staff. That woman from the coat check. Taylor watched him work the edges of the room with patience that almost looked professional if you didn't know better. Building something. I don't know what.

Guys like that build one of two things. A puff piece about a rich man's generosity, or something with teeth. Given how much shoe leather he's burning on the help instead of the guests, I'd bet on teeth.

For ten minutes, Taylor considered making him useful.

Thorne had the things she lacked: an adult face, a recognizable name, and an institution that answered when he called. He had spent the night asking questions. Questions were closer to belief than anything Taylor had received from security or the hospital administrator.

She followed him through her swarm while he interviewed a dishwasher near the kitchen doors. He began kindly, asked how long the man had worked for MedHall, laughed at a complaint about overtime, then circled toward security. Had the extra personnel arrived tonight? Did Max Anders usually inspect the loading dock himself? Had anyone been told not to use the east exit?

The dishwasher answered until he realized the answers formed a story. Then he looked toward the nearest man in a dark suit and remembered he needed to return to work.

Thorne wrote down the retreat too.

He sees it, Taylor thought.

He sees something, McClane corrected.

If I tell him about the locked door and the word “Boss,” he can call the police. He can put it in the paper.

Maybe. Or he can walk straight to Anders and ask for comment because that's what reporters do before they print an accusation.

You don't trust anyone.

I trust people to do the job they think they're doing. His job is getting a story with his name on it. Your safety comes somewhere after that, and he doesn't know you well enough to feel bad about the order.

Taylor watched Thorne close his notebook when a donor approached and reopen it the instant the donor left.

She decided not to contact him.

The decision should have protected her.

It was bad.

Taylor watched, borrowed and distant through a straggler bug drifting the edge of the crowd, as the reporter angled himself toward Anders with the boldness of a man who'd decided tonight was the night he stopped being polite about getting his story. He caught Anders between conversations, low, urgent, a hand half-extended like he was offering something instead of asking for it.

I need to get closer. I can't hear anything from here.

Don't. McClane's voice cracked through sharp, immediate. You get one bug close enough to actually hear that conversation, and if Anders's people are running any kind of counter-surveillance sweep right now, which after tonight I'd bet money they are, you just handed them exactly what they've been hunting for all night.

Then how do I know what he's telling him?

You watch his face. And you watch Anders's.

Taylor sent the bug anyway.

Not directly. She moved it along the underside of the bar, crossed behind a row of bottles, and settled beneath a folded cocktail napkin six feet from the conversation. Close enough for fragments. Far enough that finding it would not point toward her body.

McClane felt the choice and did not waste time arguing after it had been made.

"...girl near the loading dock," Thorne said. "Tall, dark hair, green dress. Asked security about Anders meeting someone. Then disappeared right when your people started circulating."

He spoke to Anders's chief of events rather than Anders himself, but Anders stood close enough to hear while appearing absorbed in another conversation.

"And you believe this concerns Mr. Anders?" the events man asked.

"I believe it concerns unusual activity at his gala. I believe the Hebert girl saw something. Her father runs the Dockworkers Association, which makes the loading dock angle play beautifully if there's a labor story underneath it." Thorne tapped his notebook against his palm. "I give you the courtesy of a warning before I ask her on record. You give me ten minutes with Max after the speeches."

The events man did not look surprised by Taylor's name. "On what subject?"

"The state review. Community-outreach contracting. Whatever has your temporary waiters guarding fire doors while the real ones hide in the kitchen." Thorne smiled as though the line were charming. "I'm offering Max a chance to get in front of it."

"And if there is no story?"

"Then ten minutes costs him nothing."

"If there is?"

Thorne rolled the notebook once through his fingers. "Then he gets to decide which version of his answer I print first."

He was not demanding truth. He was selling placement.

It was not a source traded for a byline. Taylor had never spoken to him. It was a child traded for access before she could become a source at all.

The events man looked toward Anders.

Anders did not turn. He lifted his champagne glass and touched one finger to its stem.

The events man smiled. "I'm sure Mr. Anders will make time."

Thorne relaxed.

Thorne added something about the girl repeatedly circling the service side of the building. His gesture drew the route in the air: loading dock, kitchen, mezzanine, then back toward the atrium.

"She approached building security after Mr. Anders left the loading corridor," Thorne said. "Security sent her away. Then your staff tried to pull her out of the room twice, and she disappeared. Her father has spent the last hour asking where she went."

The events man's professional smile became very still.

Thorne mistook recognition for interest. "That's the spine of it. Respected labor leader brings daughter to transparency gala. Daughter sees something at the loading dock. Host's private security isolates her. If it's nothing, Max says so on record and I kill the implication."

He saw the whole chain, Taylor thought.

Yeah, McClane said. Just never asked which person in it needed protecting.

Anders's eyes moved once, sharp and fast, toward the bar.

Toward Danny.

No. Taylor's whole borrowed sense of the room went cold at once. He gave them my dress. My hair. My dad's name.

Yeah, McClane said. Anger stripped every trace of humor from the word. He gave them enough.

Thorne kept talking, oblivious, delighted with himself, a man who thought he'd traded a rumor for an exclusive and had no idea what he had handed over. Anders nodded once, slow and thoughtful, then stepped out of his neighboring conversation as though he had only just noticed the reporter waiting.

"Marcus," Anders said warmly. "I'm told you've been doing excellent work tonight."

Thorne shook Anders's hand, pumped it twice like a man closing a deal, and Taylor felt something ugly and helpless twist in her chest. He walked away pleased with himself, already composing the story, never knowing what he had done to a fifteen-year-old girl he'd never met and a father whose only mistake had been trusting the wrong host.

"You traded a child for access," the gallery said, the movie-review distance gone. "You never even asked what it would cost her."

He gave him me.

He gave him you, McClane said. No comfort, because none remained to offer. Anders just stopped looking at two hundred people and started looking at one family.

Anders waited until Thorne had been absorbed by the crowd. Then he handed his untouched glass to the events man.

"The father?"

"At the bar. He has been asking staff to locate her."

"The girl?"

"No confirmed sighting since the dance-floor diversion. Laundry and mechanical levels were searched."

"Not well enough."

The events man accepted the judgment without defending his people. "Do you want the room closed?"

Anders looked toward the auction coordinator wheeling the state-review folder away from the display. The credential had not arrived. The guests still believed inconvenience was the worst thing happening to them. Every second of delay protected the operation's appearance and gave the unknown observer another second to transmit what she knew.

"Quietly," Anders said. "Move the father where we can keep him visible. The girl is alive until I say otherwise."

The events man touched his earpiece.

Three men answered by peeling away from their posts along the walls without any visible signal, the whole machinery of the building reorienting itself around a single point the way a compass needle swings north.

Anders turned. He wasn't looking toward the service corridor, the loading dock, or any of the doors his people had spent the last hour watching.

He was looking, slow and direct and entirely unhurried, toward Danny Hebert. Danny stood alone at the bar with a drink he'd barely touched, pretending to listen to the coordinator's newest assurance while his eyes kept searching for Taylor. He knew his daughter was missing. He did not know that fact had just made him the single most important person in the room.

McClane.

Yeah, kid.

He knows.

Yeah. Flat, honest, no version of this that came out easier however he said it. He knows.

Taylor stayed very still in the narrow pocket behind the laundry cart and watched, through a bug she could barely bring herself to keep steady, as Max Anders crossed the room toward her father with the same warm, unhurried walk he'd used all night, like nothing in the world had changed, like he wasn't about to have a conversation that could end with Danny Hebert understanding, for the first time, exactly what kind of building he'd brought his daughter into.

What do I do. It came out smaller than she meant it to, none of the careful composure she'd been holding onto for the last two hours left in it at all. McClane. What do I do.

You breathe. Steady, deliberate, a man building a floor under her one word at a time because there wasn't anything else left to build with yet. You breathe, and you let me think, and you trust that I've been in worse rooms than this one and walked more people out of them than you'd believe. We're not out of moves. We're just out of time to be careful about which ones we make.
