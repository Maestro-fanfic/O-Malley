Honest Trailer

The Honest Trailer

In a world where a New York cop can't even attend a Christmas party without a busload of terrorists showing up, and a teenage girl can't attend a single charity gala without her own personal supervillain infestation, one studio dared to ask the question nobody was asking: what if these were somehow the same movie?

This is Med Hard.

Watch, as our protagonist, a fifteen-year-old insect telepath with abandonment issues and a swarm of bugs she is legally not old enough to be trusted with, gets possessed, sorry, "passenger-seated," by a forty-something New York police officer with a body count and a marriage he can't stop thinking about. It's the story of two people who spend a month knowing each other more intimately than sight allows, meet exactly once in a cosmic air-traffic office, and still take twenty-eight chapters to define the relationship as "opinionated backup."

Because nothing says healthy mentor partnership like an omnipotent stranger obtaining consent from the adult man, forgetting to ask the teenage girl whose mind he plans to renovate, and getting called on it during the emotional epilogue.

"In my defense," O'Malley began.

The entire gallery leaned forward.

"No," he finished. "I was wrong."

The gallery leaned back, visibly deprived of a trial it had already prepared snacks for.

Follow along as our reluctant hero turns one charity gala into the world's most aggressive building inspection, using nothing but bugs, a wasp with a grudge, three tons of chandelier, one disabled elevator, two catering carts, an oxygen cylinder, and the improvisational genius of a man whose preferred tactical doctrine is "whatever the room forgot to bolt down." Witness the birth of a franchise catchphrase so committed to bowdlerization it's used four different substitute words before the book remembers the explosives under the mezzanine.

Then discover the terrorists are not terrorists, the hostage is not a hostage, the escape vehicle is actually a moving van for incriminating paperwork, and the charity gala's transparency speech is being delivered by a Nazi supervillain whose financial plan depends on nobody asking why the state-audit archive has its own demolition bracket.

That's right. It isn't merely Die Hard at a gala.

It's Die Hard at a gala with an evidence-preservation order.

"Excuse me," said a voice from the front row, familiar, air-traffic-controller flat. "I recruited that plot. Some respect."

This movie contains: one (1) knife fight in a cargo hold, zero (0) actual airplanes, a recruitment scene that is somehow both the most and least bureaucratic thing that happens all book, a gala that runs on chandeliers and chocolate fountains until it starts running on hostage counts, demolition receivers, roof charges, sprinkler mains, and one teenager's hand-drawn floor plan, plus exactly three uses of a sentence construction this narrator has personally sworn a blood oath never to use again.

"Four," Fury corrected.

"I'm choosing not to count the one in the credits."

Because let's be honest, this genre never actually stopped being one movie wearing different coats. We got Die Hard on a Plane. Die Hard on a Boat. Die Hard on this other boat, because apparently one boat wasn't enough. Die Hard on a Train. Die Hard in the White House. Die Hard on this other plane, except this time with snakes on it, because by that point the formula had officially eaten its own tail. And now, after four decades of finding new buildings to almost-not-technically remake the same movie in, we've finally arrived at the one nobody saw coming.

"Somebody was always going to do the gala eventually," Gib said. "I'm just glad it had this much heart in it when they finally got there."

So do yourself a favor, and enjoy: Die Hard At A Gala, With Superpowers, the crossover nobody commissioned, nobody expected, and, against every reasonable prediction this narrator was prepared to make going in, somehow actually earned its own ending.

Marvel, as a respected charity host turns out to be a literal Nazi supervillain, a twist absolutely nobody in-universe saw coming, despite the man checking hallway sightlines like a hostage negotiator and employing a security detail that, in retrospect, definitely should have set off more alarms than "huh, weird vibe." This is a movie that asks the hard question: what if the real villain wasn't the man with the metal hooks for hands, but the entire adult social structure of Brockton Bay that let him throw galas for a decade without anybody checking his tax returns?

Or, as it turns out, while scheduling someone to check his tax returns the next morning.

Gasp, as Kaiser's beautifully overcomplicated false flag uses two hundred civic leaders as witnesses to his own victimhood, demands an armored vehicle to remove court-preserved records through the loading dock, and rigs the archive wing to collapse if anyone notices the vehicle has eight figures of fascist accounting inside. It's a plan with everything: blackmail, insurance, political theater, murdered evidence, emergency powers, and a host whose greatest acting challenge is pretending not to recognize all five supervillains he personally invited.

The only flaw is one teenage girl noticing the archive credential arrive early.

Also the bugs.

Also the cop.

Also Miss Militia.

Also Max Anders being constitutionally incapable of letting an unknown observer exist for ninety minutes without turning his carefully rehearsed crime into the loudest possible version of itself.

So, five flaws.

Featuring an ensemble of Empire Eighty-Eight's finest, who turn MedHall into hostile territory one polite door closure at a time, then spend the rest of the night learning that declining a direct fight does not mean declining to drop the ceiling on them, trap them in elevators, bury them under catering equipment, or guide an entire PRT entry team around their powers using insects as runway lights.

Meet the official response, featuring Armsmaster as a man who sees a camera pointed by the villain and announces, "Finally, objective evidence," then sends a roof team into the ambush Tower warned him about. Watch him complete the rarest maneuver in superhero fiction: identify his own error, change tactics, help solve the problem, and later put his name on the failure without requiring a redemption death.

And meet Miss Militia, the only person outside who hears two radio clicks from a civilian source and immediately invents authentication, compartmentalization, and a code name instead of asking the teenager hiding under a banquet table to complete an intake form.

"Strong opening," McClane said.

"You judged her after two sentences," Gladly reminded him.

"And I was right."

"That's the theme," the gallery agreed, warmly, for once without a joke riding shotgun on it. "Outsmart, don't outfight. Somebody put that on a poster."

Try to keep track of the running bets on when the signature line was going to land, a betting pool so aggressive it required its own internal economy, its own scandal, and at least one physical assault committed against a man who, fair enough, absolutely deserved it for forgetting he himself was the punchline of a running joke about being the punchline of a running joke.

Watch as an anonymous gallery member, never named, never confirmed, absolutely definitely not a cyberpunk novelist's fictional surveillance van partner, quietly steals every scene he's technically not even supposed to be in, using nothing but a dry wit, an encyclopedic knowledge of a completely different 1994 action movie, and the collective refusal of everyone around him to just say his whole name out loud.

"I have a name," Gib said, wounded. "It's right there. Everyone knows it now."

"Doesn't count if we don't say it in the trailer," Fury said. "Rules are rules."

Featuring a final act that takes the phrase "use the building" as a personal challenge: a blown roof entrance, a PRT trooper hanging over a stairwell, five hostages placed beside a support charge, a covert radio confession, three evacuation routes with named civilian leaders, one partially collapsed mezzanine, and Kaiser discovering that the exits he spent all night controlling have finally been assigned to people who aren't him.

Thrill, as Taylor rescues a trooper who outweighs her by eighty pounds while barefoot on a damaged stair rail, because apparently the story looked at "walk over broken glass" and decided homage required a more ergonomic distribution of foot trauma.

Cheer, as the hostages do not transform into a perfectly obedient evacuation crowd just because the plan is clever. They jam doors. Choose the wrong route. Freeze at hidden panels. Follow public figures instead of bug arrows. Force Taylor to revise while the building is flooding, the capes are moving, and four demolition receivers are waiting to learn whether Armsmaster's electronics pulse speaks their language.

And applaud the structural engineering department, whose official contribution to the climax is "three receivers successfully disabled" followed immediately by "unfortunately the fourth one has opinions."

This is a movie about a girl who learns to read a room, a cop who learns to help without taking control, and a father who finds out exactly how close he came to losing everything, then responds by negotiating check-in procedures with the teenage supervillain hunter living in his house. Three separate character arcs, twenty-nine chapters, and somehow, against every reasonable prediction, all three land.

Watch Danny Hebert experience the full parental speedrun: fancy gala, hostage, human bomb marker, giant Nazi, secret bug daughter, invisible New York cop, and an argument about whether forty-five minutes is a reasonable patrol check-in interval. Then watch him reject both useless extremes, "you may never leave the house" and "apparently this is fine," in favor of writing NO ONE ELSE KNOWS IS NOT A PLAN on the back of a medical bill.

Parenting: now with duress authentication.

"Don't undersell it," McClane said, quiet, and there was nothing dry in it at all. "That's not nothing. That's the whole reason any of us showed up."

And ask yourself the two questions this narrator simply cannot get past. One: how does a swarm of insects, controlled telepathically by a fifteen-year-old, consistently build a better threat map than the official command vehicle outside? And two, far more pressingly: how did Max Anders think "pretend to be my own hostage while tapping orders against a councilwoman" was a plan that would survive contact with somebody who could listen through a fly under his collar?

"To be fair," Gladly offered, "rich people are very good at not looking directly at the help."

"That's the most honest thing anyone's said in this entire trailer."

Try to spot the exact moment this story stops being surveillance at a bad party and becomes a full building siege, a tonal shift announced by locked doors, confiscated phones, armed caterers, and Hookwolf putting his arm through a banquet table because subtlety has officially left the venue. Marvel as the entire architecture, laundry chute, dumbwaiter, hidden stair, fire doors, elevator, ventilation system, sprinkler mains, loading shutter, and deeply overinsured chandelier, becomes load-bearing plot infrastructure, proving once and for all that no detail introduced in Chapter Three is ever, ever wasted.

"Chekhov's laundry chute," the gallery said, delighted with itself. "Somebody frame that."

Then, after two hundred hostages, six structural charges, five named cape lieutenants, four rotating euphemisms, three exit routes, two injured fathers, and one extremely judgmental wasp, slow down for the quiet dream sequence where the partners finally meet face to face and the immortal plot facilitator receives his performance review.

Discover that "it worked out" is not a consent policy.

Learn that either partner can end the connection, immediately and without penalty, in terms so binding they are written on an air-traffic flight strip beneath glass.

And watch Taylor keep McClane, not because a crossover premise says she has to, but because once the choice is genuinely hers, she makes it.

"That's better," Fury said.

O'Malley looked at the flight strip. "Yes. It is."

Enjoy a soundtrack that exists only in this narrator's own head, because nobody actually budgeted for one, featuring exactly zero original songs and one (1) recurring string quartet that has, by the end of the book, survived more workplace trauma than most emergency responders.

Celebrate the statistically improbable achievement of a building siege with no civilian fatalities, though thirty-two people receive treatment, eleven are hospitalized, two PRT troopers are injured, four attackers die, one real security guard spends several hours misidentified because an attacker stole his blazer, and Alabaster contributes forty-three consecutive minutes of percussion from inside an elevator.

"He reset every few seconds," Gladly said. "Imagine how fresh the irritation stayed."

So c'mon, ride along with the cop who can't stop working, the kid who can't stop cataloging exits, and the gallery that can't stop betting on a rotating catchphrase nobody asked for but everybody, somehow, ended up loving anyway.

Starring:

Bruce Willis, Back When He Still Cared, as A Man Whose Vacation Insurance Clearly Does Not Cover This

Senator Fred Thompson, Yes, That Kind Of Senator, as The Guy Who Recruits People In A Room That Definitely Isn't A Real Tower

Miss Militia, as The Only Person On The Radio Who Understands That Listening Is An Action

Armsmaster, as Objective Evidence Has Logged On And Would Like To Apologize For The Roof

Danny Hebert, as World's Most Reluctant Mission Control, Operating From The Back Of A Medical Bill

That Guy Everybody Recognizes From A 90s Sitcom About A Blue-Collar Family But Can't Quite Place, Probably, As A Voice In The Cheap Seats Who Would Very Much Like It If Nobody Confirmed He Was Here

A Teenager With No Prior Screen Credits Whatsoever, Whose Extracurricular Activities Now Officially Include Domestic Terrorism Prevention

Max Anders, as Hans Gruber If Hans Owned The Building, Hosted The Party, Controlled The Charity, Scheduled The Audit, And Still Somehow Thought He Was The Subtle One

An Entire Charity Gala's Worth Of Empire Eighty-Eight Cosplaying As Rent-A-Cops, Doing Surprisingly Solid Rent-A-Cop Impressions

One (1) Wasp (Uncredited, Criminally), Doing More Damage To This Plot Than Most Named Characters

One (1) State Evidence-Preservation Order, as The Closest Thing This Movie Has To Bearer Bonds

A Horn Player Whose Union Rep Definitely Needs To Hear About This

A Marcus Thorne-Shaped Cautionary Tale About Sourcing, As Every Journalism Professor's Worst Nightmare

Directed by a Man Who Does Not Exist, As Far As Anyone Can Prove

"Yippie kay yay," the whole gallery said, together, for the first and only time all book, and let the last word hang there, unbowdlerized, unfinished, exactly the way the best endings always did.
