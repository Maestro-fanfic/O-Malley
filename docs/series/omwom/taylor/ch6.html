Chapter 6 of 29

Squawk

The rest of the evening went the way evenings like this always went for Taylor, slow and bright and loud in a way that had nothing to do with her, except now there was a second person trapped inside all of it with her, restless in a way she was starting to recognize as its own kind of language.

You're doing the fake-listening thing again, McClane observed, somewhere around the councilwoman's third circuit back to the zoning variance.

It's a skill.

It's a good skill. Wasted on her, though. She's not gonna say anything worth hearing. A pause, and she felt him doing the thing he did, the constant background scan, cataloging exits and angles and the rhythm of two hundred people none of whom knew anything was wrong. You said the bugs were slow tonight. Cold.

They warm up. Give them time.

How much time.

Numbers aren't really the problem. Not anymore. I multitask that like breathing at this point. She kept her voice down to something that barely qualified as breath, angled at a fresh ginger ale she had even less intention of drinking than the last one. They just need to be awake enough to pay attention. Cold ones are slow. Give them time to warm up and they're fine.

Then start warming them up. We don't have all night, but we've got enough.

She started anyway, and that was the problem, because the itch of it, the pull toward doing more, trying harder, finding some clean and clever use for a power she still barely trusted, felt uncomfortably good in a way she wasn't sure she was allowed to enjoy.

Doing it while remaining Danny Hebert's daughter at a charity gala turned out to be harder than doing it alone.

Danny introduced her to a city planner who remembered the ferry terminals before the storm of 1998. Taylor shook his hand while walking a beetle along a heating duct. A woman from the hospital board asked where she went to school. Taylor said Winslow without watching the woman's expression and used three flies to map the kitchen doors behind her. Someone placed a fresh ginger ale in Taylor's hand before she had finished abandoning the first one.

Every conversation demanded her face while the building demanded everything behind it.

McClane began calling turns like a driving instructor.

Smile. Not that much. Guy on your left's about to ask a question. Pull the one under the radiator back, somebody's moving a cart. Good. Now ask your dad something so you look like you're participating.

You said you weren't going to backseat-drive my power.

This is backseat-driving your cover. Different department.

Taylor asked Danny whether the hospital board chair had always talked with both hands. Danny nearly choked on his drink trying not to laugh. The city planner launched into a story about a budget meeting. Nobody noticed the first six bugs reach the service level.

The work began acquiring rhythm. Taylor did not send a continuous stream toward Anders. Continuous movement made a trail. She moved one insect, waited, changed level, sent another along a route with no visible relationship to the first. McClane had her break every pattern after three repetitions even when the pattern appeared to be working.

Anybody can follow success, he said. Professionals follow habits.

Are you calling him a professional?

I'm calling him alive and in charge. You don't get both for long by being careless.

It took the better part of an hour, working slow, working careful, never more than she could hold without her hands starting to shake, before she had a real net out. Not just the three cold, sluggish stragglers from before. A dozen, then two, drawn in through gaps under doors and along window seals, arranged in a long, thin loop that traced the edges of the room the way she'd trace it herself if she could walk the perimeter without anyone noticing.

The kitchen supplied most of them. Fruit flies lifted from the bins beneath the prep tables. Roaches emerged from the warmth behind the commercial ovens. Moths woke in the dry-storage room where sacks of flour held summer heat long after summer had ended. Taylor routed them away from food and exposed counters, unwilling to create the health violation everyone would reasonably blame on the only bug controller in the city if her identity ever came out.

The exterior ring remained thin. November had killed or buried most of what lived outside. She used spiders tucked into window corners and beetles sheltering beneath the loading ramp, creatures slow with cold but already positioned where she needed eyes.

For the first time since her power arrived, scarcity made each insect feel valuable.

She built it in layers, the way McClane described building a perimeter around a building he actually cared about: an outer ring along the exterior walls and window ledges, a middle ring threading through the service corridors and staff-only doors, and an inner ring, thin and careful, drifting the edges of the atrium itself, close enough to the crowd to catch a raised voice, far enough back that nobody's champagne glass was in any danger of gaining an uninvited passenger. Three rings, dozens of small, scattered bodies, all of it held together by a single thread of attention she was somehow, impossibly, managing not to drop.

The bugs themselves weren't the hard part. The routing was, a hundred small decisions instead of one big one, where to send a given straggler so it wouldn't cross a bright patch of floor, which gap under which door was actually wide enough to matter, all of it new in a way raw numbers never had been. She'd never had a reason to think this carefully about the path, only the destination.

You're doing the tactical thing now, McClane said, something like approval under the observation. Not just moving bugs. Planning routes. That's new for you, isn't it.

Never had anything worth planning a route for before.

Well, get used to it. It's a good habit. Wish somebody'd made me learn it slower, instead of the way I actually learned it.

That's better, McClane said, and there was something almost like approval in it, warm underneath the flatness. That's a real perimeter. You built that in an hour, on your first real try, at a party, without anybody clocking it. That's not nothing, kid.

"There's no music," said the newer voice, the warm, overeager one Taylor still didn't have a name for. "This is prose. There's no soundtrack department."

"There's always a soundtrack department. You just have to imagine it hard enough."

Taylor almost smiled, and caught herself before it showed, because a girl standing alone near a dessert table with an unreadable little smile was exactly the kind of thing that got noticed at a party like this, and getting noticed was the one thing McClane had been very clear she couldn't afford tonight.

It was the smile, or something like it, that she was still fighting down when the first bug died.

It wasn't dramatic. It never was, with something this small, just a single thread of awareness winking out along the loop she'd built, gone the way a light went out, no drama to it at all. She almost missed it entirely.

The fly had been clinging beneath a conduit eight feet above the loading corridor. No shoe could have reached it. No closing door had crossed its position. One instant it felt vibration through painted metal; the next, nothing.

What was that.

One of the bugs. Near the loading dock corridor. She was already reaching for the next one in the loop, sending it wide around the spot where the last one had died, cautious now in a way she hadn't been a second ago. Could be nothing. A door closing wrong. Somebody's shoe.

Could be somebody found it and didn't like finding it.

The second one died four minutes later, closer to the main hall this time, near enough to the loading dock corridor's mouth that the pattern was hard to argue with. Not swatted. Not stepped on by accident. Something colder than that, something that felt, in the brief half second before the awareness winked out, like being noticed back.

Taylor replaced it from the opposite direction.

That bug survived twenty-two seconds.

Before it died, she felt a narrow object touch the baseboard and move along it at a measured pace. Not a broom. Too small. Too controlled. The object stopped beneath the bug's hiding place, rose, and ended the signal.

Someone was probing every seam where an insect might conceal itself.

Not killing pests. Testing routes.

A third followed less than a minute after, and then a fourth, both from the middle ring this time, both near enough to each other that Taylor could feel the shape of a hand sweeping methodically along a stretch of hallway, patient, thorough, the work of someone who wasn't hunting randomly, but clearing a grid one square at a time. She pulled the rest of that ring back fast, hands going cold around her ginger ale glass, her own heartbeat suddenly loud enough to hear over the quartet, fear doing what four dead bugs alone hadn't quite managed.

"There it is," the gallery said, all lightness gone. "That's the turn. Somebody just started watching the watcher."

Anders, McClane said, and there was nothing uncertain in it at all, the flat, immovable certainty of a man who'd spent thirty years learning exactly when certainty mattered, and hearing it settled something cold and low in Taylor's stomach.

You don't know that.

I know somebody just killed two bugs with surgical precision, near the exact hallway where our guy stepped out for a private conversation an hour ago, in a pattern too clean to be an accident. His voice had gone flat, professional, every trace of the earlier warmth folded away. That's not a coincidence read, kid. That's somebody who's dealt with exactly this kind of surveillance before, and knew exactly what he was looking at the second he found it.

So he knows. About the bugs. About me.

He knows something's watching him. A beat, careful, deliberate, like a man choosing each word before he spent it. He doesn't know it's you. Not yet. Could be anybody in this building, as far as he knows. Could be a rival. Could be a fed. Could be a ghost story he's telling himself because he's paranoid and tonight happened to give him a reason.

But he's looking now.

He's looking now.

Taylor let the rest of the loop dissolve, called every remaining bug back the long way, slow, careful, nothing patterned enough to read as anything but random insect drift if anyone happened to be watching for it. Across the room, through the crowd, she caught one clean look at Max Anders, back from wherever he'd gone, glass in hand, laughing at something a city councilman had said.

He wasn't looking at her. He wasn't looking at anyone in particular. But there was something different in the set of his shoulders now, something coiled underneath the easy charm that hadn't been there an hour ago, a man who'd just confirmed something he didn't like and was working very hard not to let it show.

She watched him excuse himself from the councilman a beat too fast, watched his eyes flick once, briefly, toward the loading dock corridor and then away again, deliberate, like a man reminding himself not to look somewhere twice. It was such a small thing. She wouldn't have caught it a week ago. She wasn't sure she liked how easily she caught it now.

"I don't love this," the newer voice said, its eagerness gone. "I don't love this at all."

"Nobody loves this," Fury said. "That's usually when it's about to get good."

Taylor set the ginger ale down, mostly full, and didn't reach for another one, and somewhere in the back of her mind, distant and steady, she felt McClane doing the same slow, careful math she was, both of them arriving, separately and together, at the exact same uncomfortable number: whatever tonight had been when it started, it wasn't that anymore.
