Chapter 23 of 29

Clearance

The building plans arrived on the wounded trooper's forearm.

His name was Luis Ortega. Taylor learned that while a MedHall guard wrapped his knee and the small display built into his armor filled with floor diagrams, utility layers, and emergency-system routes transmitted from the PRT command vehicle outside.

The display was made to be read through a helmet. Ortega had to hold his forearm six inches from his face and close one eye to keep the lines still. Taylor knelt beside him on the laundry floor while a catering supervisor steadied his elbow. Each time the radio spoke, the plan compressed to make room for a text box, then expanded again with a different floor centered.

"Command says these came from city inspection records," Ortega said. "Last full filing was eighteen months ago."

"The building changed since then."

"Buildings aren't supposed to do that secretly."

The older MedHall guard gave a short laugh with no humor in it. "This one does a lot of things it isn't supposed to."

The official plans disagreed with Taylor's map in six places.

A storage room existed where the plans showed open office space. The west service corridor ended twelve feet earlier on paper than it did inside the wall. Two ventilation trunks had been rerouted around the locked equipment room. The elevator machine space was larger than its listed dimensions. A stair behind the clinic had been removed from the latest plan but remained physically present.

Taylor marked each disagreement on her paper map. Ortega added a symbol beside it on the digital plan and sent the correction outside. Six small acts of disobedience became six tactical routes as soon as someone admitted they existed.

The hidden stair bothered her most. It connected the clinic, the first-floor service level, and the basement without touching any public hall. Anders could leave the atrium as a hostage, vanish behind the clinic, and reach the loading dock while cameras continued showing an old loop of him seated beneath the MedHall banner.

That's his exit, McClane said. Vehicles at the dock, charges on the building, everybody outside watching the front. Whatever else he's doing, that's how the hostage becomes the survivor.

Taylor drew a square around the stair.

Renovations without permits, McClane said. His building, his secret hallways, his charges. Guy's been designing his own crime scene for years.

Taylor overlaid the plans with her swarm and found the route Kaiser's crew had used all night: equipment room to hidden corridor, hidden corridor to sound booth, sound booth to every major support without crossing a public camera.

The charges were connected by radio, not wire. Each receiver sat inside a protective case beside the explosive block. The sound booth held the master transmitter.

Ortega enlarged the only receiver Taylor could describe precisely. Her insects supplied dimensions by walking its edges. He built the shape with a gloved fingertip: antenna, battery compartment, firing circuit, arming switch.

"Commercial blasting receiver with the markings removed," he said. "Modified case. Could be command detonation, timer, dead-man trigger, or any combination."

"Can you tell from the light?"

"Green means whoever assembled it wanted green to mean something. That's all I can promise."

Taylor had expected the uniform to come with answers. Ortega gave her limits instead, without pretending they were more useful than they were. It made her trust him more.

"Can we jam it?" Taylor asked.

Ortega shook his head. "Command might. Without frequencies, they'd have to flood the spectrum. That kills our radios too, and these could be set to trigger on signal loss."

"Can you disarm one?"

"If I can reach it and if it's conventional."

His injured leg answered whether he could reach the west support.

The MedHall guard finished wrapping it and tested Ortega's foot for circulation. Ortega tried to stand anyway. His knee bent sideways before he got halfway up. The catering supervisor and Taylor caught him beneath the arms.

"That's a no," the guard said.

"I noticed."

Ortega lowered himself onto a folded tablecloth. Frustration moved across his face once and was gone. He pointed at the plan instead.

"If you can get within sight of the receiver, describe every change when it arms. Do not pull the battery unless I tell you. Do not cut a wire because you've seen people cut wires on television."

I feel unfairly targeted, McClane said.

Have you cut wires because you saw it on television?

No. I've cut them because there wasn't time to find the guy who knew better.

Taylor studied the receiver through three insects. A toggle switch. Indicator light. Battery leads. Antenna enclosed in plastic. She could enter through the gap around the switch, but entering was not the same as understanding.

The plastic case vibrated faintly. Not a timer. A transformer or relay. Her insects could feel each pulse without knowing what the pulse meant. She withdrew them before a moving contact crushed the first ant.

Don't touch what you can't identify, McClane said. We need the five people moved first. Bomb gets less useful once nobody's standing beside it.

The west support sat beneath the mezzanine, twenty feet from the concealed service door Taylor had used earlier. Two gunmen guarded the five hostages. Fenja watched from the east side of the atrium. The sound-board operator could see the whole position.

Taylor and Ortega built the attempt around those four facts.

The concealed door could take the hostages out if it stayed unnoticed. The sound booth had to lose sight of the support. Fenja had to look east or up. The two gunmen had to be disabled together, because either one could shoot a hostage before Taylor crossed twenty feet.

There was no clean way to make all four things happen.

"Then don't make them happen together," Ortega said. "Stack them. First get proof. Then break sight. Then hit the guards. Leave the cape until movement starts."

Taylor looked at the atrium through her swarm. "Fenja can cross the room in seconds."

"Then you need fewer seconds."

I like him too, McClane said.

Taylor positioned moths beneath the sound-board camera. Beetles waited inside the concealed panel's floor track. Flies filled the upper atrium near the chandelier, not enough to attack Fenja, only enough to provide something large and sudden when Taylor needed her eyes elsewhere.

Anders rose from the hostage rows.

He asked permission to check on the injured man's wife. The operator granted it after a pause calibrated for the cameras. Anders crossed toward the west support with his hands visible.

He's going to your dad, McClane said.

Taylor picked up the stolen radio.

"I need the outside channel held open," she told Miss Militia. "No one speaks until I say."

"What are you attempting?"

"Proof."

Taylor left the laundry room through the mechanical corridor. Ortega caught her wrist before she passed.

"Take this."

He handed her a flat black device with a folding grip.

"Stun charge. Twist the cap, press the center, three-second delay. It will put everyone in a small room on the floor. That includes you."

"How small?"

"Smaller than the atrium."

She put it inside the torn hem of her dress.

"Three seconds means three seconds," Ortega said. "Not three after you hear it begin charging. The cap starts the circuit. The center commits it."

"What does it do?"

"Light, sound, pressure. Your inner ear will hate you. Keep your mouth open and don't be standing."

"You were carrying this on the roof?"

"We thought we were doing reconnaissance."

The shame in the answer did not belong to him. Taylor left before she said so, because neither of them had time to make the distinction feel better.

The hidden stair behind the clinic brought her to the first-floor service level. She crawled the last twenty feet through a ventilation return too narrow to turn around in, pushing the radio ahead of her. Dust coated her palms and entered her mouth every time she breathed.

Her wrapped ankle would not bend enough to follow cleanly. She had to pull the injured leg after her, toe scraping the sheet metal, while the cut on her other foot left a dark smear along the duct. The return narrowed around a bundle of cables halfway to the grille. Taylor exhaled, pushed the radio through first, and compressed one shoulder until she slid past.

Below her, a gunman shifted his stance.

Taylor stopped with the cable bundle pressed across her ribs. She could not retreat. She could not fill her lungs. Dust tickled the back of her throat.

The gunman looked up at the grille.

A fly landed on the charge receiver. Taylor sent it walking across the indicator light.

The gunman glanced down instead and nudged the case with his shoe. The light remained green. He returned his attention to the room.

Taylor moved again.

Below the grille, Anders stopped beside Danny.

"I owe you an apology," he said.

Danny looked at the charge fixed to the column, then at the gunman six feet away. "For the party?"

"For involving Taylor. That was not part of tonight's intention."

Danny's face changed. "You know where she is?"

"No. You should be proud of that. My people are very good. She has made them look remarkably ordinary."

Taylor placed the radio against the grille and held the transmit button with two beetles wedged beneath its edge.

Outside, Miss Militia listened.

"What people?" Danny asked.

Anders glanced toward the nearest gunman. The man looked away, granting privacy to the supposed hostage.

"Tell your daughter to come forward. She has a phone or radio. You will tell her that nobody needs to die tonight."

"You've got bombs on the walls."

"Precautions."

"You brought enough precautions to bury a room full of people."

Anders lowered his voice. "I brought enough to ensure that the people outside followed instructions. Your daughter is the only person making that arrangement unstable."

"Funny. From here it looks unstable all by itself."

"And if I don't?"

"You will. Parents discover clarity when consequences stop being theoretical."

Danny studied him for a long moment. Taylor recognized the stillness. It was the same stillness he carried into union meetings when management offered terms insulting enough to remove uncertainty.

"You're the one doing this."

Anders smiled faintly. "Your daughter reached that conclusion much faster."

"She's smarter than me."

"That intelligence is why she is in danger. Help me keep it from becoming the reason other people die."

Danny looked at the other four people beside the column. The defense attorney's wrists had been tied with white plastic. Thorne had blood on one trouser knee. The injured man's wife still watched the clinic door as if looking away would change whether her husband came through it.

"You picked these people."

"My associates did."

"Your people. Your bombs. Your building. But when something goes wrong, that's a fifteen-year-old girl's fault."

Anders's expression cooled.

"She is old enough to understand consequences."

"Then you should be embarrassed she understands them better than you."

Danny looked up toward the atrium cameras.

"You want me to call her? Fine."

His voice rose.

"Taylor, if you can hear me, stay hidden. Don't trust Max Anders. He's running this."

The nearest gunman struck him across the stomach with the stock of his weapon.

Danny folded but did not fall. The injured man's wife caught one arm. The defense attorney caught the other.

The contact drew the five into a tighter cluster. Taylor saw it through insects on their clothes and understood what Danny had done. He had taken the blow, but he had also put every hostage within reach of the concealed door and forced the guards to look at him.

Her father had built her an opening without knowing her plan.

Anders's smile vanished.

"Detonate the west charge if she does not surrender in sixty seconds," he told the gunman.

Miss Militia's voice came through Taylor's earpiece, quiet and controlled. "Proof received."

Taylor released the radio button.

Anders looked up toward the ventilation grille.

"There," he said.

The gunman raised his weapon.

Taylor twisted the stun charge, pressed its center, and dropped it through the grille.

Three seconds was long enough to crawl backward twice.

The device struck the carpet on its edge, bounced once, and rolled beneath the gunman's heel.

One.

Taylor backed over her elbows.

Two.

Anders looked down.

Three.

White light filled the duct. The sound struck like a hand across her whole body. Taylor lost her grip and slid headfirst into the turn behind her. For several seconds she could not hear McClane, the radio, or herself.

Pressure punched through her open mouth and closed it. Her chin struck the metal. The duct walls flashed white, then black, then returned in doubled outlines. She tasted blood where her teeth caught her tongue.

The right side of her body forgot which direction was down.

Her bugs kept reporting.

Both gunmen were down. Anders had turned away before detonation but remained on one knee, blinded and disoriented. The five hostages lay against the column in a tangle of limbs.

Taylor kicked the grille out and dropped.

Her knees failed on landing. The injured ankle collapsed completely, throwing her onto one hip. She crawled to Danny, grabbed his jacket, and pulled.

"Taylor?"

"Move. Service door. Everybody move."

The defense attorney's wrists were still bound. Taylor pulled Ortega's trauma shears from the dress and cut the plastic while Danny rolled onto one knee. Thorne tried to stand before his balance returned and went shoulder-first into the column. The injured man's wife had lost one shoe and kept turning toward the clinic.

"My husband."

"He's alive," Taylor said. "Doctor's with him. We cannot reach that door from here."

"I'm not leaving him."

The nearest gunman groaned and reached for his weapon.

Danny kicked it beneath a banquet table. "You stay, they put you back on the bomb. Move now, come back with people who can help him."

She looked at him, then at Taylor.

Taylor pointed toward the opening panel.

The woman moved.

The defense attorney recovered first. He helped the injured man's wife stand. Thorne staggered in the wrong direction until Danny caught his collar and turned him around.

Anders reached for Taylor's ankle.

His fingers closed over the linen wrap and found the swelling beneath it. Pain went through her leg hard enough to blur the atrium.

She stepped on his hand.

Not hard enough to break anything. Hard enough to make him release her.

"You," he said, blinking through the stun.

"Me."

McClane returned as a high, distorted voice beneath the ringing in her ears.

Door, kid. Celebrate later.

Taylor could not stand without Danny's arm beneath hers. She hated needing it. She used it anyway.

Taylor pushed the extraction toward the concealed panel. Fenja saw the movement from across the atrium and began growing. The sound-board operator reached for the transmitter.

Taylor released the moths above the chandelier.

They fell as a single dark sheet between Fenja and the west support. Fenja flinched and raised one hand across her face. The distraction lasted less than a second. It was the second Ortega had told Taylor she needed to remove somewhere else.

Danny pushed Thorne through the panel. The defense attorney pulled the injured man's wife after him. The councilwoman froze at the threshold, staring at the solid wall where no door had been visible a moment earlier.

"Through," Taylor shouted.

The councilwoman went through.

The west charge's indicator changed from green to red.

The receiver's vibration stopped.

Taylor remembered Ortega's warning. A stopped relay might mean the circuit was armed and waiting. It might mean the radio command had already closed it. It might mean the light had changed colors because a man at a workbench had thought red looked threatening.

She did not pull the battery.

Taylor drove her swarm into the receiver.

Ants packed the switch housing and filled the narrow space beneath the toggle. Beetles forced themselves around the battery leads. Their bodies bridged exposed contacts. The first layer died as current passed through it. Taylor pushed the next layer over them, then another, building a wet, conductive mass where the clean separation of the circuit had been.

The indicator flickered. Red, dark, red again.

The smell reached her a second later, burned insulation mixed with something smaller and organic.

The operator pressed the trigger.

Nothing happened.

Everyone near the column stopped.

For one suspended beat, even Fenja looked at the charge.

He pressed again.

The receiver smoked.

The protective case popped at one seam. Taylor threw herself through the panel and dragged it closed, expecting the support to detonate behind her.

Nothing followed except the sound-board operator shouting for a manual team.

Taylor got Danny through the panel as Fenja crossed half the atrium in three strides. Her enlarged hand struck the wall after the door closed and cracked plaster across the corridor.

The impact blew the concealed panel inward. Danny caught it on his shoulder. Taylor and the defense attorney shoved from the other side while Fenja's fingers curled around its edge.

The panel was decorative wood over a steel service door. Fenja peeled the wood away in strips, found the metal beneath, and pulled hard enough to bow it toward the atrium.

"It opens toward us," Thorne said.

"Not anymore," Danny answered.

He seized the rolling rack Taylor had hidden behind earlier and drove it beneath the handle. The defense attorney locked its wheels. Fenja pulled again. The rack rose onto two casters, struck the ceiling, and wedged between wall and door.

"Run," Taylor said.

They ran.

The hidden stair brought them down toward the clinic. Behind them, Fenja tore the concealed door out of the wall. Ahead, Alabaster entered from the service hall.

He raised the security guard's weapon.

Taylor sent the last insects clinging to it into the trigger guard. Alabaster fired before he intended. The round struck the ceiling above Danny and showered the stair landing with tile.

The five hostages dropped. Taylor stayed upright only because Danny held her.

Taylor released the fire door between them. It struck Alabaster, knocked him backward, and shut. He began pounding against it immediately.

The latch had not caught. Alabaster's second impact opened it three inches.

Taylor pulled a red fire extinguisher from its bracket and shoved the cylinder through the crash-bar frame. The door closed against it at an angle. Alabaster forced one arm through the gap. The defense attorney kicked the extinguisher deeper until the metal handle trapped his wrist.

"That won't hold him," Thorne said.

"It doesn't have to," Taylor and Danny said together.

They looked at each other.

Okay, McClane said. That was a little weird.

"Basement," Taylor told the others. "Follow the bugs."

She laid a moving trail along the baseboard and sent the five down the hidden stair. Danny remained beside her.

"You first," he said.

"Dad."

"Not discussing it. Move."

Fenja's hand broke through the wall behind them.

Taylor tried to take the first stair alone. Her right leg folded.

Danny caught her around the waist and took her weight before she hit the steps.

"You first doesn't mean by yourself," he said.

Taylor went first because arguing would kill them both.

They descended together, Taylor hopping on her uninjured foot while Danny controlled the fall of each step. Behind them, plaster broke in slabs. Fenja's fingers entered the stairwell first, then her wrist, then an eye framed by the hole she was widening.

The defense attorney reached the basement landing and held the door. Thorne and the councilwoman pulled the injured man's wife through. Danny half carried Taylor across the threshold.

Fenja's hand closed around the upper rail.

Taylor sent beetles into the stairwell release. The basement fire door slammed. Fenja's fingers remained on the other side.

They reached the laundry room with Alabaster behind one fire door and Fenja widening the corridor above by force. Ortega and the MedHall guards pulled the five inside. The defense attorney took one look at the wounded trooper, the stolen rifle, and Taylor in her torn green dress, then decided questions could wait.

Danny did not.

"Bugs?"

Taylor looked at the trail still moving along the floor.

"Yeah."

Danny's gaze followed it to the map, then lifted to the insects stationed along the laundry ceiling. Taylor felt each one as he found it. Moths above the wounded. Flies at the door. Roaches disappearing beneath the baseboard. The room had been full of her for hours.

"All of them?"

"Most within a few blocks. I can feel through them. Not see exactly. It's complicated."

The building shook as Fenja struck the fire door above.

Danny absorbed the answer in the middle of the noise. His hand was still beneath Taylor's arm. He did not let go.

He closed his eyes once. Opened them. "Voice in your head too, or was Anders being colorful?"

I like him, McClane said.

"Yeah," Taylor admitted.

"John McClane," she added, because if she stopped after admitting the voice, she might never manage the rest. "He's a police officer. From somewhere else. He's been helping me."

Danny looked at her for several seconds.

"Does he control you?"

Good first question, McClane said.

"No. Never unless I let him, and he hasn't. He talks. I decide."

"Can he hear me?"

"Yes."

Danny looked somewhere just to the left of Taylor's face, as if politeness required giving an invisible man a place to stand.

"Then thank you," he said. "We can deal with everything else when my daughter isn't bleeding on a laundry-room floor."

McClane did not answer immediately.

When he did, his voice had lost every joke.

Tell him you're welcome.

"He says you're welcome."

"We survive first," Danny said. "You explain later."

It was more trust than she had expected to receive in a lifetime, delivered in eight words while something enormous tore apart the corridor above them.

On the radio, Armsmaster ordered the perimeter units to prepare for a forced breach. Miss Militia argued that the remaining five charges were still live. The sound-board operator announced that negotiations were over.

Taylor looked at the alarm diagram on Ortega's display.

Six evacuation zones. Magnetic fire doors. Sprinkler mains. Dedicated emergency signal to the fire department. A system designed to take control of MedHall away from every person inside it when smoke appeared.

He owns the doors, she thought.

Then make the building open them, McClane said.
