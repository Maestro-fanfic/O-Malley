Chapter 2 of 29

Now Boarding

There was no Annex Skywalk in the room he arrived in. No broken glass, no collapsed scaffolding, no Barnes bleeding behind a construction bin. No antenna array burning in the snow outside.

There was, unaccountably, a radar scope. Green light pulsing off it in a slow, patient sweep, tracking blips that didn't correspond to any airspace McClane had ever flown a commercial ticket through. A bank of consoles ran along one wall, humming the low electrical hum of every ops room he'd ever leaned in the doorway of and been told to get out of, and a coffee pot sat half-full on a burner that had clearly been on too long, because the smell of it had gone from coffee to something closer to punishment.

He didn't remember sitting down in the chair he found himself in. He was fairly sure he hadn't.

McClane didn't reach for anything. Didn't touch the door behind him, though he clocked it, the hinge side, the fact that its little illuminated EXIT sign was in English but spelled in a font no airport he'd ever worked would have paid a sign contractor to install. He counted exits before he counted his own pulse, always had, and the fact that his hands weren't shaking told him less about how calm he was than it told him this room hadn't earned a reaction out of him yet.

It felt wrong the way a forged boarding pass felt wrong. Not obviously. Not on the first pass. Everything present and accounted for, right down to the burnt coffee, and somehow still off, like a man who'd only ever heard Dulles Ops described over a radio and gotten every last detail half an inch out of true.

The wall clock ran, which was its own small wrongness once he noticed it, hands sweeping a steady, unhurried twelve hours that didn't match anything McClane's own body was telling him about how long he'd been unconscious. A corkboard hung crooked by the door, pinned with memos about runway deicing schedules and a holiday party nobody would ever attend, every word of it perfectly legible and perfectly beside the point, like a stage dressed by somebody who'd read the script once and skipped the parts that mattered.

He clocked exits again anyway. Old habit didn't care that the room made no sense.

Then he checked himself.

His shirt was still damp from the boiler room and streaked with dust from the scaffolding. His knuckles were still split. The old scar on his shoulder remained. The fresh ache from hitting the floor under several hundred pounds of construction equipment did not. Whoever had cleaned up the new damage had either been selective or had rules about what counted as part of him.

His ankle supported his weight. His lungs drew a full breath without complaint. Nothing felt broken, strained, or newly bruised.

McClane crossed to the door and opened it.

The same room waited on the other side.

Not a copy. The same room, viewed from the opposite wall. He could see his own hand holding the door open across twenty feet of impossible air. When he leaned through, the other version of him leaned in too, not a reflection but the back of his own head and shoulders occupying the place where the corridor should have been.

He shut the door.

"Okay," he told the burnt coffee. "Door's decorative. Good to know."

The radar swept again. Green returns moved around the outer edge, each slow and dim. One remained near the center, brighter than the rest. When McClane stepped closer, the central return pulsed in time with his heartbeat.

He reached through the glass. Cold closed around his fingers, followed by a sensation like radio static running beneath his skin. For an instant he heard a crowd, a string instrument, a glass touching a tray. Then it vanished.

Somebody was already on the other end.

He got up, testing the floor first with one boot before he trusted it with his full weight, the way you tested ice you didn't trust, and crossed to the radar scope on legs that felt steadier than a man who had just gone through a collapsing scaffold had any right to expect. The blips on the screen didn't behave like weather or traffic. They pulsed, slow and rhythmic, less like aircraft and more like something with a heartbeat, and when he reached out, half out of curiosity and half out of the old cop's need to touch a thing before he believed in it, his fingers passed through the glass like it wasn't there at all, cold air on the other side of a screen that had every visual right to stop him and didn't.

He pulled his hand back. Some doors you didn't need opened twice to know you didn't want to walk through them yet.

"You're doing the thing," a voice said, from somewhere behind his left shoulder. "Casing the joint before you've even said hello."

"'You're doing the thing,'" Fury repeated, flat. "I said that. Word for word. Does he not have his own opening line?"

"Maybe it's a company script," Gladly offered. "Laminated card, one per operative."

McClane didn't startle. He'd trained that particular reflex out of himself somewhere around his second year on the job, in rooms considerably less strange than this one. He turned, unhurried, and found a man in a windbreaker with DULLES OPS stenciled across the chest, standing at the radar scope with a coffee cup that had definitely not been in his hand a second ago, reading a strip of paper off the console with the unbothered attention of a guy who'd never once in his career had to explain a missing airplane to anybody who outranked him.

"Trudeau."

"That's the name on the jacket." The man didn't look up from the strip. "You gonna ask me how I know yours, or you want to skip to the part where you tell me it doesn't fit?"

"The face fits fine." McClane kept his voice level, cataloging again, not the room this time but the man: the half-second delays in how he moved, a stillness underneath the windbreaker that didn't track with a guy whose whole job was managing chaos on a headset. The real Trudeau, from the two minutes McClane had spent trading orders with him over a radio tonight, ran hot, snapped at subordinates, chewed through cigarettes like they owed him money. This one hadn't touched the pack sitting open on the console once. "The guy wearing it's the part that doesn't."

Something that might have been approval crossed the borrowed face. "There it is." He set the coffee down, made a small unhurried mark on the strip without glancing down to find the line for it, and McClane caught it anyway, upside down, before the man folded it away: SITUATIONAL AWARENESS, checked off like a controller confirming separation on two aircraft that had, against expectation, missed each other. "Most people spend the first five minutes arguing with the furniture. You went straight for the actor. I respect that."

"What happened to me."

"You dropped just after an antenna array exploded, which is a hell of a place to have a scheduling conflict." He said it like the punchline of a joke he'd already told himself once and liked. "Real Trudeau's still up in that tower right now, fighting his own war with a bunch of very serious men in very serious jackets, and he's not gonna notice you're gone, because from where he's standing, you never left. That's not a comfort. I just find people ask less follow-up if I get it out of the way early."

Something in McClane's chest did a slow, cold turn. "Barnes."

"Still behind the bin. Still bleeding. And from his point of view, you haven't gone anywhere." A shrug, easy, like spacetime was an administrative detail. "When you go back, you go back to the instant you left."

"And you want to talk paperwork while he's waiting."

"He isn't waiting. That's the part your gut isn't going to like, but the clock back there has not advanced." No apology in it, just a flat, patient correction, the kind a man gave when he'd had this exact conversation with other people before and expected to have it again. "You are not costing him a second."

"What paperwork."

"Wrong question, but I'll allow it, you're new." Trudeau, or the thing wearing him, pulled the strip back out and held it up, and McClane, despite every instinct telling him not to give a stranger the satisfaction, looked.

INCIDENT REPORT. SUBJECT: ONE (1) JOHN MCCLANE. LAST KNOWN POSITION: ANNEX SKYWALK, ARMED AND UNCOOPERATIVE. STATUS: EXTRACTED. DESTINATION: PASSENGER SEAT, SEE ATTACHED. PRIORITY: URGENT, RETROACTIVELY. CLEARANCE: APPROVED BY:

The line wasn't blank. A signature sat in the approval box, cramped and impatient, the kind of scrawl a man used when he'd already decided the form was a waste of everybody's time before he'd finished signing it.

McClane knew it the way he knew his own reflection, immediately, without having to check twice.

It was his.

He had never seen this form in his life.

"You want to explain that," he said, and was quietly satisfied to hear his own voice hadn't moved an inch, "or should I just take it up with the guy who apparently already signed off on it?"

"Keeps me honest, having you countersign yourself. Cuts down on complaints later." He tapped the box without looking at it, entirely unbothered by the question underneath the question. "Names, if you want a few. Rob O'Make O'Malley, if you want the one that's actually mine. Facilitator. Occasional air traffic controller for narratives that can't find their own runway. On my better nights, I like to think of it as search and rescue." He said the last part like a line he'd used enough times to still enjoy it, unbothered by how little of it would land with the man across from him. "Call me whatever's comfortable. I answer to most things, long as the voice sells it."

"Passenger seat," McClane said. "Your form. What does it mean?"

O'Malley picked up the coffee, considered it, and put it down again without drinking. "It means you go where she goes. You see what she sees, hear what she hears, and talk to her without anybody else hearing unless she speaks your words aloud. You bring your memory, judgment, instincts, and every bad habit you've spent a lifetime making look useful."

"And control?"

"Hers. Always."

"You put me in a fifteen-year-old's head and I can't move a hand if somebody comes at her."

"Not unless she lends it to you. If she does, she can take it back at any moment. No contest, no tug-of-war, no clever exception hidden in the fine print. Her body remains hers."

McClane looked toward the radar return at the center of the scope. It had begun moving between two of the dimmer lights.

"Can she get me out?"

"No. Neither can you."

"Can you?"

"Eventually. That answer is less useful than you want it to be."

"Can she shut me up?"

"You can both withdraw. Think around each other instead of at each other. Sleep helps. Distance does not, because there won't be any."

"Privacy."

"Possible. Learned. Imperfect at first." O'Malley met his eyes. "You will know more about each other than either of you would choose to hand a stranger. That is part of the cost, and I am telling you before you agree because consent offered without the ugly parts is advertising, not consent."

McClane gave him a long look. "You practice that line?"

"The ugly parts taught it to me."

For the first time, O'Malley's borrowed face looked older than Trudeau's actual face had any right to look.

"What happens here?" McClane asked. "Dulles. Holly. The planes."

"You return to the instant you left. Your body finishes the fight. Your wife lands. The rest of your life remains yours, including the parts you keep making harder than they need to be."

"And I remember her."

"Yes."

"She remembers me."

"Yes."

"Then 'return' isn't really the word, is it?"

"No," O'Malley said. "It usually isn't."

"The voice sells it fine. The guy behind it doesn't." McClane studied him, not the uniform this time, the man underneath it, the beat of stillness where a control tower chief should've been reaching for a radio, a phone, anything with a wire attached to a crisis. "Trudeau doesn't stand still. Not tonight. Not with aircraft stacked up over the storm and his tower held hostage. You've been parked in that same spot since I looked up."

"No," O'Malley agreed, and for a second the easy amusement thinned enough to show something underneath it that wasn't unkind exactly, but wasn't comfortable either. "I haven't got anywhere to be. That's the whole difference between us, if you want it in one sentence."

"What do you want."

"Wrong question again. Getting better, though."

"There's the 'wrong question' bit again," Fury said, dry. "He does this to everybody. It's practically a hazing ritual."

"I got graded on a curve too," Gladly said, wistful. "Took me three tries to land on the right one."

O'Malley set the coffee cup down for good and came around the console, and the borrowed face, for the first time since McClane had turned to look at it, went entirely serious. "There's a girl. Not a soldier, not a suspect, not anybody your line of work has ever had a file open on, or ever will, not through any door you'd recognize. She's about to find out the hard way that the people running things in her city aren't who they say they are, and she's got the tools to do something about it and not one day of the training that usually comes bundled with tools like that."

"How old."

"Fifteen." O'Malley let that sit a second before he went on, no theatrics in it, just weight. "Old enough to know when a room's lying to her. Young enough that nobody with any actual power in that city's bothered learning her name yet. That's about to change tonight, whether either of you wants it to or not."

"And if I say no. If I sit right here in this chair and tell you to find somebody else."

"Then I find somebody else, and you go back to Dulles with Stuart still holding the sky over your wife's head, and somewhere else, tonight, a girl finds out the hard way what it costs to be right and alone at the same time." No cruelty in the delivery, just an accounting, laid out flat. "I'm not gonna threaten you into a yes. Never have, never will. I just don't think you're the kind of man who hears the rest of that sentence and still says no."

McClane walked to the radar scope. The bright return moved near the center while the dim lights continued circling far beyond it. He put his palm through the glass this time.

The room on the other side came in pieces. White light. Music. A man's laugh polished smooth by repetition. Beneath all of it, a girl's attention moving across exits, hands, doors, and distances with the anxious precision of someone who expected rooms to turn against her because enough rooms already had.

Fifteen, O'Malley had said.

McClane thought about Lucy at fifteen. Thought about how much of her life he had missed while telling himself work was the reason and not the shelter. Thought about Holly circling above Dulles while strangers decided what her fuel gauge was worth. He had spent the whole night furious that nobody in authority would believe him. The idea of leaving a kid inside that sentence by herself landed harder than the pitch deserved.

He pulled his hand back.

"Does she know I'm coming?"

"Not yet."

"That's lousy procedure."

"You can open with an apology."

"I'm not great at those."

"One of several reasons I chose you."

McClane said nothing. It was, though he had no way of knowing it yet, the first professional silence of an entirely new career.

He'd have said no, if there'd been a version of this where saying no meant anything. Thirty years on the job had taught him the shape of a pitch, and this was a pitch, dressed up in borrowed gravitas and a coffee cup he was pretty sure hadn't existed twenty minutes ago. It was also, he had to admit, working. Not because O'Malley had made it sound easy. Because he hadn't.

"You've spent thirty years being the only guy in the room who noticed the thing everybody with more rank than you decided wasn't worth noticing," O'Malley said. "Right about the part that mattered, wrong about every channel you were supposed to go through to say so. Nobody ever thanks you for it in the moment. I'd know. I've been wearing faces that outrank you for longer than you've been alive, and every single one of them eventually had to admit you'd clocked it first." A beat, and something almost gentle crept into the words despite how dry the delivery stayed. "She's going to need somebody who reads a room that way. Not somebody who tells her what to do with what she sees. Somebody who just, finally, believes her when she says something's wrong."

He folded the strip closed on that line and stamped something across it in the same unhurried motion, a single word McClane caught only as a smear of red ink, upside down, gone before he could be sure he'd read it right.

CLEARED.

The sentence wasn't finished landing before the room was gone.

"Wait," Gladly said, sounding personally hurt. "That's the exact same last line Xander's got."

"He's got a type," Fury said. "Efficient. I respect it. Doesn't mean I love watching him reuse my own material twice in one chapter."
