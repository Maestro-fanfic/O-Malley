Final Approach

The fire system gave them six zones, five live charges, three usable exits, and one chance to make all of those numbers change at once.

The sixth structural charge lay blackened beneath the west support. Its receiver had fused around a mass of dead insects. The separate device at the roof access had already spent itself on the failed PRT entry. Five green indicators remained inside the building, and Kaiser still held the transmitter that could turn any one of them red.

Taylor spread the plans across the laundry-room floor. Danny knelt beside her. Ortega sat against a washer with his injured leg extended. The two MedHall guards watched the corridor. Around them, the people Taylor had pulled from the west support tried to become useful because the alternative was listening to the building come apart overhead.

The laundry machines transmitted each impact through their steel sides. Fenja struck the basement fire door above them. Alabaster hammered from the clinic passage. Between blows, the sound-board operator repeated his threat over every stolen radio: negotiations had ended, the inside source would be surrendered, no one would approach MedHall.

No one in the laundry suggested surrendering Taylor.

The defense attorney knew the fire code. The injured man's wife was a nurse. Marcus Thorne had stopped taking notes.

The councilwoman knew the original occupancy plan and which guests had been assigned to the mezzanine. The catering supervisor knew which kitchen doors stuck when the floor got wet. Danny knew how frightened people behaved at a union hall when one visible route became a crush point.

Taylor knew where every person inside MedHall was standing.

"Alarm releases every magnetic door," one guard said. "Main exits too."

"They pinned the east doors manually," Taylor said. "Main doors have Menja. Loading route has two armed teams."

"Sprinklers reduce visibility," Ortega said. "Works both directions."

Not both directions, McClane corrected. You've got a few thousand eyes that don't care about water.

Taylor marked the atrium, mezzanine, kitchen, clinic, basement, and parking bridge.

"The east group needs someone they recognize at the front," Danny said. "Otherwise the first gunshot turns them around."

The councilwoman touched the bruise forming along her cheek. "They know me."

"You just got off a bomb."

"So did you."

Danny considered that and nodded.

The defense attorney took the service route. He had argued with the gunmen twice already, which meant the catering workers and half the atrium had watched him refuse to be quiet. Thorne volunteered for the parking bridge because the guests upstairs knew his face and because, as he put it, someone needed to tell the cameras what the Protectorate had actually done if they survived.

"You aren't carrying the rifle," Taylor said.

"I had gathered that your trust remains conditional."

"You traded my description for an interview."

Thorne looked down at his empty notebook. "Conditional is better than I earned."

"When the alarm starts, the hostages will push toward the main entrance," she said. "Hookwolf can stop that by standing there. We need them divided before they move."

"How do you divide a hundred and sixty frightened people without talking to them?" Thorne asked.

Danny looked at the insects moving along the edge of the map. "Same way she got us here."

Taylor built three trails.

Building them was the easy part. Making strangers understand them was harder.

She ran a test along the laundry baseboard. Roaches formed a line, moved ten feet, then split around a drain. The defense attorney followed the wrong branch. Taylor stopped the insects, made them cluster into an arrowhead, and tried again.

"Better," he said. "Still looks like a nightmare."

"A nightmare pointing toward an exit," Danny said.

Taylor added pulses. Move, stop, move. At every doorway the insects would gather until a route leader crossed, then advance. Anyone who lost the trail could wait for the next wave.

The first gathered beneath the atrium tables and waited pointed toward the east doors. The second climbed the staircase and formed along the parking-bridge corridor. The third occupied the service aisle leading toward the kitchen and loading dock. Not enough insects to resemble arrows yet. Enough to become them when she gave the command.

Miss Militia listened while Taylor described the routes.

Outside units received names rather than directions. East became Engine. Parking bridge became Shield. Loading dock became Ramp. The basement group was Laundry, spoken without irony by everyone except McClane.

I've worked operations with worse names, he said. Not many, but worse.

"Parking bridge team will breach from outside on your signal," she said. "Fire crews will take the east approach. Armsmaster can direct a suppression pulse at the receiver frequency, but only after the alarm masks the power draw. We cannot guarantee the charges will fail."

"One already did," Taylor said.

"Because you destroyed its receiver," Miss Militia said. "Four of the five remaining locations are outside your safe reach."

The fifth sat behind the basement mechanical-room wall, six feet from the laundry where they were planning. The official blueprints called the space a valve recess. Kaiser's altered corridor had turned it into a protected demolition point.

Ortega looked at Taylor. "If you can open the service panel, I can talk you through that one. Then suppression only has four chances to go wrong."

Better number, McClane said. Still not a good number.

The older MedHall guard found the panel beneath three coats of paint. The defense attorney and Danny pulled on its recessed handle together. It opened two inches and stopped against a steel conduit added after the plans were filed.

Taylor lay on the floor and reached through the gap with her swarm.

Ortega could not see the receiver. Taylor described it one component at a time. He asked where the antenna entered the case, whether the battery leads shared a harness, whether the red wire reached the explosive block or only the indicator board.

"There are two red wires."

"Of course there are. Follow both."

One ended at the light. The other disappeared beneath the firing relay.

"Do not disturb that one. Is the antenna screwed to the board or soldered?"

Taylor moved beetles over the connection. "Screwed."

"Back the retaining nut off. Slow. If the light changes, stop."

Twenty ants braced against the small metal nut. More joined them. The nut moved less than the width of an insect leg, then again. Taylor felt each thread release through the bodies pushing it.

The green light remained steady.

The antenna separated from the board.

"Now bridge the test contact before you disconnect the battery," Ortega said. "We want the receiver to believe it still has continuity."

Taylor obeyed. The light dimmed but did not change color. The MedHall guard reached through with insulated forceps and removed the battery plug.

Green went dark.

No explosion followed.

Four remained.

The route leaders still had to reach their routes.

The defense attorney could begin from the service corridor with Taylor's group. Thorne's path was narrower: up the unlisted clinic stair, through a maintenance crawlspace, and into a locked closet beside the mezzanine lounge. Taylor packed beetles into the latch until it turned. Thorne climbed alone, stopping every time a guard crossed the hall beyond him.

The councilwoman's route required something worse.

"You can't reach the east side without crossing the atrium," Taylor said.

"Then I cross the atrium."

"They'll see you."

"They already want me where the cameras can see me."

She removed the blanket from her shoulders, straightened her wet jacket, and walked out through the clinic passage with both hands raised. Taylor tracked her through insects along the ceiling.

The first gunman shoved her against the wall and demanded where the others had gone.

"Your bomb went off in my face," she said. "I woke up alone."

He searched her, found no radio or weapon, and returned her to the atrium because Max Anders gave one small nod from the hostage rows. The councilwoman sat where they put her. She did not look toward any insect. She did not look toward the east doors.

Thorne reached the mezzanine closet and waited in darkness.

All three route leaders were in place.

Better number, McClane said. Still not a good number.

"If the pulse triggers them?" Danny asked.

Miss Militia did not soften the answer. "We lose structural sections of the building."

"And if we do nothing?"

"Kaiser retains the triggers."

"He also has a hostage on the roof," Ortega said.

Roof One remained bound beside the skylight with Cricket between him and every exterior approach. Through insects sheltered in the gravel, Taylor felt him working one wrist against the plastic restraint. Each movement stopped when Cricket turned her head.

Miss Militia answered. "Shield team takes the bridge, then the north stair. We recover him during the evacuation."

"Cricket knows you're coming."

"Yes."

There was no promise that knowing would be enough.

Hearing the name spoken outside Taylor's head changed the room. Max Anders had become Kaiser over an open government channel. Danny absorbed it with one slow breath.

"Then we don't leave him the triggers," he said.

Taylor reached for the detector outside the linen room.

Her insects entered six alarm housings at once. She packed bodies into sensing chambers, bridged test contacts, and held them short of activation.

"Ready," she told Miss Militia.

The word passed outward through three command nets. Firefighters lowered face shields. PRT troopers checked the person ahead of them and fixed gloved hands to the backs of shields. Ambulance crews opened every rear door and laid triage tags across the hoods. Police moved the press line another fifty yards from the glass face of MedHall.

Inside, Taylor moved insects into place on collars, sleeves, table legs, and door frames. She counted the hostages one last time.

One hundred and fifty-seven in the atrium now, including the returned councilwoman. Twenty-two waited on the mezzanine after Thorne joined them. Fourteen catering workers remained in the kitchen. The injured donor and anesthesiologist were locked in the clinic. Eight wounded and hidden personnel occupied the laundry with Ortega. Four people, including Taylor and Danny, were about to leave it.

The totals matched.

"Parking team ready. Fire crews ready. Electronic suppression ready."

Armsmaster entered the channel. "On your initiation, Tower."

No argument now. No dismissal. Only a command structure acknowledging where the useful information lived.

Taylor looked at Danny.

He put one hand over hers. "Bring the roof down if you have to. Just make sure we're not under it."

"That's not comforting."

"Wasn't meant to be."

He tore a clean strip from a tablecloth and wrapped it around Taylor's bleeding left foot. There was no time to improve the splint on her right ankle. He tied the cloth, checked that she could feel her toes, and put her hand on his shoulder when she stood.

"I can move."

"I know. You're still leaning on me until we split."

McClane laughed once inside her head.

Taylor closed the alarm contacts.

MedHall screamed.

The first horn caught everyone unprepared despite the warning. Danny flinched. Ortega grabbed for a weapon he no longer carried. The injured man's wife closed her eyes and whispered her husband's name once.

Then the laundry group moved.

Horns struck every floor. Magnetic locks released. Fire doors swung. Emergency lights strobed while the central panel notified a fire department already waiting at the curb.

The signal also killed the elevators, opened smoke vents, released kitchen gas valves, and unlocked a dozen ordinary doors Kaiser's crew had trusted the hostages not to test. Across Taylor's swarm, MedHall changed shape. Barriers vanished. New barriers dropped. Air began moving through corridors that had been still all night.

The sprinklers followed when Taylor opened the deluge test valve through a mass of beetles packed around its lever. Water hammered the atrium, flattened table linens, and turned polished stone into a mirror broken by running feet.

The hostages rose.

Not together. The people closest to the doors stood first. Those in the center saw movement and followed without knowing why. Someone shouted fire. Someone else shouted bomb. A man near the stage tried to lift his wife before she had her feet beneath her and pulled both of them down.

Anders's gunmen raised their weapons over a crowd that no longer offered clean lines of fire.

"Stay down!" the sound-board operator shouted.

The insects became arrows.

They poured from beneath tables in black streams, gathered at three aisle mouths, and advanced in pulses. Screams broke out near the first trail. Then the councilwoman stepped into the east aisle and followed it.

"East doors! Stay with me! Do not run!"

Her authority did what the insects could not. People recognized her voice. The first rows turned.

The defense attorney climbed onto a fallen chair beside the service aisle. "Kitchen and loading dock! If you can walk, this route! Leave the main doors clear!"

Thorne came out of the mezzanine closet with one hand on the wall because the stun charge still owned part of his balance. "Bridge route! This floor, toward the parking lights!"

Three human voices converted Taylor's arrows into choices.

One third of the crowd followed the east trail. Another surged toward the kitchen. Guests on the mezzanine ran for the parking bridge as the outside team broke its doors inward.

The split was not even. Too many people chose the loading route because it looked farthest from Fenja. A knot formed at the service door. The catering supervisor, still in the laundry, called instructions through Ortega's radio. Danny repeated them into the corridor.

"Both kitchen doors! Use the pantry too!"

Taylor diverted half the insect trail beneath the prep counters and through the second door. A cook saw it, understood, and began pulling people around the bottleneck.

Kaiser's people tried to stop three evacuations at once.

Fenja grew across the east doors. Firefighters on the other side drove a hydraulic spreader between the frames while guests pressed back from her widening feet.

The doors bowed inward around the tool. Fenja planted both hands against the lintel and grew until her shoulders struck the atrium ceiling. Plaster broke around her. The councilwoman stopped ten feet away and threw both arms wide to keep the group from compacting behind her.

"Back three steps! Give them room!"

The first ranks obeyed. The people behind them did not hear. Pressure moved forward through the crowd.

Taylor reversed the nearest insect arrow. Its point turned sideways and crawled toward a secondary conference-room door. The councilwoman saw the change.

"Split left! Left side with the bugs!"

Half the group peeled away before the crush reached the doors.

Menja moved toward the main entrance and left the kitchen route open for four seconds.

Four seconds emptied thirty people into the service corridor.

Then Menja understood the feint and turned. Her enlarged hand swept the aisle at shoulder height.

People dropped beneath it. The defense attorney did not. Danny tackled him behind a banquet table as Menja's fingers took the chair off its feet and sent it skidding across the wet floor.

Taylor sent every beetle in the nearby table linens into Menja's shoes. They could not hurt her. They made the polished floor move beneath her like gravel. Her next step slid six inches.

Six inches gave twelve more people the service door.

Cricket met the parking team in ankle-deep sprinkler water. Miss Militia came through the breached doors behind a shield line, saw Cricket begin moving before the first weapon cleared, and ordered the team to stop advancing.

The order ran backward through the formation. Boots planted. Shields locked. The trooper at the rear nearly drove the line forward before the person ahead absorbed the impact.

Cricket had already shifted left to avoid the attack their momentum predicted.

Nothing entered the space she abandoned.

For the first time, Taylor felt hesitation in the movement of the cape who had spent the night arriving before everyone else.

Cricket adjusted for an attack that did not arrive.

Miss Militia fired into the ceiling above her. Tile and insulation collapsed across the whole corridor, not aimed at Cricket, not readable as a strike on her body. She flinched from the same field of noise Taylor had used.

The shield line did not charge. The lead trooper dropped flat. The second went high over him. A foam projector behind them fired at the floor, the wall, and the ceiling in three separate bursts. None targeted Cricket. Expanding containment foam met in the space around her and removed every route at once.

Cricket jumped for the railing.

Miss Militia's weapon became a hooked launcher between one heartbeat and the next. The line caught the rail ahead of Cricket, not her body, and tore the whole section sideways. Cricket landed where the rail had been and went through the gap to the lower landing.

She hit hard, rolled, and still began moving before the nearest trooper aimed.

The team stopped again.

Taylor filled the landing with insects flying in different directions. Cricket read a thousand false beginnings. Miss Militia advanced through the center of them with her shield raised and drove Cricket away from the bridge doors.

The PRT line moved through it.

Behind them, two troopers broke toward the north stairs and the captive on the roof.

I definitely like her, McClane said.

Hookwolf entered the atrium at full size.

Metal consumed the shape of the man beneath it. Blades rolled over one another in a body taller than the stage, broad enough to close the main aisle. He tore through tables and drove the hostages away from the exits by existing in front of them.

The crowd recoiled as one body. People who had been following three routes forgot all of them and moved away from the moving metal. The east group collided with the service group. Thorne's bridge line stopped at the corridor mouth. The insect arrows vanished beneath trampling shoes.

Hookwolf struck the nearest table with one forelimb. It split down the center. Plates, silverware, and broken crystal joined the sprinkler water.

"Back on the routes!" Danny shouted.

No one heard him over the alarms and Hookwolf.

Taylor made the insects climb.

Instead of trails along the floor, they rose up tablecloths, walls, and people's wet clothes. Three columns formed above the crowd, each leaning toward an exit. Heads lifted to follow them. The councilwoman saw hers first and began moving again.

Hookwolf saw the insects too.

His bladed head turned toward the service corridor.

Toward Taylor.

Taylor felt the chandelier above him.

All night she had counted its weight, cables, and position without knowing why.

Now she found the maintenance winch above the third-floor ceiling. A safety pawl held the drum. The electrical release had been disconnected from the building controls during the lockdown, but the manual mechanism remained. Insects entered the housing. They packed beneath the pawl, pushed where one body could not matter and hundreds could.

The winch had not been serviced in years. Grease trapped the first ants before they reached the pawl. Roaches wedged between gear teeth and were crushed when the drum shifted under the chandelier's weight. Taylor replaced every lost insect without looking away from Hookwolf.

He began moving toward her.

Danny saw him turn. "Taylor."

"I know."

Now that's a distraction, McClane said.

His forward blades dug furrows through the marble. Momentum carried the rest of him on.

Not onto the hostages. Not onto Hookwolf. Between him and the crowd, exactly where three tons of crystal and steel could become a wall instead of a weapon.

Hookwolf did not stay buried. Blades punched through the fallen lattice before the last crystal stopped moving. He tore at cables wrapped around his body, but three tons of frame had divided him from the hostages and forced him to spend time becoming dangerous again.

The fire door had not held him. One shoulder remained crushed from forcing it, then reset while Taylor watched, bone and fabric returning to the state they had occupied seconds before.

He resets, she thought.

Then stop trying to damage him. Put him somewhere returning to normal doesn't help.

He stepped forward.

The fourth triggered.

Miss Militia's team had cleared the bridge corridor seconds earlier. The hostages beneath it had followed Taylor's trail toward the east doors. The collapse found empty floor.

Mostly empty.

There was no reason left to perform.

People who had spent the night accepting comfort from Max Anders saw the blades emerge from his skin.

The hostage camera saw them too.

"Go," Taylor said.

You don't fight him, McClane said.

I know.

You make him fight the building.

The first door released behind Kaiser instead of ahead of him. It struck his back and forced him into the corridor. The second divided him from two gunmen trying to follow. The third closed between Taylor and the loading ramp.

She had trapped herself on his side.

That one part of the plan? McClane asked.

No.

Good. Hate it when the bad part was on purpose.

He reached the rifle.

Kaiser kicked it behind him without touching it.

He had seen the bait.

The suppression cloud killed visibility for human eyes. Taylor tracked him through flies on the ceiling, roaches beneath the wall trim, and a beetle carried on the cuff of his ruined jacket. He moved more slowly inside the white, one blade held ahead to find obstacles.

She reached the clinic junction and fell behind an overturned instrument cart.

Kaiser heard the impact.

He changed direction.

"You have no idea what you've interrupted," he said.

"You keep saying that like it makes the bombs less yours."

"This city is held together by arrangements you cannot begin to understand. Tonight would have removed a threat to those arrangements without harming anyone who mattered."

Taylor thought of the server under the railing. Ortega hanging in the stairwell. The donor bleeding in the clinic. The dishwasher pointing a gunman away from her. One hundred and ninety people chosen as scenery for Max Anders's survival story.

"That's your problem," she said. "You keep deciding who matters."

"A charity gala?"

The answer carried McClane's cadence and Taylor's anger.

Kaiser lunged.

Taylor dropped beneath the first blade. It cut the wall where her neck had been. She threw the stolen rifle past him instead of trying to fire it. His eyes followed the weapon for one fraction of a second.

The second blade came low. Taylor rolled beneath the instrument cart. Steel cut through its upper tray and sent sealed syringes across the floor. Kaiser kicked the cart aside.

Taylor tried to stand.

Her ankle refused.

Kaiser raised one blade over her.

A cloud of insects struck his face from behind. He had expected them all night. His steel mask formed before they arrived.

That was not the attack.

The beetle on his cuff crawled beneath one overlapping plate and forced him to glance down. Taylor seized the cart's severed frame and drove it between his ankles. It did not knock him over. It made him take one step.

One step put him in the cylinder's path.

Her swarm found the rolling oxygen cylinder beside the clinic door.

She released its brake.

Danny, twenty feet down the ramp, saw it move and kicked it with both feet.

He had come back.

The cylinder rolled into Kaiser's knees. He cut it open by reflex.

Compressed gas erupted. The cylinder became a missile inside the narrow corridor, spinning, striking walls, wrapping its regulator hose around Kaiser's legs. He went down beneath a snarl of steel and white vapor.

The torn cylinder struck the ceiling, rebounded, and hit the floor behind him. Its valve assembly shattered against the baseboard. The remaining pressure drove it down the corridor through the door Kaiser had cut apart.

Steel blades grew from the floor around Taylor.

Kaiser had fallen, not lost control. Narrow fins rose through tile and caged the space around her. One caught the torn hem of her dress. Another formed beside her hand.

Danny reached the top of the ramp.

"Don't," Taylor shouted.

Kaiser pulled the regulator hose from his legs and began to rise.

Taylor sent insects into the sprinkler water pooled around the steel growths. Not to short anything. To find the shallowest path between them. She turned sideways, tore the dress free, and crawled through while the gap remained wide enough.

A blade closed behind her and cut a line across her back.

Danny caught both her wrists and dragged her clear.

Taylor ran.

They reached the loading dock as firefighters forced the outer shutter upward. Cold rain blew through the widening gap. One by one, the civilians crawled beneath it into floodlights, ambulances, and armed PRT lines.

The shutter rose only three feet before its motor failed. Firefighters held it with cribbing while the defense attorney crawled through first and pulled from outside. The injured man's wife followed, then stopped beneath the edge when she saw the ambulance crews.

"My husband is in the clinic! Gray suit, head wound, anesthesiologist with him!"

A paramedic repeated the description into a radio and sent a team toward the east breach.

Across the parking lane, Thorne emerged behind the bridge shield team with his empty hands raised for the cameras. The councilwoman came out through the east breach fifty yards away with someone else's blood down one sleeve. Above them, PRT troopers brought the rescued roof officer down an aerial ladder.

Three routes delivered three fragments of the same hostage population into a perimeter that had prepared for one door.

Ambulance crews adapted. Colored lights moved. Triage tarps opened. Names began replacing Taylor's numbers.

Danny went through, then turned and held out his hand.

Taylor took it.

Her injured leg would not clear the shutter. Danny lay flat in the rain, caught her beneath both arms, and pulled while a firefighter lifted the metal another inch. The torn fabric at Taylor's back snagged on the threshold and released.

She landed against Danny on wet pavement.

Behind her, Kaiser cut himself free.

He reached the loading door with one sleeve gone, steel masking half his face, and the hose still tangled around one leg. Floodlights erased every shadow he might have used. Police cameras, PRT cameras, news cameras beyond the line, and dozens of witnesses watched Max Anders walk out wearing Kaiser.

Miss Militia entered the corridor from the atrium with her weapon trained on him. Armsmaster came from the clinic side. Kaiser stopped between them, blades extended, every route finally owned by somebody else.

"Max Anders," Miss Militia said, loud enough for the loading dock and every open channel. "Retract the blades. Kneel. Put both hands on the floor."

Kaiser looked through the shutter at Taylor.

Steel began growing from the dock frame.

Armsmaster drove the end of his halberd into the wall. A blue pulse crossed the metal Kaiser had formed. The growth fractured at its base before it could close the opening.

Miss Militia's weapon changed into a compact launcher.

"Last instruction."

For a second Taylor believed Kaiser would force them to shoot him in front of the cameras, making one final attempt to choose the story.

Then he looked at the ruined atrium behind Miss Militia, the clinic corridor behind Armsmaster, and the civilians on the pavement beyond Taylor.

The blades withdrew one inch.

Then another.

He went to his knees.

For one second Taylor saw him as McClane had seen him at the beginning: not a gracious host, not an untouchable civic leader, only a man checking exits because he knew what he was.

There were no exits left.

Outside, rain struck Taylor's face. Danny pulled her against him while MedHall burned light and water through its broken glass behind them.

The moment she stopped moving, everything hurt separately. Her ankle. Her cut foot. Her wrist where Alabaster had held it. The line across her back. Her tongue. The deep bruising left by the stun charge. Adrenaline had held them together as one instruction. Now they became a list.

Danny felt her knees give and lowered her to the pavement instead of letting her fall.

"Medic!"

Taylor caught his sleeve. "Count first."

"Taylor."

"Please."

He's safe, McClane said.

Taylor watched the remaining hostages emerge through three separate routes, counted until every group matched the numbers on her map, and let herself believe it.

The anesthesiologist appeared at the east doors between two firefighters, one hand holding the dressing against her patient's head. His wife broke from the triage line and reached him before anyone could stop her.

The catering workers came through the loading dock in a group of fourteen. The kitchen dishwasher saw Taylor on the pavement and lifted one hand. She returned the gesture.

Ortega emerged last from the basement route on a rigid litter, furious about the straps and awake enough to give Taylor a thumbs-up.

One hundred and fifty-seven. Twenty-two. Fourteen. Clinic two. Laundry eight. Her extracted group. Roof team two.

No missing pockets remained inside her swarm.

"Yeah," she whispered. "He is."
