Chapter 1 of 29

The Gate Closes

The tower had lost its voice.

Every radio and landing aid the aircraft over Dulles depended on had gone down together, and the men who had taken them were now speaking through the one frequency they had left behind. Colonel Stuart had made the terms plain. Nobody landed. Nobody left. Nobody tried to repair anything. General Esperanza came in safely, or the people circling above the storm paid for it.

Barnes had one possible answer. The new terminal annex was not finished, but its antenna array was. Everything needed to restore communications was already in place at the far end of the Annex Skywalk. He could bring it online if Lorenzo gave him an escort.

McClane looked from Barnes to Trudeau to Lorenzo and felt the answer arrive before any of them had finished admiring the question.

It was obvious.

That was the problem.

Stuart and his people had dismantled Dulles's systems with military precision. They had anticipated the tower's responses, tapped its communications, and left behind exactly one conspicuous route back onto the air. Men that thorough would know about the annex. They would know Barnes would think of it. They would know how Lorenzo would protect him.

McClane said so.

Lorenzo heard one more intrusion from the New York cop who had been underfoot all night. He ordered McClane out. Samantha Coleman, who had forced her way into the tower with a cameraman and a federal contempt for closed doors, was removed with him.

The elevator doors shut on the crisis.

Coleman was furious about being thrown out. McClane was more interested in what she had learned. Stuart had called her station directly before taking over the airport. That meant the colonel knew how the news would react and how to use it. It also meant he had lines into places he should not have been able to reach.

The elevator started down.

McClane stopped it between floors and went looking for another route.

He found one below the public terminal, where the polished tile gave way to concrete, pipes, maintenance corridors, and the private kingdom of a janitor named Marvin. Marvin had a cot, a television, enough salvaged airport property to stock a small department store, and strong opinions about armed strangers entering without knocking.

McClane put him against a wall before either of them had time to establish etiquette.

Marvin explained that he worked there. McClane explained that he was a cop. They reached an understanding measured mostly by how quickly McClane lowered the gun.

Then a telephone rang.

It was not supposed to ring there. Marvin had tapped into the airport's internal lines for reasons McClane chose not to investigate. Through that improvised connection came voices from the tower: Barnes was moving to the Annex Skywalk with five of Lorenzo's men. They were going to activate the replacement array.

McClane asked for a map.

Marvin produced one from his collection.

The unfinished skywalk was a long glass-sided gallery connecting the terminal to the new annex. One way in. The array at the far end. Construction equipment and nowhere useful to go once shooting started.

A bottleneck.

An ambush with architectural drawings.

Marvin knew a route the gunmen would not be watching. Unfortunately, it was a ventilation duct running through the service levels, past the boiler room and into the annex. McClane shed his sweater when the heat made it useless and kept crawling.

He had wanted a normal Christmas. Pick up Holly. Find the car. Go home. The list had not included crawling through another building's ventilation system while terrorists tried to kill his wife by remote control.

Some traditions did not require consent.

Ahead of him, Barnes and the five-man team entered the unfinished gallery.

The place looked open without being open. Floor-to-ceiling glass showed the snow outside and the plastic-wrapped antenna dish beyond it. Inside, unfinished ceiling panels exposed beams and wiring. Scaffolding stood beneath a half-completed mural. Drop cloths, bins, boards, and tools occupied the edges. A moving walkway ran down the center toward the array.

Four construction workers were still on the job.

That should have been the second warning.

The first had been that Stuart had left them something they could fix.

Barnes called the tower and reported that he could see the dish. He and the officers stepped onto the moving walkway and hurried toward it.

At the far end, one of the workers opened the walkway's control panel and flipped a switch.

The belt stopped hard enough to stagger all six men.

One of the officers shouted at the worker to start it again. The man ignored him. The officer ran forward, demanding to know what he looked like.

The worker turned with a gun in his hand.

"A sitting duck."

The gallery erupted.

The other three workers abandoned the disguise at the same instant. Automatic fire swept the walkway. One officer went down before the others cleared the rail. Another was hit trying to move. Glass burst inward. Barnes took fragments across his arm and dropped behind a construction bin with the case containing the array equipment.

The surviving officers returned fire from whatever cover the unfinished corridor offered. One of Stuart's men fell. The officer who shot him died a moment later. The last of Lorenzo's team broke from cover and was cut down.

Then there was silence.

Barnes crouched alone behind the bin, bleeding into his sleeve. Footsteps approached on the other side.

Mulkey came around the cover with his weapon ready.

The ventilation grille beside his head kicked outward.

He caught it by reflex, which protected him from the metal and did nothing about McClane coming through behind it. McClane dropped into the gallery firing. Bullets struck the grate, punched through it, and drove Mulkey backward.

McClane rolled clear and fired toward O'Reilly at the far end of the gallery. O'Reilly ducked away. Gunfire hammered the floor around McClane from above.

Sheldon was on the scaffolding.

McClane ran beneath it. Sheldon tracked him through the gaps and fired downward while O'Reilly worked for an angle from the floor. McClane could not stop long enough to shoot either of them.

So he hit the scaffolding.

Shoulder into one crossbar. Then the next. Then another, running the length of it while rounds snapped off the metal behind him. The structure shuddered. Connections slipped. One side folded under Sheldon and dropped him onto the floor beside Barnes.

McClane had just enough time to appreciate the result before the rest came down after him.

He dived. Plywood, metal poles, paint supplies, and part of the unfinished mural crashed across the gallery. A board swept his legs out. His pistol skittered away onto the moving walkway.

O'Reilly had survived the collapse.

He was six feet away, already rising, already reaching for the machine pistol lying on the belt.

McClane's hand closed around a loose length of scaffold. He could not reach O'Reilly with it. He could reach the open control box beside the walkway.

He drove the metal into the electronics.

Blue-white sparks filled the panel. The walkway did not merely restart. It tore into high gear.

O'Reilly's feet vanished out from under him. The belt hurled him over McClane and into the wall at the end of the gallery headfirst.

He did not get up.

For a few seconds, the moving walkway was the loudest thing in the annex.

McClane recovered his pistol, checked the bodies, and went to Barnes. Barnes was conscious. Hurt, but conscious. His first concern was still the antenna array.

McClane followed his look through the glass.

The dish exploded.

They ducked as the windows came apart and pieces of the array blew through the snow. When the debris settled, nothing usable remained outside.

McClane stood slowly.

The array had never been an oversight. It had been bait: something obvious enough to pull Barnes out of the tower, important enough to make Lorenzo send his best men, and exposed enough to turn their response into a slaughter. Stuart had made them spend men and minutes they could not replace.

The planes above Dulles had even less to spare.

McClane looked up through the broken glass.

The Annex Skywalk disappeared.

Not behind smoke. Not in darkness. It simply ceased to be there, along with Barnes, the bodies, the ruined dish, the storm, and the moving belt still racing uselessly toward the wall.

For one impossible beat McClane remained exactly as he had been: pistol in hand, shirt soaked through with boiler-room sweat, weight pitched forward to meet a danger that had just been removed from the universe.

Then a chair arrived beneath him.

Hell of a time for the gate to close.
