The Gate Closes

The cargo hold under Terminal C smelled like jet fuel and wet cardboard, and forty feet up, past two floors of drywall and bad acoustics, somewhere in a control tower McClane hadn't been able to get anyone to listen to him inside of, seven planes were circling Dulles on fumes because a bunch of ex-military lunatics wanted their general back badly enough to let a few hundred strangers freeze to death thirty thousand feet up in the process.

Somewhere above him, muffled through concrete and rebar, he heard it again: the low, rolling concussion of something big enough to rattle the light fixtures, followed a half-second later by the flat silence of an alarm that had been triggered and then, deliberately, cut. Third one tonight. He'd stopped counting which parts of the terminal each blast had come from an hour ago, mostly because counting implied he had a plan for doing anything about it, and right now his plan began and ended with the guy currently trying to introduce a knife to his kidneys.

He tried not to think about the fuel gauges. Thinking about the fuel gauges made his hands shake, and his hands were currently the only thing standing between him and a knife.

The luggage belt kept grinding along on its own schedule above him, oblivious, hauling somebody's golf clubs toward a carousel nobody was going to be around to claim them from if this went much longer. McClane had noticed that about airports tonight: nothing in the building seemed to know there was a war on. The PA system kept apologizing for delays. A cart of duty-free liquor sat abandoned twenty feet away, glowing faintly under an emergency light, like it was waiting for somebody to remember it existed.

The hold had been built for machines and people who knew better than to stand where the machines wanted to go. Two belts crossed at different heights, one carrying bags in from the apron, the other feeding them toward the terminal. Steel guide rails divided the concrete floor into lanes. Yellow cages waited beside a roll-up door that had jammed three feet above the ground, leaving a strip of night beneath it and blowing needling snow across the floor. Beyond that gap, blue taxiway lights burned through the weather. Every few seconds a jet passed somewhere outside, unseen but close enough to push a deep vibration through the door and into McClane's teeth.

There were exits. There were always exits. A fire door behind the mercenary led toward the service stairs. The jammed cargo door might lift if someone reached the chain control on the wall. A maintenance ladder climbed to a grated catwalk above the belts. All three were useless while the man with the knife owned the middle of the room, but useless and nonexistent were different categories. McClane kept counting them because counting was what he had left.

The mercenary had counted too. He had kicked the fire door's crash bar loose before closing in, jamming the latch with a strip of torn baggage tag. He had put himself between McClane and the wall control. He had glanced once toward the ladder and never needed to glance again. That made four pieces of information McClane didn't enjoy and one he could use: the guy thought the ladder mattered.

He didn't have a gun anymore. He'd had one twenty minutes ago, back when this had still felt like a plan instead of a fistfight he was losing. Somewhere under the Chivas Regal, probably, if anybody ever bothered to look. What he had instead was a length of pipe he'd torn off a luggage cart rail, a shirt with more of him showing through it than fabric left to cover, and a guy in Army surplus fatigues who fought like he'd actually earned the uniform once, before he'd started renting himself out to whoever was willing to hold an airport hostage this Christmas.

It had started, technically, with a phone call. Holly's flight, a delay he could've lived with, a plan to grab coffee and pretend the last however-many months apart hadn't happened. Somewhere between the coffee and the second delay announcement, a bunch of mercenaries with a grudge and a general to spring had decided Dulles's tower made a fine hostage, and McClane, who had never once in his career managed to be somewhere bad things weren't also happening, had gone looking for a working radio and found himself, four fights and one dead priest impersonator later, exactly here: underground, bleeding, out of good ideas, with nobody topside inclined to believe a word he said about any of it.

The guy had a knife. Military-issue, well-kept, the kind of blade a man sharpened on his own time because he respected it.

"Ah, the well-maintained blade: cinema's oldest shorthand for 'this one's a professional, folks,'" the reviewer noted, warming up.

"Two in one chapter?!" the reviewer said, sounding almost personally offended by his own pacing.

McClane had opinions about people who brought knives to airports. He'd had opinions about people who brought guns to airports too, twenty minutes ago, and about people who impersonated priests forty minutes before that, and by this point in the evening he was starting to think Dulles just attracted a certain kind of clientele.

He was in the process of sharing exactly one of those opinions, wordlessly, with the pipe, when the knife came in under his guard and caught him low along the ribs. A line of cold, not pain yet, pain took a second to catch up to news like that, and for one useless heartbeat all he registered was the temperature.

"Okay," he said, mostly to himself, mostly to keep from thinking about the part where thinking about it would slow him down. "Okay. That's gonna be a thing later."

The guy didn't answer. He wasn't the talking kind, this one, unlike the last three, who'd all wanted McClane to know exactly how outmatched he was before they tried to prove it. This one just reset his stance, unhurried, like a man who'd done this enough times to know panic cost more than it bought.

McClane let the end of the pipe dip.

The mercenary's eyes followed it for a fraction of a second. Not much. Enough.

McClane threw the pipe at the cargo-door control instead of at him.

It struck the metal housing with a flat clang and mashed the green button beneath its end. The motor above the door coughed awake. Chain rattled through a guide. The jammed door lurched six inches upward, caught, and shook hard enough to dump a shelf of packed snow down through the opening. Wind punched into the hold, lifted loose baggage tags into the air, and sent the nearest empty luggage tub rolling across the concrete.

The mercenary moved before the tub reached him. He stepped onto its low plastic lip, let its momentum carry him sideways past the obstruction, and came off it with the knife already driving forward.

"Yeah," McClane muttered, backing toward the belts. "Had to be the one guy who knows how to use public transportation."

He came in low, testing, a feint toward McClane's ribs that wasn't really a feint at all, more an invitation to flinch wrong, and McClane didn't take it, brought the pipe around instead in a short, ugly arc that connected with the guy's forearm hard enough to numb it for exactly as long as it took him to switch the knife to his other hand and keep coming, unbothered, like the arm had belonged to somebody else the whole time. That was the part that worried McClane most, more than the knife, more than the blood he could feel soaking warm down one side. Pain that didn't slow a man down wasn't the same animal as bravery. It was just a different, more patient kind of dangerous.

The second cut opened his sleeve above the elbow. Shallow. Expensive anyway. McClane gave ground, caught the edge of a yellow baggage cage with one hand, and shoved it between them. The mercenary didn't waste time trying to go around. He drove a boot into the wire side. The whole cage jumped backward and trapped McClane's wrist against a guide rail.

The knife came through one of the squares.

McClane turned his head. Steel scraped the rail beside his cheek with a sound like somebody sharpening a kitchen knife directly inside his ear. He let go of the cage, dropped below the second thrust, and hauled the emergency release mounted at its base.

Three hundred pounds of suitcases spilled out.

Hard shells, soft canvas, garment bags, a child's pink case with one wheel already missing. They hit the floor and spread between the two men. The mercenary had to shorten his next step. McClane didn't. He planted one foot on the pink case, rode it half a yard like the world's worst skateboard, and hit the man in the mouth with the heel of his hand.

It bought him blood, one broken front tooth, and no meaningful improvement in the mercenary's attitude.

The man caught McClane by the shirt and drove him backward into the steel cage. Wire punched a grid into his spine. The knife rose between them. McClane trapped the wrist with both hands, but the man's other forearm pressed across his throat, compressing everything down to a hot, airless contest measured in inches.

Outside, landing lights passed low across the strip beneath the cargo door. A jet engine thundered close enough to shake snow out of the opening and make the hanging fluorescent fixtures sway. For one second the whole room pulsed with moving shadows.

The mercenary looked toward the door.

McClane bit him.

Not dignified. Not in any manual. Effective enough to get the forearm off his throat.

He wrenched the knife hand down against the cage, once, twice. On the third impact the fingers opened. The knife dropped among the luggage and vanished beneath a black duffel.

Both men went after it.

McClane kicked the duffel onto the lower belt. The bag vanished through the rubber curtain toward the terminal with the knife underneath it, a little gift for whichever baggage handler came back to work after the airport stopped being occupied by terrorists.

The mercenary hit him before he could enjoy that thought. A fist caught him behind the ear. The hold tilted. McClane stumbled across the moving belt, grabbed at a suspended sign, tore it loose instead, and came down with the aluminum panel in both hands.

NO RIDERS, it said in red block letters.

He hit the mercenary with it.

The panel folded around the man's shoulder. The man tore it away, and McClane got one good second to wish regulations were made of stronger material.

McClane got a hand on his wrist before the knife could come back around for a better angle. The guy got a knee into McClane's stomach that emptied every ounce of air he owned and put a bright white flash behind his eyes that had nothing to do with the fluorescents overhead. They went down together into a stack of unclaimed suitcases, hard enough that something inside one of them, glass, maybe, or a bottle wrapped for gifting, gave way with a soft, apologetic crunch.

They came up together too, tangled, neither one willing to let go first, and staggered a full three steps sideways into the belt itself, McClane's shoulder blades slamming down onto cold rubber that immediately, indifferently, started trying to carry him toward the terminal wall at the same slow, patient speed it had been carrying luggage all night. The guy rode him down onto it, weight pinning, knife hand straining against McClane's grip an inch at a time, close enough now that McClane could see, past the blade, a face that wasn't angry at all, just professionally disappointed, a man annoyed at how long a job was taking rather than afraid of losing it. McClane got a boot up against a support strut and shoved, hard, sending them both rolling off the belt's edge in a graceless tangle of limbs and a second, louder crunch that this time was somebody's ankle finding the concrete wrong, and for one confused half-second neither of them was entirely sure whose.

It wasn't his. He found that out the hard way, trying to stand on it, and only the guy's own hiss of pain, quickly swallowed, told him whose bad luck it had actually been.

He didn't wait for the guy to recover from it. Some lessons Dulles had already taught him twice tonight and he wasn't about to need a third reminder, and he drove a shoulder into the guy's chest while the bad ankle was still buckling under him, both of them going down again, harder this time, no belt to soften it, just concrete and a fresh stack of suitcases that hadn't asked to be involved in any of this.

He landed wrong. He landed on the ribs the knife had already found, and for a second the whole hold went narrow and bright and very far away, sound arriving late, like the room had put itself on a half-second delay just to spite him.

He got the pipe up anyway. Some things you did on muscle memory once thought stopped being fast enough to keep up.

His last clear thought, the kind he'd have been embarrassed to say out loud if there'd been anyone around who wasn't currently trying to open him up like carry-on luggage, was that Holly was going to kill him worse than this guy was even attempting to, if he missed her flight over something as stupid as bleeding out next to Baggage Claim 4. Twelve years married, most of it apart, and this was going to be the thing that finally did it. Not the distance. Not the job. A rented mercenary with good posture and a knife he clearly loved more than most people loved their kids.

He got the pipe moving.

It never landed.

There wasn't a sound so much as an absence of one, every grinding belt and every distant, oblivious PA announcement and the guy's breathing and his own, all of it switched off at once, like someone had found the master breaker for the whole building and thrown it without so much as a warning flicker first. The cold from the knife wound didn't finish arriving. The pipe didn't finish swinging, hung instead in some half-completed arc that no longer had a target worth completing it for. McClane didn't fall, because falling required a floor, and the floor had just stopped being a promise anybody was in a position to keep him.

"'So much as'? That's a trifecta, right there in the very first chapter," the reviewer said, visibly pleased with himself for counting.

He didn't lose consciousness so much as lose the room around it. The suitcases went first, then the light, then the personal weight of a knife wound that had been, up until a second ago, the most urgent piece of information his body had to offer him. It all just stopped mattering, all at once, the way nothing ever actually stopped mattering in real life, which was how he knew, somewhere underneath the part of him still trying to finish a punch, that whatever this was, it wasn't anything he'd trained for.

He'd been shot. He'd been stabbed, technically, currently, ongoing. He'd fallen off a building once and lived to make a joke about it. None of that had ever felt like this: not pain, not fear, just a very quiet, very total unhooking, like a plane easing off a jet bridge with the door already sealed behind it.

The last thing that made it through, thin and already fading, wasn't a threat assessment or an exit count or any of the dozen things thirty years on the job had trained him to reach for first. It was simpler than that, and stupider, and entirely his own, delivered in the same flat, running-commentary voice he used on everything else life had thrown at him tonight:

Hell of a time for the gate to close.
