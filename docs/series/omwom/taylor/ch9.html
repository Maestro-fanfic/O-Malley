Chapter 9 of 29

Final Call

The woman approached four minutes into Taylor's promised eighteen.

Danny had gone to confirm that his name was not somehow attached to a five-hundred-dollar harbor photograph. Taylor remained at the edge of the auction display, close enough to call him back, far enough that doing so would interrupt three people. She had been watching the slow hand of the wall clock above the coat check when the woman detached herself from the coffee service and came directly toward her.

She had the same catering uniform as the others, the same trained pleasantness, and Taylor knew, before she'd said a single word, that this wasn't about hors d'oeuvres. The woman carried a clipboard with one blank page clipped to it. She held it angled toward the room as if protecting confidential information that did not exist.

"Miss Hebert?" A clipboard, held loose, more prop than purpose. "There's a call for you. Front desk. They said it was important."

Nobody knows I'm here except my dad, Taylor thought, careful, quiet, angled at the woman's badge instead of her face. And he's twelve feet away.

Don't say that out loud. Smile. Ask who's calling.

"Did they say who?"

"A school administrator, I believe. Something about a scheduling matter." The woman's smile didn't waver, professional, patient, reciting a line she'd clearly been handed rather than one she'd written herself. "If you'd like to follow me, the front desk has a phone you can use privately."

She stepped half a pace aside as she said it, opening a path toward the coat-check lobby. It was a courtesy movement, the sort meant to keep Taylor from feeling directed. Behind her, the man at the main doors turned to accept a claim ticket from an elderly guest and placed himself squarely between Taylor and the outer vestibule.

The phone might be real. The call might even contain a voice prepared to sound like Blackwell. None of that made the path private by accident.

"Which administrator?"

The woman did not hesitate. "Principal Blackwell."

Taylor's first hope, that the story would collapse under one follow-up question, disappeared.

She got the name right, Taylor thought.

Means they did homework. Doesn't make the call real.

"What scheduling matter?"

"She didn't give me details." The woman's expression added just enough sympathy to imply school trouble without saying it. "She did say it couldn't wait until Monday."

Blackwell knew Taylor's father handled school calls. Every form in Winslow's office listed Danny's number first. The woman had the right name and the wrong procedure, which made the lie more frightening than a careless one would have been.

Someone had not guessed at Taylor. Someone had looked her up.

Or they had looked up everyone.

Taylor remembered the printed guest list at the coat-check desk, the board packets upstairs, the blue audit folder moving through the room. Danny had not simply bought two tickets. MedHall knew his employer, his position at the Dockworkers Association, his home address for the receipt, and the name of the daughter attending with him. From that, Winslow took minutes to find.

Prepared background, McClane said. Not necessarily prepared for you. Guy planning to control a room full of people wants a pressure point for everybody worth controlling. Employers. Spouses. Kids. Schools. Doesn't mean they solved us. Means they had a file and picked the page most likely to make a teenager walk away from her dad.

That was not comforting. It did put the danger back where it belonged. Taylor had become a candidate, not an answer.

That's not a school calling. McClane's voice had gone flat, fast, all business. Nobody administrative works this late on a Saturday, and even if they did, they'd call your dad's cell, not track down a fifteen-year-old at a party through the venue's front desk. This is a pull. They want you somewhere with fewer people in it.

So what do I do.

You don't go anywhere alone with a stranger holding a clipboard, that's rule one, doesn't matter what world you're from. A beat, calculating, fast. But you also can't just say no. That reads as guilty, or scared, either one gives them exactly the data point they're fishing for. You need a reason that isn't a refusal.

"Actually," Taylor said, already turning, already reaching, "my dad's right over there, I'll just grab him, he'll want to take it too if it's about school."

Something flickered behind the woman's professional pleasantness, brief, controlled, gone almost before it registered. "Of course. Whatever's easiest."

She did not leave. She waited for Taylor to act on the answer.

"Nicely done," Gladly said, delighted. "Redirected the whole play without ever saying no. That's, what, improv? That's improv. I'm very proud."

"She had help," Fury said.

"She had help fast. That's still hers."

Taylor crossed the room toward Danny because failing to do so would turn a good excuse into a visible lie. The woman followed for three steps, then stopped beside the auction display where she could watch without appearing to.

Danny saw Taylor coming and excused himself from the harbor-photograph argument.

"Please tell me you didn't bid on it."

"I was finding out who did. Apparently the frame is reclaimed pier timber."

"We have reclaimed pier timber in the garage."

"That's not reclaimed. I keep meaning to throw it away."

Taylor reached him. The woman remained in place, patient, still expecting a father to be collected for the school call.

"Front desk says somebody called from Winslow," Taylor said. "Probably wrong number, but they want you too."

Danny's attention sharpened. "Who from Winslow?"

"They said Blackwell."

"At this hour?"

"That's what I thought."

He looked toward the woman. Taylor felt the danger in the movement before it completed, but Danny did not study her or the doors behind her. He lifted one hand and beckoned her over, perfectly willing to make a school administrator wait on the line while he demanded an explanation in public.

The woman smiled, shook her head, and tapped the empty page on her clipboard. She pantomimed checking, then held up one apologetic finger. A mistake. One moment.

She turned away before Danny could cross to her.

"Efficient operation," Danny muttered.

"Maybe the chocolate fountain handles messages too."

He checked his watch. "Fourteen minutes."

"I'm counting."

Taylor stayed beside him until the woman disappeared through the service door. Then a councilwoman reclaimed Danny with a question about the review, and Taylor moved only far enough to avoid being folded into it.

A bug on the wall watched the woman reach the service corridor, discard the pleasant expression before the door had finished closing, and speak one sentence into the radio beneath her collar.

"She brought up the father."

The answer came from the man beyond the loading-dock fork, too low for the wall insect and nearly lost beneath a cart rattling across the kitchen tile.

"Expected. She asked to leave after the toast. We verify the reaction, then clear her."

The woman touched two fingers to her radio. "She questioned procedure. Father questioned the hour."

"Then the father stays out of it. Try the host."

The woman looked through the frosted glass toward Danny.

Then she walked away.

There, McClane said. That's why you. Asking to leave put you on a list. They don't know what you saw. They don't know what you can do. They know the timing made you worth checking.

And if I pass?

They clear you and move to the next person who looked wrong after the toast.

If I don't?

They stop asking you questions they already know the answer to.

That's one. McClane's voice had lost the tactical edge, gone flatter, grimmer. There'll be another. Guy running a play like that doesn't stop at one attempt just because the first one didn't land.

How do you know.

Because I've run plays like that. Different context. Same shape. You don't get lucky once and call it a night, not if the thing you're after actually matters to you.

How long till the next one, then.

Depends how patient he is. Some guys wait an hour, let you relax first. Some guys can't stand not knowing and come back in five minutes. A pause, weighing it. Guy who just watched his own security detail lose somebody? My money's on the second kind.

He wasn't wrong.

The second attempt came eleven minutes later, with three minutes left on Danny's clock.

In the interval, Taylor saw the auction coordinator receive the blue review folder from the board member, lock it inside a wheeled document case, and hand the key to a MedHall attorney. She saw the man at the mezzanine stairs report twice into his collar without appearing to move his mouth. She felt the woman from the false call take a station in the kitchen corridor rather than return to the party.

Nobody else approached Taylor. That was how she knew the second attempt had begun before the man reached her.

He wore a suit, not catering black-and-white this time, actual suit, actual confidence, and steered close enough that Taylor felt the wrongness of someone entering her space with intent rather than accident. He had come from the direction of the mezzanine stairs, which meant somebody up there had a clean line of sight down onto the whole atrium floor. Someone had been watching from above while she'd been busy tracking men at eye level.

The man chose his approach carefully. Danny stood beside the councilwoman near the bar. A pair of hospital administrators crossed between him and Taylor just as the man arrived. Behind Taylor, the dessert table and a velvet auction rope limited how far she could step back without making the encounter look like something she wanted to escape.

"Miss Hebert. Mr. Anders would love a word, if you have a moment. Somewhere a little quieter."

There it is. McClane's voice had gone very still, a man doing math he didn't like the answer to. That's not a clipboard anymore. That's a direct ask, from someone who works for him, using his name like it's supposed to open every door in the building.

What do I say?

The truth, mostly. You're not supposed to be alone with strange men at parties, and your dad would want to know if the host is asking. Make it about manners, not suspicion. Manners he can't argue with.

"That's so nice of him," Taylor said, bright, easy, a girl who'd clearly been raised right. "Could you let him know I'll come find him in a bit? I promised my dad I'd stick close for the toast photos."

The man's smile held. Whatever was underneath it didn't quite manage to.

"It won't take more than a minute. Mr. Anders prefers to thank the families of tonight's community partners personally."

It was better than the school call. It used something Anders had already done in public, gave Taylor a harmless reason to feel chosen, and made refusal sound rude rather than frightened.

"Then he won't mind thanking Dad at the same time."

"Mr. Hebert appears occupied."

"He'll forgive us."

Taylor lifted her hand toward Danny.

The man moved half a step, enough to interrupt her line of sight without openly blocking it. "There's no need to disturb him."

The sentence changed the encounter. Not loudly. Not enough for the administrators beside them to hear it as a threat. But the pleasant fiction required Taylor to accept that a stranger knew when she was permitted to involve her father.

McClane did not give her a line. He had already given her the rule. Make the pressure visible without accusing anyone of applying it.

Taylor leaned sideways and spoke past the man's shoulder.

"Dad? Mr. Anders wants me somewhere private."

She pitched it one degree too loud for an ordinary aside. The nearer hospital administrator glanced over. The second followed her attention. Danny stopped speaking in the middle of the councilwoman's answer.

The man in the suit stepped clear at once.

"A brief misunderstanding," he said, raising one hand toward Danny. "Please, don't let us interrupt you."

His retreat was smooth enough to preserve the room's interpretation. Taylor had misunderstood an invitation. The staff member had corrected her. An awkward second passed, then the administrators turned back to their conversation.

Danny did not.

"Taylor?"

"He wanted both of us for another picture," she said. "I told him we're leaving."

The man was still close enough to hear. That was intentional.

Danny checked his watch. "Three minutes. Then coats. No extensions."

"Works for me."

He kept watching until the man made a small apologetic bow and moved away.

"Toast photos," Gib said, appreciative. "A lie built entirely out of things that actually happened. I respect the craftsmanship."

"Taylor's the one containing multitudes right now," O'Malley said quietly. "That's her whole schtick."

Taylor watched the man thread back through the crowd toward the service corridor, toward, she was fairly sure, wherever Max Anders was standing right now, working out what came next.

Two. McClane's voice had no humor left in it at all. That's two real attempts in under fifteen minutes, kid. He's not fishing anymore. He's actively trying to get you somewhere quiet, and he's running out of polite ways to ask.

He still doesn't know it's me. You said so.

I said he doesn't know you're the one watching him. Careful now, deliberate, weighing a distinction that mattered more than it sounded like it should. He knew your name before the toast. You're on his guest list and he used your dad in the speech. That's not the same as knowing what you did. Two attempts on you specifically, in fifteen minutes, means you've become somebody's best guess, even if you're not their confirmed answer yet. Best guess is close enough to be dangerous.

Taylor looked across the room, once, and found Danny back in conversation, his attention divided now, one eye returning to her between the councilwoman's sentences. He knew his daughter had been bothered twice. He did not know both approaches belonged to the same search, that the people conducting it watched every exit, or that the gracious host apparently wanted her isolated before Danny's three minutes expired.

"Three minutes," Fury said. "Plenty of warning, right up until it isn't."

You still with me? McClane asked, once she'd put a solid twenty feet and a cluster of chattering board members between herself and the last place anyone had seen her standing.

Still here.

Good. Keep moving. Don't stop long enough to be findable.

Taylor set her shoulders, found the nearest cluster of guests thick enough to disappear into, and started walking, unhurried, toward the safest, most crowded corner of a room that no longer felt safe at all. Above her, on the mezzanine, she caught the barest flicker of movement, someone stepping back from the railing just as she looked up, gone before she could be sure she'd seen anything at all.

You catch that, she thought.

Yeah. Grim, certain. Guy up top. Different angle, same job. We're not dealing with a handful of guys anymore, kid. We're dealing with a building.
