The Hour Before

In our last episode: a mid-level SHIELD paper-pusher gets recruited into a fantasy crossover by dropping dead in the middle of a very important discussion about grappling rigs, because nothing says "chosen one" like a man whose last conscious act on his own Earth was arguing about equipment allowances.

Somewhere behind a door with entirely too many other doors, a folder gets stamped, a joke about eyebrows does not happen yet, and our protagonist gets handed his entire new personality in one line: "I've called you my Eye more than once." Riveting stuff. Ten out of ten, no notes, except several notes, several of which are about the paperwork.

"He requisitioned himself," said one voice from the gallery, delighted, plainly a man who'd filled out entirely too many procurement forms in a past professional life. "I've read a great many recruitment files. That's the only one I've ever seen with its own chain of custody."

A ripple of agreement went through the anonymous watchers crowded into the seats behind him, a low, appreciative murmur, the sound a crowd made when a joke landed exactly as intended. Somebody, unseen, asked whether the beverage machine took requests. Nobody answered. Nobody ever did.

Now: cut to Sunnydale, California, a town whose primary industry, as far as this narrator can determine, is dying, and our hero rides shotgun toward the vineyard where he is, this movie assures us, about to have a genuinely terrible day.

The drive out to the vineyard took twenty minutes nobody talked much through, six of them crammed into Giles's ancient sedan with weapons wrapped in beach towels on their laps like the world's least convincing family road trip, and Xander spent most of it with his forehead against the window, watching Sunnydale's tired little houses give way to hills that hadn't seen rain in weeks.

Buffy drove. Faith rode shotgun, boots up on the dash in a way that would have gotten anyone else yelled at, and in the back, wedged between Xander and a girl named Chloe who hadn't said six words since she'd been Called three days ago, Molly sat with both hands wrapped around a stake like she was afraid it might try to leave without her. Across from them, a sharp-eyed girl named Caridad watched the whole car with the quiet, assessing calm of someone who'd already decided panicking wasn't going to be her particular contribution to the day.

"You built a model," Faith said, twisting around from the front seat, half amused. "Like an actual model. With popsicle sticks."

"A model," someone in the anonymous rows repeated, delighted. "Popsicle sticks. This production had a craft budget and spent it on a teenager's diorama."

"Cheaper than CGI," another voice pointed out.

"Everything's cheaper than CGI."

"I like to be thorough." Xander didn't look away from the window. "Some of us don't get super strength as a backup plan when thorough doesn't work out."

"There it is. That's the whole show in one sentence. Somebody frame that."

"Didn't say it was a bad idea." Faith's grin flashed, gone as fast as it came. "Just didn't expect Watcher's Council levels of arts and crafts out of you, is all."

"Giles was thrilled. You should've seen his face."

"I did see his face. Looked like Christmas came early and brought glue."

Buffy caught Xander's eye in the rearview mirror, something steady and grateful in it that didn't need words, and he held it a beat before looking away, because if he thought too hard about the fact that people were trusting a plan he'd built with a shoebox lid and four hundred imagined versions of today, he was going to start doubting the whole thing right there in the back seat, and there wasn't room in this car for that particular flavor of panic.

The car parked half a mile out, in the dead lot of an abandoned feed store, the kind of place that had gone under sometime around when Sunnydale's whole economy had started quietly organizing itself around vampire attacks and unexplained disappearances. From there it was on foot, single file along a dry irrigation ditch, the vineyard's rows rising up ahead of them in the gray early light like something out of a war movie nobody had asked to star in.

Buffy split the group at the tree line. Herself and Faith with the main strike team, circling wide toward what scouting had suggested was Caleb's actual base of operations, the old winery building itself. A second, smaller group toward the east rows, feeling out the perimeter. And Xander, at his own insistence, staying back with the four Potentials too green or too recently arrived to be trusted anywhere near the real fighting yet, running last-minute logistics, making sure everyone actually understood the plan instead of just nodding along because nodding was easier than admitting you were scared.

"You've got maybe an hour before we need eyes forward," Buffy told him, low, just between them, her hand briefly on his shoulder. "Get them settled. Get yourself settled too, okay? You've been weird since yesterday."

"I'm always weird. It's kind of my whole thing."

"Weirder than usual." She studied him a second longer, something searching in it he didn't have an answer for yet, and then Faith was calling her name from up the ridge and the moment closed over, gone, filed away for whenever there was time to come back to it.

There wasn't going to be time. Xander understood that already, some cold, certain part of him that had learned, over the years, to recognize the shape of a day that wasn't going to give anybody the luxury of finishing their sentences.

He found the third row exactly where his model said it would be, the light already going gray and strange under the vine canopy, and unfolded the hand-drawn map against a fence post while Molly and the others gathered around, quiet, waiting.

"Okay," he said, mostly to himself, and then, because Molly was watching him with the desperate attention of someone who needed him to have an answer, "okay, so, here's the thing about grapes."

"'Here's the thing about grapes,'" someone in the anonymous rows repeated, savoring it. "I've sat through action-movie briefings with worse opening lines than that. This kid should be teaching a seminar."

"He is, technically. Look at the audience."

Molly didn't laugh. She was maybe sixteen, hollow-eyed in the way all of them were now, three weeks off a plane from somewhere with a name Xander kept meaning to look up and never did, and behind her the other three Potentials had arranged themselves in the loose, unconscious half-circle people made around whoever seemed like they might actually know something.

"The thing about grapes," Xander said again, "is nobody plants them in a straight line because it looks nice. They're planted so a truck can get down the row without murdering half the crop. Which means every row you're looking at is basically a hallway. Caleb's people can only come at us single file, or close to it, once we're between the vines. That's not nothing."

"You built a model," Molly said. Not quite a question.

"I built a model. Popsicle sticks and a shoebox lid, very high-tech, very Watcher's Council, I'm sure Giles is thrilled." He tapped the map. "Point is, I've walked this ground in my head about four hundred times since Tuesday. I know where the light's bad. I know where the ground's soft enough to trip on. None of that makes today safe. It just means when it stops being safe, you'll already know which way you were planning to run."

"That's not the same as safe."

"No," Xander agreed. "It's really not. I'm not going to stand here and tell you it is, because you'd know I was lying, and then you'd stop trusting anything else I said, and I need you to trust the next part more than the first part." He crouched down to her eye level, the way you did with kids, except she wasn't a kid, not really, not anymore, none of them were once the scythe or whatever passed for one found them. "The next part is: if it goes bad, you don't look for me. You don't look for Buffy. You look for the nearest gap in the vines and you go through it low and fast and you do not stop to see if anyone's behind you. Guilt is a luxury item. You can afford it later."

Molly's mouth did something that wasn't quite a smile. "You're weird for a guy with no powers."

"I prefer 'unconventionally credentialed.'"

That got the almost-smile a little further along, and behind her, one of the other Potentials, a quiet girl named Deb who'd barely spoken since arriving, actually laughed, short and surprised, like she hadn't expected to have one left in her this morning. Caridad, beside her, didn't laugh, but something in her sharp, watchful expression eased half a degree, as if she were quietly filing away that the funny one might actually know what he was doing. It wasn't much. It felt, right then, like the only useful thing he'd built all week, more useful than the shoebox, more useful than the four hundred imaginary walks down four hundred imaginary rows.

He sent the other three off to double-check the gap points he'd marked, an actual task, something with edges to it, small enough to give scared hands somewhere to put themselves, and kept Molly back a moment longer, because something in her had been holding on a little too tight since the car.

That was when the ground tilted.

Not literally. Nothing under his feet actually moved. But something in the architecture of his own skull shifted half an inch to the left and then, just as fast, corrected itself, that odd disorientation of hearing a word you were sure you'd only thought, echo back at you from somewhere that wasn't your own voice at all.

Where am I.

"Oh, HERE we go," the gallery's voice said, sitting up so fast this narrator could hear it happen. "New arrival. Everybody quiet, this is the good part."

Not his own thought. He knew his own thoughts, had spent a lifetime getting intimately, uncomfortably familiar with every single one of them, and this one arrived shaped wrong, too calm for the volume of wrongness it should have been carrying, a man's voice, unfamiliar, running some kind of quiet triage on a situation he clearly hadn't chosen to be in.

Okay, Xander thought back, with the weary competence of a man who had once had a hyena spirit take up temporary residence behind his eyes and was, therefore, considerably less rattled by this flavor of impossible than a reasonable person had any right to be. New guy. Nice of you to knock.

"'Nice of you to knock,'" someone repeated, wheezing. "He's not even fazed. Fifteen years old, hyena spirit, apparently a whole BACKLOG of impossible. This kid's résumé is unreal."

The voice, when it came again, had gone very still in a way that felt less like fear and more like a man doing inventory under fire. You can hear that.

"Yes, obviously, we all can, that's the premise, keep up."

Loud and clear. You want to tell me who you are, or should I just start narrating the vineyard tour and see if you flinch at anything?

"HA. There it is. First joke under pressure. Somebody start a tally."

Phillip Coulson. A beat, the mental equivalent of someone squaring their shoulders. I don't have a framework for this. I'm going to need you to be patient with me for approximately as long as it takes me to build one, which, going on past performance, should be under a minute.

"'Going on past performance,'" another voice echoed, delighted. "The man's been conscious in a stranger's skull for ninety seconds and he's already citing his own track record. That's a professional."

Buddy, I don't have a framework for anything that's happened to me since I was fifteen. Xander kept his face doing the things a face was supposed to do while he was mid-conversation with a Potential, which took more effort than he would have guessed, given how many other things his brain was apparently equipped to run in parallel these days. Welcome to the crowded house. There's a spirit hyena joke I could make here but I don't actually know you well enough yet to know if you'd get it.

Try me.

"Oh, he's GAME. Look at that. Thirty seconds in and he's already leaning into the bit."

Later. Kind of in the middle of something.

"'Kind of.' Note it."

"We're noting everything tonight, aren't we."

He glanced back at Molly, who was watching him with the alarm of someone who had just watched a grown man's eyes go somewhere else entirely for three full seconds. "Sorry. Where was I."

"You went somewhere," Molly said carefully. "Somewhere that wasn't here."

"Yeah." Xander rubbed the back of his neck. There wasn't a version of the truth here that would help her more than it would scare her, not with less than an hour left before people started arriving with knives, so he reached for the smallest true thing available instead. "Turns out I've got company today. Long story, not the important one. The important one's still the vines and the running and the not looking back for me."

Molly considered this with the flat, fast-adjusting calm of someone who had already recalibrated for stranger things this month than her mentor briefly going somewhere else mid-sentence. "Is the company going to help?"

"Working on finding out," Xander said, which was, as far as he could tell, entirely true.

She trusts you, Coulson said, and there was something in the observation that wasn't quite surprise, more the sound of a hypothesis holding up under early testing. Actually trusts you. Not the performance of it. I've spent a career reading the difference and that wasn't performance.

Yeah, well. Xander watched Molly go back to turning the stake over in her hands, methodical, a fidget that was really just a way of keeping her hands from finding something worse to do. Not a lot of competition for the job around here. Buffy's got the whole army to run. Giles has the whole history of everything to keep straight. Willow's basically a walking apocalypse-prevention system these days. Somebody's gotta be the guy who remembers which sixteen-year-old needs a pep talk before the knives show up, and it's not exactly a role people are lining up for.

That's not a complaint. I want to be clear I heard that as information, not a complaint.

It wasn't a complaint. And it mostly wasn't, which was, if Xander was being honest with the part of his head that apparently now had an audience, more interesting than the fact that a stranger had said it back to him accurately. It's just the job. Somebody's gotta watch the people nobody else has time to watch. Turns out I'm decent at it. Turns out decent at it doesn't come with a title.

I know exactly what that job looks like from the inside, Coulson said, and there was something dry underneath it, self-aware in a way that read like a man who'd made peace with an old joke a long time ago. I've spent the better part of two decades being extremely good at a job most of the building assumes is filing.

"'Most of the building assumes is filing,'" someone repeated, delighted. "This narrator has met that guy. Every organization has that guy. Never trust the quiet one with the binder."

Huh. Xander turned that over. You're a filing guy who got teleported into a Buffy fic. This day keeps getting weirder.

"'A Buffy fic,'" several voices said at once, and then, overlapping, a ragged little chorus of "he knows," "does he know," "how does he know," before someone finally shouted them all down.

I'm not going to ask what that means.

"Smart man. Ignorance is the only thing keeping him functional right now."

Smart. Save it for later, we've got, what, forty minutes?

Thirty-eight.

You're timing it?

"Of course he's timing it. Look at him. That's a man who has never once in his life not known exactly how much time is left."

Occupational habit. A pause, and something in the quality of the silence shifted, careful in a new direction. Can I ask you something. About her. He meant Molly, Xander understood immediately, without needing the clarification. How long have you known her.

Three weeks. Since she landed. Xander watched her check her stake grip for the fifth time in as many minutes, the small compulsive ritual of someone keeping her hands busy so her head didn't have room to go somewhere worse. You don't need years for this part. You just need to actually be there for the three weeks you get.

That's a better answer than most people twice your age would give me.

I get that a lot. The 'better answer than expected' thing. Not usually meant as a compliment.

I meant it as one.

Xander didn't have a quick joke ready for that, which was, itself, unusual enough to notice.

He crouched back down to Molly's level instead, tapped the map one more time, walked her through the third row and the gap by the broken irrigation post and the exact spot where the light went bad enough that anyone chasing her would lose a full second finding their footing, a second that might, with reasonable luck, be the whole difference. She listened the way she'd been listening the whole conversation, hungry for it in a way that had nothing to do with tactics and everything to do with somebody bothering to explain instead of just ordering. When the others drifted back from checking the gap points, he ran through it again for all four of them together, patient, unhurried, until even Chloe, who still hadn't said much of anything, managed a small, determined nod.

Thirty minutes became twenty. Twenty became twelve, time that didn't announce itself shrinking so much as simply arrived at almost nothing, all at once, the way water finally boils after appearing to sit still for ages.

You're good at this, Coulson said, quiet, somewhere around the eight-minute mark, and it didn't feel like flattery so much as a man updating a file in real time. I want you to know I'm not saying that to manage you. I don't have a reason to manage you yet. I'm saying it because it's true and because, from where I'm sitting, I don't think anyone's told you.

Careful, Xander said, and meant it mostly as a joke, mostly. Keep talking like that and I might start liking you.

Would that be so terrible.

Ask me again in an hour.

"What? What'd he say?"

"Nothing. Never mind. Watch the row."

He didn't know, saying it, how literally the hour would come to matter, or that the last easy thing he'd think for a long while was going to be that exact sentence, tossed off light, half a joke, an offhand line that filled a silence he didn't yet know was about to end.

Molly's head came up first. She had good ears, better than his, better than most of the Potentials, one of the few genuine advantages the Calling seemed to have handed her early. "Someone's coming," she said, low, and the fear in it was different from the fear she'd been carrying for the last twenty minutes, sharper, more immediate, the difference between being afraid of a thing and being afraid of this thing, right now, close.

Xander stood. Down the third row, the one with the good sightline he'd built the whole plan around, a shape was walking toward them, unhurried, taking his time about it in a way that had nothing to do with confidence and everything to do with certainty.

Xander, Coulson said, and the dryness had gone out of him entirely, replaced by something flat and focused, a professional's attention locking onto a threat, a register Xander would only much later learn to recognize on sight, that's him, isn't it.

Yeah, Xander said, and felt Molly's hand find his sleeve, gripping hard enough to hurt. That's him.

"ffffuuuuuuuu..." someone in the anonymous rows managed, before the sound cut off entirely, along with everything else, the whole gallery, this narrator included, holding its breath for whatever came next.
