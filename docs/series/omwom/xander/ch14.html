Chapter 14 of 43

Dug In

By midmorning the house had gone from wrecked to grimly functional, someone had boarded over the bay window, someone else had gotten a pot of coffee going despite the general lack of anyone in a state to drink it, and Xander was sweeping glass out of the dining room with the joyless focus of a man who badly wanted a task simple enough not to think about anything else while he did it.

"Aftermath episode," the gallery's voice said, quieter than usual, none of its earlier appetite for jokes in it. "These are always the hard ones to sit through. Watch how carefully everyone avoids saying the actual number out loud."

Nobody in the anonymous rows had anything to add to that.

We need to talk about what happens next, Coulson said, and there was nothing tentative in it now, no restraint at all, the professional urgency Xander had first heard at the vineyard fully returned. A real plan. Chain of command that doesn't collapse the first time someone's confidence outruns their preparation. Contingencies built in from the start, not stitched on after people are already bleeding.

"I know what happened," Xander said out loud, low, sweeping harder than the glass strictly required. "I was there. I don't need the after-action report."

I'm not giving you an after-action report. I'm telling you what needs to change, and it needs to come from someone in this house, and I don't think it's going to be Faith.

"So what, now you want to run things? Twelve hours ago you were the guy telling me you didn't have a plan, just an opinion nobody asked for."

I have more than an opinion. I have twenty years of watching exactly this kind of failure happen in exactly this kind of vacuum, and I know what fixes it.

"Yeah, well, maybe I'm not real interested in your fix right now." The words came out sharper than Xander meant them to, sharper than the moment strictly called for, and some distant, more reasonable part of him noted the disproportion even as the rest of him kept swinging the broom like it owed him something. "Funny how you were right about the retreat path and now suddenly you've got the whole playbook. Real convenient timing."

That's not fair.

"Maybe not. Don't much care right now."

He knew, even as he said it, that the anger wasn't really about Coulson's timing, wasn't really about the fix or the playbook or any of the specific words being traded between them. It was about being wrong, freshly and publicly wrong, in a way that had cost Robin Wood most of his blood and nearly cost the house a great deal more, and some old, familiar part of Xander, the part that had been digging in against being told what to do since long before any of this started, had decided that admitting Coulson's approach might actually be the right one now meant admitting the whole argument they'd been circling since the kitchen table, not just this one call, and some stubborn, wounded corner of him needed Coulson to be wrong about something, anything, badly enough to keep swinging at shadows rather than let that happen.

You're digging in, Coulson observed, and there wasn't judgment in it, just a flat, careful read. I understand why. I'd probably do something similar in your position. I still think you're wrong to do it right now, but I understand it.

"Great. Glad we're on the same page about how wrong I am."

That's not what I said.

"Feels like what you meant."

He swept the last of the glass into a dustpan he didn't remember picking up, dumped it into a garbage bag someone had already half-filled with the wreckage of the night before, and kept moving, kept his hands busy, because stillness right now felt like an invitation for the rest of it to catch up with him.

Willow passed through the dining room while he worked, arms full of first-aid supplies, and paused just long enough to catch his eye. "You okay?"

"Peachy," Xander said, not looking up from the broom. "Just doing some light home renovation. Very therapeutic."

"Xander."

"I'm okay, Will. Really." He managed something close to a real look at her, softening it. "Go take care of Robin. I've got this."

She lingered a moment longer, clearly unconvinced, but the house had a hundred urgent things needing her attention this morning, and she squeezed his shoulder once and moved on, leaving him alone again with the broom and the argument still simmering behind his ribs.

You want to know what actually bothers me, Xander said, eventually, the anger not gone but banked lower, simmering instead of boiling. It's not that you were right. It's that being right doesn't feel like anything. I thought if I ever actually proved my gut mattered, it'd feel like something. Instead two people are dead and Robin's barely breathing and all I've got is you, very calmly, very professionally, telling me what to fix next.

I don't have a better feeling to offer you, Coulson said, quiet. I wish I did. In my experience, being right about a danger nobody listened to never feels like winning. It just feels like carrying the weight of having seen it coming and not been enough to stop it. That doesn't go away. You just get more practiced at carrying it.

That's supposed to help?

No, Coulson admitted. I don't think there's a version of this conversation that helps. I think there's a version that's honest, and I'd rather give you that than something that sounds better and isn't true.

You said you're used to carrying it. How many times, exactly.

More than I'd like to count, Coulson said, quiet. A missed threat in Prague, once, early in my career, that cost two field agents their lives. A miscalculated timeline in a hostage situation I still think about more than I'd admit under normal circumstances. Being right doesn't inoculate you against the cost of it happening anyway. If anything, it makes the cost feel more personal, because you know exactly how it could have gone differently, and it didn't.

How do you keep doing the job, knowing that.

Because the alternative is not showing up the next time, and the next time might be the one where being right actually saves someone. A pause, something almost gentle underneath the professional flatness. You don't get to stop caring just because caring hurts. You just learn to carry it forward instead of letting it stop you.

Xander didn't answer that right away, standing in the middle of a dining room still gritty with glass dust, the argument having burned through most of its own fuel without either of them actually resolving anything, and found, underneath the exhaustion, that the honesty landed harder than any reassurance would have.

"Yeah," he said, finally, out loud, quiet, mostly to himself. "Yeah, okay. Forward it is."

"'Into the valley of Death rode the six hundred,'" someone in the anonymous rows murmured, half under their breath, not really a joke, just an old line borrowed because none of their own quite fit. Nobody asked them to explain it. Nobody needed to.
