Growing Pains

The eleven girls from Africa didn't fold seamlessly into Cleveland's existing population, and Xander should have expected that, probably, if he'd thought about it properly instead of just being relieved to have finally gotten everyone somewhere safe.

The friction started small. Different languages, different customs, different assumptions about how a shared living space was supposed to work, all of it compressed into hallways and common rooms that had never been designed to hold this much difference at once. Priya got into a sharp, unhappy argument with one of the original Sunnydale survivors over kitchen scheduling that had nothing to do with kitchens and everything to do with two groups of scared, displaced girls each feeling like outsiders in a space the other one had claimed first.

It wasn't just Priya. Small misunderstandings kept surfacing everywhere Xander looked, a training exercise that meant something entirely different to a girl raised on one continent's fighting traditions than to one raised on another's, a joke that landed as playful teasing to one group and genuine insult to the other, mealtimes that turned tense over nothing more complicated than unfamiliar food and homesickness for meals nobody here knew how to properly recreate. One of the younger girls asked him, out of nowhere one Tuesday, whether he could help her put together a small cake to bring to school, some tradition from home she couldn't fully explain and didn't seem to expect him to understand, just needed, badly, for someone to help her have.

"Okay, this is getting far too specific," the same voice said, flat, something almost unsettled under the dryness this time. "A cake. For school. Why does that detail feel like it's been lifted wholesale out of somewhere else entirely."

"You said yourself you couldn't see the shape of it yet."

"I still can't. I just keep bumping into the edges of it."

"This isn't working the way I thought it would," Xander admitted, late one evening, watching two more small conflicts play out across the common room in the space of an hour.

It rarely does, this fast, Coulson said. You built something real out there, a genuine community with its own rhythms and trust. Uprooting that and dropping it into an existing structure was always going to require real friction before it settled. This isn't a failure. It's just the actual cost of the thing you already knew you were choosing.

Doesn't make it feel better watching it happen.

No, Coulson agreed. I don't imagine it does.

He spent the next several weeks doing the same patient, unglamorous work he'd built his whole reputation on, this time in a language everyone in the building actually shared. He mediated the kitchen dispute, patient and fair, finding a schedule that gave both groups real ownership instead of one side simply yielding to the other. When two of the younger girls kept sneaking out past curfew despite three separate conversations about it, he didn't raise his voice or hand out punishment duty. He just quietly suspended movie night until they'd gone a full week following the rules on their own, and watched, with real satisfaction, how much faster peer pressure solved the problem than any lecture of his ever could have. When the ice cream started disappearing from the kitchen freezer faster than the house's actual budget accounted for, he didn't lecture anybody about that either. He just quietly stopped buying it, replaced it with a truly depressing amount of fat-free sherbet, and let the resulting outrage sort out its own culprits within about thirty-six hours.

"Man," the same voice said, faint, defeated, all the earlier unease collapsed down into pure, helpless disbelief. "I just... can't even."

Nobody asked him to finish the sentence. This narrator suspects there wasn't a version of it that would've helped.

"That's kind of sneaky," Priya observed, watching the two chastened girls petition their housemates for solidarity on the curfew issue.

"It IS sneaky," someone in the anonymous rows said, delighted. "And it's the single most competent parenting we've seen in this entire production. Somebody give this man custody of something."

"He has custody of eleven teenage Slayers and a federal agent. I think he's covered."

"I prefer 'effective.'" Xander managed a small, unrepentant grin. "Nobody's ever been yelled into good behavior long-term. Nobody's ever said no to getting their friends' movie night back, though." He sat with Priya through a long, difficult evening of homesickness that had nothing to do with logistics and everything to do with grief for a life left behind, again, for the second time in less than a year.

"I don't belong here," Priya said, miserable, curled up on her bed in the small shared room she'd been assigned. "Everyone already knows each other. Everyone already has their people. I'm just the new girl from somewhere far away that nobody actually understands."

"I felt that too, first year I really started paying attention to who I was in a room full of people who already had their roles figured out," Xander said, sitting on the floor beside her bed, unhurried. "Took me a long time to realize belonging isn't something that's handed to you all at once. It's built, slow, one actual connection at a time. You don't have to belong to everybody today. You just have to find one person, then another, then another, and eventually you look up and realize you built the whole thing without noticing."

"How long did it take you?"

"Honestly? Kind of my whole life. Still building it, some days." He managed a small, wry smile. "But I got there. You will too. I promise."

Priya studied him a long moment, something in her posture finally loosening. "You actually believe that."

"I actually do."

It took real time, real patient work, but slowly, the friction started giving way to something steadier. Priya found her footing with a small group of the original Cleveland Slayers who shared her sharp, dry sense of humor, an unlikely friendship built out of shared sarcasm rather than shared history. The kitchen schedule, once actually fair, stopped generating conflict entirely. Small kindnesses started accumulating between the two groups, shared meals, shared training sessions, the slow, unglamorous work of thirty-some strangers becoming, gradually, something closer to a single community.

You did this, Coulson observed, one evening, watching the common room's easy, mixed chatter, girls from two different continents laughing together over something Xander couldn't quite catch from across the room.

We did this, Xander corrected, easy now, no longer needing to defend the distinction at all. Same as always. Your read on where the friction was actually coming from, my read on how to actually fix it without making anybody feel like they lost.

I'll take the credit split. It's accurate.

"Look at them, negotiating credit splits like a normal old married couple," the gallery's voice said, warm. "This narrator did not expect to get invested, and yet."

Xander watched the room a while longer, feeling something settle in his chest that had nothing to do with crisis management and everything to do with simple, quiet satisfaction, the reward of watching something you'd built with your own patient, unglamorous hands actually take root and grow.

Worth all the friction? Coulson asked, quiet.

Yeah, Xander said, watching Priya throw her head back laughing at something one of the Cleveland girls had said, entirely at home now in a room that had felt like enemy territory just weeks ago. Yeah. Every bit of it.
