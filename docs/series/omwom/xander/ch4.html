Chapter 4 of 43

Revello Drive

Previously: our folksy antagonist made good on every promise his introduction implied, our protagonist lost an eye in the process, and the newly-recruited SHIELD handler learned, in the worst possible classroom, exactly where the limits of his new arrangement actually sat.

Nobody argued. Even the beverage machine, several rows back, seemed to know better than to hum.

He came back to himself properly somewhere in the back of Giles's car, jolting over the same dry ruts they'd driven in on, someone's jacket pressed hard against the side of his face and someone else's hand gripping his so tight it hurt in a small, distant way that barely registered against everything else.

"Stay with us," Buffy kept saying, low, urgent, from somewhere near his head. "Xander. Stay with us."

He wanted to tell her he wasn't going anywhere, that going anywhere wasn't really on the table given how thoroughly the world had narrowed down to a single burning point behind his left eye, but talking turned out to require a coordination he didn't have available yet, so he just held onto her hand and let the car eat up the miles back toward town.

The vineyard had cost more than his eye. He learned that in pieces over the drive, Faith's voice clipped and furious in the front seat relaying a body count that hadn't finished counting yet, two Potentials down and not getting back up, a third torn up bad enough that somebody was already talking about a hospital they couldn't actually risk using. Molly made it. He held onto that fact the way he held onto Buffy's hand, small and solid against everything else trying to pull him under.

The kitchen at Revello Drive smelled like antiseptic and burnt coffee by the time Willow got the last of the gauze taped into place, the house around them full of the low, awful murmur of people counting their own losses in whispers, trying not to let the newest arrivals hear just how bad the night had actually gone.

"So on the bright side," Xander said, to nobody in particular, to Willow's hands still shaking slightly as she smoothed down the final edge of the bandage, "I was never using that eye for anything important. Depth perception's overrated. Who needs to judge distance? Not me. Not anymore. I'm basically a Cyclops now, except with worse superpowers and a much smaller support team."

"Xander." Willow's voice cracked on the second syllable.

"I'm fine. I'm hilarious. Those are basically the same thing right now, it's a package deal."

He wasn't fine. Nobody in the room believed he was fine, least of all the voice that had gone very, very quiet somewhere behind his working eye since the vineyard, present but not talking, standing very still the way someone did when they didn't trust what would come out if they opened their mouth.

Willow finished the tape, pressed her palm briefly to his good cheek, a touch that didn't ask anything of him, and left him alone in the kitchen with a glass of water he wasn't going to drink and the low murmur of the rest of the house rebuilding itself around a version of tonight that had, somehow, other casualties to grieve too, other things that had gone wrong at other parts of the vineyard that he hadn't seen and wasn't ready yet to ask about.

The quiet stretched.

You still in there? Xander thought, eventually, when the silence had gone on long enough to start feeling less like respect and more like something worse. Or did you check out somewhere back at the third row?

Nothing, for a second. Then, low, unsteady in a way that didn't match anything Xander had heard from him yet: I'm here.

You don't sound here.

I know.

Another silence. Xander waited it out, because there wasn't much else to do with one working eye and a kitchen full of people carefully not looking at him too long, and eventually the voice started again, and it didn't sound like it was talking to him at all.

There was a room, Coulson said, or thought, the distinction had stopped mattering somewhere around the vineyard, early on. First real op I ran point on, low-level, nothing that should have mattered to anyone above me. I was twenty-six. There were four other agents in the debrief, all of them bigger than me, all of them with better files, the kind of men a room naturally arranges itself around without anyone deciding to. I didn't say much. I never said much in those rooms. Learned early that saying less and noticing more got you further than the other way around, though I don't think I understood yet why that was true, just that it worked.

Xander didn't say anything. Something in the rhythm of it told him not to, the way you didn't interrupt a man walking across a narrow ledge.

There was a discrepancy in the intel, Coulson went on. Small. A timestamp that didn't match a radio log by about ninety seconds, nothing anyone in that room had a reason to look for, because ninety seconds isn't the kind of thing four confident men go looking for when the bigger picture already looks solved. I looked for it. Not because I was smarter than anyone in that room. Because looking at small things nobody else was watching was the only tool I had, so I used it constantly, out of necessity more than talent, and eventually it stopped being a compensating habit and just became the thing I was actually good at.

What was the ninety seconds, Xander asked, quiet, mostly to keep the thread going, mostly because some part of him needed the story to keep moving more than he needed to understand exactly why.

A mole, Coulson said. Feeding a timestamp that was almost right. Almost is the whole game, in that line of work. Almost gets people killed just as dead as an obvious lie does, it just takes longer and costs more when it finally lands. I flagged it. Turned out to matter. Turned out to matter a great deal, actually, more than a twenty-six-year-old with no real file to speak of had any business being responsible for.

And Fury started calling you his Eye.

"Of course I did!" the voice from the gallery broke in, sudden and delighted, all the earlier quiet gone out of it at once, a man recognizing his own line coming back at him secondhand. "Best nickname I ever gave anybody. Still stand by it."

Half a joke, at first. Something in the telling shifted, warmer and more exposed at once, a man handing over something he hadn't planned on handing over. He has a habit of it, nicknames, most of them barbed enough to leave a mark. This one never quite turned into a title. Just something he said, now and then, when it was the two of us and nobody else needed to hear it. I never asked him to stop. I don't think I ever told anyone what it meant to me that he kept using it.

You're telling me now.

A pause, longer this time, and Xander got the distinct sense, even without a body to read, of a man realizing exactly what he'd just done.

I wasn't telling you anything, Coulson said, and there was something almost startled in it, the sound of a composed man catching himself mid-fall. I was talking. There's a difference. I don't usually do the second one out loud, or whatever the equivalent of out loud is, currently, for either of us.

Yeah, Xander said. I noticed.

He had. That was the thing nobody in the kitchen would have guessed, looking at him, one eye gone, half his face wrapped in gauze, cracking jokes that weren't landing because nobody had the heart to laugh at them tonight. He'd noticed exactly what had just happened, the crack in something that had, up until ninety seconds ago, presented itself as entirely, professionally composed. A man who talked for a living, who'd probably talked his way out of a hundred rooms that should have gone worse, had just, without meaning to, without deciding to, let something slip that he'd apparently been carrying quietly, unspoken, for the better part of two decades.

Losing the eye hadn't touched that part of him. If anything, it had sharpened it, the way exhaustion sometimes stripped everything down to the parts that were actually load-bearing. He'd have missed it a week ago, probably, too busy performing okay to actually watch for the thing underneath somebody else's performance of okay.

You didn't have to tell me that, Xander said, eventually, quiet, careful with it the way you were careful with something you'd caught somebody dropping by accident.

No, Coulson agreed. I didn't.

Why'd you do it, then.

There was a long pause before the answer came, long enough that Xander thought maybe there wasn't going to be one.

Because you're sitting in that chair with half your face gone and everyone in this house too scared to actually look at you, and I didn't have hands to hold, or a shoulder to offer, or one single useful thing I could physically do for you, Coulson said, finally, quiet, none of the earlier dryness left in it at all. This was the only thing I had. So I gave it to you. I'm not entirely sure I meant to. I'm not going to pretend that makes it less true.

"You know," the voice from the gallery said, quiet now too, none of the earlier delight left in it, "this is the reason I gave Coulson this job."

Down the hall, someone was crying, quiet and worn out, one of the newer Potentials being walked upstairs by a girl who'd survived the night herself and had somehow found the reserves left to comfort someone else. Xander listened to it fade toward the second floor, turning over what Coulson had just given him, the water glass sweating a ring onto the table, one working eye fixed on nothing in particular.

"Okay," he said, out loud, quiet enough that nobody else in the kitchen turned to look. "Okay. Noted."

It wasn't much of a response. It was, for that particular night, all either of them had left to give.
