Too Many Girls

By the fifth month, Xander wasn't traveling alone anymore.

"And here's where the premise finally runs into arithmetic," the voice from the gallery said. "Every found-family story hits this wall eventually. Turns out 'gather everyone who needs you' doesn't scale. Who could've predicted."

"Everyone," someone in the anonymous rows said. "Everyone could've predicted it."

It had happened gradually, the way most real changes did, one girl becoming two, two becoming a small, cautious cluster, each new arrival needing somewhere to actually go once she'd been found, steadied, and readied for whatever came next. He'd set up a kind of way station in the largest of the villages he worked, a compound loaned to him by a local family grateful for something he'd done for them early on, and it had filled, slowly and then not so slowly, until by the fifth month he had eleven girls under his informal care at once, ranging from barely-Called to nearly two months into learning what their new strength actually meant.

It should have felt like a victory. In a lot of ways it was one, eleven girls who might otherwise have been lost to fear, exploitation, or simple bad luck, all of them safer now than they'd been the day he found them.

It was also, Coulson pointed out one evening, watching the compound's evening routine unfold from the shade of the porch, a genuine, serious problem.

Eleven Slayers in one place, no formal security, minimal defenses, and a growing local reputation that anyone with an interest in finding them wouldn't need much effort to trace, he said, flat, professional, the exact register he'd used the night he'd first flagged the retreat-path gap Xander had ignored. I want to be direct about this, because I don't think you've fully clocked the shape of the risk yet. You've built something wonderful here. You've also built something that reads, to anyone thinking tactically, as a single, high-value target instead of eleven separate, harder-to-find ones.

Xander felt something cold settle in his stomach, recognizing, even before Coulson finished the thought, the shape of a mistake he'd made once before, at a house on Revello Drive, in a plan built entirely around holding rather than around what happened if holding failed.

You're saying this is the retreat-path thing again.

"OH," several voices in the anonymous rows said at once, a ragged, overlapping chorus. "The callback! The actual structural callback! Somebody get the frame back out, we're gonna need it for this one too."

"At this rate the wall's gonna run out of room before the book does."

I'm saying it's the same category of failure. Concentration without contingency. It worked while the group was small enough not to be worth anyone's notice. It stops working the moment the group becomes large enough to be worth someone's while.

How much time do we have?

I don't know precisely. I know it's not open-ended. A pause, careful. I also want to say, this isn't a criticism of what you've built. Every single decision that got you here was the right one, individually. Find the girl, bring her somewhere safe, repeat. Nobody planned for the aggregate. That's not a failure of judgment. It's just what happens when good instincts compound faster than the infrastructure around them.

Xander turned that over a long while, watching the compound's evening routine, girls training in loose pairs in the fading light, others cooking, others simply sitting together in the easy companionship of people who'd found something like family in the strangest possible circumstances. It was good. It was real. And it was, exactly as Coulson said, a problem waiting to happen.

What do we do, Xander asked, eventually, no longer resisting the read the way he might have, once, a lifetime ago, in a kitchen in Sunnydale arguing about whose version of seeing counted more.

"You call Giles, you idiot!" someone in the anonymous rows shouted, the exact pitch of an audience member who has lost all patience with a protagonist standing three feet from an obvious answer.

"He's going to. Give the scene a second to breathe."

"I've given it plenty of seconds! Call the man!"

We need somewhere organized to consolidate this, Coulson said. Somewhere with real defenses, real infrastructure, not an improvised compound in a village that was never built to hold this concentration of the exact power that draws exactly the wrong kind of attention.

The Council's been talking about Cleveland. Xander turned the thought over as it arrived, the shape of it settling into place with the same quiet certainty he'd felt outside the crypt, all those months ago, arriving at an answer that felt discovered rather than argued into existence. New Hellmouth. Real base of operations, actual defenses, actual coordination instead of everybody improvising in their own corner of the world.

That fits, Coulson agreed. A defensible position instead of an accumulating one. I'd support that recommendation.

Yeah. Xander watched the girls a while longer, already turning over, in his head, the practical shape of what moving eleven of them halfway across the world would actually require. Yeah, me too.

It wasn't a decision either of them reached alone, exactly, the way the best decisions between them rarely were anymore. It was the two of them looking at the same problem from two different angles, Coulson's trained operational instinct naming the risk plainly, Xander's own read on what the girls actually needed to feel safe rather than simply be safe, and landing, together, on an answer neither of them would have found quite as cleanly on their own.

He called Giles that same night, before he let himself second-guess any of it, the satellite phone's connection crackling through half a second of delay that made every exchange feel like talking across more distance than it actually was.

"It's late there," Giles said, by way of hello, already alert in the way of a man who'd learned, over the last year, that a call at this hour from Xander specifically usually meant something worth losing sleep over.

"Yeah, sorry. Couldn't wait till morning, this one's been sitting wrong in my chest all evening." Xander stepped out of earshot of the compound, the evening chorus of insects the only company for what he needed to say next. "I've got eleven Slayers in one place, Giles. Eleven, in a compound that used to be somebody's storage shed, no real security, and I just had it explained to me, in pretty stark terms, exactly how bad an idea that's about to become."

A pause, papers rustling faintly on the other end, the sound of a man already reaching for a pen before he'd finished processing the problem. "Eleven. Good Lord. I confess I hadn't tracked the number climbing quite that high."

"Neither had I, honestly, not till somebody made me actually add it up." Xander glanced back at the compound's lit windows, the ordinary evening business of thirty-some lives he'd have to explain this to eventually. "I need somewhere real to send them. Somewhere with actual walls, actual coordination, not me improvising security out of whatever a village happens to have lying around."

"I take it you have a specific somewhere in mind," Giles said, dry, already half a step ahead of him.

"Cleveland." Xander said it plain, no more circling around it. "New Hellmouth, actual infrastructure, and last I heard the Council's putting real money into building something permanent there anyway. Feels less like inventing a new plan and more like plugging into one that already exists."

Giles was quiet a moment, and Xander could hear, even across an ocean and a bad connection, the sound of a careful man actually weighing something rather than simply agreeing to be agreeable. "It's sound," Giles said, finally. "More than sound, honestly, it's rather overdue. We've been discussing consolidation in the abstract for weeks without anyone actually proposing a number to build toward. Eleven gives the conversation a concrete shape it's been lacking."

"So it's a yes."

"It's a yes, contingent on logistics neither of us can solve over a satellite phone at this hour. Housing, transport, integration with the Slayers already settling in. I'll need a fortnight, perhaps three weeks, to have something real in place rather than a promise." A pause, and something almost fond crept into his voice. "For what it's worth, Xander, I'm glad you called instead of simply managing this alone. That's not always been your instinct."

"Yeah, well." Xander watched the compound's lights a while longer, feeling something ease in his chest that had been sitting tight there since Coulson first named the problem. "Had a good teacher, somewhere along the way, about not carrying everything by myself. Guess it finally stuck."

A pause on the line, longer than the satellite delay could account for on its own. "For what it's worth," Giles said, quieter than before, none of the usual careful formality left in it, "I'm proud of the man you've become, son."

"...Hang on." The gallery's voice again, but changed now, something almost startled in it. "That's the first time that word's been used. Either direction. This whole book, this whole year, and that's the first time."

Nobody in the anonymous rows argued the point, or tried to follow it with a joke.

Xander didn't have anything ready for that either. It took him a moment to find his own voice again. "...Thanks, Giles. That actually means a lot."

"Aw," someone in the anonymous rows said, soft. "There's the moral of the episode. Somebody write that one down."

"Nobody's writing it down. It's not a fortune cookie, it's a phone call."

"It could be both."

They talked another twenty minutes after that, working through the rough shape of a plan neither of them would finish that night, and by the time Xander finally lowered the phone, the compound had gone quiet around him, thirty-some lives sleeping easy in a place that was, he now understood clearly, on borrowed time in the best possible way.

"There. Happy now?"

"Ecstatic."

He spent the next several weeks preparing the move, quiet, methodical work, securing transport, coordinating with the skeleton Council staff already setting up shop in Cleveland, breaking the news to eleven girls who'd only just started to feel settled that settled, for now, meant somewhere new again.

Most of them took it better than he expected. A few didn't, and he spent long evenings with those few, patient, unhurried, the same steady presence he'd been building his whole reputation on out here, helping them understand that this wasn't another uprooting, another loss, but a step toward something more permanent than anything he'd been able to offer them so far.

"We're not running," he told the last, most reluctant holdout, a fierce girl named Priya who'd been with him since nearly the beginning. "We're going somewhere that can actually hold all of us properly. Somewhere with walls that mean something and people who know what they're doing. This isn't the end of what we built here. It's the next part of it."

Priya studied him a long moment, scrutinizing him the way someone did when deciding whether to trust a promise, and finally, slowly, nodded.

"Okay," she said. "I trust you."

That's the whole job, isn't it, Coulson said, quiet, once she'd gone to pack. Not the tactics. Not the defenses. Just, somehow, earning that.

Yeah, Xander agreed, watching the compound begin its slow, deliberate dismantling around him, eleven lives about to be reorganized around a single, careful decision he'd made in partnership with a man who'd once been a stranger in his head and had somehow, over the better part of a year, become something closer to family. Yeah. That's the whole job.
