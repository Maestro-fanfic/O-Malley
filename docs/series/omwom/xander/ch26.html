Chapter 26 of 43

Giles

"Giles" (POV):

I'd been noticing things, small things, about the man young Alexander was becoming since the vineyard, longer, I suspect, than I let on to anyone, including myself. A certain steadiness that hadn't been there before. A way of reading a room that put me, uncomfortably, in mind of my own early training, the sort of attentiveness one is drilled into rather than born with. I told myself, for some time, that grief and injury simply matured people faster than the ordinary run of years managed on its own, and left it there, because it was easier than asking the question underneath it.

Buffy answered that question for me, in the end, in a motel parking lot outside Barstow. I confess I did not sleep especially well the two nights that followed. Not from fear, I should say, though there was certainly some of that too, but from the restlessness of a man who has just been handed an entirely new category of phenomenon to understand and cannot, for the life of him, stop turning it over.

By the second day I had run out of patience with my own restraint.

"Hang on," someone in the anonymous rows said, sitting up. "Is this the whole chapter? Are we doing this for the whole chapter?"

"Apparently so," Fury's voice said, and there was something almost approving in it. "Watchers earn that much, once in a while. Don't get used to it."

I found him two days after the motel conversation with Buffy, tracking him down in the small, cramped room he'd been sharing with three other survivors, a stack of borrowed notebooks tucked under one arm and, I have no doubt, the barely-contained energy of a man who had finally found something new worth studying. I am aware of how I must have looked, arriving like that. I did not, in the moment, much care.

"I hope this isn't an imposition," I said, settling into the room's single rickety chair without quite waiting for permission. "I have questions. A great many of them, if you're willing. I promise to stop the moment it becomes uncomfortable for either of you."

"I've shouted at this screen plenty," muttered someone from the anonymous rows, unrepentant.

Xander managed a tired laugh. "Straight to the research, huh? No easing into it?"

"I find easing into things rarely accomplishes what people hope it will." I opened the top notebook, pen already in hand. "Might I begin with the mechanics? You mentioned he can, under certain circumstances, take physical control. I'd like to understand the actual boundaries of that arrangement, if you're comfortable discussing it."

Something crossed his face just then, brief, inward, a stillness I recognized as a man listening to a voice I couldn't hear, and I found myself absurdly curious what was being said about me in that half-second, whether I'd just been catalogued in turn.

"He can't take control without me choosing to lend it," Xander said, out loud, once whatever had passed between them had passed. "It's happened twice. Once during a fight I couldn't have won on my own skill, once during the battle at the school, briefly. Both times I made the call. Both times I could've taken it back whenever I wanted."

"Fascinating." I wrote it down, quick and precise. "And the sensory experience. Do you share pain? Emotion? I understand from Buffy's account that the connection seems to run rather deep."

"All three eventually. It wasn't like that at first. Took time to, I don't know, wear the walls down between us." He seemed, if anything, surprisingly comfortable explaining it, now that the first, hardest reveal was behind him. "Sensation was immediate, actually, right from the start. The emotion and memory stuff came slower."

"And this began at the vineyard. The same incident that cost you your eye."

"Same day. Same hour, actually. Kind of a package deal, losing the eye and gaining the roommate."

"'Package deal,'" someone in the anonymous rows repeated, wincing. "Worst two-for-one in the history of retail."

"Free shipping, though."

"That is not helping."

My pen stilled for a moment. I hadn't meant to let anything show, and I suspect I failed at it. "That must have been extraordinarily difficult. To lose something so significant and gain something so strange, in the same breath, with no time to process either separately."

"Yeah." Something in him seemed to loosen, hearing it put that plainly. "Yeah, it kind of was."

He went quiet again, that same brief inward stillness, and this time I didn't bother pretending I hadn't noticed it. I have spent a great many years watching people listen to things I couldn't hear, gods and demons and the occasional overzealous ancestor, and I flatter myself that I've gotten reasonably good at reading the shape of a conversation from only one half of it.

"One more question, if you'll indulge an old man's curiosity," I said, flipping to a fresh page. "Does he dream? Or whatever the equivalent might be, for a consciousness in his circumstance."

"Yeah, actually. First few weeks, mostly about his old life. Missions, people he worked with. Lately, though, they've kind of started blending with mine. Weird crossover dreams. Had one last week where he was trying to brief Giles-from-my-childhood-memories about grappling rig paperwork, except there was also a lizard running around on the ceiling the whole time, grinning at everybody, and nobody in the dream thought that part was worth mentioning. Genuinely bizarre."

"Nobody's going to ask about the lizard?" someone in the anonymous rows said, plaintive. "We're just letting the lizard go?"

"We're letting the lizard go."

I wrote that down with, I'll admit, real and rather undignified delight. "Fascinating. The psychological literature on shared consciousness is, of course, entirely theoretical where humans are concerned, but if dream content is beginning to blend, that suggests the boundary between you is genuinely permeable, not merely cohabitational."

"You're enjoying this way too much."

"I am enjoying this precisely the correct amount," I said, entirely serious, and he laughed, real and unguarded, the tension of the whole strange interview finally, fully breaking.

"'Precisely the correct amount,'" the gallery's voice repeated, delighted. "Best delivery in the whole book. Somebody give this man a line reading award."

"He'd want it in a very specific font. Nothing gaudy."

"Times New Roman. Twelve point. I'd bet the beverage machine on it."

I asked a dozen more questions over the next hour, methodical, careful, occasionally pausing to make sure he was still comfortable before pressing further, and somewhere in the middle of it, the conversation shifted from interrogation into something closer to genuine conversation, two people working through something strange and new together rather than one simply extracting information from the other. I had not expected that part. I am glad, in retrospect, that it happened.

"I want to say something, if I may," I said, finally, closing the notebook. "I've watched you this past year, Xander, watched you become someone considerably more than this group ever properly credited you for being. I don't think that transformation began with Agent Coulson's arrival. I think it began well before that, and I think his presence has simply given you a mirror for something that was already true."

"That's a nice thing to say."

"It's not merely nice. It's accurate, and I think accuracy matters rather more than niceness, generally speaking." I managed, I hope, something like a genuine smile. "Whatever this partnership turns out to be, long-term, I want you to know you have my full support. Not merely my tolerance. My support."

Something moved across his face, hearing it, unguarded in a way I don't think he intended to let me see. "Thanks, Giles. That actually means a lot."

"Good." I stood, tucking my notebooks back under my arm. "Now. I don't suppose Agent Coulson would be willing to submit to a follow-up conversation sometime this week? I have considerably more questions about the SHIELD organizational structure than I've had opportunity to ask, and I confess a professional Watcher's curiosity about how another world's intelligence apparatus handles supernatural contingencies."

Xander went still again, that same brief listening quiet, and this time I found I didn't mind not knowing what was being said, not entirely. There was something rather touching about a conversation happening a few inches from me that I would never be fully privy to, two men working out, in a language I couldn't hear, whether a third man was worth trusting with more of their story.

"He says he'd be happy to," Xander told me, and whatever expression I made in response, I did not attempt to disguise it. I have been called many things over the years. A scholar's delight has never, to my knowledge, been mistaken for anything else.
