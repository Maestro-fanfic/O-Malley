Chapter 5 of 43

Explain That

The house went quiet somewhere around two in the morning, the way a place got quiet when too many people crammed into too few rooms finally ran out of adrenaline all at once. Sleeping bags covered most of the living room floor, a tangle of exhausted, grieving girls who'd fought a war today and would be expected to get up and maybe fight another piece of it tomorrow, and somewhere above all of it, in the small room he still technically shared with nobody because nobody had wanted to make him share it tonight, Xander lay on his back in the dark.

The ceiling swam slightly whenever he turned his head too fast. He was still relearning, minute by minute, how far away the corners of a room actually were, some old, confident map of space folding itself down to half its usual reliability and refusing, so far, to redraw itself into anything he trusted.

He wasn't tired. He was fairly sure he wasn't going to be tired again for a long time, in the way that mattered, the kind of tired sleep actually fixed.

"There it is again," said the voice from the gallery, mild, unhurried, the tone of a man who had, in fact, been keeping count. "'The kind of.' Third one tonight. I'd almost think somebody back home has a favorite scaffold."

A pause, considering, and then, dryly: "Someone fetch the beverage. This one's earned."

Somewhere behind him, an anonymous watcher made a small, satisfied noise, a running joke finally getting its due, and nothing further was said about it, though this narrator notes, for the record, that nothing further being said about it did not mean nothing further was going to happen about it.

You awake, he thought, into the quiet, not really expecting silence to be the answer.

I don't sleep the way you do. I'm not sure I sleep at all, currently. A pause. So, yes.

"'I'm not sure I sleep at all,'" someone in the anonymous rows repeated, delighted. "Buy this man a nightlight."

Good. So, you gonna explain that?

Explain what.

The kitchen. The whole "twenty-six years old, ninety seconds, my Eye" thing. Xander laced his hands behind his head, stared at the ceiling that kept insisting on being further away than it was. You don't strike me as a guy who just hands that kind of thing out. And I don't think you meant to hand it to me either. So. Explain it.

"There it is," someone murmured. "Kid's better at this than the professional. Write that down."

There was a real pause this time, long enough that Xander started to wonder if the answer was just going to be silence again, the professional retreat he'd half expected the first time and hadn't gotten.

I don't have a clean answer, Coulson said, finally, and it sounded like the truth, unpolished in a way his earlier telling hadn't quite been. I told you because you were sitting there with half your face gone and I had nothing else to give you, and somewhere in the middle of not having anything else to give, that came out instead. I've turned it over a dozen times in the last few hours trying to find the version where I meant to do that deliberately, and I can't. It just happened.

You've had feelings leak out of you before. Rooms of professional liars. I bet you're better at holding the line than that.

I am, Coulson agreed. Considerably better, as a rule.

"Modest," someone snorted.

"He's not wrong, though," another voice put in. "Twenty years. Give the man his due."

"I'm just saying, 'considerably better, as a rule' is a very funny thing to say two seconds after admitting you cried in a kitchen."

"He didn't cry."

"He might as well have."

So why not this time.

Another pause, shorter. I don't know yet. I'd like to, for what it's worth. I don't enjoy not knowing my own reasons.

Xander turned that over, watching the ceiling do its slow, dishonest drift. It would have been easier, somehow, if the answer had come out smooth, some line about strategy or bonding or whatever a trained professional was supposed to say to a scared kid with a bandage over half his face. The not-knowing was harder to argue with. He didn't have a joke built for not-knowing.

Here's the thing, Xander said, eventually, and felt the shape of the real thing arriving before he'd fully decided to say it. You told me that story like it was supposed to mean something to me. Like, hey, look, we're the same, huh? Guy in the room nobody's watching, guy who notices the ninety seconds nobody else looks for. His jaw tightened, and it wasn't the bandage causing it. I'm not a spy. I'm the guy who carries bags. There's a difference.

"Ohh, he's mad," someone in the anonymous rows said, with real relish. "I love it when he's mad."

Is there.

"Two words. Devastating. This narrator's seen entire arguments lose to a well-placed 'is there.'"

Yeah, there's a difference. He heard his own voice come out sharper than he meant it, didn't soften it. You've got training. An organization. Twenty years of somebody paying you to get good at noticing things, backing you up when you're right, catching you when you're wrong. I've got nothing behind me. No file. No handler. No org chart. Just, whatever I managed to figure out on my own, mostly by almost dying a bunch of times and paying really close attention to what almost killed me. That's not the same skill wearing a different uniform. That's a completely different thing that happens to look similar from a distance.

"Somebody give the boy a monologue trophy."

"Shh, he's not done being wrong yet."

I don't think it's as different as you want it to be.

You don't get a vote on that. You've known me for, what, twelve hours?

Fair, Coulson said, and didn't push further, didn't argue, didn't do the thing Xander had half-braced for, the smooth professional reframe that would make the objection sound smaller than it was. I'll grant you the twelve hours. I'm not going to pretend I've earned the right to tell you what your own life is made of.

"He folded," someone in the anonymous rows said, almost offended on Coulson's behalf. "Twenty years of training and the man just folded."

"That's not folding. That's strategy. Watch."

But you don't actually agree with me.

"Called it," said the same voice that had called the strategy a beat earlier, smug about it.

No, Coulson said, plainly. I don't. I think you built something real out of nothing, which is harder than what I did, not easier, and I think some part of you needs it to be a lesser thing so you don't have to carry what it would mean if it wasn't. I'm not going to argue you out of that tonight. I don't think arguing would work, and I don't think tonight's the night to try.

"Oof," several voices said, in ragged, overlapping unison.

"That's not a comeback. That's a diagnosis."

"Somebody check on the kid."

Xander didn't have a comeback for that either. He lay there a while longer, letting the ceiling keep its slow, unconvincing drift, letting the silence sit instead of filling it with something clever, and found, somewhere under the resentment, that the not-arguing bothered him more than an actual argument would have.

You've done this before, Xander said, eventually, turning it a different direction. Talked somebody down off a ledge they didn't know they were standing on. That's a whole skill set, isn't it. Reading somebody, knowing exactly how hard to push and when to back off.

It is.

So which is this. You reading me, or you actually meaning it.

Both, I think, Coulson said, unhurried. I don't consider those mutually exclusive. I read people for a living because it matters that I get them right, not because getting them right lets me manage them into whatever I've already decided they should think. If I only meant the reading and not the substance underneath it, I don't think it would have cost me anything to say it in the kitchen. It did cost me something. That's usually how I tell the difference myself.

Xander let that sit, unwilling to grant it outright, unwilling, too, to argue it away.

You're not gonna let it go, though, Xander said, eventually. Not quite a question.

No, Coulson agreed. I'm not.

Great. Xander closed his working eye, felt the exhaustion finally start pulling at the edges of him, the real kind, the kind that might actually work tonight. Can't wait for round two.

Get some sleep, Xander.

Bossy for a guy who's been in my head half a day.

I contain multitudes.

"Hey!" someone in the anonymous rows said, sitting bolt upright. "That's Xander's line!"

"It's Whitman's line, technically," another voice corrected, prim about it.

"It's whoever says it best's line, and right now that's not the guy who's supposed to be quoting poetry at two in the morning."

"...He's picking it up already, isn't he," the gallery's voice said, slow, something almost fond creeping into the observation. "That's not a bit. That's actual bleed. Somebody make a note."

Nobody made a note, out loud, but this narrator did.

Xander almost laughed at that, the first almost-laugh since the vineyard, small and involuntary and gone as fast as it came, and somewhere behind his ribs, in the hollow place where a fight he hadn't fully finished having was still quietly refusing to resolve itself, he understood without being able to say why that this argument, unlike most of the arguments in his life, wasn't going to end with either of them winning.

He fell asleep somewhere in the middle of trying to figure out whether that thought was supposed to be comforting.
