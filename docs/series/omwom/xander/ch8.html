Chapter 8 of 43

Empty Places

"Buffy." Anya's voice climbed before anyone had decided the conversation was actually happening yet, sharp enough to cut through the low hum of the living room and pull every eye in the house toward her. "I want to talk about the vineyard."

"Okay." Buffy set down the supply clipboard, careful, unhurried, a calm that plainly cost her real effort to hold. "Talk."

"People died," Anya said, not shouting, worse than shouting, flat and furious in a way that filled the room without needing volume. "People we brought with us died because of a plan that didn't work, and I would like someone to explain to me why we keep trusting the person whose plans keep not working."

"There's no trope name for this one," Fury's voice said, quiet, the joking entirely gone out of it. "Fear doing this to a room isn't a genre convention. It's just what fear does. Let's watch."

Nobody in the anonymous rows said anything further. This narrator, for once, had nothing clever to add either.

"That's not fair," Willow said, too fast, the reflexive defense of someone who hadn't finished deciding whether she believed it.

"Isn't it?" Anya's eyes found Buffy across the room. "Isn't it exactly fair? You said Caleb would be manageable. You said the numbers worked. Half of us didn't come back from that vineyard, Buffy, and Xander's missing an eye, and I would like, just once, for someone to say the word wrong out loud instead of pretending we're all still fine with the plan just because the plan came from you."

Xander felt his own mouth open, felt the shape of a defense already forming, and felt it die there, unfinished, because the terrible thing under Anya's fury wasn't that she was being unfair. It was that she wasn't, not entirely, not in the way that would have made this easy to argue against.

Say something, Coulson said, low, urgent, the first real push he'd offered since the vineyard. You know this isn't the whole truth. You were there. Tell them what actually happened.

And say what, Xander thought back, and heard the bitterness in his own head before he'd decided to let it show. That I built the plan too? That I'm the one who told her the vineyard timing would work? Doesn't exactly help her case, buddy.

It's still the truth.

The truth doesn't always help.

Around the room, other voices were rising, a Potential Xander only half-recognized asking, quiet but audible, whether anyone actually knew what tonight's plan even was, whether there was a plan at all beyond hoping the last one hadn't taught them anything. Another girl near the stairs was crying, silent, the exhaustion of someone who'd used up everything she had just getting through the last week and had nothing left over for whatever came next.

"I'm not saying it to be cruel," Anya went on, quieter now, which was somehow worse. "I'm saying it because somebody has to, and because I watched two girls I didn't even know the last names of get carried out of that vineyard, and I don't think either of them would want us pretending everything's fine just because pretending is more comfortable than admitting we're scared."

"I'm not asking anyone to pretend," Buffy said, and her voice had gone very steady, very careful, holding a great deal very still to keep it that way. "I know it went wrong. I know people died. I don't have a version of tonight where that isn't true."

"Then what do we do differently?" Anya pressed. "Because I don't think you actually know. I think you're going to tell us to trust you again, and I don't think trust is a plan."

Giles closed his book, finally, set it aside, and said something careful and measured about process, about reconvening, about not making decisions in the heat of grief, and it didn't land, not really, the room too far gone into its own fear to be steered by careful measured words from a man everyone respected but nobody, right now, was in the mood to be managed by.

It was Faith's voice that actually cut through, low and certain in a way that carried further than Anya's fury had.

"Maybe it's time somebody else calls it for a while."

The room went still the way rooms did right before something irreversible happened.

Buffy didn't argue. That was, Xander thought later, the worst part, the part that stayed with him longest, that she didn't fight it, didn't defend herself, just stood there absorbing it with the flat, exhausted acceptance of someone who had already, privately, wondered the same thing about herself. Her eyes moved once around the room, slow, cataloging every face that wouldn't quite meet hers, and something in her posture settled, resigned, before anyone had actually finished deciding anything.

Nobody voted. Nobody needed to. The room simply, quietly, reorganized itself around a different center of gravity, glances that used to find her automatically now finding Faith instead, half a decision made by thirty small, silent recalibrations rather than one loud one.

"Okay," Buffy said, finally, quiet. "Okay."

She went upstairs without another word. Xander watched her go, watched the set of her shoulders, straight, controlled, determined not to let the room see anything more than she'd already given it, and felt the useless, cowardly weight of his own silence settle deeper into his chest with every step she took.

Twenty minutes later she came back down with a bag over her shoulder and nowhere in particular she'd said she was going, and nobody stopped her, and the door closed behind her with a sound that felt bigger than a door should.

Xander hadn't said one word in her defense the entire time.

I saw this exact shape before, Coulson said, quiet, into the silence that followed, and it wasn't advice, wasn't a push, just a man annotating something he recognized in his bones. Chain of command breaking down under pressure. Fear finding the nearest available target and calling it a decision. I've watched rooms do this to good commanding officers more times than I'd like to count. It never looks like malice while it's happening. It just looks like relief that someone finally said the thing everyone was already thinking.

You gonna tell me that makes it okay?

No, Coulson said. I'm telling you I recognize it. Recognizing it and approving of it aren't the same thing, and for what it's worth, I don't think I approve of much that just happened in that room. A pause, careful. I also think you're allowed to have not known what to say. That's not the same as having done nothing wrong. But it's also not nothing.

Around him, the house was already reorganizing itself, Faith accepting a question from one of the Potentials with the easy, unbothered confidence of someone stepping into a role she'd half-expected to land in eventually, Giles retreating to his books, still in the way of a man deciding whether to fight a losing battle tonight or save his strength for tomorrow, Willow disappearing toward the kitchen with her arms wrapped around herself.

Xander sat where he was for a long while, not moving, the coffee mug gone cold and forgotten in his hands, watching the shape of the house rearrange itself around an absence that had, until twenty minutes ago, been the thing holding all of it together.

Nobody in the anonymous rows had said a single word since Faith's line about somebody else calling it. This narrator checked, twice, in case a joke had been sitting there unnoticed. There wasn't one. Some scenes just don't have one in them.
