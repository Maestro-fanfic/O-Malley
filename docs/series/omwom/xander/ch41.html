The Poncho

He found her by asking, mostly, three phone calls and a fourth that finally landed somewhere useful, a Council contact who'd heard from somebody who'd heard from somebody that a woman matching Faith's description had set up something like a halfway house on the edge of a town in Ohio too small to have its own zip code worth remembering, taking in girls the Network hadn't gotten to yet and a few it never would have known to look for.

You didn't have to do this, Coulson said, watching the flat, gray Midwestern sky roll by outside a rental car's windshield, rain coming down steady enough that the wipers were losing the argument.

Yeah, I did. Xander kept his eyes on the road, the last turn coming up according to directions scrawled on a gas station receipt. She called me first. Out of everybody in her whole life, she called me. Doesn't seem like the kind of thing you follow up with a fruit basket.

"A fruit basket," someone in the anonymous rows repeated, delighted. "This narrator would pay good money to see that scene instead."

"Don't give the author ideas."

The house, when he found it, was exactly what he'd have pictured if anyone had asked him to picture Faith running a halfway house: sturdy, unglamorous, a porch light on in the middle of the afternoon because the storm had turned the sky the color of old bruises, and out in the yard, in the rain, without so much as a jacket that made sense, a lone figure hauling a fallen branch off a fence line before it could take the fence down with it.

She'd found some old thing to throw on against the weather, too big for her, more hole than fabric in a couple places, and she moved through the mud and the wet with the same flat, competent economy she brought to everything, dragging a branch twice her size like it had personally offended her.

Xander sat in the car a second longer than he needed to, just looking. Not at the branch. At the jacket, if you could call it that, soaked through and doing nothing for her, the kind of thing a person wore because it was what was hanging by the door and not because anybody had ever thought to ask what she'd actually want.

You're doing the math, Coulson observed, quiet.

Yeah. Give me a second.

He got out into the rain without an umbrella, jogged back to the trunk, and came back with the poncho he'd grabbed on impulse from a gas station two exits back, cheap and olive drab and about the least romantic object he'd ever bought anybody, six dollars, no ceremony to it at all.

"Hey!" he called out, over the rain, before he'd fully thought through what he was going to say. "You're gonna catch pneumonia in that. Here."

Faith's head came up fast, branch still in both hands, wariness flashing across her face before she placed him, and something in her expression cracked open into surprise as he crossed the yard and just handed it over, no speech attached, no angle in it anywhere.

She looked at it a second too long.

"What's this."

"A poncho. You looked cold. That thing you're wearing's basically decorative at this point." He shrugged, already soaked through himself, already not caring. "Figured somebody should do something about it before you actually got sick."

"There's no punchline coming," Fury's voice said, quiet, almost surprised by it. "Watch. He's not gonna make this a bit."

"Good. Some things shouldn't be a bit."

Faith turned it over in her hands, studying it like she was waiting for the part where it turned out to be about something else, where it turned out to cost her something later. It didn't happen. There wasn't a version of this where it was going to happen.

"Nobody buys me stuff that isn't about them," she said, finally, quiet, pulling the old ruined jacket off and shrugging into the new one instead. "Not once, in my whole life. You didn't even think about it, did you. Just saw I was cold."

"Wasn't thinking about anything except you standing out here soaked to the bone hauling a tree around by yourself." Xander spread his hands, rain running down both their faces. "Didn't seem like it needed to be more complicated than that."

"Clint," someone in the anonymous rows said, delighted, unable to help themselves any longer. "Somebody has to say it. Look at her. She's got the whole squint going now."

"You look like a tiny, damp Clint Eastwood," Xander said, grinning despite himself, despite the rain, despite everything. "All you're missing's a cigarillo and a reason to shoot somebody."

Faith let out a laugh, startled loose before she'd decided to allow it, and dropped the branch where she stood. "Clint. Clint who."

"Man with No Name. You're doing the whole gunslinger thing, whether you meant to or not." He spread his hands, already soaked through, already not caring. "Figured somebody should say it before you started billing yourself as one full time."

"Hey!" a new voice said, indignant, from somewhere deep in the anonymous rows. "That's my poncho!"

"Nobody asked you, Blondie."

"That's not even my nickname, that's a DIFFERENT..." The voice gave up, muttering something about royalties and squinting into the middle distance, and did not get another word in for the rest of the scene.

"You drove all this way to buy me rain gear and make fun of it in the same breath," Faith said, arms crossed now, the poncho already doing its job better than whatever she'd had on before.

"I drove all this way 'cause you called me at two in the morning from a bus station and I never actually got to see for myself that you landed on your feet." He looked at her, really looked, past the poncho and the mud and the branch still lying in the wet grass between them. "Looks like you did, though. This place is yours?"

"Mine. Bought it cheap, fixed most of it myself, take in whoever the Network's too slow or too far to catch first." Something proud and a little defensive moved across her face, daring him to make it smaller than it was. "It's not Cleveland. It's not gonna be Cleveland. But it's mine, and it works, and nobody in it needed a résumé or a good reason to be scared."

"It's not smaller than Cleveland," Xander said, simple, meaning it. "It's just yours instead of somebody else's. Different thing entirely."

Faith studied him a long moment, rain running down both their faces, and something in her shoulders came down half an inch, the wariness she'd been carrying since a study door and a raised hand finally finding somewhere safe enough to set itself down.

She's still waiting for the catch, Coulson said, quiet. Even now. It's going to take more than one gesture to unteach that.

Yeah. I know. Figure it's a start, though.

"You gonna stand out here in the rain being sincere at me all day, or you wanna come inside before we both catch something?"

"Depends. Is 'something' code for a home-cooked meal, or am I gonna regret asking?"

"Guess you'll find out." She picked the branch back up on her way past him, hauling it toward the fence line without breaking stride, and called back over her shoulder, not quite hiding the smile in it. "Clint doesn't get to skip chores, by the way. New house rule."

"THERE IT IS," a voice bellowed, somewhere deep in the anonymous rows, on its feet so fast the sound of it nearly drowned out the rain. "THERE'S THE REFERENCE! I KNEW I RECOGNIZED IT FROM SOMEWHERE!"

Footsteps, heavy and deliberate, stomped their way out of the gallery entirely. A door, somewhere this narrator has no name for, slammed hard enough to rattle whatever passed for the rafters.

Nobody went after him. Nobody explained what he meant, either.

"Figures," Xander said, and grabbed the other end of the branch, and followed her in.

"There it is," Fury's voice said, quiet, once the two of them had gone, the rain the only sound left in the empty yard. "That's the one she's gonna keep. Not the branch. Not even the joke. Six dollars off a gas station rack, bought because it was raining and nothing else."

Nobody in the anonymous rows argued the point. This narrator, for what it's worth, doesn't intend to either.
