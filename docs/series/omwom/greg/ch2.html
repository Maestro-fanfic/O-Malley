The Waiting Room

The silence lasted exactly as long as it took Greg to realize he was standing up.

That was wrong. He'd been falling. He'd been falling and there'd been weight and noise and an absolute certainty that things were about to stop being okay in a way nothing afterward was going to fix, and now there was solid ground under his sneakers, real and firm and entirely too solid for a guy who'd just watched dogs finish what a forum post started, and he was standing on it, breathing, which he was fairly sure he shouldn't still be able to do.

"Okay," he said out loud, to nobody, because saying things out loud had always been how he figured out what he thought, a habit that had gotten him laughed at more times than he could count and had never once stopped being useful to him personally. "Okay, that's, that's a thing that's happening. I'm dead. I'm definitely dead, this is the dead thing, this is what dead looks like, and it looks like..."

He looked around and the sentence died on its own.

It looked like a desert.

Not his desert, obviously. Greg had never been anywhere that qualified as one. Dust gave under his sneakers, fine and pale, and low tents rose from it on dark wooden frames. The marks on their banners almost became readable when he looked past them, then slid sideways the instant he tried to focus. A cookfire burned in a ring of stones without any wood he could see. Above it all hung too many stars, none where his brain expected them, and two moons close enough together that for one disorienting second he tried to read them as the same moon twice.

He took a step. His sneaker sank into the dust with a real, dumb, physical give, and that somehow scared him more than anything so far. Dead people's shoes didn't sink into sand. He was fairly sure of that. He wasn't sure of much else.

There was a horizon, or the idea of one, a line where the pale dust met the too-full sky, and when Greg made himself walk toward it there was nothing beyond it at all. Not darkness, exactly. Absence, the kind that made his eyes want to slide off it the same way they slid off the banners' half-formed marks. He reached a hand out toward it anyway and the air there was warm, which was somehow the worst detail of all, warm like real sun on a real body, in a place that plainly had no sun to give it.

He turned back around and Mr. Gladly hadn't been there a second ago.

"Mr. Gladly?"

Because it was, somehow, impossibly, standing incongruous against a war camp's dust in the same rumpled button-down he always wore like he'd bought it because a magazine told him teachers dressed that way to seem relatable, arms crossed, weight rocked back on his heels the exact way he stood right before he tried to get a class to see themselves in whatever chapter they were supposed to have read and hadn't. Except Mr. Gladly did not teach Greg's fourth period anything, ever, outside of an actual school, and Greg was extremely sure Mr. Gladly did not know his name. He'd have bet on it. He'd have bet on it right up until about four seconds ago.

"Well," Mr. Gladly said, and Greg's whole body went cold at the sound of it, mild, almost amused, carrying the particular disappointment of a teacher pitched for a kid who'd shown up to class without his homework. "That didn't turn out like you thought it would, did it?"

Something crossed Mr. Gladly's face on the silence that followed, something Greg had genuinely never seen there before, not once, not in a year and a half of half-listening from the third row. A scowl. Real, sharp, the look of a man biting down hard on something too large for the face currently holding it.

And then the face came apart entirely, not violently, not even quickly, more the way a held breath finally lets go, the rumpled button-down and eager slouch dissolving out of the space they'd occupied like a photograph left too long in the sun, and where a homeroom teacher had been standing there was, for one impossible stretched second, nothing at all, and then there was someone else.

Older. Broader through the shoulders than a man had any right to be at whatever age this one actually was. Skin marked with a pattern Greg's eyes kept sliding off exactly the way they'd slid off the banners, a weight in the stillness of him that made the desert around them feel less like scenery and more like the only setting that had ever actually fit.

Greg's whole body went very, very still.

"It has been brought to my attention," the new voice said, unhurried, carrying none of Gladly's brightness and none of his apology either, a voice built for open ground and long silences rather than fourth-period homerooms, "that you arrive already spilling everything you feel onto whoever is standing nearest you. That is not a flaw I intend to shame you for. It is, however, a flaw that will get you killed a second time, having only just now finished being killed the first. You require discipline, boy. Not to silence what you feel. To aim it."

Greg's mouth opened. Nothing resembling words came out of it.

"It has also been brought to my attention," the stranger went on, entirely without missing a beat, the same flat, ancient gravity carrying every syllable of it exactly as evenly as everything that had come before, "that there is someone, elsewhere, who desperately needs to get the stick out of his butt."

Cut to the gallery.

FURY: Still can't believe he actually said that to a kid.

GLADLY: It needed saying!

FURY: It did. Doesn't mean it wasn't weird.

✳

"Who," Greg finally managed, both hands coming up like he could physically hold the desert still long enough to get a sentence out, "are you, is this actually happening, are you even real, is that a joke about a stick, do I know a guy with a stick, I genuinely don't think I know anyone with a..."

"Mr. Veder."

Something in the way he said it, flat, final, cut through the spiral fast enough that Greg actually stopped, mouth still half open around whatever had been coming next.

"There is not a great deal of time," the stranger said. His patience looked older than the face carrying it. "You are not the only one in need of a lesson tonight. Try, if you can manage it, not to be the reason the other student fails his."

"What other stud..."

The sentence never finished. Neither did the desert.
