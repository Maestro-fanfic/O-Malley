Chapter 1: What Gets Him Killed



Greg had rehearsed this conversation nine different ways on the walk over, and every single version of it ended with somebody finally admitting he'd been right.

Version one had him cool about it, arms crossed, barely looking up from whatever he was doing when she showed. Version four had actual dialogue, back-and-forth, the kind of exchange where he got off a line good enough that she almost smiled before catching herself. Version seven was the one he liked best and trusted least, the one where she didn't threaten him at all, where she just looked at him a beat too long and said something like huh, and in his head that single syllable did more work than any speech could have. He knew, in the part of his brain that occasionally got a word in edgewise, that real conversations didn't go the way you rehearsed them. He'd had enough of those, with enough people, to know better. He kept rehearsing anyway.

* * *

*Cut to the gallery.*

**GLADLY:** Oh, I love this part. Nine drafts and none of them are good.

**THE WATCHER:** Everybody rehearses the version where they win. Nobody rehearses the version where they don't even get to finish the sentence.

**FURY:** You're gonna feel a lot less for him in about ten minutes.

* * *

That was the part he kept coming back to, walking. Not the danger, though he'd thought about that too, in the abstract, the way you think about a highway on-ramp you've never actually merged onto, a thing that was real and bad for other people and would obviously sort itself out fine once it was actually his turn. The part that kept his feet moving down Ellsworth past the last working streetlight, past the shuttered nail salon and the lot where a gas station used to be, was the ending. The one where Bitch, or somebody who mattered, somebody who'd tell somebody else, looked at him and went, huh. Guess Veder wasn't just talk.

He had it narrowed down. Three blocks, maybe four, of half-collapsed chain-link and gravel lots gone to weeds, warehouse roofs sagging in the middle like they'd given up holding a shape. Somewhere behind one of these fences was where she kept them between jobs. He'd cross-referenced sightings, three separate threads that each thought they had the only good lead, none of them noticing the others said almost the same thing from a different angle. He'd read every one of them twice, the real ones, not the joke threads people made about him for caring this much, the ones where somebody screenshotted his last post and somebody else added a laughing emoji and that was the whole reply. He was close, with the specific, itchy certainty of a guy who already knows he's right and just needs the world to catch up. He could feel how close he was the way you feel a test answer sitting right on the tip of your tongue, so close that stopping now would have been its own kind of failure, worse somehow than never trying.

Nobody else thought this was a good idea. His mom hadn't said anything about it, because his mom never said anything about anything he did anymore, one way or the other, which was its own kind of answer if he was being honest with himself, and he generally wasn't, not about that, not tonight. His dad would have called it exactly the kind of stunt that proved Greg couldn't be trusted with anything that mattered, and then, somehow, in a way Greg had never once managed to argue his way out of, made it Greg's fault that his dad had to say so. And the two people online who'd actually bothered to reply to his plan, out of a whole thread's worth of silence otherwise, had both said, in slightly different words, dude, don't. One of them had typed out three separate paragraphs about it, actually cared enough to make an argument instead of just a joke, and Greg had skimmed maybe half of it before deciding the guy just didn't get it.

He'd told them that, more or less. They didn't get it. What none of them got was that this wasn't really about Bitch at all. It was about being the guy who found her. The guy who wasn't scared of the scary cape, who walked right up and proved, once, to somebody who'd remember it, that he'd earned a seat at whatever table these people all seemed to have and never once thought to pull a chair out for him.

The fence line ended in a gap somebody had bent wide open a long time ago, rust-bled and sagging on its hinge, and beyond it a lot full of rusted machinery, a stack of pallets gone gray and soft with weather, and one low, dark building that didn't look like anything from the street. No lights. No sound he could place, not yet, though the night was holding its breath in a way that made him certain, the way certainty always feels right before it turns out wrong, that he had the right place.

He almost turned around there. He'd remember that later, or he would have, if there'd been a later that worked the way laters were supposed to, that one real flicker of a thought that maybe this was exactly the kind of thing the two guys online had meant. He looked at it for a second too long, the dark building sitting there with the stillness of a place that's pretending not to be occupied, and something old and sensible in him said, quietly, you could just go home. Nobody would even know you got this close. He could still tell himself that part, at least. That he'd gotten close.

He didn't go home. He found, a few feet inside the gap, an empty sack of dog food gone soft with rain, the plastic torn open and licked clean, and something about the ordinariness of it, generic brand, a coupon sticker still stuck to one corner, made the whole thing feel suddenly, sickeningly real in a way three separate forum threads never quite had. This was it. This was actually it. He felt the last of his nerve trying to talk him out of it one more time, and did what he always did with that voice: talked over it, louder, faster, until it gave up and went quiet.

Greg stood at the gap a second longer than he needed to, working up the last of it, cataloguing the whole walk over one more time like it was proof of something. He'd come this far. He'd done the research nobody else bothered to do. He thought, distinctly, in the voice he used for narrating his own life back to himself, the voice that made even standing alone in a scrapyard at night feel like it was building toward something: this is the part where it either goes great or it doesn't.

* * *

*Cut to the gallery. Gladly opens his mouth.*

**FURY:** Don't.

**GLADLY:** I wasn't going to say anything.

*Nobody says anything else until it's over.*

* * *

It didn't.

He heard the first one before he saw it, a sound too big and too fast to be anything on two legs, gravel scattering under something with real weight behind it, and by the time his brain caught up enough to supply the word dog the word had already stopped being useful. This wasn't a dog the way anything he'd ever met was a dog. It came out of the dark at a size that didn't make sense against the shape of the lot around it, bone spurs catching what little light there was, and Greg's whole carefully rehearsed nine-version speech evaporated into one single, useless thought, over and over, on a loop: this was a mistake, this was a mistake, this was a mistake.

He turned to run and there was a second one already behind him, cutting the gap in the fence off before he'd covered half the distance to it, and somewhere in the part of his mind that hadn't caught up yet to how bad this actually was, he had time to notice, with a kind of horrified, cheated indignation, that none of this felt like it did in his head. There was no clever line. There was no moment where he found the nerve from somewhere and it turned out he'd had it in him all along, the way it always worked out in version seven. There was just noise, and weight, and the ground coming up fast, and every single thing he'd told himself about who he was going to be tonight turning out to be exactly as true as everyone had warned him it wasn't.

He didn't get to finish the thought.

The world stopped, mid-motion, mid-fall, mid-everything, and went perfectly, completely silent.

Chapter 2: The Waiting Room



The silence lasted exactly as long as it took Greg to realize he was standing up.

That was wrong. He'd been falling. He'd been falling and there'd been weight and noise and an absolute certainty that things were about to stop being okay in a way nothing afterward was going to fix, and now there was solid ground under his sneakers, real and firm and entirely too solid for a guy who'd just watched dogs finish what a forum post started, and he was standing on it, breathing, which he was fairly sure he shouldn't still be able to do.

"Okay," he said out loud, to nobody, because saying things out loud had always been how he figured out what he thought, a habit that had gotten him laughed at more times than he could count and had never once stopped being useful to him personally. "Okay, that's, that's a thing that's happening. I'm dead. I'm definitely dead, this is the dead thing, this is what dead looks like, and it looks like..."

He looked around and the sentence died on its own.

It looked like a desert.

Not his desert, obviously. Greg had never been anywhere that qualified as one. Dust gave under his sneakers, fine and pale, and low tents rose from it on dark wooden frames. The marks on their banners almost became readable when he looked past them, then slid sideways the instant he tried to focus. A cookfire burned in a ring of stones without any wood he could see. Above it all hung too many stars, none where his brain expected them, and two moons close enough together that for one disorienting second he tried to read them as the same moon twice.

He took a step. His sneaker sank into the dust with a real, dumb, physical give, and that somehow scared him more than anything so far. Dead people's shoes didn't sink into sand. He was fairly sure of that. He wasn't sure of much else.

There was a horizon, or the idea of one, a line where the pale dust met the too-full sky, and when Greg made himself walk toward it there was nothing beyond it at all. Not darkness, exactly. Absence, the kind that made his eyes want to slide off it the same way they slid off the banners' half-formed marks. He reached a hand out toward it anyway and the air there was warm, which was somehow the worst detail of all, warm like real sun on a real body, in a place that plainly had no sun to give it.

He turned back around and Mr. Gladly hadn't been there a second ago.

"Mr. Gladly?"

Because it was, somehow, impossibly, standing incongruous against a war camp's dust in the same rumpled button-down he always wore like he'd bought it because a magazine told him teachers dressed that way to seem relatable, arms crossed, weight rocked back on his heels the exact way he stood right before he tried to get a class to see themselves in whatever chapter they were supposed to have read and hadn't. Except Mr. Gladly did not teach Greg's fourth period anything, ever, outside of an actual school, and Greg was extremely sure Mr. Gladly did not know his name. He'd have bet on it. He'd have bet on it right up until about four seconds ago.

"Well," Mr. Gladly said, and Greg's whole body went cold at the sound of it, mild, almost amused, carrying the particular disappointment of a teacher pitched for a kid who'd shown up to class without his homework. "That didn't turn out like you thought it would, did it?"

* * *

*Cut to the gallery.*

**FURY:** "The particular disappointment." Second one already.

**THE SKEPTIC:** He's counting a prose tic. It's going to become a whole thing.

* * *

Greg had no answer for that. There wasn't one.

* * *

*Cut to the gallery.*

**GLADLY:** Oh, I forgot I did the scowl there.

**AN ENTHUSIAST:** I remember the scowl. Underrated.

**FURY:** A third of a second, tops.

**GLADLY:** It counts.

* * *

Something crossed Mr. Gladly's face on the silence that followed, something Greg had genuinely never seen there before, not once, not in a year and a half of half-listening from the third row. A scowl. Real, sharp, the look of a man biting down hard on something too large for the face currently holding it.

And then the face came apart entirely, not violently, not even quickly, more the way a held breath finally lets go, the rumpled button-down and eager slouch dissolving out of the space they'd occupied like a photograph left too long in the sun, and where a homeroom teacher had been standing there was, for one impossible stretched second, nothing at all, and then there was someone else.

Older. Broader through the shoulders than a man had any right to be at whatever age this one actually was. Skin marked with a pattern Greg's eyes kept sliding off exactly the way they'd slid off the banners, a weight in the stillness of him that made the desert around them feel less like scenery and more like the only setting that had ever actually fit.

Greg's whole body went very, very still.

"It has been brought to my attention," the new voice said, unhurried, carrying none of Gladly's brightness and none of his apology either, a voice built for open ground and long silences rather than fourth-period homerooms, "that you arrive already spilling everything you feel onto whoever is standing nearest you. That is not a flaw I intend to shame you for. It is, however, a flaw that will get you killed a second time, having only just now finished being killed the first. You require discipline, boy. Not to silence what you feel. To aim it."

Greg's mouth opened. Nothing resembling words came out of it.

"It has also been brought to my attention," the stranger went on, entirely without missing a beat, the same flat, ancient gravity carrying every syllable of it exactly as evenly as everything that had come before, "that there is someone, elsewhere, who desperately needs to get the stick out of his butt."

* * *

*Cut to the gallery.*

**FURY:** Still can't believe he actually said that to a kid.

**GLADLY:** It needed saying!

**FURY:** It did. Doesn't mean it wasn't weird.

* * *

"Who," Greg finally managed, both hands coming up like he could physically hold the desert still long enough to get a sentence out, "are you, is this actually happening, are you even real, is that a joke about a stick, do I know a guy with a stick, I genuinely don't think I know anyone with a..."

"Mr. Veder."

Something in the way he said it, flat, final, cut through the spiral fast enough that Greg actually stopped, mouth still half open around whatever had been coming next.

"There is not a great deal of time," the stranger said. His patience looked older than the face carrying it. "You are not the only one in need of a lesson tonight. Try, if you can manage it, not to be the reason the other student fails his."

"What other stud..."

The sentence never finished. Neither did the desert.

Chapter 3: Arrival



The event horizon took Teal'c the way it always did, a rush of cold and light and unmaking that lasted no length of time at all, and delivered him, as it always did, whole.

He stepped through onto the packed dirt of P3X-866 a half pace behind Colonel O'Neill, staff weapon already angled to cover the tree line, the motion as automatic to him now as breathing. Major Carter came through next, then Daniel Jackson, and the Stargate behind them shut itself down into silence, leaving the four of them standing in the gray, close-treed light of an alien morning that looked, for all the wonder Daniel Jackson still insisted on finding in such things, very much like a forest in Minnesota. Mist sat low between the trunks. Somewhere off to the east, birds, or something that filled the same ecological niche birds did here, were making a great deal of noise about the sunrise.

It was in the half second between the wormhole's release and his own next breath that the second mind arrived inside him.

There was no gradual awareness of it, no soft edge to ease the transition. One moment there was only Teal'c, ordered and entire, the way he had trained himself to be ordered and entire even under a symbiote's constant low murmur, a discipline so old it no longer felt like effort. The next, there was noise. Not sound. A presence, vast and formless and already, somehow, talking, a wash of thought too fast and too unshaped to be language yet, fear layered on confusion layered on something that, absurdly, felt very much like delight, all three arriving at once, with no order to them, the way a landslide arrives all at once.

Teal'c did not stumble. He had trained twenty years and more to give an enemy nothing, and whatever this was, until he understood it, he would extend it no more courtesy than he extended a Jaffa patrol. He held his stance, staff weapon steady, eyes on the tree line, and inside the perfect stillness of his own skull, something that had not existed a moment ago began, in a rising and increasingly panicked internal voice that was somehow already fully formed, to lose its mind.

::oh my god oh my god okay that's a real Stargate that is a REAL Stargate I just walked through a REAL Stargate that's not, that's not a set, nobody builds a set that big, holy crap is that O'Neill, that's actually Colonel O'Neill, wait no, hang on, is that...::

* * *

*Cut to the gallery.*

**A VOICE:** For the record, “you good” is doing a lot of heavy lifting as a check-in. I'd know.

**FURY:** Nobody asked you.

**A VOICE:** Never does.

* * *

O'Neill glanced back over one shoulder, rifle loose in his hands even as his eyes kept working the tree line. "Teal'c. You good?"

"Indeed," Teal'c said, and the word came out exactly as level as it always did, which took, just then, more of him than four years of Kel-no-reem had ever asked for at once.

::HE TALKED, oh my god he actually talks like that, that's not just a show thing, that's really how he... wait. Wait wait wait. Wormhole X-Treme. This is Wormhole X-Treme. No, hang on, THIS is what Wormhole X-Treme was ripping off, this is the REAL THING, I used to make fun of that show, I used to make fun of the actual guy who says "indeed" in real life, oh my god he actually SAYS it...::

* * *

Teal'c did not know what a Wormhole X-Treme was. He filed the phrase away regardless, the way he filed away any unfamiliar term uttered by an enemy or an ally, to be understood later, at a time of his own choosing rather than one forced upon him. For now there was only the fact of the thing itself: a second consciousness, undeniably present, undeniably distinct from his own, undeniably not going anywhere, occupying the same skull he had spent his entire life learning to master.

He had done this before. Not this precisely; no enemy, no ally, no god posing as either, had ever managed precisely this. But the shape of it was not entirely foreign to him. A presence within, demanding acknowledgment, requiring discipline rather than force to hold at bay. He had carried the symbiote, the one O'Neill had taken to calling Junior, a jibe Teal'c tolerated without ever once using himself, since the day of his first prillar, had learned the patient stillness required to keep a second will from ever once dictating his own, a skill honed across years he did not care to dwell on. Whatever this was, it was not that. It did not press for control the way a symbiote occasionally, subtly, tested its boundaries, feeling for weakness the way water feels for a crack in stone. It simply talked. Endlessly, without apparent capacity to stop, a boy's voice, young, frightened beneath the noise in a way that made itself known even through the excitement layered over top of it, the fear and the delight tangled together so thoroughly that Teal'c doubted the boy himself could have told them apart.

::okay okay okay focus, Greg, focus, you're not dead, or you are dead, or, no wait, you're in someone, you're literally IN someone, this is that thing, this is the thing Mr. Gladly, if that was even really Mr. Gladly, the other student, oh no, am I the passenger, is this guy the driver, is this guy going to be able to hear me, hey. Hey! Can you hear me? Big guy? Mister Symbol-on-the-Forehead? Can you...::

* * *

"Carter, what've we got?" O'Neill said, already moving off toward the tree line, and Major Carter fell in beside him with her recorder out, reading off some low string of readings Teal'c heard and, for once, did not fully process. Daniel Jackson had drifted a few paces to the left, already crouched over a half-buried stone with a marking on its face, murmuring to himself the way he always did when he'd forgotten anyone else was present. "Sam, come look at this, this glyph sequence isn't Goa'uld standard, I think this might actually be older than..."

"Later, Daniel," O'Neill said, without looking back, carrying the weary patience of a man who had said some version of that sentence more times than he could count and expected to say it many more before he retired.

The ordinary business of an ordinary mission, continuing entirely without him, entirely unaware that anything at all had changed. Teal'c fell into position at the rear of the loose formation, the shape his body had taken on missions like this one so many times before that it required no thought at all, watching the tree line, watching the angles a threat might come from, while a voice that had not existed an hour ago kept talking, underneath everything, without pause.

::seriously, is anyone going to answer me, hello, big guy, I can feel you not answering me, that's a thing I can apparently feel now, is that normal, is that a normal thing that happens, oh my god what if this is normal, what if this happens to people all the time and nobody talks about it because they all signed something...::

Teal'c closed his eyes for the length of a single breath, the smallest concession he would allow himself.

Nothing on his face had moved. Nothing would. He had learned that lesson longer ago and at far greater cost than anything a symbiote or a System Lord had ever taught him directly, learned it in rooms where showing anything at all could be the difference between a man's life and his death, and he was not about to unlearn it now for the sake of a voice that, whatever else it was, had not yet actually threatened him.

Inside, where no one else could see it, Teal'c gathered every year of discipline he had built against Apophis's cruelty, his own grief, and a lifetime spent giving enemies nothing to use. Then he turned all of it toward not screaming back.

He had not yet mastered it. He had only, in that first long moment, decided that he would, and fell into step behind his team, staff weapon steady, face like stone, one voice too many now living behind it.

* * *

Chapter 4: Kel-no-reem, Interrupted



P3X-866 gave up nothing more interesting than a set of ruins Daniel Jackson would be cheerfully cataloguing for the next three weeks, and SG-1 came home through the gate a few hours after local sunset, carrying the plain tiredness of a long, uneventful mission rather than anything sharper. Teal'c filed his report standing, as he always did, gave General Hammond the same handful of clipped, accurate sentences he gave after every mission, and retreated to his quarters the moment he was released.

He needed, more than he had needed anything in a very long time, to be alone with his own mind. Or as alone as that was still going to mean.

His quarters at the SGC held little enough to occupy anyone's attention: a bunk, a footlocker, a single candle set on the small shelf he used for exactly this purpose, and nothing else that spoke to a life beyond the mountain. He had never seen the need to acquire more. A First Prime learned early that possessions were leverage in someone else's hands before they were comfort in his own, and the habit had outlasted the reasons for it by a good many years now.

::okay so this is your room, this is a real room on a real military base, there's a candle, why is there a candle, is that a religious thing, is that a Jaffa thing, oh my god I have so many questions, do you guys have, like, a whole culture, obviously you have a culture, that was a dumb thing to think, sorry, I don't know why I said sorry, you can't even hear me say sorry, can you hear me say sorry...::

Teal'c knelt before the candle regardless, back straight, hands resting on his knees, and began the breathing that had carried him through worse nights than this one. Slow, even, a rhythm laid down so many years ago that his body no longer needed his mind's permission to find it. Kel-no-reem did not require sleep. It required stillness, a deliberate quieting of every current running beneath the surface of a man's thoughts, until there was nothing left but the candle flame and the breath and the self.

The self, tonight, was no longer only his.

::is this the meditation thing, is this Kel-no-reem, I know that word, I actually know that word, I feel bad that the only reason I know it is a fake TV show but I know it, okay, I'll be quiet, I can be quiet, I'm being quiet right now, this is me being quiet...::

It did not work.

* * *

Teal'c had expected resistance from without, an enemy's blade, a Goa'uld's will pressed against his own, and had built his entire discipline around meeting exactly that kind of pressure without yielding. He had not built it to meet a boy who wanted, with an intensity that had nothing manipulative in it at all, simply to be heard. The stillness he reached for kept arriving fractured, a held breath with a voice threaded straight through the middle of it, apologizing for existing and then, a heartbeat later, unable to stop existing quite so loudly anyway.

He tried again. Breath in, held, released. The candle flame steadied in front of him, and for the length of perhaps four full breaths together, something close to quiet actually took hold, quiet enough that he let himself believe, briefly, that the discipline was going to hold the way it always had.

::sorry, sorry, I'm trying, I really am, it's just really hard to think about nothing when there's, like, everything, there's SO much everything right now, I don't even know where to start not thinking about it...::

The quiet broke. Teal'c exhaled slowly, deliberately, refusing to let the failure show in his breathing even here, alone, with no one to see it fail.

He opened his eyes.

"You are not being quiet," Teal'c said, aloud, to the empty room, because there was no other way yet to speak to something that lived behind his own eyes rather than in front of them.

::oh my god you can hear me, wait, was that a rhetorical, no, you're actually, hi, hi, I'm Greg, I don't know if you got that from the yelling earlier, it's Greg, Greg Veder, and I am so sorry, I really am trying, it's just, this is a lot, this is genuinely the most a lot has ever a lot-ed at me, and I don't really have an off switch, my mom used to say that, actually she still says it, said it, whatever tense that's supposed to be now that I'm apparently living inside a Stargate person...::

"Teal'c," he said, cutting cleanly through it, the way he might have interrupted a nervous recruit spiraling toward a mistake. "I am called Teal'c."

::Teal'c. Okay. Teal'c. That's, that's a great name, that's a genuinely cool name, I'm not just saying that, although I guess I'd say that either way because you could probably kill me with your bare hands, no offense, that's not a threat thing, that's a fact thing, you have very large hands...::

"Greg Veder." Teal'c said the name slowly, testing its shape the way a man weighs an unfamiliar weapon before deciding whether to trust it in a fight. "You are afraid."

The chatter stopped. Not all at once, but enough that Teal'c felt the shape of the silence change, something in the noise pulling back on itself.

::...yeah. Yeah, I am. Is that, is that weird, I feel like I should be handling this better, I feel like there's a version of me that would be handling this way better, that's, that's kind of my whole thing actually, there's always a version of me handling it better, in my head, and then the real me shows up and...::

"You died," Teal'c said, with a soldier's preference for truth before comfort. "And you did not choose this. Neither did I."

::no. No, I didn't. Either of those.::

"Then we are, for the present, required to endure each other." Teal'c closed his eyes again, sought the breath, found it harder to hold than it had been a few minutes before, not because the boy was still talking, but because he had stopped, and the quiet left behind by that stopping was, in its own way, its own kind of demand. "I ask only this. When I require silence, you will grant it if you are able. When you cannot, you will tell me so, rather than simply failing to provide it."

::...okay. Yeah. I can do that. I mean, I'll probably be bad at it, fair warning, but I can try to do that.::

* * *

*Cut to the gallery.*

**GLADLY:** He's gonna fail at that miserably, isn't he.

**A RECOGNIZABLE VOICE:** Indeed.

**GLADLY:** Nope. Not asking.

* * *

It was not Kel-no-reem. There was no true stillness, no full quiet, nothing his masters would have recognized as mastery. But the pressure behind his eyes had eased by one degree, and Teal'c had learned not to discard measurable progress merely because it was small.

He blew out the candle eventually, more from habit than necessity, and sat a while longer in the dark that followed, turning the evening over the way he turned over anything unfamiliar before deciding what to make of it. He had spent his whole life being told, by masters and gods alike, that a Jaffa's strength lay in what he refused to show. No one had ever told him what to do with a strength like that once something had moved in behind his eyes and simply refused to leave.

::hey, um. Thanks. For listening. I know that's probably not, like, a big thing for you, but it kind of was for me.::

"Rest, Greg Veder," Teal'c said, quietly, to the dark. "Tomorrow will ask more of both of us than tonight did."

* * *

Teal'c did not sleep that night, Jaffa did not require it, but for the first time since the gate had opened onto P3X-866's gray morning, he sat with the second voice in his skull and did not feel, entirely, alone in carrying it.

Chapter 5: First Mission



The next gate address on the schedule board belonged to P4X-212, a farming world with a Goa'uld-descended population and, per the MALP telemetry, no visible military presence, which was exactly the kind of assessment that made O'Neill visibly relax and Teal'c, privately, trust a little less than everyone else seemed to.

::okay, wheat, that's, that's a lot of wheat, is that normal, do aliens grow wheat, why does everything always grow wheat, you'd think somewhere in this whole galaxy somebody would be growing something a little more interesting...::

* * *

Teal'c walked the perimeter of the village square with the rest of the team, staff weapon held low but ready, and did his best to hold the outward shape of a man listening only to Daniel Jackson, who'd fallen into easy conversation with the elder inside a minute, no interpreter, no phrasebook, just two people talking, the same shared-descent linguistic-drift theory Daniel loved explaining to anyone who'd stand still for it and nobody ever really needed explained. Internally, the second voice had not stopped since the gate, a running commentary on the thatched roofs, the smell of the livestock pens, a woman carrying water who reminded him, apparently, of somebody's aunt.

The village elder received them with the measured, over-careful courtesy of a people who had learned, at some cost, to be wary of strangers who arrived through a ring of standing stone. Her hands shook faintly around the cup of something bitter she offered each of them in turn, a custom none dared refuse. She was old enough to remember this world before the current arrangement, though she offered nothing unprompted and Daniel, wisely, did not press.

* * *

Tribute, she explained, was due within the week. The word she used for the ones who collected it carried its own weight regardless of language, some things translate themselves; Teal'c had heard variations of it in a dozen villages across a dozen worlds, in his own mouth, once, when he had been the one arriving to collect. He kept his face as still as he kept it for everything else, and did not look at Daniel while the word passed between them, unwilling to see whatever understanding might be sitting in the man's eyes about exactly what Teal'c had once been to people like these.

* * *

*Cut to the gallery.*

**GLADLY:** Wait, they just... speak English? All of them?

**A VOICE:** For the record, I asked that exact question. Out loud. Multiple times, over multiple decades, to multiple people who all gave me some version of the same shrug.

**FURY:** Because there isn't a good answer.

* * *

::wait, tribute, like, actual tribute, like the show, oh my god, this is the tribute episode, this is literally the tribute episode, except it's real, and it's not funny at all, why did I ever think that episode was funny...::

* * *

O'Neill crouched by the well at the square's center, running a hand along a support post that had been recently, badly repaired. "So when do the collectors usually show?"

The elder's answer was worse than any of them wanted. Tomorrow. Possibly tonight.

O'Neill made the call to hold position rather than risk the gate address a second time before dark, half a day's walk back through unfamiliar terrain against staying put in a village that, whatever else was true of it, hadn't shown them a single hostile gesture yet. Carter ran a slow perimeter sweep with her scanner while there was still light to do it by, Daniel stayed with the elder trying to coax loose whatever else the woman wasn't saying, and Teal'c took first watch without being asked, the way he generally did, since sleep was a human problem and he had never seen the sense in taking a human's turn at rest before offering his own first.

They made camp on the village's edge rather than push their luck any further before dark, and it was there, an hour past midnight, crouched at watch beside a low fire that had mostly burned down to coals, that Greg's voice cut through the quiet with something Teal'c had not heard from him before: focus.

::hey, hey, wait, that mark, on the post O'Neill was looking at, the one that got fixed, I know that mark, I mean I don't know it, I don't actually know it, but I've seen it, it was in the Wormhole X-Treme episode, the tribute one, it was on the crates the collectors brought, and in the show it was supposed to be like a house sigil or something, some Goa'uld's personal symbol, and if this is the same guy, if it's actually the same, then...::

Teal'c did not immediately trust it. A children's entertainment, however loosely built atop some fractured, secondhand version of the truth, was not a reliable intelligence source, and he had no wish to act on a stranger's half-remembered television program in the middle of hostile territory. But the mark itself, once Greg's insistence made him look at it properly in the dying firelight, was real, and it matched nothing he had personally catalogued.

"O'Neill." He kept his voice low. "The repaired post bears a sigil I do not recognize. I would examine it before we commit to a position."

O'Neill gave him a look carrying the particular skepticism of a man silently asking since when do you flag decorative woodwork, and got up anyway, because in four years of fighting beside the man Teal'c had never once raised a concern that turned out to be nothing.

* * *

*Cut to the gallery.*

**THE SKEPTIC:** Third. “The particular X of a man Y-ing.”

* * *

Carter's scanner found a hollow space beneath the post. Inside lay a cache of mostly primitive but Goa'uld-forged weapons, hidden by villagers who had planned resistance long before four strangers walked out of their gate. It was not proof of an ambush. It was proof the tribute collectors were expected to be fought rather than endured, which changed every calculation O'Neill had been making about the next day.

"Well, that's not ominous at all," O'Neill said, crouched over the open cache, flat and dry in the way he got about things that were, in fact, extremely ominous.

"Indeed," Teal'c said.

"Carter, options."

* * *

Carter had them by the time the fire burned back down, a redrawn watch rotation, a fallback position if the collectors arrived heavier than expected, contingencies stacked on contingencies the way she always built them, methodical even half-asleep on four hours in the last two days. Teal'c listened to all of it with the part of his attention that belonged to the mission, and let the rest of it sit with the small, unfamiliar warmth still lodged somewhere behind his sternum.

::I knew it, oh my god, I actually knew something, that's, wait, is this bad, is this actually terrible, we might be about to be in a fight because of me, why does knowing something always immediately turn into a worse problem than not knowing it...::

"Your knowledge was useful, Greg Veder," Teal'c said, quietly enough that no one but the boy in his own skull could hear it, and felt, for the length of that single sentence, something that was not quite pride and not quite relief, but sat somewhere close to both.

::...thanks. That's, yeah. That actually means a lot. You have no idea how much that means, coming from, well, from anyone, honestly, but especially from you.::

Teal'c kept watching the tree line until dawn. The feeling stayed.

Chapter 6: False Confidence



The collectors arrived at midday, three Jaffa in the service of a minor Goa'uld none of them had a name for yet, escorting a hovering supply sled and moving with the particular unhurried confidence of men who had never once been refused. Their armor bore a mark Teal'c did recognize, a lesser house, one of a dozen such houses that rose and fell in Apophis's shadow without ever quite mattering enough to be remembered by name, which told him less about their strength than about their arrogance. Lesser houses overcompensated. He had served long enough to know that much without needing to see it proven.

SG-1 watched from the tree line, close enough to act, far enough, O'Neill hoped, to avoid having to. The village had gone quiet around them in a way that felt rehearsed, doors shut, animals penned, the whole square arranged like a stage set for a performance everyone present had given more than once before.

::okay, okay, I've got this, I actually know this one, in the episode the collectors always back off if you damage the tribute crate in front of them, because it's supposed to be, like, a sacred insult thing, an honor thing, they can't be seen accepting a spoiled tribute, so if we just, like, break one of the jars before they load it, they'll leave, that's literally the whole plan from the show, it works every single time...::

* * *

*Cut to the gallery.*

**GLADLY:** Oh, I remember this plan! This is a good plan!

**FURY:** It's not a good plan.

**GLADLY:** It worked in the show.

* * *

Teal'c did not immediately dismiss it. The previous night's mark had been real, useful, earned him something he had not expected to feel about the boy's borrowed knowledge. That success sat close enough behind him that it made the next voice easier to hear than it should have been.

"O'Neill." He kept his own voice low, level, the way he delivered every piece of intelligence he had ever passed his commanding officer. "There may be a custom regarding damaged tribute. Spoiled goods refused rather than accepted."

O'Neill's eyebrow went up, but four years of Teal'c never once passing along something worthless bought the idea more weight than it had earned. "You sure about that?"

Teal'c hesitated, the length of that hesitation the only honest answer he had. He was not sure. He was relying on a source he did not fully trust, delivered by a voice he had only just begun to.

::yeah, no, I mean, pretty sure, I mean it worked in the show, and shows are usually based on something real, right, that's like, the whole thing you said, THIS is what the show was based on, so if the show got that detail, it probably came from somewhere real, right?::

It was, Teal'c would think later, exactly the reasoning that got soldiers killed. Not malice. Not even carelessness, precisely. Just a chain of if-thens built on hope instead of verification, each link plausible enough on its own that the whole chain felt sturdier than any single piece of it actually was.

"I believe it worth attempting," Teal'c said.

Carter moved before he'd finished the sentence, close enough to the sled to put a hand on the nearest jar, and cracked it hard enough against the sled's edge to shatter it in full view of the collecting party.

* * *

*Cut to the gallery.*

**PEANUT GALLERY:** I could cite an entire page of reasons why this is a terrible idea.

**FURY:** In the show, nobody bleeds.

* * *

The Jaffa did not retreat.

The Jaffa raised weapons, all three at once, staff blasts scorching the dirt a body's width from where Carter had been standing a half second before Teal'c's own shout put her flat behind cover, and what followed was not a negotiation, it was a fight neither side had planned to have this early, chaotic and close, villagers scattering into the doorways they'd left cracked open for exactly this, O'Neill's return fire buying just enough seconds for the team to break contact and pull back into the trees rather than the clean, bloodless retreat the borrowed plan had promised.

It lasted perhaps ninety seconds. Teal'c put himself between Carter and the nearest line of fire twice and took a glancing staff blast across one shoulder plate that would leave a bruise he had no intention of mentioning. By the time they broke for the trees, one Jaffa was down, the other two were falling back toward the sled, and SG-1 was running hard for the gate.

::oh my god oh my god I'm so sorry, I didn't, I really thought, I was so sure, I was SO sure, oh my god is Carter okay, please tell me Carter's okay...::

"Major Carter is unharmed," Teal'c said, once they'd put real distance behind them, once the gate address was keyed and the wormhole's blue wash lit the trees around them. His voice, to anyone listening, gave away exactly as much as it always did. Nothing.

O'Neill clapped him once on the good shoulder on the way past, still running hot on adrenaline, too wired to fully process the near miss yet, and said something about buying the intel desk a drink for the bum steer on "no visible military presence," and then they were all through the gate and it was, as far as the rest of SG-1 was concerned, simply another mission that had gone sideways the way missions sometimes did.

Internally was a different account entirely.

"You were certain," he said, and there was an edge in it now he did not try to soften, "of something you had no true grounds to be certain of. A story built for entertainment, and you presented it as knowledge."

::I know. I know, okay, I know, you don't have to, I already feel like garbage about it, you don't have to make it worse...::

"I am not attempting to make it worse. I am telling you that Major Carter was nearly killed because I permitted a guess to be spoken as though it were fact, and I permitted it because the last time you offered something, it proved true, and I allowed that single success to purchase more trust than it had earned." He stepped through the event horizon a half pace behind O'Neill, the cold rush of it no distraction at all from what he was still saying, even mid-transit. "That failure is mine as much as yours. But I require you to understand the difference, in the future, between what you have seen and what you merely believe."

::...yeah. Yeah, okay. I get it. I'm sorry. I really am.::

He said nothing else on the walk through the SGC's gate room. He did not trust the next words to come out measured.

Chapter 8: Loyalist Jaffa



P2X-908 was not supposed to be anything. A resupply cache, three days old, left by an SG team that had come and gone without incident, buried under a cairn of loose stone half a klick from the gate, and O'Neill's read on the mission going in was retrieval, not reconnaissance, in and out before lunch. The gate had deposited them into a wide, grassy clearing ringed by dense forest, quiet enough that Daniel had spent the walk in speculating cheerfully about whether the cairn itself might turn out to be older than the cache stored beneath it, some previous culture's marker worth a return trip regardless of what the SGC actually needed from the planet. Carter found the cache exactly where the coordinates said it would be, packed crates still sealed, and for a good twenty minutes the whole exercise felt like exactly the kind of quiet, forgettable Tuesday a team could use after the last few weeks.

::okay, this one seems chill, right? Chill planet. Nothing's gonna...::

The patrol came out of the tree line before Greg finished the thought, six Jaffa in formation too tight and too practiced to be anything but real soldiers, and Teal'c knew what they were before he could make out a single face, the armor alone telling him everything the rest of him didn't want to hear. Not a lesser house's colors. Apophis's own mark, worn the way Teal'c himself had once worn it, First Prime's guard, the elite among the elite.

* * *

Their leader stepped forward alone, and Teal'c felt something in his own chest go very still and very cold before the man had said a single word, recognition arriving faster than thought, the particular gait, the set of the shoulders. Va'lar. They had trained together, once, two boys handed staff weapons before they were old enough to understand what they were being handed, and Va'lar had stayed exactly where Teal'c had once stood, loyal, decorated, unbothered by any of the things that had eventually driven Teal'c to walk away.

* * *

*Cut to the gallery.*

**THE SKEPTIC:** “The particular gait.” Fourth.

**THE SKEPTIC:** Mine has columns.

**A DRY VOICE FROM THE BACK:** Of course it has columns.

* * *

"Teal'c." Va'lar said the name the way a man says a word he's been saving. "First Prime. Or you were, before you ran to hide behind the Tau'ri like a coward with no god left to answer to."

Teal'c said nothing. Silence had always cost him less than the alternative.

"Your wife still asks after you," Va'lar went on, closing the distance at an unhurried walk, savoring it, "though less than she once did, I think. And your son. Growing, I'm told, into something you'd hardly recognize. Apophis keeps them close. A kindness, he calls it, though I confess I've never fully understood the word applied to a cage."

::oh god. Oh god, this is real, this isn't a mission-of-the-week thing, this is actually about his family, I can feel it, I can actually feel how bad this is, this isn't funny, none of this is funny...::

Behind Va'lar, the six-man patrol held formation, weapons not yet raised but not lowered either, waiting on a word Teal'c could feel building toward its arrival. Beside him, O'Neill had gone quiet the way he always went quiet before a firefight, all easy sarcasm burned off, hand already drifting toward his weapon. Carter's read on the numbers would already be running, Teal'c knew, the same math he was running himself: six against four, no cover worth the name, forty yards of open ground between here and the gate.

There was no deflection in it this time, no joke reaching for the surface, no trailer-voice narration standing between Greg and what he was feeling. Just the fear, plain and correctly sized for once, matching the danger instead of running wildly past or short of it, and Teal'c, in the one still-functioning corner of his attention not occupied by Va'lar, noted the difference the way he noted any change in a known quantity.

* * *

"You have said what you came to say," Teal'c said, and if his voice shook at all, it did not show it, four years of discipline holding the line his heart wasn't holding nearly so well. "Say the rest of it, or step aside."

Va'lar smiled, and it was not a kind expression. "There is no rest of it, Teal'c. Only this."

His hand came up, and the staff weapons behind him rose with it, six trained soldiers against four, the numbers wrong in every direction that mattered, and O'Neill's shout of "Cover!" arrived a half second before the first blast scorched the dirt where Daniel had been standing.

The fight that followed had none of the ragged, improvised chaos of P4X-212. This was disciplined, coordinated, Jaffa who had trained together for years pressing an advantage SG-1 had walked into with no warning and no plan for. Carter went down hard behind a fallen log, favoring one leg. Daniel was pinned flat, no clean angle to return fire from. O'Neill was shouting something about the gate, the gate that was still forty yards and an entire kill zone away, laying down what cover he could while Teal'c held the center, the only one of them standing close enough to Va'lar's line to actually blunt it.

He fought the way he always fought, economical, no wasted motion, dropping one Jaffa with a staff blast at close range and taking the return hit across the ribs without breaking stride. It bought seconds. It didn't buy enough of them. Two more of Va'lar's men were already flanking wide, cutting the angle toward the gate down to nothing, and Carter's leg wasn't going to carry her that distance even if the angle had still been open.

::we're gonna die, we're actually gonna die, this isn't the tribute episode, this isn't ANY episode, nobody's coming to save this...::

Teal'c took a second hit, this one across the shoulder, staggered, and felt, for the first time since a boy's voice had arrived uninvited in his skull, something that was not quite a thought and not quite a decision, but sat exactly between the two.

Daniel, from behind the log he shared with Carter, was shouting something at O'Neill about the treeline to the east, a possible second angle to the gate if they could just buy the seconds to reach it, and O'Neill was shouting back that seconds were exactly the thing in shortest supply. The whole clearing had narrowed, for Teal'c, down to the staff weapon in his hands, the two Jaffa still flanking, and the growing, undeniable certainty that his own hands, however disciplined, were not going to be enough.

He was going to need more than his own two hands were going to give him.

Chapter 9: The First Handoff



Teal'c had never once, in his life, handed control of his own body to anyone. Not to Apophis, not to any master he had served before his defection, not even to Junior, whose low murmur he had spent years learning to hold at arm's length precisely so this exact moment would never come. But Va'lar had trained beside him for years before Teal'c ever walked away, had drilled against his exact style so many times that the two of them could have fought each other blind, and every motion Teal'c made now, however sound, was a motion Va'lar's men had already seen, already countered, already built an answer for. He had taken two hits proving it. He was going to take a third fighting the same way a fourth time.

There was one thing in this fight Va'lar's men had no answer prepared for at all, because it wasn't a technique, wasn't a style, wasn't anything a soldier could drill against: someone who didn't know how to fight, occupying a body that did.

There was a second reason, one he would admit only later. Directing a body through a fight left room for nothing but the next block, strike, and half second. Teal'c needed to see the whole ambush rather than merely survive its next instant, and he could not do that while his own hands were still moving.

Teal'c had a half second to decide, staff blasts scorching the ground close enough to feel the heat off them, and he made the decision the way he made every decision that mattered: fast, and without asking himself twice.

::wait, what, what are you...::

*Now,* Teal'c thought, with everything he had, and let go.

* * *

Greg had never operated a body that wasn't, technically, dying at the time. This one was very much alive, six and a half feet of trained muscle responding to input he had no idea how to give, and his first attempt at *forward* sent them stumbling sideways into a tree hard enough to rattle teeth that weren't, strictly, his to rattle. A staff weapon he didn't remember picking up was somehow already in both hands, heavier than anything he'd ever held, and every instinct he actually possessed screamed at him to drop it and run, an instinct that turned out to be spectacularly wrong for a body built to do neither.

::okay okay okay how do arms work, how do THESE arms work, there's gotta be a, there's gotta be some kind of, oh my god is that a guy, there's a guy, why is there a guy right there...::

The guy right there was one of Va'lar's Jaffa, staff weapon already leveled, and the shot that should have ended things there missed by a margin wide enough to be almost insulting, because Greg, mid-panic, had flinched the entire body sideways a half second before the trigger pull, an utterly untrained, utterly graceless flail that no combat instructor alive would have taught and that happened, by pure accident, to save his life.

* * *

It went on like that. Greg swung the staff weapon like a bat because that was the only frame of reference he had for holding a long stick with both hands, connected with exactly nothing on purpose, and twice put Teal'c's body flat on the ground avoiding blows that a single moment of actual training would have parried standing up. It should have gotten them both killed inside of ten seconds. It didn't, because Va'lar's Jaffa, disciplined and drilled and utterly unprepared for an opponent moving like nothing they had ever trained against, kept overcorrecting for attacks that weren't coming and missing the ones that were, thrown off by a fighting style that wasn't a style at all, just a terrified teenager doing whatever kept the pain away for one more second.

::I don't know what I'm doing, I really don't know what I'm doing, why isn't anybody stopping me, somebody should really be stopping me...::

Internally, Teal'c watched, and did the one thing he had bought himself the room to do: he counted. Six Jaffa at the start, one down at the treeline, one more Carter had taken from behind the log, leaving three, two flanking wide and one holding the center, a formation with a gap in it that would close again in seconds if nobody moved through it first. He had no hands to point with, no voice loud enough to carry the instruction the way he needed to. He had only the same channel Greg had been shouting through since the moment he'd arrived.

::there. The gap between the two on your left. Go now, not toward them, past them.::

Greg didn't answer, didn't have time to, but Teal'c felt the borrowed body react anyway, some fraction of the thought landing even through the panic, close enough to right to matter. The opening came from Carter a half second later, a shot from behind the fallen log that took one more of Va'lar's men off his feet, and Greg, already moving on the direction he'd barely registered receiving, threw the body bodily through the gap toward the trees, dragging two more Jaffa's attention with him and buying, by an accident that was not entirely an accident, exactly the chaos O'Neill needed to get Daniel moving toward the gate.

* * *

*Cut to the gallery.*

A precise, mild voice cut in. "Directing a body you can't fully see through a fight you can't fully follow, with lives depending on it, never stops being terrifying."

Underneath him, something stitched together from a dozen borrowed tones added: *"good... boy... he did... good."*

**GLADLY:** I don't want that explained.

**FURY:** Wise.

* * *

::give it back, give it back, I don't want this, I don't want to be the one who...::

Teal'c felt the wheel offered back before Greg finished the thought, an instinct as raw and untrained as everything else about him, panic reaching for the one thing it actually knew how to do, hand off the hard part. Teal'c took it without hesitation, and the body that had been flailing a half second ago became, in the space of a single step, exactly what it had always been: economical, precise, a First Prime's discipline reasserting itself all at once. Two more staff blasts, controlled, deliberate, and the line broke, Va'lar's remaining men falling back rather than pressing an advantage that had just gotten considerably more dangerous.

Va'lar himself was already gone, retreated with the others rather than risk being the one still standing when the rest of SG-1 reached the gate. That, too, felt like something Teal'c would need to think about later, when there was time.

There was not, yet, time.

"Go!" O'Neill was already moving, Daniel half-carrying Carter, and Teal'c fell in behind them, staff weapon steady in hands that had, until a few seconds ago, belonged entirely to someone else.

::I'm sorry. I'm so sorry, that was, I don't even know what that was, I probably made it worse, I definitely made it worse...::

"You did not," Teal'c said, quiet enough that only Greg would ever hear it, the gate's blue wash rising ahead of them. "You bought seconds none of us had. That is not nothing."

He did not say what it had cost to watch his own body move through a stranger's fear. That could wait until they were home.

Chapter 10: What Greg's Actually For



They hadn't meant to take a prisoner. It happened in the last chaotic seconds before the gate, one of Va'lar's Jaffa cut off from the retreat by his own side's collapsing line, and O'Neill's call, fast and unglamorous, had been to zat him rather than leave a wounded eighteen-year-old bleeding out on an alien planet neither of them would ever see again. He'd come through the gate unconscious and left the infirmary two days later still refusing to give his name, which was, General Hammond noted dryly, technically an improvement over refusing to speak at all.

Teal'c volunteered for the interrogation. Nobody else on base spoke to a captured Jaffa quite the way he did, soldier to soldier, the shared grammar of a life neither Daniel's diplomacy nor O'Neill's charm could reach.

It was not going well.

The room was small, gray, deliberately unremarkable, a table bolted to the floor and two chairs, one of which Teal'c had not yet used. He had opened with the standard approach, the one that had worked on a dozen prisoners before this one: his own history laid out plainly, First Prime once, defector now, proof that the path away from Apophis existed and could be survived. It had worked on soldiers who still believed, on some level, that loyalty was a question of conviction. This one didn't so much as blink at it.

"You will not be harmed," Teal'c said, for perhaps the fourth time, standing rather than sitting across the small table, because sitting had gotten him nothing either. "Cooperation earns you consideration. Apophis's reach does not extend into this facility."

The boy, and he was a boy, barely older than Rya'c would be by now, said nothing, jaw set, eyes fixed somewhere over Teal'c's shoulder.

::he's not being stubborn. Well, he is, but that's not the actual thing. Look at his hands.::

Teal'c kept his eyes on the boy's face and registered the observation anyway.

::his hands are doing this whole thing where they keep almost reaching for his collar, right where a family mark would sit if he had one visible, and every time you say "cooperation" he does it again. He's not scared of you. He's scared of what happens to somebody back home if word gets out he talked.::

*Cut to the gallery.*

**AN ENTHUSIAST:** He's good at this part.

**THE WATCHER:** He's good at people. Nobody ever notices because he works so hard to make sure they notice everything else first.

**A DRY VOICE FROM THE BACK:** And also because he puts real effort into showing the world how little he gets. The Void Cowboy brand does not maintain itself.

**GLADLY:** I'd put it on the trailer.

Teal'c considered the read for the length of a single breath, weighing a passenger's unverifiable instinct against a lifetime of interrogations conducted the disciplined, procedural way, and found, somewhat to his own surprise, that he trusted it. Not because Greg had been right before, though he had, once, with the sigil. Because it matched something Teal'c himself half-recognized and hadn't let himself look at directly: the particular stillness of a man protecting someone he can't protect from here.

*Cut to the gallery.*

**THE SKEPTIC:** Fifth.

**AN ENTHUSIAST:** I missed it.

**THE SKEPTIC:** That's why the spreadsheet exists.

He pulled out the chair across from the boy and sat, for the first time, close enough that the gesture itself meant something.

::that's it, that's exactly it, oh my god, you're actually good at this, why didn't you just do this from the start...::

It was the first time Teal'c had answered him mid-scene, silent and internal, no external word given away, and Greg went quiet in a way that had nothing to do with being told to.

The boy talked. Not everything, not at first, halting, careful, testing each admission against whatever consequence he still feared before offering the next one. "My sister," he said eventually, the first real crack in his guard. "She is young. Twelve summers. If word reached Apophis that I had spoken to the Tau'ri, it would not be my safety in question. It would be hers."

It was a name, a village, and enough of a thread for Daniel and Carter to start pulling. Then came the detail Teal'c had not prepared for: Apophis had recently consolidated forces and tightened his hold on the outlying households he kept as leverage. Drey'auc and Rya'c were among them, though the boy did not know those names meant anything to the man across the table.

Teal'c did not let it show. He had learned that lesson longer ago than anything else he owned.

* * *

"You have done well," Teal'c told the boy, and meant it, before General Hammond's people came to take him somewhere safer than a holding cell. Alone in the corridor afterward, staff weapon absent, no mission clock counting down, no team waiting on a call, Teal'c allowed himself, finally, to feel the thing he'd kept off his face for the better part of an hour.

::hey. You okay?::

::No,:: Teal'c said, which was, itself, a kind of first. ::But I know a door to try now, when I am ready. That is more than I had this morning.::

He stood in the corridor under the fluorescent hum. There was no mission clock counting down toward Drey'auc and Rya'c, no order to give, nothing he could do with the knowledge tonight. Greg stayed with him anyway.

Chapter 11: Downtime



Three days passed without a gate assignment, which at the SGC counted as a small miracle, and Teal'c spent the first morning of it exactly the way he spent every morning off-world assignments allowed him to: three hundred push-ups, then three hundred sit-ups, counted in silence, in the same corner of the gym he'd claimed as his own since his first week on base.

::okay, three hundred, THREE HUNDRED, I want to be clear that I am once again inside a body doing something completely unreasonable and you're not even breathing hard...::

"You are welcome to attempt the same number," Teal'c said, between reps, "should you ever again possess a body of your own to attempt it in."

::wow. Wow, okay, low blow, big guy, real low blow before nine in the morning...::

Teal'c did not smile, but Greg caught the satisfaction anyway.

* * *

*Cut to the gallery.*

**AN ENTHUSIAST:** He made a joke.

**THE WATCHER:** Don't crowd it. You'll scare it off.

* * *

The afternoon found him in the officers' lounge, off-duty, in front of the television with the specific, unhurried patience of a man who had genuinely nowhere else he needed to be, and Greg discovered, with a kind of delighted horror that took several minutes to fully process, that Teal'c's chosen viewing was a syndicated Earth western, decades old, playing reruns at an hour nobody sensible would schedule anything against.

* * *

*Cut to the gallery.*

**THE SKEPTIC:** Sixth.

**A DRY VOICE:** Still counting?

* * *

::wait. Wait wait wait. You watch this? You, First Prime, staff-weapon guy, you watch a guy in a cowboy hat solve crimes with karate?::

"His conduct exemplifies discipline," Teal'c said, entirely serious, eyes on the screen. "He does not waver. He does not negotiate with wrongdoing. I find it instructive."

::it's not instructive, it's a guy roundhouse kicking people off of horses...::

"Precisely."

Greg gave up, which felt, even to him, like a genuinely new experience, losing an argument about taste to a four-thousand-year-old alien warrior monk with the world's most sincere face. He settled in instead, unable to do anything else, and found, twenty minutes later, that he was actually watching it too, invested, against every instinct he had, in whether the guy in the hat was going to get to the ranch in time.

* * *

By early evening Greg had picked a side, loudly, in the show's ongoing feud between the sheriff and a visiting cattle baron, invested against every instinct he had in whether the guy in the hat was going to sort it out before sundown. Teal'c did not confirm or deny an opinion of his own, though the small, unfamiliar warmth of an ordinary afternoon allowed to simply exist, unexamined, uncomplicated by anything either of them owed the other, sat with him regardless.

He ate that evening in the commissary with the rest of the team, a rare enough occurrence on its own that O'Neill made a small production of it, sliding into the seat across from him with a tray of something the SGC's kitchen insisted was meatloaf.

"Teal'c. Off duty. In the actual chair, at the actual table. Miracles happen." O'Neill pointed a fork at him, and then, for just a beat longer than the joke needed, actually looked at him, an officer's assessing quiet, four years' worth of learning exactly what his people look like when something's different about them, even before he can name what it is. "You're in a good mood. I'm suspicious."

"I am simply well-rested, O'Neill."

It was, as far as it went, not untrue. It was also not the whole of it, and Teal'c held O'Neill's gaze the extra second it took to be certain the lie, such as it was, had landed, before O'Neill let the beat pass. "Uh-huh." He didn't buy it, not entirely, Teal'c could see that much in the set of his mouth even as he let it go, and didn't push either, which was its own kind of gift Teal'c had learned to appreciate about the man over four years, a willingness to notice a thing and not yet demand an accounting for it. Daniel talked at length about the glyph sequence from P3X-866, still unresolved, still bothering him the way an unsolved puzzle always did; Carter needled him gently about it being, in her professional opinion, probably just decorative.

"It's not decorative," Daniel said, for what was clearly not the first time this argument had happened.

"Indeed," Teal'c said, mildly, "it is decorative."

Daniel pointed his fork at him, betrayed. "Not you too." Teal'c ate in his usual near-silence, and found, without quite meaning to, that he was tracking the whole table's rhythm the way Greg tracked things, who was deflecting for whom, who needed the conversation kept light and who didn't, a kind of attention he wasn't accustomed to paying and wasn't entirely sure, yet, belonged to him or to the voice that had taught it to him.

::you're doing the thing.::

* * *

::What thing.::

::The people thing. You're doing my thing.::

Teal'c did not have an answer for that one ready. He filed it away instead, the way he filed most things he did not yet know what to do with, and let the meal go on.

By the third episode of the evening's second western, Greg had picked a side, loudly, and lost an argument with Teal'c about whether the sheriff's deputy could plausibly have made that shot from that distance, an argument Teal'c won not by being right, necessarily, but by simply repeating "he made the shot" in the same flat tone until Greg gave up out of sheer attrition.

::this is so stupid. This is so stupid and I don't want it to end.::

Teal'c let the program run. The ease would not last. For one evening, it did not have to.

Chapter 12: The Question Neither Asks



It came up almost by accident, the second night of downtime, Teal'c seated in his quarters with the candle unlit for once, no Kel-no-reem attempted, just the ordinary quiet of a man with nowhere he needed to be. He'd spent the better part of an hour simply sitting with it, an unfamiliar luxury, no mission to prepare for, no report to file, and had almost let himself believe the whole day might pass without either of them reaching for anything heavier than the previous afternoon's argument about a fictional sheriff's marksmanship.

::hey, so, can I ask you something? About, um. About your family?::

He felt the question arrive the way he'd learned to feel all of Greg's questions now, a small pressure behind his own thoughts before the words themselves resolved, and something in him, old and well-practiced, began closing the door before he'd even decided to.

"Another time," Teal'c said, and the words weren't unkind, only final.

::yeah. Okay. Another time.::

Neither of them said anything else for a while. The silence sat between them, not unfriendly, just occupied, the shape of a door two people both know is there and have both agreed, for now, not to open.

* * *

*Cut to the gallery.*

**GLADLY:** That's not a real answer, though. "Another time" isn't an answer.

**FURY:** It's an answer. Just not the one you wanted.

**GLADLY:** He's sitting with it this time. No candle. No meditation.

**FURY:** Don't make him perform the progress.

* * *

He read one of the few Earth novels he had found worth returning to. The words moved past without landing; his attention kept circling the question he had refused. Greg had gone quiet in the way that meant he was busy not saying something, a restraint that cost him more effort than chatter ever did.

::you can ask me back, you know. If you wanted to. I wouldn't hold the not-answering against you the way you're probably expecting me to.::

::I am aware,:: Teal'c said. ::I am choosing the moment regardless.::

::why?::

::Because a question asked carelessly deserves a careless answer, and neither of us, I think, would be well served by that tonight.::

Greg didn't have a reply ready for that one, which was, in itself, its own small answer.

It was Teal'c who almost asked, an hour later, into a silence that had gone comfortable again without either of them noticing the shift.

"Your own family," he said, careful, testing the shape of the question the way he tested most things before trusting them. "You have mentioned a mother. Rarely, and never at length. And a father, mentioned not at all."

He felt Greg go very still, a stillness with none of the ease theirs had built up over the last two days, something older and more guarded underneath it. Teal'c had felt fragments of this before, in passing, unbidden, a flinch here, a flash of something raw during an unrelated conversation there, never enough to form a full picture, only enough to know a shape was missing from it, the way a room can feel wrong before you've counted what's not in it.

::yeah. There's, uh. There's not a lot to say.::

Teal'c heard the lie as clearly as Greg must have heard his refusal an hour before. He did not press on ground he had declined to cross himself.

"Then we will leave it," Teal'c said, "for another time."

::...yeah. Thanks.::

* * *

Nothing more passed between them that night. But something had shifted regardless, a mutual recognition neither one said aloud: two doors, closed for now by mutual, unspoken agreement, both of them still standing, both of them, for the first time, acknowledged rather than simply avoided. That was not nothing. Teal'c, increasingly, was coming to suspect that very little about this particular arrangement was ever going to be nothing.

* * *

*Cut to the gallery.*

**THE SKEPTIC:** Seventh.

**AN ENTHUSIAST:** I didn't even hear it that time.


* * *

Teal'c blew out the candle he'd never lit and sat a while longer in the dark that followed anyway, out of habit more than need. Somewhere behind his own eyes, a boy who talked constantly about everything else in the universe was, for once, saying nothing at all, and Teal'c found he did not mind sitting in that silence together, not tonight. There would be another time. He was beginning, cautiously, to believe that.

Before sleep came, he thought of the door he had kept shut for four years and the one Greg guarded just as carefully. Neither had opened. Both had finally been acknowledged.

Chapter 13: Leak



The mission itself was almost nothing, a routine perimeter check on P9X-441, a rocky, wind-scoured world with no population to speak of and no reason for SG-1 to be there beyond confirming a naquadah reading Carter's scanner had flagged from orbit. It went wrong only in the smallest possible way, an unstable ledge giving out underfoot on the climb down, a rockslide that caught Teal'c's leg and pinned him hard enough that pulling free took real time, real effort, and enough pain that his usual walls, the ones that had held against gods and torture both, found themselves briefly, uselessly occupied elsewhere.

The climb down had been unremarkable right up until it wasn't, loose scree underfoot the whole way, Carter calling the naquadah reading closer with every step, Daniel picking his way along a few yards off complaining good-naturedly about his knees. Nobody had flagged the ledge as a risk. Nobody flags the ledge, until it is one.

It was into that gap that the memory came through.

Not Greg's voice, not his usual rambling narration. Something rawer, unfiltered, arriving whole and uninvited the way sensation arrived, the way pain arrived: a kitchen, fluorescent-lit, the bulb overhead gone an ugly yellow that should have been replaced years ago. A boy at a table, younger than the one Teal'c knew, maybe twelve, holding a report card he already knew wasn't good enough before anyone else had seen it. A man's voice, flat, already disappointed before the conversation had properly started, tired and contemptuous, a man who had stopped expecting anything and was simply confirming it once more for the record. *You know what your problem is? You want everybody to like you so bad you never once try to be somebody worth liking.*

Teal'c, pinned under a slab of stone he could not yet shift, felt the words land as if they had been said to him.

::oh god. Oh god, no, not that one, not that one, I didn't mean for you to...::

"I am here," Teal'c said, aloud, low enough that only Carter, working the stone above him, might have caught the strangeness in his voice and not the words. "I am not going anywhere."

::I didn't want you to see that. I really didn't want you to see that one.::

The memory did not fully recede, not the way sensation usually did once its cause had passed. It sat instead, lodged somewhere behind Teal'c's own thoughts, a kitchen he had never stood in, a father's voice he had never heard, and he understood, with a clarity that had nothing gentle in it, that this was not a story Greg had chosen to tell him. It was a wound that had simply, finally, torn open under enough pressure to stop being containable.

* * *

*Cut to the gallery.*

**GLADLY:** ...That's a real one.

**FURY:** That's the difference between a bit and a scar. The bit keeps people from seeing where it hurts.

* * *

Carter got the stone shifted a moment later, real time and pain reasserting themselves, Daniel and O'Neill both hauling on the same lever arm she'd wedged under it, and the rest of the extraction happened the ordinary way, hands and shoulders and O'Neill barking coordinates at the gate team. Teal'c said nothing about any of it, walked out under his own power despite what the leg clearly cost him, refused the infirmary's insistence twice before finally submitting to it a third time, mostly to end the argument rather than out of any conviction he needed it.

::you okay?::

::That is my question to ask you,:: Teal'c said, and meant it entirely.

::yeah. I mean. Now you know. That's, that's the actual thing, that's not the version I tell people, that's just, that's what actually happened, more or less, a hundred more or less like it.::

Teal'c lay with his leg elevated and considered the kitchen he had never seen until today. He would not set it aside merely because examining it hurt.

"He was wrong," Teal'c said finally.

::yeah, well. Doesn't really matter if he was wrong. He was there every day. Being wrong every day.::

::how old were you? In the memory.::

::Twelve, give or take. Report cards were a whole production in my house. Still are, I guess, if my mom still bothers to make me look at hers.::

::And your mother. In that room. Where was she.::

::Kitchen doorway, mostly. Not saying anything. She never really said anything, when he got going. I used to think that meant she agreed with him. Took me a long time to figure out it probably just meant she was scared of him too, in her own way.::

Teal'c considered a mother's silence that might have been agreement or fear, perhaps indistinguishable even to the woman standing in the doorway.

* * *

Teal'c could not undo twelve years in a kitchen he had never entered. He could refuse to look away from them. Greg felt him make that choice and stay.

Chapter 14: Drey'auc and Rya'c



The infirmary released him the next morning, the leg bruised badly but nothing broken, and Teal'c did not return to his quarters. He walked instead to the small, unused briefing room on Level 22 that nobody bothered with after hours, sat, and did something he had not done in the whole of the time since a boy's voice had first arrived behind his eyes.

He opened the door himself.

::hey. You don't have to, you know. After yesterday. I wasn't trying to trade you into it.::

"I know," Teal'c said. "I am not doing this because you gave me something and I owe you a return. I am doing this because I have carried it alone for longer than I can any longer justify, and you are, whatever else is true, the only one who has ever actually been inside the carrying of it with me."

He did not ease into it. He had never been a man who eased into anything that mattered.

"Her name is Drey'auc. My wife." The word sat strangely in his mouth, unused this long. "My son is called Rya'c. He was a boy when I left them, younger than you are now. He will not be a boy any longer."

::how long has it been?::

"Four years, by the reckoning of this world. I do not know how Apophis's domain marks time against it, only that it has not stopped moving without me in it."

::what was she like? Drey'auc. If you don't mind me asking.::

Something in Teal'c's chest, already raw, gave a little further at the question, gently asked, no agenda behind it beyond the wanting to know. "Sharper than I was, in most of the ways that mattered. She saw the rot in Apophis's service before I allowed myself to see it, said as much to me more than once, in whatever privacy two people can find inside a god's household. I did not listen quickly enough. When I finally did, it was already too late to leave together."

::that's not on you. The timing.::

"It is entirely on me, Greg Veder. That is not self-pity. It is simply the fact of it."

Greg did not argue. The empty briefing room held the silence without asking either of them to fill it.

The memory, when it came, came on his own terms this time, offered rather than torn loose: the night of his defection, Drey'auc's face in the doorway, not pleading, never pleading, a woman who had married a First Prime and understood exactly what that meant about the choices available to both of them. Rya'c asleep in the next room, too young to be woken for a goodbye that would have meant nothing to him yet. Teal'c had not said goodbye to his son at all. He had told himself, at the time, that it was a mercy. He had spent four years since increasingly unsure that it had been anything but a coward's math, dressed up as kindness.

* * *

::do they know? That you think about them? That you're, I don't know, out here trying to figure out how to...::

"I do not know what they believe of me," Teal'c said, and this, more than anything else he had said, was the part he had never once permitted himself to say aloud. "Apophis will have shaped it to his own purpose. A traitor. A coward. Whatever tale serves him best against them, that is the tale they have been given, and I have had no way, in four years, to offer them a different one."

::the kid we brought back. From the ambush. He said Apophis is tightening up, keeping people closer. That's them, isn't it. That's why it landed the way it landed.::

"Yes."

He did not soften it further than that single word, and Greg, to his credit, did not ask him to.

::okay. So what do we do.::

"I do not yet know," Teal'c said, and the admission cost him something separate from everything else he had already given away in this room, a First Prime's whole training built around always, always knowing what came next. "I know only that I am no longer carrying the not-knowing by myself. That is a different thing than it was yesterday."

* * *

Teal'c stayed in the empty briefing room, with no candle and no Kel-no-reem to turn the confession into ritual. One other person now knew. It was not relief, but it was lighter than what he had carried in alone.

::for what it's worth,:: Greg said, quiet, none of his usual rush behind it, ::Rya'c's got a dad who never once stopped trying to get back to him. That's not nothing either. Whatever else Apophis has told him.::

Teal'c did not trust his own voice with an answer to that. He let the silence stand in for it instead, and found, somewhat to his own surprise, that the silence said enough.

Chapter 15: The Crack



P6X-334 turned out to be nothing more dangerous than a trading outpost, minor, unaffiliated, the kind of world SG-1 visited a dozen times a year without incident, stalls and awnings and the low hum of a market day going on regardless of who'd just walked in through the standing stone. The local quartermaster, an anxious, overworked man named Bel who reminded Greg, with an intensity he couldn't fully explain, of every substitute teacher who'd ever tried too hard to be liked, was in the middle of a genuine crisis: a shipment of grain seed had arrived contaminated, half the settlement's spring planting at risk, and nobody in his own chain of command willing to take responsibility for the decision to destroy it and start over. He'd cornered Daniel first, hoping an off-worlder might carry more weight than his own superiors seemed willing to lend him, and Daniel, deep in unrelated trade terms, had gently redirected him toward the rest of the team without much hope it would help.

::oh my god, I know this guy. Not literally, but I know this guy, this is a guy who needs somebody to just tell him it's okay to make the call...::

Daniel was mid-negotiation over trade terms entirely unrelated to the seed crisis, O'Neill visibly counting the minutes until they could reasonably leave, and it was Greg, unable to help himself, unable to do anything else, who fed Teal'c the words.

::tell him it's not his fault the shipment came bad. Tell him the actual failure was whoever signed off on it before it ever reached him, and that catching it now, before planting, is the only reason this isn't a disaster instead of an inconvenience. He needs somebody bigger than him to say it's not his call to feel guilty about.::

Teal'c said exactly that, in his own voice, his own cadence, no trace of Greg's rambling delivery anywhere in it, and Bel's whole posture changed, a man visibly setting down a weight he'd been carrying since the shipment arrived.

"You have my thanks," Bel said, and then, entirely unprompted, entirely earnest, "You have a very calming presence. Like the old stone wardens carved into our west gate, from before my grandmother's time. They say a look like that alone used to be worth more than a garrison."

::oh my god. Oh my god, "stone wardens," that's, that's literally the Stone Guardians, that's the exact thing from the show, this guy's never even heard of Earth and his own actual culture has the same... okay, wait, that actually tracks, doesn't it, if the show really was based on leaked reports, somebody who wrote it must have gotten a description of an actual world and just, I don't know, translated it into a costume budget...::

There was a pause, the kind that could have gone several directions.

"I do not know what a Wormhole X-Treme is," Teal'c said, entirely deadpan, to a man who had never once heard those words in his life and had absolutely no idea what to do with them, "but I am given to understand it is not what it appears to be."

Bel blinked, visibly recalculating, and decided, with the diplomatic instincts of a man who has learned not to question off-worlders too closely, to simply nod as though that had been a completely normal thing to say.

::YOU DID THE BIT. YOU ACTUALLY DID THE BIT, AND HE HAS NO IDEA WHAT YOU'RE TALKING ABOUT, THAT'S EVEN FUNNIER, I've been trying to get you to do a bit for FIFTEEN CHAPTERS...::

Teal'c laughed before four years of practiced stillness could stop him. It was not a large sound, but it was the first anyone on that team had heard from him.

* * *

*Cut to the gallery.*

**AN ENTHUSIAST:** He laughed.

**THE WATCHER:** He let somebody hear him. That's the miracle.

* * *

Daniel turned first, trade terms abandoned, delight and shock fighting across his face. Carter followed with a small smile she did not hide. O'Neill kept watching the horizon. Four years beside the man had taught him when looking directly at a thing would only make it retreat.

Bel, for his part, looked simply pleased with himself, entirely unaware he'd just done anything worth remarking on, already moving on to thank Daniel a second time for the introduction.

Teal'c recovered himself within a second, the laugh already gone, his face already settling back into its ordinary stillness. But a second had been enough. A second, once heard, could not be unheard by the people standing close enough to catch it.

::I'm sorry. I didn't mean to, I wasn't trying to make you...::

"Do not apologize," Teal'c said, quiet enough that it stayed between them. "I have not laughed in four years, Greg Veder. I did not know, until this moment, how much I had missed the sound of it."

Chapter 16: Noticed



The laugh did not do it by itself. O'Neill had been collecting smaller changes for weeks: Teal'c answering jokes he once ignored, relaxing half an inch during a briefing, changing a gym schedule he had treated as immovable for four years. None belonged in an incident report. Together, they belonged in a conversation.

He caught Teal'c after an uneventful debrief, when the paperwork was signed and the rest of the team had dispersed toward showers and downtime. O'Neill stayed in his chair. Teal'c stayed in his.

Not a debrief anymore. Not really. Just two men and an empty room, and whatever this actually was going to be.

He didn't lead with the laugh. Teal'c had expected him to, had half-rehearsed an answer for it, and got, instead, something he hadn't prepared for at all.

"You remember the commissary. Couple weeks back. I said you were in a good mood, I was suspicious." O'Neill wasn't smiling, not exactly, flat and serious in the way he only got when a conversation actually mattered to him rather than when he was performing not caring. "I wasn't just messing with you, Teal'c. I've had four years of watching your face do exactly one thing, and it's not one thing anymore. Hasn't been for a while. Little bit different every week, if I'm honest, some days I can't even put my finger on what, just, off. And then P6X-334 happens and you laugh, actually laugh, in front of God and Daniel and everybody, and that's not a one-time thing anymore either, buddy, that's a pattern, and I've been sitting on this a lot longer than I probably should have before saying something."

Teal'c had promised himself he would not lie when this conversation came.

Chapter 21: Bra'tac's Warning

Chapter 22: The Rescue Plan



Bra'tac found O'Neill alone for perhaps thirty seconds before the rest of the team filed into the briefing room, and used every one of them.

"You did not," Bra'tac said, entirely without humor. "You did nothing to earn taking it back. Just as you did nothing to earn it in the first place." He did not elaborate further before turning to find his seat as the rest of the team began filing in.

O'Neill's options landed on Hammond's desk at 1745 hours, fifteen minutes ahead of the deadline, and by 1900 the full team, plus Bra'tac, plus a visibly uncomfortable Air Force colonel from a division Teal'c had never worked with directly, were assembled in the largest briefing room the mountain had, staring at a three-dimensional projection of the compound rendered in more detail than the morning's flat imagery had allowed.

Gunnery Sergeant Turnpike arrived last, freshly read into the program and looking like a man who still was not entirely convinced any of this was real. Captain Marcus J. For one brief, strange moment the room came up out of its chairs to meet Turnpike, Hammond included, full attention and a salute held a beat longer than protocol strictly demanded. "Three options," O'Neill said, pointing with the same fork-like gesture he used on actual food, minus the fork. Turnpike sat.

Carter's eyebrows, which had never quite come back down, went up a second time. Daniel let out something that started as a laugh and got smothered halfway into a cough that fooled nobody. Chapter 23: Turnpike



Gunnery Sergeant Turnpike sat through the tail end of a briefing full of words like tunnel network and cloaked insertion, delivered by officers who said them the same flat way they'd say chow line, and let his mind do what thirty years of discipline had never quite managed to train out of him: it wandered backward, the way it always did in the last quiet stretch before something real got asked of him.

None of it had started in that room. It started five months earlier and two states away, in an office that still smelled like burnt coffee.

* * *

The hearing officer had Turnpike's full record open on the desk in front of him, twenty-eight years of it, and took long enough paging through the relevant sections that Turnpike had time to count every crack in the ceiling tile directly above the man's head before he finally looked up. Major Ellery Cobb, Turnpike's commanding officer for the last two years, sat off to one side with the exhausted patience of a man attending the latest performance of a play he had never enjoyed the first time.

"Master Sergeant Delacroix says you put him through a mess hall table."

Turnpike shifted in the chair as if the furniture had offended him personally, one shoulder riding higher than the other and the beginning of a grin tugging at his mouth. "Not through it, sir. Table held."

It was not the answer of a man bored by the accusation. It was the answer of a man who found the distinction important, the result satisfactory, and the ceremony surrounding both of them faintly ridiculous. He knew exactly how much trouble he was in. That knowledge had simply failed to make him repentant.

The hearing officer, a light colonel not yet forty, closed the file and studied him a long moment instead of reading from it. "You realize I could end your career over this."

"I realize you could try."

"You've got two Silver Stars in here, Gunny. A Bronze. A Medal of Honor sitting in a box somewhere I'd bet good money you've never actually worn outside a ceremony you were ordered to attend. Enough commendations besides that I ran out of drawer space just skimming them." The colonel leaned back, something that wasn't quite amusement working at the corner of his mouth. "And a disciplinary file thick enough to double as a doorstop. I genuinely cannot decide if the Corps has been wasting you or protecting itself from you for the last three decades."

"Both, sir. Ask anybody who's actually served under me. Nobody's ever called it boring."

The colonel closed the second file too, decision apparently already made somewhere Turnpike hadn't been invited to watch it happen. "There's a TDY attachment that just came open. Joint program, half classified, mostly training work, out at a mountain facility in Colorado I don't have clearance to know the actual purpose of. Temporary, on paper. Ninety days, extendable. Somebody Air Force side asked for a Marine gunny who could build a working unit out of whatever gets handed to him with no notice and no complaints. Your name came up."

"Somebody's idea of a joke."

"Somebody's idea of getting you very far away from Camp Lejeune for a while, Gunny, before Sergeant Delacroix finds a lawyer with better instincts than the last one." The colonel slid a folder across the desk. "Pack light. You leave in the morning."

Turnpike glanced toward Cobb. "You volunteer me, sir?"

Cobb rubbed one hand over his face. "I answered the telephone, Gunny. At this point, that was close enough."

* * *

The transport put him wheels-down at Peterson before noon, and Peterson, it turned out, was as far as anybody there was actually willing to explain anything, which suited Turnpike considerably better once he understood it was also where he might be spending the next several months. He spent the better part of four hours in a windowless processing office being handed forms by an airman who could not answer a single question beyond what the forms themselves already said, a badge issued for the base he was already standing on and nothing further, a security briefing delivered in the flat, rehearsed cadence of somebody reciting a script for the several-hundredth time and finding it no more interesting now than the first.

He met his current commanding officer in an office on the base's unclassified side before he had even found his own quarters. Major Warren Rusk rose from behind the desk with the precise economy of a man who had made an art form out of looking military without ever appearing rumpled by the work. His aide, a first lieutenant named Purcell who agreed with every sentence out of the major's mouth roughly half a beat before the major finished saying it, hovered at his shoulder like a man auditioning for a permanent position there. Or perhaps a couple of feet south.

Rusk looked Turnpike over slowly, taking in the ribbons, the weathered face, and the posture that had never learned how to become less dangerous merely because it was indoors. "Your record makes fascinating reading, Gunnery Sergeant. In a historical sense. Men like you belong behind glass, with instructions to break it if another war gets inconvenient."

Turnpike gave Rusk the same beginning of a grin he had given the hearing officer. "Glass breaks easy. That's generally when people come looking for me."

Purcell smiled as though the major had just won an argument Turnpike had not realized they were having.

"You've got a platoon," Rusk said. "Twenty-two Marines, pulled together from four different postings over the last two weeks. Most of them are exactly what you'd want and exactly what you'd expect, competent, unremarkable, the kind of people who never once end up in an office like this one. Six of them are not that. Those six are why you're standing in my office and not somebody with a quieter service record."

"They generally aren't the quiet ones you end up needing, sir," Turnpike said, entirely unbothered by how that landed. "That's usually when the ones who actually matter get found."

"That's an optimistic read on six men whose collective disciplinary record could give yours a run for its money." Rusk slid a folder across, thinner than the one the colonel back at Lejeune had closed on him. "The other sixteen can hold their own without you standing over them, they've done this before and they know their jobs. These six are the ones you'll have this base, and however long it actually takes, to get squared away. Once I am satisfied they can be trusted near anything sensitive, and only then, they get read into the program proper. Until that day, they train here, they live here, and not one of them so much as sees the inside of the facility itself. That part is not negotiable, Gunny, and neither, frankly, is my patience for hearing why it's taking longer than it should."

"Understood." He waited one beat, exactly long enough to make the separation unmistakable. "Sir."

"Sir," Purcell added, entirely unprompted, "the major means that constructively."

Turnpike looked at the lieutenant long enough to make the silence itself the answer, then picked up the folder and left without waiting to be dismissed, having already decided, somewhere between "in a historical sense" and "constructively," exactly how much of that assessment he intended to accept as final.

* * *

He found the six of them in their assigned barracks forty minutes later, and understood immediately why Major Rusk hadn't bothered pretending otherwise.

Doyle had claimed the room's only mirror and appeared to be conducting a serious personal relationship with it. Kowalczyk was holding forth to nobody in particular about some grievance with the mess hall's coffee, loudly, at a volume suggesting he had been doing it uninterrupted for some time already. Radio Benitez sat cross-legged on an unmade bunk with headphones on, present in the room the way furniture was present in a room. Corporal Alvarez had chosen the bunk nearest the door and was pretending the disorder around him belonged to people he had no authority over. Bishop was stripping and reassembling a rifle that was already clean, each movement technically perfect and half a beat too careful. Haney had opened a field radio casing with a mess knife and was doing something to its wiring no supply officer would have approved in writing.

Gear lay scattered across every surface in a configuration that answered to no system Turnpike could identify, and the whole space carried the loose disorder of six Marines who had spent two weeks operating as six separate arguments for why somebody else was the real problem.

Turnpike did not raise his voice. He did not need to. He crossed the room in four strides, put his boot through the leg of the room's only table hard enough to send it sideways into the wall, and let the crash do the introduction his voice hadn't gotten around to yet.

The room went dead silent. Doyle's hand actually froze halfway to his own hair.

"My name is Gunnery Sergeant Turnpike," he said, into the silence he had just made, unhurried now that he finally had it. "I have been told, by two separate officers in two separate states, that the six of you are not first pick material. I have also been told that something considerably more consequential than looking presentable is going to be asked of every single one of you, eventually, once I decide you're actually ready for it, and not one minute before." He let his eyes move down the row of them, one at a time, cataloging without comment. "I intend to prove those two officers wrong. Whether any of you enjoy the process is entirely optional. Whether you survive it with your dignity intact is up to you."

Nobody said anything.

"Gear squared away in ten minutes," Turnpike said. "I will be timing it."

He did not have to say it twice.

* * *

Ten minutes later the gear was not squared away so much as hidden in six bunks and lockers with the doors forced shut. Turnpike made them pull it all back out.

The second attempt took twenty-three minutes and produced an actual inventory.

The third took twelve.

Turnpike said nothing complimentary when it was done. He walked the line once, opened Haney's locker, removed the mess knife and the radio component Haney had neglected to return, and held one in each hand.

"Tomorrow," he said, "I start finding out whether the six of you are careless because nobody ever taught you better, or because somebody taught you and you decided the lesson did not apply. Those are different problems. I only keep Marines who can learn the difference."

He left them with the broken table still against the wall. Behind him, Alvarez began assigning the cleanup without once asking whether anyone had put him in charge.

Turnpike heard it. He did not turn around.

Chapter 24: Standards



The first month was not a montage. Montages were what people invented afterward because repetition was boring to anybody who had not needed it to stay alive.

Turnpike began with records, then distrusted half of what the records claimed. Six disciplinary files had reduced six Marines to six convenient defects. Doyle was vain. Kowalczyk could not stop arguing. Benitez ignored orders. Alvarez had failed as a team leader. Bishop froze. Haney cut corners. Every sentence was true. None of them was useful until Turnpike knew when it became true and what the Marine had been trying to accomplish when it happened.

Doyle's inspection record was a disaster everywhere except weapons and optics. Those were immaculate. He noticed scratches, loose mounts, and changes measured in fractions because he spent his whole life looking for imperfections, mostly in himself and occasionally in equipment. Turnpike stopped trying to cure the mirror and made him responsible for pre-mission equipment checks. The first time Doyle found a cracked sling buckle on another Marine's kit, Turnpike made the whole platoon hear who had found it.

Kowalczyk argued because bad plans offended him physically. His file called it insubordination. Turnpike put him at the front of every terrain brief and required him to state the objection in ten words or fewer, then offer an alternative in ten more. The first week, Kowalczyk could not do it. By the fourth, he could dismantle a route in one sentence and replace it in the next.

Benitez wore headphones because silence made every other sound arrive at once. Radios did the opposite. A net had order, priorities, call signs, a rule for who spoke next. Turnpike put him under the platoon's senior communicator and stopped calling the headphones a discipline problem once Benitez proved he could remove them before being told whenever work began. The nickname Radio stopped being an insult sometime during week three. Nobody announced the change.

Alvarez had been acting team leader when an officer changed a live-fire plan after movement began, then blamed him for the confusion that followed. Nobody died. The paperwork still taught him the safest way to lead was to wait until authority became impossible to dispute. Turnpike gave him decisions too small to hide from and then refused to rescue him from making them. Chow order. Route order. Training pairs. By the time the decisions became tactical, Alvarez had stopped looking over his shoulder for permission already given.

Bishop did not freeze under fire. He froze under contradictory rules of engagement, which was a considerably more intelligent problem than his file made it sound. Turnpike ran judgment drills until Bishop could say what he saw, what he believed, and what condition would make him fire, all before the target finished changing. Haney's shortcuts were rarely lazy. Most were attempts to make bad equipment do a job it had not been designed to do. Turnpike paired him with an armorer who made him document every modification, test it, reverse it, then test the original configuration beside it. Half Haney's ideas failed. The half that survived stopped being shortcuts and became tools.

Major Rusk wanted the six trained together, contained together, and evaluated together. Turnpike refused.

"You put every problem in one team, sir, you haven't built a team. You've built a place the rest of the platoon can point when anything goes wrong."

"Those six are why you're here."

"The platoon is why I'm here. Those six are just where it tells the truth loudest."

So Turnpike broke them apart. Each rotated through the platoon's established teams. The other sixteen resented being turned into instructors for Marines they had not asked for, right up until they discovered instruction exposed their own lazy habits just as efficiently. Standards that had existed as tradition became standards somebody had to explain. A knot tied from memory had to be defended. A field of fire had to be named. A radio check could not be dismissed as obvious when Benitez asked what failure sounded like and nobody had a precise answer.

At the end of day nineteen, Rusk asked for a readiness report.

"Not ready," Turnpike said.

Rusk looked down at the calendar as if Turnpike might have forgotten how one worked. "Your original estimate was three weeks."

"No, sir. Your patience was three weeks. My estimate was however long it took."

Purcell inhaled, preparing to translate the sentence into something more respectful.

Rusk stopped him with one raised hand. "What are they missing?"

"A consequence they cannot blame on training staff. Right now they perform because I am watching. I need to know what they do when the plan fails and all they have left is each other."

The consequence arrived during a thirty-six-hour navigation and surveillance exercise in the mountains west of Peterson. The map marked a drainage crossing that spring runoff had removed. The approved route ended at a fifteen-foot cut filled with fast water and broken timber. Alvarez halted the platoon. Kowalczyk identified a longer contour route before anyone asked. Bishop found overwatch. Haney started building a line across the cut anyway, convinced he could save the schedule. Doyle noticed the anchor tree moving at the roots. Benitez reported the delay exactly as trained.

Then they waited.

They waited for Turnpike to choose between Kowalczyk's route and Haney's line. They waited long enough for the surveillance target to cross the observation area and disappear. Nobody got hurt. The platoon failed the mission completely.

Turnpike conducted the after-action review beside the washed-out crossing while their socks were still wet.

"Who had the decision?"

Alvarez stared at the water. "I did."

"Who had the information?"

"All of us," Kowalczyk said.

"Then why am I the only man everybody looked at?"

Nobody answered.

"You are not here to perform competence until a senior man supplies judgment. You are here to act inside commander's intent when the route, the radio, or the commander is gone. Today the map failed. Then you failed because you trusted the missing bridge longer than you trusted one another. We run it again next week. New ground. New map. I will not be where you can see me."

The next exercise failed differently. Bishop called a threat too late. The one after that, Haney changed a piece of equipment without telling Alvarez. On the fourth, the evaluator removed Benitez and the primary radio ten minutes after insertion. Doyle carried messages until Haney built a field-expedient antenna from wire he had signed out properly, Alvarez changed the route without waiting for Turnpike, Kowalczyk stated one objection and one alternative, and Bishop covered the movement without needing anyone to reconcile his orders for him.

They found the target. They observed it for six hours. They withdrew without the evaluation team detecting them once.

It was day sixty-three when Rusk signed the clearance recommendation.

"Your ninety-day attachment has twenty-seven days left," he said. "The extension paperwork is already waiting."

"That a request, sir?"

"No, Gunny. It is the closest thing to an admission you are getting from me."

The Gate came after another week of compartmented briefings, medical examinations, nondisclosure agreements, and increasingly implausible questions nobody would explain. Twenty-two Marines stood in the embarkation room and watched a ring of stone fill with something that looked like water standing vertically.

Doyle forgot to check his reflection. Kowalczyk did not have an opinion ready. Benitez removed his headphones and held them in one hand. Alvarez looked to Turnpike once, received a nod, and gave the order himself.

Their first trip was to an empty world with two moons and no tactical importance beyond proving they could cross, orient, communicate, and return without treating wonder as an excuse to stop doing their jobs. Their second was a concealed observation post overlooking a trade road on an allied world, three days of rain and no contact, useful precisely because nothing happened and they remained disciplined anyway. Their third put them on perimeter security for a geological survey while SG personnel evaluated how the platoon worked around civilians who did not understand why silence mattered.

Each mission added access. None added certainty. Turnpike trusted that distinction more than he trusted the phrase mission-ready stamped across any folder.

By the end of the fourth month, Rusk no longer called them the six problem cases. He called them Turnpike's lead team, usually with the expression of a man irritated that language had been forced to acknowledge evidence.

Chapter 25: Same Dirt



The mission that changed the platoon's status looked safer than the three before it.

A three-person science team had left a research cache on an allied world eight months earlier when a medical emergency forced an immediate evacuation. The settlement nearest the Gate had confirmed the site remained untouched. The route was known. The local government had approved the recovery. Turnpike's platoon would provide reconnaissance and security, retrieve the cache, and come home before dinner.

Routine, on paper.

Turnpike had lived long enough to regard the phrase as a warning label.

He sent Doyle's team ahead of the scientists, with Kowalczyk reading the ground and Benitez carrying the forward radio. Alvarez had the team. Bishop carried the long glass. Haney carried equipment he had inventoried twice and modified not at all without authorization. The other sixteen Marines moved in offset support, far enough apart that one burst of fire could not turn a platoon into a casualty list.

The first sign the brief was wrong was not a person. It was a fresh boot mark inside the abandoned site's western drainage ditch, sharp-edged despite the previous night's rain.

Doyle found it.

Alvarez halted movement without looking back for permission. Kowalczyk gave him the likely approach in nine words. Bishop found a position covering it. Benitez reported. Haney checked the cache building for the wire and disturbed ground that would announce a trap.

For thirty seconds, the platoon did everything right.

Then one of the scientists stepped forward to see what Doyle had found.

Six armed locals rose from concealment around the drainage line, territorial rather than ideological, protecting salvage rights to a cache they had apparently been stripping for months. The lead man shouted something the translator did not resolve quickly enough. The scientist answered with empty hands and a carefully rehearsed greeting. Someone behind the lead man fired before either side understood the other.

Doyle saw the flanking position as the first shot landed and called it while dropping into cover. The second shot grazed his forearm. He kept calling distance and direction while blood ran into his glove.

Kowalczyk said four words on the radio. "Left ditch, two moving." Then he stopped talking and covered the movement he had named.

Bishop had civilians behind his first angle and did not fire. He stated the obstruction, shifted three steps, reacquired, and fired only when the line was clear. The hesitation in his record did not appear. Judgment did.

Haney threw smoke where Alvarez pointed rather than where instinct first suggested. Benitez kept the platoon net ordered while the science team shouted over one another. Alvarez moved the civilians, assigned suppression, and began the withdrawal before Turnpike gave him anything more than commander's intent.

Turnpike put a round into the flanking position Doyle had identified. The threat stopped moving. He did not waste time deciding whether that made the morning a victory.

The other sixteen Marines closed around the lead team by sections, not as a crowd running toward gunfire. One element covered. One moved. One took the scientists and cache. Benitez walked backward through the final hundred yards to the Gate, weapon up between transmissions, his voice low and steady enough that the civilians obeyed him without ever understanding how close the mission had come to ending differently.

Nobody in the platoon died. Doyle needed six stitches and acquired a story he improved slightly every time he told it. One local was dead and two wounded by the time their government reached the site. The scientists came home with their cache, their bodies intact, and their assumptions considerably less so.

Turnpike's after-action report ran nine pages.

The first three documented the platoon's actions. The fourth identified the intelligence failures. The fifth asked why an eight-month-old abandonment site had been labeled secure based on a settlement's assurance rather than direct observation. The sixth through eighth recommended changes to escort doctrine, advance reconnaissance, translator placement, and civilian control. The ninth listed every Marine who had made a decision without waiting for Turnpike and whether the decision had served the mission.

Hammond read it personally. He summoned Turnpike to the mountain for the first time two days later.

"You wrote more about what failed above your platoon than what succeeded inside it," Hammond said, the report open between them.

"My Marines already know what they did, sir. The people writing the next brief need to know what they didn't."

"Major Rusk tells me your attachment expires next week."

"Major Rusk has been telling me that for two months."

The corner of Hammond's mouth moved without quite becoming a smile. "I'm extending it. More than that, I am expanding the Marine reconnaissance capability attached to this command. Your platoon will be the first piece, not the whole of it. Major Rusk's authority over the attachment ends when the new orders take effect. You will have a company commander. Somebody who understands what this program needs and what your Marines need from it."

"That combination narrow the list much?"

"Considerably."

The door opened behind Turnpike.

"Still writing reports nobody wants to read, Gunny?"

Turnpike knew the voice before he turned. Marcus Mercer had been a lieutenant the last time they had occupied the same patch of ground, young enough to believe the signed route on a map deserved more confidence than the sergeant telling him the hillside had moved. The hillside had moved. The enemy had moved with it. Turnpike had brought Mercer's Marines out through a route neither map nor plan contained, and Mercer had spent the following month writing the recommendation that eventually put a Medal of Honor in a box Turnpike preferred not to open.

Mercer wore captain's bars now and the same expression he had worn after that earlier extraction, relief compressed so tightly it presented as irritation.

"Still arriving after the shooting stops, Lieutenant?" Turnpike asked.

"Captain."

"We'll see."

Hammond folded his hands over the open report. "Captain Mercer learned you were attached to this program four days ago. Since then, he has called in favors from two commands, one general officer, and somebody at Headquarters Marine Corps who declined to tell me what Captain Mercer had on him."

"Nothing worth putting in writing, sir," Mercer said.

Turnpike looked him over again, this time less amused. "You did all that to inherit three platoons and a classified posting you weren't cleared to ask about?"

"I heard somebody was sending you up another hill." Mercer met his eyes. "Last time, you went up it with me. I wasn't letting you take this one without me."

Hammond looked between them. "I take it introductions are unnecessary."

"We've chewed some of the same dirt," Mercer said.

"He means I kept him from eating all of it," Turnpike said.

Mercer ignored that with the ease of long practice. "I read the report. Map failed again."

"Marines didn't."

"That's why I'm here."

The next six weeks were not spent pretending three platoons became a company because an organization chart placed them under one captain. Mercer brought two established Force Recon platoons into the compartment. Turnpike's platoon knew the Gate and the program; the other two knew each other, their own methods, and exactly how little they enjoyed being treated as late arrivals to somebody else's secret war.

Mercer made command relationships explicit. Turnpike made standards common. The platoons exchanged evaluators, communicators, medics, and opposing forces. They learned one set of hand signals, one casualty procedure, one reporting format. They ran insertion drills in aircraft hangars before any of them knew the transport they would eventually use was not built on Earth. They rehearsed loss of communications until initiative stopped looking like disobedience and started looking like intent surviving contact.

Turnpike did not turn the other platoons into copies of his. Mercer would not have allowed it, and Turnpike had no interest in trying. A company did not require everyone to think alike. It required them to understand what everyone else would do when thinking produced different answers.

Five months after Turnpike left Camp Lejeune, he sat beside Mercer in General Hammond's briefing room while officers argued about a fortress, two prisoners, one remembered tunnel, and how many people ought to be trusted with the distance between a landing site and a locked door.

Now the plan needed the platoon he had rebuilt as its forward eyes. It needed the two platoons Mercer had bound to it for everything eyes could not do. It needed a company because the mission contained company-sized responsibilities whether anybody chose to name them or not.

None of it had been luck. More importantly, none of it had happened in three weeks.

Chapter 26: The Buck Stops Here



Hammond waited until the last straggler had actually cleared the door before he said anything else, and when he did, his voice carried none of the formal weight it had held through the debate, just plain, direct concern.

Teal'c let the silence hold a moment, weighing exactly how much of the truth this moment actually required of him, and found he could not let all of it pass unanswered.

"Good." Hammond's hand found his shoulder, briefly, the same gesture Teal'c had watched him extend to exactly a handful of people across four years, none of them lightly chosen. "Get some rest if you can manage it. Bring your team home."

Three floors up and on the other side of the mountain, sixty-four Marines filled the largest room Peterson could give them, with four Navy corpsmen along the side wall and Captain Mercer standing beneath a projection of the same compound the planning group had spent the evening arguing over. Turnpike's twenty-two occupied the front rows. The rest formed the reserve at the release point, one bound behind the assault, not back aboard the ship. If security called a threat it could not stop, if the assault lost bodies, if a second entrance had to be opened, or if communications died, the reserve commander had pre-briefed triggers and did not need to wait for Turnpike to rediscover the plan over a radio.

"Now you're catching on," Turnpike said. "Recon is first in and leads the withdrawal. Security is first to take its blocking positions and last to leave them. Assault does not stop to win the building. It gets the package and moves. Support does not go hunting for a better shot. It makes the assault possible. Nobody spoke.

"Didn't figure you would," Turnpike said. Pride sat under the rasp without softening it. "Captain?"

Mercer stepped forward again. "Rehearsal starts at zero four hundred. By the time that ramp lowers, nobody in this company should need to ask what happens next."

Chapter 27: Preparing



The task-organized company got two days to prepare for a mission most of its Marines would never be told the personal shape of at all. Turnpike's six knew only that the recovered personnel mattered enough to make General Hammond spend a company on bringing them home. It was enough. Doyle still spent more time on his hair than strictly necessary. Kowalczyk still had an opinion about everything. Radio still hummed through half of every briefing like it was background music for something more interesting happening in his own head. None of it worried Turnpike now. Habits were not failures of discipline if they disappeared the moment the work began.

Day one was the ship. Bra'tac's Tel'tak had carried massed Jaffa before, but never sixty-four Marines trying to disembark in doctrinal order through one narrow ramp without turning the cargo hold into a traffic jam. They rehearsed the load backward from the order of movement: reserve deepest, then assault, support, security, command, reconnaissance nearest the ramp. Equipment was tied to function and placed with its element. Casualty litters remained reachable even with every seat occupied. The four corpsmen walked their routes blindfolded until they could reach the collection point through a hold full of bodies without asking anyone to move. Bra'tac imposed hand signals once the cloak went active and voice traffic became a liability. Doyle complained about the cargo hold's lighting. Turnpike told him enemy transport design was unlikely to protect his complexion.

Day two belonged to the raid. The training bay became a rough objective area: chalk ship, taped tree line, tunnel mouth, release point, blocking positions, holding block. They rehearsed each element separately, then together. Reconnaissance cleared and marked. Security occupied without bunching on the route. Support found firing positions that did not cross the assault's path. The breach team opened three different mock doors and refused a fourth because the hostage marker had been placed inside the blast danger area. The reserve rehearsed four commitment triggers, including total loss of communications. Then they ran withdrawal in doctrinal order until the recovered-personnel markers reached the ship, security came off last, and Captain Mercer could account for every element without a single voice asking who was missing.

On the ninth full run, a mock casualty in the assault element forced support to shift, the reserve to replace a security team, and recon to use the alternate route. The company lost forty-three seconds and no one lost the shape of the operation. Turnpike made them run it again anyway. By the end, Teal'c no longer saw sixty-four Marines performing adjacent tasks. He saw one plan retaining its shape while its parts changed places.

::do the Marines know? About me, I mean.::

::No,:: Teal'c said. ::Nor will they, unless circumstance requires it. Their role does not require the knowledge, and I have learned this year the value of only extending a truth this size to those who have genuine cause to carry it.::

By the second evening, watching the company complete its final withdrawal from the observation deck above the training bay, Teal'c found himself unexpectedly reassured. Whatever else went wrong tomorrow, nobody below was relying on courage to supply a missing piece of the plan. Every piece had an owner, an alternate, and a condition under which it changed.

While the Marines drilled a landing zone that would only ever exist as chalk lines and satellite photographs, Teal'c ran a drill of his own, one he had never once needed before Greg Veder and had no intention of walking into tomorrow without having run at least once, deliberately, under conditions calm enough to actually learn something.

They used the base's empty gymnasium after hours, a familiar room with mats, open floor, and no staff weapons in reach. Teal'c handed control over on his own terms and let Greg practice moving the body: walking a straight line, catching a thrown weight, the ordinary mechanics of a life Greg had never gotten to finish learning in his own.

Chapter 28: Into Apophis's Domain



Bra'tac's Tel'tak set down in a forest clearing forty minutes before dawn, cloak holding steady the whole descent, close enough to the compound's outer perimeter that the walk from landing site to tree line took minutes rather than hours, the scoured, cultivated ground Apophis's domains always favored close to a stronghold already visible even in the dark, land worked hard enough by conscripted labor that nothing grew there but what served the garrison directly.

The company moved in the order it had loaded. Turnpike's reconnaissance element came off first, Doyle's team through the narrow ramp and into the tree line before anyone else put a boot on alien soil. They cleared the landing zone, marked the ship with an infrared reference only the Tau'ri could see, and pushed toward the release point on two offset routes rather than drawing one line every following element would be forced to reuse.

Mercer and the command cell followed reconnaissance far enough to establish control clear of the ramp. Security came next and divided without stopping. One team disappeared into the trees around the landing zone. Two more moved toward the ground overlooking the tunnel approaches, already choosing blocking positions from which they could isolate the objective without silhouetting themselves against it. Support carried the weight after them: crew-served weapons broken into man-portable loads, breaching kit, extra radios, litters. The assault element and SG-1 came next, then the reserve. Sixty-four Marines cleared one ramp without bunching because every one of them had known the order before the ship left Earth.

Only when Turnpike reported the release point secure did the assault and recovery elements move beyond it. His reconnaissance platoon stayed ahead. Bra'tac walked beside its point team now, reading terrain the way Teal'c had watched him read it a hundred times before, while Turnpike's Marines marked the primary route and quietly began looking for the alternate the briefing had required. Behind them, security owned the space they had passed through, support waited where it could move either inward or back toward the ship, and the reserve remained one bound behind the assault instead of a radio call and a long march away.

Nobody spoke beyond what the mission required. Carter checked her scanner readings every few hundred meters, silent, methodical; Daniel carried a pack heavier than his usual field kit, extra medical supplies nobody had needed to ask him to bring; O'Neill walked point beside Bra'tac with the loose, deceptively casual alertness of a man who had done this exact kind of approach more times than he'd ever bothered counting.

::this is really happening. This is actually really happening.::

::It is,:: Teal'c said. ::I need you steady, Greg Veder. Not silent. Steady. There is a difference, and I will need you able to feel the difference before this day is finished.::

::yeah. Okay. Steady. I can do steady.::

The tunnel entrance, when they found it, sat exactly where Bra'tac's four-hundred-year-old memory had placed it, a collapsed section of old irrigation channel that hadn't been an irrigation channel in generations, disguised well enough that O'Neill walked past it twice before Bra'tac corrected his path with a single raised hand.

"Four hundred years," O'Neill muttered, "and the man still remembers where the secret door is. I hate how good that is."

"Discipline, Colonel O'Neill," Bra'tac said, already lowering himself into the gap. "It is available to anyone willing to pay its price."

* * *

The tunnel itself ran narrow and low, forcing even Teal'c into a half-crouch for most of its length, air close and stale with centuries of disuse. Turnpike's lead team went first with shaded lights and Bra'tac at its shoulder. SG-1 and the Marine breach-and-assault team followed. Support brought only what the interior might require; the reserve halted at the release point, exactly where it had rehearsed, while security closed around both ends of the route. Bra'tac's memory held true turn after turn, a testament either to the genuine sharpness of a four-hundred-year-old mind or to how little this fortress had changed, likely both at once.

::how are you not freaking out right now. I'm freaking out and I'm not even the one who has to crawl through this.::

::I have crawled through worse tunnels toward worse ends,:: Teal'c said. ::This one, at least, I crawl through toward something I actually want at the far side of it.::

They emerged, forty minutes later, into a storage cellar beneath what Bra'tac identified, in a whisper, as the compound's secondary garrison quarters. Reconnaissance cleared the cellar and the first two junctions beyond it. One security team came forward to isolate the tunnel exit; support established a position in the cellar; the reserve remained below, ready to move on four different triggers without needing a fresh order. O'Neill brought the assault and recovery elements through only after each report arrived.

Nothing. Silence, and the ordinary distant sounds of a garrison going about a morning that did not yet know anything was wrong.

"So far, so good," O'Neill breathed.

"Indeed," Teal'c said, though he did not yet trust it.

"Daniel, Carter, you're with Bra'tac and Second Team. Get eyes on the holding block. Teal'c, First Team is with us." O'Neill looked to the Marine assault leader, received one nod, and returned it. "Both approaches move on my signal. Reserve stays at the release point unless Mercer commits it."

Carter caught Teal'c's eye before the team split, a brief, steady look that said more than the moment had time for. "We'll get them out," she said, quiet, certain. "All of them."

"Thank you, Major Carter."

Chapter 36: Shield-Brother



Three days is not very long, as these things went. Teal'c had lived through weeks that changed less than the last three had, and yet the base had already folded both of them, quietly, back into its own ordinary rhythm: reports filed, watches stood, a routine mission to a routine planet that produced nothing more dramatic than an argument between Daniel and a village elder over pottery dating techniques. It was, Teal'c thought, walking the corridor toward the commissary on the third such ordinary morning, precisely the kind of quiet a man learned not to trust, not because it was false, but because it never lasted, and some part of him was still waiting for whatever came next.

He had not told O'Neill the word's meaning. He had decided, that first night, that some things asked to be earned rather than simply given, and he had held the decision through the debrief, through Hammond's quiet endorsement in front of the visiting brass, through every ordinary day since, telling himself the man had asked for nothing more than the fact of the word itself, and that anything beyond it was a gift Teal'c reserved the right to withhold a while longer.

He should have known better than to expect that arrangement to hold. Four years of watching O'Neill work should have told him exactly how long "a while longer" was ever going to last, once curiosity actually had its hooks all the way in.

He heard them before he saw them, passing the open door of Daniel's office on his way to the commissary, and stopped, because something in the shape of the two voices inside told him this was not an ordinary Tuesday conversation about pottery.

"...and you're sure that's how it's spelled?" O'Neill's voice, half-apologetic, half-stubborn. "I might be butchering the sounds. I was kind of distracted when I heard it."

"Reasonably sure, given what you've given me to work with." Daniel was already flipping through one of his reference notebooks, the focus he reserved for an unfamiliar phrase taking over completely. "*Sha'reth kanar.* This has been rattling around in my head since Teal'c said it to my face and wouldn't translate it. O'Neill's eyes found Teal'c in the doorway before he answered, caught, and something in his face made it plain he hadn't meant for any of this to be overheard, though he didn't look especially sorry about it either.

"Had a hunch it mattered," he said, simple, unembarrassed, the words aimed half at Daniel and half, obviously, at the man he'd just been caught in front of. "Figured it was worth knowing what I was actually being called."

"Yeah." O'Neill's voice had gone quiet, all the half-apologetic stubbornness drained out of it, and he glanced back toward the doorway, toward Teal'c, something settling into place on his face that had clearly been sitting unresolved for three days and had just, finally, found somewhere to land. Chapter 38: The Waiting Room, Again

"You will not remember this conversation," the figure added, gently, "not in the way you remember the rest of it. That is simply how this works, and I will not pretend otherwise to either of you. "Then say the rest of it," Teal'c said, "while there is still time to hear it."

The figure considered them both a long moment, warmth in old eyes wearing a familiar face. "You gave each other exactly what the other one needed and never once knew how to ask for. That is rarer than either of you likely realizes, and rarer still that it happened by accident rather than by design." A pause, something almost fond in it. "Go on, now. Both of you. There is a book waiting to close, and I find, against my own better professional judgment, that I would rather you were there for the end of it than standing here with me a moment longer than necessary."

It turned to go, and stopped itself before it had gone more than a step, the horizon answering a second time, a second shape resolving out of the same absence that had produced the first.

This one did not wear the face with ease. It was simply his own, aged by four hundred real years rather than chosen and weathered by a life the figure beside him had only borrowed for an afternoon.

::wait,:: Greg said. ::wait, there's two of him...::

"There is one of me," the real Bra'tac said, dry, "and one more polished imitation, whom I understand I have you both to thank for putting to good use." He inclined his head toward the figure beside him. "I do not fully understand how I came to be standing here. I understand only that when a debt of this kind calls, a man answers it, however strange the summons."

The figure inclined its head in turn, something like genuine respect passing between the two faces. "I borrowed nothing I did not already recognize as worth borrowing," it said, and then, without further ceremony, stepped backward into the horizon and was simply gone, leaving the real Bra'tac standing alone in the sand.

Greg found himself, for possibly the first time in this entire ordeal, genuinely speechless.

"Greg Veder." Nothing was borrowed in the real Bra'tac's voice now. "I have lived four hundred years learning to be surprised by very little. You, it seems, are the exception."

"...hi," Greg managed, finally. "I've, um. I've heard a lot about you. Obviously. Through," he gestured vaguely at Teal'c, "the whole shared-body thing."

"As I have heard a great deal about you," Bra'tac said, "though secondhand, through a man who does not waste words on things unworthy of them, which I have come to understand is the highest compliment he is capable of paying anyone." He studied Greg a long moment, warmth working into an expression that had spent four hundred years learning not to show it easily. "Sha'reth kanar. I taught Teal'c that word before you were born, on a world you have never heard of, for reasons that had nothing to do with either of us knowing this day would ever come. I find I am glad, beyond anything I expected to feel about it, that the word found a second home."

Teal'c, watching the two of them, found he had nothing useful left to add, and settled instead for simply standing there, letting the moment be exactly as large as it actually was.

* * *

The desert began to fade the way it always had, sand losing its grip on solidity, and Teal'c felt, rather than saw, Greg's separate shape dissolving back into something that was simply presence again, familiar, shared, no longer requiring its own shadow to be real.

::hey,:: Greg said, quieter now, already half something else again. ::for what it's worth. Best insertion job I've ever heard of. Ten out of ten. Would get accidentally merged with a four-hundred-year-old alien warrior again.::

::Considering the alternative was, by your own account, Rockville,:: Teal'c said, ::I have heard that comparison enough times now to understand precisely the compliment it actually is.::

::Yeah. Yeah, exactly.::

The fading paused, just once, one last interruption before the desert finally let them go.

::wait,:: Greg said. ::there's more of you out there, isn't there. Watching. This whole time.::

"Considerably more than either of you were ever told," said a voice neither of them recognized, dry, familiar in a way that took a full second to place, and then, all at once, entirely placed. "Consider this a professional courtesy. Most of the room doesn't get names. I'm making an exception."

A different voice, delighted, warm, entirely too pleased with itself for the moment to fully explain why, spoke up before Greg could answer. "Oh, I *love* this part. 'A voice.' No name, no face, just doing the heavy lifting from somewhere nobody's looking, and everybody assumes it's simple until it turns out to matter more than the person standing in front of them. That's my favorite kind of story. I have, personally, a great deal of experience being exactly that kind of voice."

**FURY:** Nobody asked for your resume.

"I wasn't asking permission," the voice said, cheerfully. "I was just being nice about announcing myself first. Most of the time I don't even bother with that part."

Greg's whole face did something complicated. ::wait. Wait, is that...::

"Yeah," the voice said, and there was no mistaking it now, the exact cadence of a man constitutionally incapable of leaving a silence alone. "It's me. Don't make it weird."

::YOU'VE BEEN IN THE GALLERY THIS WHOLE TIME? Oh my god, oh my god, that explains so much, I knew some of those lines had a voice I couldn't place...::

"In my defense," Jack said, "nobody actually told me I wasn't allowed to talk. I found out there was a room, and I assumed the usual rules applied. Turns out the usual rules were 'two guys do all the talking, everybody else sits quietly,' which, and I cannot stress this enough, has never once in my life been a rule I successfully followed."

**GLADLY:** We tried so hard.

**FURY:** He found a loophole in a rule we didn't even know we were enforcing.

Be still my heart. I do love a story that knows exactly where to bleed for maximum effect."

Chapter 39: The Honest Trailer



In a world where a four-hundred-year-old alien warrior had already defected from one god, buried a marriage he never got to save, and spent four full years perfecting the art of not needing anybody, one studio dared to ask the question nobody was asking: what if we gave him a teenage roommate he never agreed to?

This is Staff Infection.

Watch, as our stoic, taciturn protagonist, a First Prime with a vocabulary of maybe four words on a good day, gets passenger-seated by a sixteen-year-old forum poster with main-character syndrome and a documented, catastrophic inability to read a room. It's the story of two people who share one body, one brain, and, for roughly the first six chapters, zero mutual respect whatsoever.

*"I earned that respect,"* Gladly protested, from somewhere off to the side. *"Eventually. Give the kid a chapter or two."*

*"Nobody's arguing with you,"* Fury said. *"Sit down."*

Follow along as our reluctant, extremely reluctant, host discovers that his new passenger's one documented superpower is recognizing sigils from a canceled sci-fi parody show, a skill that saves a village, nearly gets a teammate killed the very next mission, and somehow, against every reasonable expectation, ends up being the exact reason a First Prime finally learns how to delegate.

This movie contains: one (1) recruitment scene set somewhere considerably more sand-based than advertised, four (4) years of unprocessed grief finally getting processed, exactly one (1) team-wide "we're basically five of us now" conversation this narrator will not apologize for finding genuinely moving, a rescue mission staffed by considerably more Marines than the studio originally budgeted for, and precisely zero (0) staff weapons harmed in the making of a single card game.

*"That poker scene earned its own line item,"* Gladly said. *"I stand by it."*

*"Nobody's stopping you."*

Because let's be honest, this genre never actually stopped being one premise wearing different faces: a driver who doesn't want company, a passenger who doesn't know how to be quiet, and a slow, grudging, entirely earned realization that maybe the company was the point all along. We got the reluctant-host version. We got the found-family version. We got the "actually it was always about the kid you didn't ask for" version. And now, four hundred years and one entire hostage rescue later, we've finally got the version where the alien warrior learns to laugh out loud, in front of witnesses.

*"First time in four years,"* Fury noted. *"Somebody wrote that down. Multiple times."*

So do yourself a favor, and enjoy: an ancient warrior code, a borrowed body, and a swordfight where the most dangerous person in the room was never actually the one holding the sword.

Marvel, as a stoic warrior's four-year emotional shutdown gets slowly, painstakingly dismantled by a teenager who cannot stop narrating his own life out loud, a process that involves exactly one dodgeball anecdote, one deeply regrettable cancelled-sci-fi-parody bit, and roughly a dozen instances of a very specific prose tic this narrator has personally sworn, on at least three separate occasions, to stop counting.

*"You didn't stop counting,"* Gladly said.

*"I said I'd swear to it. I didn't say I'd succeed."*

Featuring a rescue mission proposed by four different professionals, then rebuilt live into an actual company raid by a Recon gunnery sergeant with a Medal of Honor, six doctrinal boxes, and zero patience for any plan that assigns five of them to whoever happens to survive the first one.

*"Underrated MVP of this whole book,"* Fury said. *"Write that down somewhere it'll actually get read."*

*"Way ahead of you."*

Try to keep track of the running tally on a certain overused sentence construction, a tally so committed to its own bit it required its own explicit narration, its own decline curve, and at least one character catching the narrator doing it too.

*"We are still not discussing that."*

*"We're discussing it right now."*

Watch as a four-hundred-year-old rebel Jaffa steals an entire chapter he's technically only in for a scene, using nothing but a warrior's directness, a genuinely funny disciplinary metaphor, and the collective willingness of everyone around him to admit, out loud, that he was right the whole time.

*"He usually is,"* Gladly said, almost fondly. *"Infuriating trait in a mentor figure."*

Featuring an ending that doesn't resolve with a bigger fight, but with a father and son relearning how to be in the same room, a warrior finally saying a word out loud that he'd been carrying silently for months, and a passenger who spent his whole life not mattering to anybody discovering, somewhere in the middle of a staff-weapon fight against an actual god, that he mattered enough to be worth someone else's life.

*"Don't undersell it,"* Fury said, quiet, and there was nothing dry in it at all. *"That's the whole reason any of us showed up."*

And ask yourself the two questions this narrator simply cannot get past. One: how does a sixteen-year-old with no combat training, no discipline, and no volume control end up being the tactical difference-maker in three separate life-or-death situations? And two, far more pressingly: why did it take four years, one hostage rescue, and a room full of visiting brass for anybody to actually tell this man he was allowed to laugh?

*"To be fair,"* Gladly offered, *"First Primes are very good at not looking directly at their own feelings."*

*"That's the most honest thing anyone's said in this entire trailer."*

So c'mon, ride along with the warrior who can't stop protecting people, the kid who can't stop narrating, and the gallery that can't stop rooting for both of them, even when neither one asked.

**Starring:**

Christopher Judge, Speaking Approximately Nine Total Sentences In The First Six Chapters And Making Every One Of Them Count, as A Man Who Really Did Not Ask For Any Of This

Richard Dean Anderson, Deploying A Full Arsenal Of Deflective Sarcasm Against An Opponent Who Cannot Actually Hear Him Joke, as The Guy Who Noticed First And Said Nothing About It For Three Chapters On Purpose

Amanda Tapping, Radiating Barely Suppressed Scientific Curiosity At A Phenomenon Her Own Field Has No Actual Category For, as The One Who Asks The Follow-Up Question Everybody Else Was Too Polite To

Michael Shanks, Immediately And Correctly Identifying This As A Linguistics Problem, as The Man Who Will In Fact Translate Your Feelings Whether You Wanted Him To Or Not

Don S. Davis, Setting A New Personal Record For Number Of Times A General Says "Understood" While Clearly Understanding Considerably More Than He's Letting On, as The Boss Everybody Actually Deserved

Tony Amendola, Wearing Four Hundred Years Of Earned Weariness Like A Well-Fitted Coat, as The Grandfather Figure Nobody Knew They Needed Until He Showed Up And Immediately Started Bullying Everyone For Their Own Good

And Introducing An Unnamed, Uncredited Passenger, Never Once Physically Seen On Screen, Somehow Stealing Every Scene He's Technically Not Even In

Chapter 40: Staff Infection, A Retrospective



*The gallery, one last time. The doors behind them, all thirty-nine of them, stand quiet now.*

**GLADLY:** Okay. Big one. You want to do the honors, or should I?

**FURY:** You've done every honors so far. Go ahead.

**GLADLY:** *(clearing a throat that doesn't exist)* In a world where one Winslow sophomore's forum research got him mauled by dogs before he could finish a sentence he'd been building his whole short life toward... a teenager who spent years narrating himself as the hero of somebody else's show woke up as the world's least consensual roommate, inside a man whose entire vocabulary could fit on an index card. Neither of them asked for this. Both of them, thirty-nine chapters later, would tell you they wouldn't trade it back.

**FURY:** Not bad.

**GLADLY:** I've had time to workshop the whole-book version.

* * *

**GLADLY:** Can we talk about the waiting room? My scene. Before any of this.

**FURY:** The scowl.

**GLADLY:** The scowl! Nobody's brought up the scowl since chapter two, and I think about it constantly.

**FURY:** You meant that scowl, Gladly. That wasn't a script note. You actually felt something, right before you handed the rest of it off.

**GLADLY:** I did. Weirdest part of the whole assignment, honestly, getting exactly one line and a face I didn't plan on making, and that being the part that stuck. I said my one line, "well, that didn't turn out like you thought it would," and the kid just froze, and something about watching him freeze made the whole thing feel less like a bit and more like actually catching somebody at the worst second of his life. Which, you know. It was. Then I was gone and somebody else did the real work. But I got my one second, and I meant every word of it.

**FURY:** That's the job, though. Playful in the delivery, exact in the execution. You held both. First time out, too, no less.

**GLADLY:** I didn't know if I was doing it right.

**FURY:** Thirty-nine chapters later, kid's laughing at card games with his dad. You did it right.

* * *

**GLADLY:** Let's do the numbers. Self-control. Started at zero. Ended at... still not great, if we're honest, but he folded a bad hand on request in the last chapter without arguing about it first. That's growth.

**FURY:** Stick removal: also incomplete. But he laughed. Actually laughed, out loud, in front of witnesses, more than once by the end. First time in four years.

**GLADLY:** So neither diagnosis fully landed.

**FURY:** Neither diagnosis was ever supposed to fully land. Gladly said "a lesson in self control," not "total mastery of self control." He said "get the stick out of his butt," not "surgically remove the stick." Read the actual prescription next time.

**GLADLY:** ...Fair.

* * *

**GLADLY:** Okay, favorite bits. Go.

**FURY:** The swordfight line. "The most dangerous person in a swordfight was never the swordsman." Paid off twice. Once against a patrol, once against an actual god. That's a callback that earned its keep.

**GLADLY:** Mine's the card game. Last chapter. "Tell him his hand is terrible and he should fold." No stakes, no danger, just a dad talking to his kid through a stranger's voice, and it's funnier and sadder than half the fight scenes.

**FURY:** That's the whole show in one line, honestly.

**GLADLY:** What's yours *actually*, though? The real one, not the tactic one.

**FURY:** *(a pause)* Rya'c folding the weapon down an inch at a time. Nobody wrote that as a big moment. It was the biggest moment in the book.

* * *

**GLADLY:** Should we talk about the tic?

**FURY:** We should absolutely talk about the tic.

**GLADLY:** Eight, nine, ten times through the middle stretch, by my count. Twice a chapter at the start. Once every other chapter through the middle. And then...

**FURY:** Nothing. Last dozen chapters. Clean.

**GLADLY:** Even the reveal earlier, where I caught you doing it...

**FURY:** We are still not discussing that.

**GLADLY:** ...even that stopped happening. Somebody actually learned.

**FURY:** Second book anybody's written all the way through in this whole sandbox. Wasn't sure it'd happen this fast. Glad it did.

* * *

**GLADLY:** Okay. The bigger question. Was this worth it? All of it? The dogs, the interrogation room, the kid with the staff weapon at the end?

**FURY:** Ask the guy who folded a bad poker hand and laughed about it a week after nearly dying in a hostage rescue. Ask his son, who called him "father" for the first time in four years and meant it. Ask the passenger who spent his whole life not mattering to anybody and ended up mattering enough to talk down a god's entire garrison from inside somebody else's skull.

**GLADLY:** That's not really an answer.

**FURY:** It's the only answer that was ever going to be honest. Nobody in this story got everything fixed. They got further along than they'd have gotten alone. That was always the deal. Read the series bible.

* * *

**GLADLY:** What happens now? To them, I mean. After the last page.

**FURY:** Same thing that happens to everybody in this sandbox once the door closes on their story. They keep living it. We just don't get to watch anymore.

**GLADLY:** That's kind of sad.

**FURY:** It's kind of the point. We're not the main characters, Gladly. Never were. We're just the ones who got to watch somebody else figure out how to be less alone.

* * *

**GLADLY:** One more thing. Before we go.

**FURY:** What.

**GLADLY:** Was I any good at this? The riffing. The commentary. All forty chapters of it.

**FURY:** *(a long pause, the kind he doesn't usually give anyone)* Yeah, Gladly. You were good at this.

**GLADLY:** ...Thanks.

**FURY:** Don't make it weird.

**GLADLY:** Too late. Already weird. Been weird since chapter two.

*A door opens somewhere behind them, quiet, unhurried, the next entry's own beginning waiting on the other side of it. Neither of them walks through it. That's not their story to tell.*

*Fade. Staff Infection, complete. Thirty-nine chapters, one stubborn stick, one slowly-learning self-control problem, and, somewhere on the other side of a door neither man ever fully closed on the other, two people who turned out to be exactly what the other one had always needed and never once thought to ask for.*

*Indeed.*
