The Buck Stops Here

Hammond waited until the last straggler had actually cleared the door before he said anything else, and when he did, his voice carried none of the formal weight it had held through the debate, just plain, direct concern.

"You know, Teal'c. We have a saying here on Earth. One very well earned. And it's one I can't in good conscience ignore." He met Teal'c's eyes directly. "The buck stops here."

"Sir?"

"I've been doing this job a long time, son. Long enough to know the difference between a man having a good week and a man who's quietly become somebody I don't fully recognize anymore, in ways that keep showing up sideways in briefings I'm supposed to be running. An acquaintance with unusually specific tactical opinions. A fighting style nobody's ever been able to fully explain to my satisfaction. A quiet I didn't used to see in you, and don't, quite as often, anymore."

Teal'c let the silence hold a moment, weighing exactly how much of the truth this moment actually required of him, and found he could not let all of it pass unanswered.

"The acquaintance I posited as the source of these insights is not a lie, exactly," he said. "But it is a prevarication. One I have no wish to continue perpetrating toward someone I respect."

Hammond set the folder down. "I appreciate you telling me that much, and I'm not going to pretend it doesn't change how I hear the rest. But I'm not asking for the whole explanation tonight. You've got a rescue to survive tomorrow, and I won't load you down with a second reckoning before it. Hear me clearly, though. I know something is different. I trust you enough not to demand its shape tonight. That trust has a limit. When this is over, win or lose, you and I are going to sit down and have an actual conversation. Not a debrief. A conversation."

"Understood, sir."

"Good." Hammond's hand found his shoulder, briefly, the same gesture Teal'c had watched him extend to exactly a handful of people across four years, none of them lightly chosen. "Get some rest if you can manage it. Bring your team home."

Hammond left him beside the projection, alone now in truth rather than merely in appearance.

::he knows something's up. Like, actually knows, not just vibes.::

::He does,:: Teal'c said. ::And has chosen, for reasons I suspect owe more to kindness than to caution, not to demand the answer tonight.::

::that's... actually a really good boss, huh.::

::He is,:: Teal'c said. ::I have not always allowed myself to say so.::

::we're really doing this.::

::We are,:: Teal'c said. ::Together, this time. That is the part I could not have managed alone, four years ago.::

Three floors up and on the other side of the mountain, sixty-four Marines filled the largest room Peterson could give them, with four Navy corpsmen along the side wall and Captain Mercer standing beneath a projection of the same compound the planning group had spent the evening arguing over. Turnpike's twenty-two occupied the front rows. His original six sat together by habit, not privilege now, the problems he had inherited five months ago folded into a platoon that no longer treated them as separate pieces.

Mercer handled the things a captain had to handle: authority, command succession, communications failure, abort criteria, the exact line beyond which O'Neill's go or no-go became the raid force's movement order. Then he stepped aside.

"Gunny built the scheme," Mercer said. "Gunny briefs the scheme."

Turnpike went to the map without notes. Six words were written down its left side in block capitals: COMMAND. RECONNAISSANCE. SECURITY. SUPPORT. ASSAULT. RESERVE.

"Those are not departments," he said. "They're promises. Every promise has a name beside it before we leave this room."

He began at the top. Mercer and the company command cell would stay where they could control the raid instead of chasing the loudest sound. Turnpike's platoon was reconnaissance. Doyle's team would be first off the ramp, clear the landing zone, find the release point, confirm the tunnel entrance, and mark both the primary and alternate withdrawal routes. The rest of the platoon would keep the reconnaissance forward as the raid moved, watching the terrain and the enemy instead of getting absorbed into somebody else's firefight.

The second platoon divided by task. Its security teams would isolate the landing zone, the tunnel mouth, and the two most likely garrison approaches. They were not there to defend ground forever. They were there to keep outside forces outside until the recovery team crossed back through. Its support team carried the heavier weapons, demolition kit, extra communications, and casualty equipment. It would establish positions from which it could support the assault without firing through the people the assault had gone there to recover.

The third platoon supplied the Marine breach-and-assault team that would enter with SG-1 and Bra'tac. The rest formed the reserve at the release point, one bound behind the assault, not back aboard the ship. If security called a threat it could not stop, if the assault lost bodies, if a second entrance had to be opened, or if communications died, the reserve commander had pre-briefed triggers and did not need to wait for Turnpike to rediscover the plan over a radio.

"So we're walking point for a bunch of officers into God knows what," Doyle said, studying the arrows now instead of his reflection, "then turning around and walking point back out."

"Now you're catching on," Turnpike said. "Recon is first in and leads the withdrawal. Security is first to take its blocking positions and last to leave them. Assault does not stop to win the building. It gets the package and moves. Support does not go hunting for a better shot. It makes the assault possible. Reserve does not invent itself when somebody gets scared. It moves on the conditions written beside its name."

Kowalczyk raised a hand without waiting to be recognized. "And if the four-hundred-year-old tunnel isn't there?"

"Then Colonel O'Neill calls no-go at the release point and every element reverses its movement without advertising that we visited. Recon leads us out. Security comes out last. Same order, no objective. We do not turn uncertainty into courage by walking deeper into it."

Radio had hummed something unplaceable through the administrative half of the briefing. He stopped when Turnpike reached the casualty plan. Four corpsmen, one with the assault, one with support, two at the collection point aboard the Tel'tak. Every element carried its own first-line evacuation responsibility; nobody abandoned security because someone fifty yards away shouted for a medic.

Turnpike looked across all sixty-four of them when he finished. "Anybody wants out, speak now. No black mark. This is not the kind of operation you talk yourself into because the room went quiet and you got embarrassed."

Nobody spoke.

"Didn't figure you would," Turnpike said. Pride sat under the rasp without softening it. "Captain?"

Mercer stepped forward again. "Rehearsal starts at zero four hundred. By the time that ramp lowers, nobody in this company should need to ask what happens next."
