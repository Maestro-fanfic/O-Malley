What Gets Him Killed

Greg had rehearsed this conversation nine different ways on the walk over, and every single version of it ended with somebody finally admitting he'd been right.

Version one had him cool about it, arms crossed, barely looking up from whatever he was doing when she showed. Version four had actual dialogue, back-and-forth, the kind of exchange where he got off a line good enough that she almost smiled before catching herself. Version seven was the one he liked best and trusted least, the one where she didn't threaten him at all, where she just looked at him a beat too long and said something like huh, and in his head that single syllable did more work than any speech could have. He knew, in the part of his brain that occasionally got a word in edgewise, that real conversations didn't go the way you rehearsed them. He'd had enough of those, with enough people, to know better. He kept rehearsing anyway.

✳

Cut to the gallery.

GLADLY: Oh, I love this part. Nine drafts and none of them are good.

THE WATCHER: Everybody rehearses the version where they win. Nobody rehearses the version where they don't even get to finish the sentence.

FURY: You're gonna feel a lot less for him in about ten minutes.

✳

That was the part he kept coming back to, walking. Not the danger, though he'd thought about that too, in the abstract, the way you think about a highway on-ramp you've never actually merged onto, a thing that was real and bad for other people and would obviously sort itself out fine once it was actually his turn. The part that kept his feet moving down Ellsworth past the last working streetlight, past the shuttered nail salon and the lot where a gas station used to be, was the ending. The one where Bitch, or somebody who mattered, somebody who'd tell somebody else, looked at him and went, huh. Guess Veder wasn't just talk.

He had it narrowed down. Three blocks, maybe four, of half-collapsed chain-link and gravel lots gone to weeds, warehouse roofs sagging in the middle like they'd given up holding a shape. Somewhere behind one of these fences was where she kept them between jobs. He'd cross-referenced sightings, three separate threads that each thought they had the only good lead, none of them noticing the others said almost the same thing from a different angle. He'd read every one of them twice, the real ones, not the joke threads people made about him for caring this much, the ones where somebody screenshotted his last post and somebody else added a laughing emoji and that was the whole reply. He was close, with the specific, itchy certainty of a guy who already knows he's right and just needs the world to catch up. He could feel how close he was the way you feel a test answer sitting right on the tip of your tongue, so close that stopping now would have been its own kind of failure, worse somehow than never trying.

Nobody else thought this was a good idea. His mom hadn't said anything about it, because his mom never said anything about anything he did anymore, one way or the other, which was its own kind of answer if he was being honest with himself, and he generally wasn't, not about that, not tonight. His dad would have called it exactly the kind of stunt that proved Greg couldn't be trusted with anything that mattered, and then, somehow, in a way Greg had never once managed to argue his way out of, made it Greg's fault that his dad had to say so. And the two people online who'd actually bothered to reply to his plan, out of a whole thread's worth of silence otherwise, had both said, in slightly different words, dude, don't. One of them had typed out three separate paragraphs about it, actually cared enough to make an argument instead of just a joke, and Greg had skimmed maybe half of it before deciding the guy just didn't get it.

He'd told them that, more or less. They didn't get it. What none of them got was that this wasn't really about Bitch at all. It was about being the guy who found her. The guy who wasn't scared of the scary cape, who walked right up and proved, once, to somebody who'd remember it, that he'd earned a seat at whatever table these people all seemed to have and never once thought to pull a chair out for him.

The fence line ended in a gap somebody had bent wide open a long time ago, rust-bled and sagging on its hinge, and beyond it a lot full of rusted machinery, a stack of pallets gone gray and soft with weather, and one low, dark building that didn't look like anything from the street. No lights. No sound he could place, not yet, though the night was holding its breath in a way that made him certain, the way certainty always feels right before it turns out wrong, that he had the right place.

He almost turned around there. He'd remember that later, or he would have, if there'd been a later that worked the way laters were supposed to, that one real flicker of a thought that maybe this was exactly the kind of thing the two guys online had meant. He looked at it for a second too long, the dark building sitting there with the stillness of a place that's pretending not to be occupied, and something old and sensible in him said, quietly, you could just go home. Nobody would even know you got this close. He could still tell himself that part, at least. That he'd gotten close.

He didn't go home. He found, a few feet inside the gap, an empty sack of dog food gone soft with rain, the plastic torn open and licked clean, and something about the ordinariness of it, generic brand, a coupon sticker still stuck to one corner, made the whole thing feel suddenly, sickeningly real in a way three separate forum threads never quite had. This was it. This was actually it. He felt the last of his nerve trying to talk him out of it one more time, and did what he always did with that voice: talked over it, louder, faster, until it gave up and went quiet.

Greg stood at the gap a second longer than he needed to, working up the last of it, cataloguing the whole walk over one more time like it was proof of something. He'd come this far. He'd done the research nobody else bothered to do. He thought, distinctly, in the voice he used for narrating his own life back to himself, the voice that made even standing alone in a scrapyard at night feel like it was building toward something: this is the part where it either goes great or it doesn't.

✳

Cut to the gallery. Gladly opens his mouth.

FURY: Don't.

GLADLY: I wasn't going to say anything.

Nobody says anything else until it's over.

✳

It didn't.

He heard the first one before he saw it, a sound too big and too fast to be anything on two legs, gravel scattering under something with real weight behind it, and by the time his brain caught up enough to supply the word dog the word had already stopped being useful. This wasn't a dog the way anything he'd ever met was a dog. It came out of the dark at a size that didn't make sense against the shape of the lot around it, bone spurs catching what little light there was, and Greg's whole carefully rehearsed nine-version speech evaporated into one single, useless thought, over and over, on a loop: this was a mistake, this was a mistake, this was a mistake.

He turned to run and there was a second one already behind him, cutting the gap in the fence off before he'd covered half the distance to it, and somewhere in the part of his mind that hadn't caught up yet to how bad this actually was, he had time to notice, with a kind of horrified, cheated indignation, that none of this felt like it did in his head. There was no clever line. There was no moment where he found the nerve from somewhere and it turned out he'd had it in him all along, the way it always worked out in version seven. There was just noise, and weight, and the ground coming up fast, and every single thing he'd told himself about who he was going to be tonight turning out to be exactly as true as everyone had warned him it wasn't.

He didn't get to finish the thought.

The world stopped, mid-motion, mid-fall, mid-everything, and went perfectly, completely silent.
