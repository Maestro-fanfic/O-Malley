Chapter 25 of 40

Same Dirt

The mission that changed the platoon's status looked safer than the three before it.

A three-person science team had left a research cache on an allied world eight months earlier when a medical emergency forced an immediate evacuation. The settlement nearest the Gate had confirmed the site remained untouched. The route was known. The local government had approved the recovery. Turnpike's platoon would provide reconnaissance and security, retrieve the cache, and come home before dinner.

Routine, on paper.

Turnpike had lived long enough to regard the phrase as a warning label.

He sent Doyle's team ahead of the scientists, with Kowalczyk reading the ground and Benitez carrying the forward radio. Alvarez had the team. Bishop carried the long glass. Haney carried equipment he had inventoried twice and modified not at all without authorization. The other sixteen Marines moved in offset support, far enough apart that one burst of fire could not turn a platoon into a casualty list.

The first sign the brief was wrong was not a person. It was a fresh boot mark inside the abandoned site's western drainage ditch, sharp-edged despite the previous night's rain.

Doyle found it.

Alvarez halted movement without looking back for permission. Kowalczyk gave him the likely approach in nine words. Bishop found a position covering it. Benitez reported. Haney checked the cache building for the wire and disturbed ground that would announce a trap.

For thirty seconds, the platoon did everything right.

Then one of the scientists stepped forward to see what Doyle had found.

Six armed locals rose from concealment around the drainage line, territorial rather than ideological, protecting salvage rights to a cache they had apparently been stripping for months. The lead man shouted something the translator did not resolve quickly enough. The scientist answered with empty hands and a carefully rehearsed greeting. Someone behind the lead man fired before either side understood the other.

Doyle saw the flanking position as the first shot landed and called it while dropping into cover. The second shot grazed his forearm. He kept calling distance and direction while blood ran into his glove.

Kowalczyk said four words on the radio. "Left ditch, two moving." Then he stopped talking and covered the movement he had named.

Bishop had civilians behind his first angle and did not fire. He stated the obstruction, shifted three steps, reacquired, and fired only when the line was clear. The hesitation in his record did not appear. Judgment did.

Haney threw smoke where Alvarez pointed rather than where instinct first suggested. Benitez kept the platoon net ordered while the science team shouted over one another. Alvarez moved the civilians, assigned suppression, and began the withdrawal before Turnpike gave him anything more than commander's intent.

Turnpike put a round into the flanking position Doyle had identified. The threat stopped moving. He did not waste time deciding whether that made the morning a victory.

The other sixteen Marines closed around the lead team by sections, not as a crowd running toward gunfire. One element covered. One moved. One took the scientists and cache. Benitez walked backward through the final hundred yards to the Gate, weapon up between transmissions, his voice low and steady enough that the civilians obeyed him without ever understanding how close the mission had come to ending differently.

Nobody in the platoon died. Doyle needed six stitches and acquired a story he improved slightly every time he told it. One local was dead and two wounded by the time their government reached the site. The scientists came home with their cache, their bodies intact, and their assumptions considerably less so.

Turnpike's after-action report ran nine pages.

The first three documented the platoon's actions. The fourth identified the intelligence failures. The fifth asked why an eight-month-old abandonment site had been labeled secure based on a settlement's assurance rather than direct observation. The sixth through eighth recommended changes to escort doctrine, advance reconnaissance, translator placement, and civilian control. The ninth listed every Marine who had made a decision without waiting for Turnpike and whether the decision had served the mission.

Hammond read it personally. He summoned Turnpike to the mountain for the first time two days later.

"You wrote more about what failed above your platoon than what succeeded inside it," Hammond said, the report open between them.

"My Marines already know what they did, sir. The people writing the next brief need to know what they didn't."

"Major Rusk tells me your attachment expires next week."

"Major Rusk has been telling me that for two months."

The corner of Hammond's mouth moved without quite becoming a smile. "I'm extending it. More than that, I am expanding the Marine reconnaissance capability attached to this command. Your platoon will be the first piece, not the whole of it. Major Rusk's authority over the attachment ends when the new orders take effect. You will have a company commander. Somebody who understands what this program needs and what your Marines need from it."

"That combination narrow the list much?"

"Considerably."

The door opened behind Turnpike.

"Still writing reports nobody wants to read, Gunny?"

Turnpike knew the voice before he turned. Marcus Mercer had been a lieutenant the last time they had occupied the same patch of ground, young enough to believe the signed route on a map deserved more confidence than the sergeant telling him the hillside had moved. The hillside had moved. The enemy had moved with it. Turnpike had brought Mercer's Marines out through a route neither map nor plan contained, and Mercer had spent the following month writing the recommendation that eventually put a Medal of Honor in a box Turnpike preferred not to open.

Mercer wore captain's bars now and the same expression he had worn after that earlier extraction, relief compressed so tightly it presented as irritation.

"Still arriving after the shooting stops, Lieutenant?" Turnpike asked.

"Captain."

"We'll see."

Hammond folded his hands over the open report. "Captain Mercer learned you were attached to this program four days ago. Since then, he has called in favors from two commands, one general officer, and somebody at Headquarters Marine Corps who declined to tell me what Captain Mercer had on him."

"Nothing worth putting in writing, sir," Mercer said.

Turnpike looked him over again, this time less amused. "You did all that to inherit three platoons and a classified posting you weren't cleared to ask about?"

"I heard somebody was sending you up another hill." Mercer met his eyes. "Last time, you went up it with me. I wasn't letting you take this one without me."

Hammond looked between them. "I take it introductions are unnecessary."

"We've chewed some of the same dirt," Mercer said.

"He means I kept him from eating all of it," Turnpike said.

Mercer ignored that with the ease of long practice. "I read the report. Map failed again."

"Marines didn't."

"That's why I'm here."

The next six weeks were not spent pretending three platoons became a company because an organization chart placed them under one captain. Mercer brought two established Force Recon platoons into the compartment. Turnpike's platoon knew the Gate and the program; the other two knew each other, their own methods, and exactly how little they enjoyed being treated as late arrivals to somebody else's secret war.

Mercer made command relationships explicit. Turnpike made standards common. The platoons exchanged evaluators, communicators, medics, and opposing forces. They learned one set of hand signals, one casualty procedure, one reporting format. They ran insertion drills in aircraft hangars before any of them knew the transport they would eventually use was not built on Earth. They rehearsed loss of communications until initiative stopped looking like disobedience and started looking like intent surviving contact.

Turnpike did not turn the other platoons into copies of his. Mercer would not have allowed it, and Turnpike had no interest in trying. A company did not require everyone to think alike. It required them to understand what everyone else would do when thinking produced different answers.

Five months after Turnpike left Camp Lejeune, he sat beside Mercer in General Hammond's briefing room while officers argued about a fortress, two prisoners, one remembered tunnel, and how many people ought to be trusted with the distance between a landing site and a locked door.

Now the plan needed the platoon he had rebuilt as its forward eyes. It needed the two platoons Mercer had bound to it for everything eyes could not do. It needed a company because the mission contained company-sized responsibilities whether anybody chose to name them or not.

None of it had been luck. More importantly, none of it had happened in three weeks.
