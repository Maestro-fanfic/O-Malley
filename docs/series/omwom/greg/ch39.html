The Honest Trailer

In a world where a four-hundred-year-old alien warrior had already defected from one god, buried a marriage he never got to save, and spent four full years perfecting the art of not needing anybody, one studio dared to ask the question nobody was asking: what if we gave him a teenage roommate he never agreed to?

This is Staff Infection.

Watch, as our stoic, taciturn protagonist, a First Prime with a vocabulary of maybe four words on a good day, gets passenger-seated by a sixteen-year-old forum poster with main-character syndrome and a documented, catastrophic inability to read a room. It's the story of two people who share one body, one brain, and, for roughly the first six chapters, zero mutual respect whatsoever.

"I earned that respect," Gladly protested, from somewhere off to the side. "Eventually. Give the kid a chapter or two."

"Nobody's arguing with you," Fury said. "Sit down."

Follow along as our reluctant, extremely reluctant, host discovers that his new passenger's one documented superpower is recognizing sigils from a canceled sci-fi parody show, a skill that saves a village, nearly gets a teammate killed the very next mission, and somehow, against every reasonable expectation, ends up being the exact reason a First Prime finally learns how to delegate.

This movie contains: one (1) recruitment scene set somewhere considerably more sand-based than advertised, four (4) years of unprocessed grief finally getting processed, exactly one (1) team-wide "we're basically five of us now" conversation this narrator will not apologize for finding genuinely moving, a rescue mission staffed by considerably more Marines than the studio originally budgeted for, and precisely zero (0) staff weapons harmed in the making of a single card game.

"That poker scene earned its own line item," Gladly said. "I stand by it."

"Nobody's stopping you."

Because let's be honest, this genre never actually stopped being one premise wearing different faces: a driver who doesn't want company, a passenger who doesn't know how to be quiet, and a slow, grudging, entirely earned realization that maybe the company was the point all along. We got the reluctant-host version. We got the found-family version. We got the "actually it was always about the kid you didn't ask for" version. And now, four hundred years and one entire hostage rescue later, we've finally got the version where the alien warrior learns to laugh out loud, in front of witnesses.

"First time in four years," Fury noted. "Somebody wrote that down. Multiple times."

So do yourself a favor, and enjoy: an ancient warrior code, a borrowed body, and a swordfight where the most dangerous person in the room was never actually the one holding the sword.

Marvel, as a stoic warrior's four-year emotional shutdown gets slowly, painstakingly dismantled by a teenager who cannot stop narrating his own life out loud, a process that involves exactly one dodgeball anecdote, one deeply regrettable cancelled-sci-fi-parody bit, and roughly a dozen instances of a very specific prose tic this narrator has personally sworn, on at least three separate occasions, to stop counting.

"You didn't stop counting," Gladly said.

"I said I'd swear to it. I didn't say I'd succeed."

Featuring a rescue mission proposed by four different professionals, then rebuilt live into an actual company raid by a Recon gunnery sergeant with a Medal of Honor, six doctrinal boxes, and zero patience for any plan that assigns five of them to whoever happens to survive the first one.

"Underrated MVP of this whole book," Fury said. "Write that down somewhere it'll actually get read."

"Way ahead of you."

Try to keep track of the running tally on a certain overused sentence construction, a tally so committed to its own bit it required its own explicit narration, its own decline curve, and at least one character catching the narrator doing it too.

"We are still not discussing that."

"We're discussing it right now."

Watch as a four-hundred-year-old rebel Jaffa steals an entire chapter he's technically only in for a scene, using nothing but a warrior's directness, a genuinely funny disciplinary metaphor, and the collective willingness of everyone around him to admit, out loud, that he was right the whole time.

"He usually is," Gladly said, almost fondly. "Infuriating trait in a mentor figure."

Featuring an ending that doesn't resolve with a bigger fight, but with a father and son relearning how to be in the same room, a warrior finally saying a word out loud that he'd been carrying silently for months, and a passenger who spent his whole life not mattering to anybody discovering, somewhere in the middle of a staff-weapon fight against an actual god, that he mattered enough to be worth someone else's life.

"Don't undersell it," Fury said, quiet, and there was nothing dry in it at all. "That's the whole reason any of us showed up."

And ask yourself the two questions this narrator simply cannot get past. One: how does a sixteen-year-old with no combat training, no discipline, and no volume control end up being the tactical difference-maker in three separate life-or-death situations? And two, far more pressingly: why did it take four years, one hostage rescue, and a room full of visiting brass for anybody to actually tell this man he was allowed to laugh?

"To be fair," Gladly offered, "First Primes are very good at not looking directly at their own feelings."

"That's the most honest thing anyone's said in this entire trailer."

So c'mon, ride along with the warrior who can't stop protecting people, the kid who can't stop narrating, and the gallery that can't stop rooting for both of them, even when neither one asked.

Starring:

Christopher Judge, Speaking Approximately Nine Total Sentences In The First Six Chapters And Making Every One Of Them Count, as A Man Who Really Did Not Ask For Any Of This

Richard Dean Anderson, Deploying A Full Arsenal Of Deflective Sarcasm Against An Opponent Who Cannot Actually Hear Him Joke, as The Guy Who Noticed First And Said Nothing About It For Three Chapters On Purpose

Amanda Tapping, Radiating Barely Suppressed Scientific Curiosity At A Phenomenon Her Own Field Has No Actual Category For, as The One Who Asks The Follow-Up Question Everybody Else Was Too Polite To

Michael Shanks, Immediately And Correctly Identifying This As A Linguistics Problem, as The Man Who Will In Fact Translate Your Feelings Whether You Wanted Him To Or Not

Don S. Davis, Setting A New Personal Record For Number Of Times A General Says "Understood" While Clearly Understanding Considerably More Than He's Letting On, as The Boss Everybody Actually Deserved

Tony Amendola, Wearing Four Hundred Years Of Earned Weariness Like A Well-Fitted Coat, as The Grandfather Figure Nobody Knew They Needed Until He Showed Up And Immediately Started Bullying Everyone For Their Own Good

And Introducing An Unnamed, Uncredited Passenger, Never Once Physically Seen On Screen, Somehow Stealing Every Scene He's Technically Not Even In
