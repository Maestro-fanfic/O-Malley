The Rescue Plan

Bra'tac found O'Neill alone for perhaps thirty seconds before the rest of the team filed into the briefing room, and used every one of them.

"Colonel." He said it plainly, no preamble, the same directness he brought to anything that actually mattered to him. "I owe you a correction, and an apology alongside it, though I suspect you have no memory of the offense itself. I have, for longer than I care to admit, carried a private name for you, one I gave you the day we met and never once revisited since. It was unearned then. It has been unearned every day since. I am sorry for the years I spent not bothering to notice that."

O'Neill blinked, clearly working to catch up to a conversation that had started somewhere he hadn't been invited to yet. "Okay. I'm gonna need at least a little more context for that apology to land the way I think you want it to."

"You do not need the word itself. You need only know that I was wrong to hold it, and that I do not hold it any longer." Bra'tac's hand found his shoulder, brief, firm. "Teal'c has named you Sha'reth kanar, Colonel. He told me so himself, plainly, without my having to ask twice. That, more than anything else he could have said, convinces me I was wrong about you in ways I had not even finished accounting for. That is the whole of what I came over here to say. I did not want the honesty to wait for a better moment. Better moments have a way of never actually arriving."

Something in O'Neill's face settled, the look of a man handed something he didn't quite know how to hold yet. "Yeah. Okay. Don't know what I did to earn taking it back, but I'll take it." A beat, the corner of his mouth twitching despite himself. "For the record, whatever the word was, I probably deserved it for at least the first two years."

"You did not," Bra'tac said, entirely without humor. "You did nothing to earn taking it back. Just as you did nothing to earn it in the first place." He did not elaborate further before turning to find his seat as the rest of the team began filing in.

✳

O'Neill's options landed on Hammond's desk at 1745 hours, fifteen minutes ahead of the deadline, and by 1900 the full team, plus Bra'tac, plus a visibly uncomfortable Air Force colonel from a division Teal'c had never worked with directly, were assembled in the largest briefing room the mountain had, staring at a three-dimensional projection of the compound rendered in more detail than the morning's flat imagery had allowed.

Gunnery Sergeant Turnpike arrived last, freshly read into the program and looking like a man who still was not entirely convinced any of this was real. Captain Marcus J. Mercer came in beside him, lean, gray beginning at both temples, carrying himself with the unadvertised certainty of an officer who had learned command somewhere less forgiving than a classroom. For one brief, strange moment the room came up out of its chairs to meet Turnpike, Hammond included, full attention and a salute held a beat longer than protocol strictly demanded. Turnpike returned it, flat and unbothered, already angling for the nearest empty chair before the gesture had finished, a man plainly used to a reaction he had never learned to enjoy. Mercer did not look surprised by the room's response. He was the only person there, Teal'c noticed, who watched Turnpike instead, making certain the honor had been received before he took his own seat.

✳

"Three options," O'Neill said, pointing with the same fork-like gesture he used on actual food, minus the fork. "Door one: full tactical assault, hit the main gate hard and fast, overwhelming force, in and out before the garrison fully mobilizes. Pros: fast. Cons: extremely loud, extremely visible, and gives Apophis roughly zero warning time to do something we'd all regret if he decides hostages are more trouble than they're worth."

Turnpike, who had spent considerably more of his career on the business end of hit it hard and fast than anyone else currently in the room, said nothing for a moment, then said it anyway. "Overwhelming force works fine when you know what you're overwhelming. I've run some version of that exact plan more times than I care to count, and it works right up until the compound isn't laid out the way intel said it was, or the people you're there to save aren't standing where intel said they'd be either. You go in loud and hard against a floor plan you're guessing at, best case you win ugly. Worst case, the two of them end up caught in your own crossfire before anybody on your side even sees them." A beat, deliberate, long enough that nobody in the room could mistake it for forgetting. He'd said everything he actually meant to say first, in the order he meant to say it, and only then, plainly, on his own terms and nobody else's, added the one word rank technically required of him. "Sir."

✳

"Which he might," Carter said, quietly. "If he reads it as a rescue attempt rather than a raid, the incentive structure changes fast."

"Door two." O'Neill kept moving.

"If I may," the colonel said, cutting in before O'Neill got a second word into it. "The approach I'd favor for door two is the straightforward one. Gate address two kilometers out. Hike the tree line, approach on foot before dawn, let the terrain and the hour do the work of keeping us unseen. Simple. Minimal resource commitment. Easy to brief and easier to execute."

Turnpike didn't bother hiding what he thought of that one either, and didn't bother dressing it up for the rank in the room. "That's how you get people killed for no reason. Two klicks of open ground, on foot, in the dark, banking on nobody watching the one fixed coordinate every hostile with a brain already knows to watch. That's not economy of force. That's just hoping." A beat, unhurried, the same delayed courtesy he'd already spent once this evening and clearly wasn't in any hurry to spend twice. "Sir."

That was the one that finally did it.

The colonel came up out of his chair fast enough to scrape it a full foot across the floor behind him. "Just who in the hell do you think you are, Gunnery Sergeant, to sit there and grade officers on strategy in front of a general?"

Turnpike stood too, unhurried, and closed the distance until there was nothing left between them but the small, charged space two men occupy right before somebody throws a punch neither of them is actually going to throw. He knew exactly why he was allowed to be standing there at all: the best Force Recon gunny the Corps had fielded in longer than most of the people in that room had been alive, and a Medal of Honor on his record that bought him considerably more room to speak plainly than his rank alone ever would have. He had never once traded on it deliberately. He had also never once pretended it wasn't there. His voice, when it came, was a low rasp, thirty years and considerably more unfiltered cigarettes than any doctor had ever successfully talked him out of.

"My name's Gunnery Sergeant Turnpike and I've drunk more beer and banged more quiff and pissed more blood and stomped more ass that all of you numbnuts put together. General Hammond put me in this room because I have already proven, more than once, exactly what I am capable of when it counts." A beat, his voice dropping lower still. "And there's a medal sitting in a box back home that says my own government agrees with him." Another beat, longer than the first. "Sir."

Nobody moved for a full second.

"Colonel, that's enough." Hammond's voice, when it came, carried none of the measured patience it usually held even at its most clipped. "If the only contribution you have left to offer this meeting is besmirching the honor of a man who has more than earned this country's highest decoration, then you can get the hell out of this room. And off my mountain while you're at it."

The colonel didn't move, whether from shock or some lingering hope the order might soften if he simply waited it out.

It didn't. "Airmen!" Hammond's voice went up several more degrees, filling the room in a way none of them had heard from him before. "Get this man off my mountain."

Two security airmen who had clearly been standing just outside the door for exactly this kind of contingency stepped in before the last word had finished landing. The colonel, to whatever credit remained to him, didn't fight it, allowing himself to be walked out with his back straight and his eyes forward, dignity being the only thing left in the room he still had any say over.

O'Neill broke first, a short, startled laugh escaping before he could stop it, more admiration in it than he'd probably intended to let show. Daniel's expression stalled somewhere between alarmed and genuinely unsure whether the meeting was still technically a meeting. Carter's mouth had actually fallen open, the closest thing to visible shock Teal'c had seen from her all evening, not at Turnpike this time, but at Hammond.

"Gunny." Hammond's voice gentled considerably, several degrees quieter than it had been a moment ago, meant for Turnpike alone. "Sit down."

Turnpike sat.

Carter's eyebrows, which had never quite come back down, went up a second time. Daniel let out something that started as a laugh and got smothered halfway into a cough that fooled nobody. Bra'tac said one short, guttural word that needed no translation whatsoever to land as an insult, whether aimed at the plan, the colonel, or the entire preceding minute left admirably unclear.

"That," O'Neill said, recovering something like his usual composure, "was not what door two actually is, for the record. Though I'll give him this much, wherever he's currently being walked to: he really committed to being this meeting's cautionary tale, on more than one front." He let the moment breathe a second longer, just long enough to make sure the room had properly finished recovering from it.

"Everybody landed on the same problem from a different direction, too, which is usually a sign he actually should have listened." O'Neill held up one finger. "I've walked into enough of these to know a gate address is the single most predictable point on any map for somebody watching for one. It's the only fixed coordinate that ever changes hands between worlds. Anybody on the other side already knows to watch it."

✳

"The numbers agree with him." Hammond's first real contribution to the debate, unhurried, absolute in the way his rare interjections always were. "I had our analysts run the exposure risk on any approach that touches a known gate address within a day's travel of the target. A chokepoint that narrow doesn't stay unwatched for long once the other side is already suspicious, and by every report on my desk, Apophis already is."

"And a Goa'uld garrison thinks in precisely the terms General Hammond just described, whether or not any of them would ever admit borrowing the logic from a human," Bra'tac said. "I served one long enough to know it firsthand. A gate address left unwatched is a mistake no System Lord's household guard survives making twice."

"Nor would Apophis himself have risen as high as he has, tolerating it," Teal'c added.

::okay, not that anyone asked the guy who's technically also in the room, but also, from a purely map-design perspective, the gate is a chokepoint. Textbook. Any strategy game worth its file size teaches you that in the tutorial. You don't leave the one guaranteed entrance unwatched. That's not even a Goa'uld thing. That's just level design.::

"I have an acquaintance," Teal'c said aloud, the lie arriving easier than he expected it to, "who spends a considerable amount of his free time on tactical simulations. He assures me the same principle holds even in games built for entertainment rather than survival. Something about level design."

"Your acquaintance has a point," Hammond said, mild and unhurried, already filing the reference away alongside every other one he'd been quietly collecting all evening.

O'Neill raised an eyebrow. "You have a gamer friend."

"I contain multitudes," Teal'c said, and did not elaborate further.

"So, moving on to the actual plan." O'Neill waved off the colonel's proposal, already fading from the room's collective memory, and gestured at the projection instead. "Covert infiltration. Bra'tac's Tel'tak gets SG-1 and a Marine recon platoon within striking distance of the tunnel network Teal'c and Bra'tac flagged, cloaked the entire way in. The Marines clear the landing site and lead us to the tunnel. Once we're inside, they secure the ship and extraction route while the five of us..."

"Stop there, sir." Turnpike had not raised his voice. He did not need to. "That's a list of people and places. It isn't a scheme of maneuver."

O'Neill lowered his hand. He looked more interested than offended. "All right, Gunny. Show your work."

Turnpike stood and crossed to the projection. He touched the landing site first, then the tunnel mouth, then the compound above it. "This is a raid on a fortified objective. Company-level problem. That doesn't mean marching a whole administrative company through the woods because a book says so. It means organizing the force the way a company raid gets organized, so every job that can kill you belongs to somebody before the ramp comes down. Command. Reconnaissance. Security. Support. Assault. Reserve. Six functions. Right now you've assigned the first one to yourself, half the second to my platoon, and all the rest to whoever happens to be standing closest when something goes wrong."

He moved his finger back to the clearing. "Reconnaissance goes first. It confirms the landing zone, the route to the release point, the tunnel, and at least one way back that is not merely the way we came in reversed. Security follows and isolates the objective. One part owns the ship and landing zone. Another owns the tunnel approaches and blocks the garrison from turning one alarm into a closed fist around the assault force. Support carries the weapons, communications, medical capability, and breaching tools the assault may need but cannot carry while doing its own job."

His finger settled over the holding block. "The assault element goes to the objective. That includes SG-1 and Bra'tac as the recovery team, because they're the people who can identify the prisoners and navigate the interior, but it does not mean five people assault a fortress while sixty Marines wait outside to admire the scenery. A Marine breach and assault team goes through the tunnel with you. The reserve stays one bound behind the assault, close enough to reinforce, replace casualties, or open a second route in minutes, not read about the need ninety minutes later. On withdrawal, recon leads everybody out. The recovered personnel and assault element follow, then reserve, support, and command. Security is last off the objective."

The room had gone still in the attentive way it had not managed for the colonel.

"That's more than twenty-two Marines," Hammond said.

"Yes, sir." Turnpike did not soften it. "A platoon cannot reconnoiter the route, isolate two pieces of ground, support a breach, put bodies on the objective, hold a real reserve, and then cover its own withdrawal. It can pretend to do all six. That's different. Bring a lean, task-organized Force Recon company: my platoon as the reconnaissance element, a second platoon split between security and support, a third providing the Marine assault team and the reserve, plus a command cell and attached corpsmen. Sixty-four Marines. Captain Mercer keeps formal command of the Marine force. Colonel O'Neill owns the mission. I lead the reconnaissance element and make certain the plan on paper remains the plan on the ground."

Mercer had remained against the wall through the whole exchange, leaving the floor to the man who had seen the flaw first. He stepped forward only when Turnpike named him.

"General Hammond, Gunny Turnpike and I have chewed some of the same dirt," he said. His voice was quiet, almost conversational. "Long enough ago that I was still a lieutenant and foolish enough to think a map stayed true because an officer signed it. He brought my Marines out when it didn't. I wrote the recommendation for the medal sitting in that box he mentioned, and I have spent every year since knowing the citation left out the half of it." He looked at Turnpike once, no sentimentality in it, only recognition between two men who did not need the history explained to each other. "If he tells this room a platoon can only pretend to cover six company functions, he's right. If you authorize the company, I will command it exactly as briefed."

Turnpike's expression did not change. The fractional angle of his head toward Mercer was acknowledgment enough.

O'Neill studied the projection, following the order with his eyes. "Recon first in. Security isolates. Support sets the table. Assault does the job. Reserve close enough to matter. Recon first out with the package, security last."

"Yes, sir."

"Huah," O'Neill said, quieter this time, genuine approval without the joke. "That's an actual plan."

"It is also a considerably larger footprint aboard my vessel," Bra'tac observed.

"Can it carry them under cloak?" Hammond asked.

"It once carried ninety Jaffa and their equipment into a campaign Apophis had not troubled himself to supply honestly," Bra'tac said. "Seventy-two additional passengers will offend its dignity, not its capacity."

O'Neill nodded once. "Pros: no gate approach, no warning, every phase has an owner, and if the map is wrong we have enough force in the right place to adapt. Cons: more bodies to insert, more movement to hide, and we're still betting the interior on a four-hundred-year-old memory. If the tunnel has changed, collapsed, or moved in some way tunnels generally don't, the company can keep us from being surrounded. It cannot invent another door."

::finally! FINALLY! Do you have any idea how many missions in every book, movie, show, and video game go sideways because nobody thought to leave literally anyone at the extraction point? It's not even a smart-guy thing, it's just, bring backup, that's the whole lesson, took humanity a genuinely embarrassing amount of time to learn something so simple::

"My acquaintance," Teal'c said aloud, dry, "wishes it noted for the record that he has been waiting his entire life for somebody to actually bring backup."

"Tell your acquaintance," O'Neill said, without missing a beat, "that we appreciate the validation."

Cut to the gallery.

A VOICE: Finally. Somebody willing to admit it.

ANOTHER VOICE: Something anybody with a brain could see.

✳

"I have walked worse odds," Bra'tac said, dry, unbothered.

"Indeed you have," Teal'c said. "Twice, that I witnessed personally."

"I don't doubt it. I'd still like better ones if we can find them." O'Neill turned to the third and final projection. "Door three. Diplomatic feint, straight off the colonel's own written brief, since he's no longer available to make the case for it himself. Approach through an intermediary, offer trade terms attractive enough that Apophis relocates the hostages somewhere less defended as a show of good faith, then hit that location instead."

"That could take weeks," Daniel said. "Weeks we don't necessarily have, if Word From Apophis is right that he's already suspicious."

Turnpike didn't wait to be invited into this one either, and didn't reach for the politeness to soften it. "I've spent thirty years watching diplomacy buy time for the wrong side to use it, sir. Every week spent talking is a week Apophis has to decide two prisoners aren't worth the trouble anymore. I'm not telling anybody how to run foreign policy." A pause, entirely unapologetic. "I'm telling you what I've watched happen to people on the losing end of a patient negotiation."

Hammond was the one who answered that, taking up an argument its own advocate was no longer in the room to make, a fairness Teal'c suspected cost him something after the last ten minutes. "The diplomatic option is the only one on this table that doesn't risk starting a war we don't currently have the resources to finish. I understand the personal stakes involved here better than most of you probably give me credit for. That doesn't make the math different, and it doesn't stop being a legitimate option just because the man who wrote it down isn't sitting in this room to defend it anymore."

"Nobody's saying the math's wrong," O'Neill said. "We're saying the math isn't the only thing on the table."

"It rarely is," Hammond allowed. "I'm not going to pretend I enjoy being the villain of this meeting twice in one evening. But somebody in this room needs to keep counting the cost nobody else wants to count out loud, and until a better candidate turns up, I intend to keep doing that job myself."

Cut to the gallery.

THE FLAT VOICE: Gate two kilometers out, hike in before dawn, hope nobody notices. That's not a plan. That's cardio with casualties.

A DRY VOICE FROM THE BACK: Bra'tac had a word for the rest of it.

THE FLAT VOICE: I want that word.

✳

The debate ran long, the same institutional back-and-forth Teal'c had learned to expect from this program and had, in four years, come to respect even when it frustrated him personally. Hammond kept the diplomatic option alive in its absent author's place, cautious, slow, and unwilling to discard the only approach least likely to begin a war. O'Neill favored the tunnel raid, trusting Bra'tac's memory enough to put Turnpike's reconnaissance element in front of it rather than treating memory as proof. Carter kept pressing on what they still did not know: the tunnel's present condition, the garrison's readiness, the time between detection and reinforcement. Turnpike agreed with every uncertainty she named and rejected the idea that uncertainty required indefinite delay; reconnaissance existed to answer those questions at the release point before the assault committed. Mercer tested command succession, abort criteria, and the reserve's triggers while Bra'tac answered every question about the vessel and route he could answer honestly. Nobody argued that the company plan removed the risk. They argued over whether it contained enough of the risk to act.

::you're doing the thing where you don't say anything but I can feel you wanting to.::

::I am aware that my urgency is not the same as tactical soundness,:: Teal'c said, silent, careful. ::I am attempting not to let the one override the other.::

::it's okay to want it fast, though. Even if you don't say it out loud. I just wanted you to know somebody clocked it.::

"Teal'c." O'Neill's voice, direct, cutting through the debate to land on him specifically. "You've been quiet. Too quiet, for a guy this is actually about. What's your read?"

Every eye in the room turned to him, and Hammond's lingered a beat longer than the rest, the look of a man who had just noticed something in that exchange and chosen, deliberately, to file it rather than derail the mission decision with a personal question. He said nothing about it. Teal'c suspected, catching the look, that he would remember it regardless. Teal'c took a moment before answering, weighing the pull toward speed against everything Bra'tac had warned him of that morning, a man who could not fully separate the soldier from the husband and father, and chose, deliberately, to let both halves of himself speak rather than pretending only one of them was in the room.

"The diplomatic option costs time we may not have. I will not pretend otherwise." He let that land before continuing. "But a rescue conducted in panic, driven by my own fear rather than sound planning, serves no one, least of all the people I am attempting to save. I recommend the tunnel infiltration, contingent on Bra'tac's confidence in the route once we have eyes on it directly rather than memory alone. If that confidence proves unfounded, I will accept the slower option without further argument."

Hammond let the silence sit after Teal'c's recommendation, then looked to Turnpike. "Sixty-four Marines. You're certain every one of them has a necessary job, not merely a comforting place on the manifest."

"Yes, sir. And I want the words exact before anybody signs it." Turnpike indicated each point on the projection in the same order he had before. "This is not reconnaissance in force. Nobody is provoking Apophis to expose himself, and nobody is feeding him a false picture to study his reaction. This is a raid. Quiet if the plan survives contact, violent if it does not, temporary in either case. We enter to recover two people, not seize terrain. Every element leaves."

"Command relationships," Hammond said.

"You retain operational authority. Colonel O'Neill commands the raid and makes the go or no-go decision at the release point after Bra'tac inspects the tunnel. Captain Mercer commands the Marine company. I lead the reconnaissance element and stay forward. Colonel O'Neill leads the recovery team inside the assault element. If communications fail, every element commander already knows the objective, the withdrawal order, and the conditions that trigger the alternate route. Nobody waits for a radio to tell him the job he was briefed to do."

Hammond looked from Turnpike to O'Neill, then to Bra'tac. "Can the ship carry the force?"

"It can," Bra'tac said.

"Can you prepare it in two days?"

"We can," Turnpike said. Not I. We. The distinction landed.

Hammond nodded once, decision made. "Tunnel raid, cloaked insertion via Bra'tac's vessel. Task-organized Force Recon company with command, reconnaissance, security, support, assault, and reserve elements as briefed. SG-1 and Bra'tac form the recovery team within the assault element. Go or no-go at the tunnel remains Colonel O'Neill's decision, contingent on Bra'tac's direct assessment. I want primary and alternate withdrawal routes, casualty collection aboard the ship, and the diplomatic option maintained as a separate contingency, not confused with this operation once it begins."

Turnpike gave one sharp nod. "Now we have a raid, sir."

"Yes, sir," O'Neill said, and the room began, at last, to move, the ordinary business of real planning replacing the debate that had produced it.

Teal'c lingered by the projection a moment longer than the others, staring at a compound he would, within days, be walking into on purpose for the first time since the night he'd walked out of a life built inside one very much like it.

"Teal'c." Hammond's voice, from the doorway, pitched for a room that had otherwise already emptied. "Stay a moment."
