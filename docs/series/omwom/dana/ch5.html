Need to Know

Dana skipped the bus. Told herself it was because she needed the air, which wasn't exactly a lie, and started walking the long way home instead, backpack slung over one shoulder, streets emptying out around her the further she got from school.

Okay, she thought, once the last knot of kids from her bus stop had peeled off toward their own houses and there was nobody left close enough to make talking to herself look insane. We're doing this properly. Who are you.

Told you already. Faith.

That's a name. Not an answer.

A pause, the kind that felt like someone deciding how much rope to give a question.

Faith Lehane. Nineteen. From a town you've never heard of that's since had some structural issues. A beat. I'm a Slayer.

A what?

Vampire Slayer. One girl in all the world, chosen to fight the forces of darkness, yadda yadda, there's a whole speech, I'm skipping most of it.

Dana kept walking, waiting for the part of her brain that filed things under obviously a joke to kick in. It didn't.

Vampires are real.

Vampires are real. Demons too, mostly the ugly kind, some worse. There's a hole under my hometown that used to lead somewhere you don't want directions to. I've been doing this since I was fifteen.

Fifteen.

Yeah.

Dana let that sit, crossing a street she'd crossed a thousand times without ever once thinking about what might be underneath it.

Is there more? Watchers, other Slayers, an actual organization, or is it just you punching stuff in an alley.

There's more. Whole Council, actually, used to be, kind of a mess these days. Another Slayer, name's Buffy, she's the one everybody actually likes. Something dry crept into it. Long story. You want the whole thing, we got time. You don't, I won't make you sit through it.

Later, maybe. Dana filed it away, the way she filed away most things that felt too big to hold all at once. Okay. Next one. Is this permanent.

The pause this time was longer, long enough that Dana's own footsteps on the sidewalk filled it.

I don't know.

That's not a real answer.

It's the honest one. Nobody's told me it isn't. Nobody's told me it is, either. I got 'reasons of my own that'll become clear' and then a shove into your head. That's the whole file, same as I told you at lunch.

Dana kicked a loose bit of gravel off the sidewalk ahead of her, watched it skitter into the gutter. "Okay," she said out loud this time, quiet, testing the sound of her own voice still being entirely her own. "Why just the talking thing. Why can you do that and nothing else."

The chick in the room said there was a reason. A beat. Not sure what that is yet.

"Can it happen again? The mouth thing. Without warning."

No clue.

"That's a lot of no clues."

Yeah, well, welcome to the file. It's thin.

Dana slowed at the corner, waited for a car that wasn't actually coming, crossed anyway. "What about my actual life. School. Trent. Is anybody gonna notice something's different."

Probably. No hedge in that one at all. People who know what to look for always see something slightly off, a beat too long before you answer, a word that doesn't sound like you. Whether they connect the dots is entirely on them. Most people don't. Most people'd rather not. A beat. Back home, we had an actual name for that. Called it Sunnydale Syndrome.

"Great." Dana blew out a breath, watched it do nothing visible in the warm afternoon air. "So I get to just wait and see who notices."

Pretty much.

She walked another block in silence, letting the ordinary rhythm of houses and driveways and somebody's sprinkler running be a safer thing to focus on than any of this. Then, quieter: "What's the deal with my dad."

No clue about your dad. Faith didn't dress it up. But the facts I noticed are, one, he's got a guy handing him glasses with a video feed built in. Two, that video feed's running off a CCD camera hidden in a pack of smokes. Three, that's not a novelty-store gadget, that's actual field equipment, somebody paid real money and knew what they were doing. None of that adds up to insurance salesman. Not even close.

"So he's, what, a spy."

Didn't say that. Said the pieces don't fit the story you've been told. Different thing.

The rest of it, since you're asking. He saw you take the money, obviously, that part's not exactly subtle. But he saw the wink too. Nobody said so out loud, not that I heard, but I clocked it off his body language out in the yard. That's not a "my kid's acting weird" reaction. That's a very specific kind of freaked out.

"Okay, that's a tic that's not a tic." A voice, grudging. "It actually lands on the page with both feet."

Dana chewed on that for most of a block, long enough that her own house came into view before she'd worked out what to say next.

"Okay." She slowed her pace without meaning to, and this time there was something almost sly in it, turnabout occurring to her all at once. "Your turn. Who were you. Before all this."

Nothing.

"Faith."

Don't really do that part.

"That's not fair, I just told you my whole..."

I know. Doesn't make me any more interested in doing it tonight. Not cruel, just closed, a door shutting somewhere well-practiced at shutting doors. Ask me again some other time. Might get a different answer.

Dana let it go. She didn't entirely know why, except that something in the flatness of the refusal felt less like a wall and more like a bruise she'd almost pressed on by accident.

She reached her own driveway a minute later, alone with a voice that wasn't going anywhere, in a house that had absolutely no idea what had actually walked in the front door in her body that afternoon.

The house itself was empty when she let herself in, quiet the way it always was this time of day, her mom's car still gone from the driveway. Helen didn't get home from work until closer to six, some office job Dana had never bothered asking many questions about, the same way she'd never bothered asking many questions about anything, apparently.

"And THERE's the tic that's out of place."

"The assistant can't land 'em all, after all."

Gizmo met her at the door anyway, the ugly little dog going into his full, undignified routine, spinning in place, sneezing, throwing his whole body sideways against her shins like enthusiasm alone might be enough to knock her over.

"Hey, buddy." Dana dropped to the floor right there in the hallway and let him climb into her lap, unbothered by school clothes or homework waiting or anything else, purely delighted that someone had finally come home.

Now that, Faith said, is a face I trust more than most of the humans I've met today.

"He's hideous."

Yeah. Doesn't mean he's wrong about anything.

Dana carried him back to her room, dropped her backpack for real this time, and stretched out on the floor with Gizmo sprawled across her stomach, scratching behind one ear while he groaned with the specific, shameless satisfaction of a dog who had never once in his life had a complicated day.

"There's the tic again," somebody muttered from the back, not especially bothered about it this time. "Somebody give the dog a wider vocabulary before we lose count."

"So." She kept her voice low, more out of habit than necessity, given the empty house. "You gonna tell me anything else, or was that the whole download."

Depends what you're asking.

"I don't know. Anything. What it's actually like. Being you. Being a Slayer."

Faith was quiet a second, not a door shutting this time, just someone deciding how to actually answer instead of deflecting it.

Tiring, she said finally. Mostly. People think it's the fighting part that wears you out. It's not. It's everybody always needing something from you and never really asking if you're okay carrying it.

Dana turned that over, scratching Gizmo's ear on autopilot. "That's kind of what today's felt like. For me."

Yeah. No argument in it. Guess we've got that much in common already.

They stayed like that a while, girl and dog and the voice of a dead-tired nineteen-year-old occupying the same quiet room, neither of them in any hurry to be the one who spoke next.

It was Faith who broke it, eventually, which surprised Dana more than what came next did.

My turn for one, then.

"Mom won't be home for a couple hours." Dana set Gizmo down, unbothered by his protesting groan, and dug through her closet for something that wasn't a school uniform. "Let's get comfortable first."

She changed quickly, jeans and a T-shirt swapped in for whatever the day had demanded of her, and had barely gotten the closet door shut again when Faith spoke up.

Huh. Not one piece of leather in that whole closet.

"...that's what you noticed."

Course it's what I noticed. Some things you just clock.

"Of course it's what she'd notice. She's Miss Leather Fetish."

"HEY!" Xander's voice, unmistakable, indignant on her behalf from wherever he actually was. "That's my friend you're talking about. One more outta you and I'll come down and do something damned manly to you."

"That's so weird."

Says the girl who steals for fun. We're not exactly the normal ones in this conversation, kid.

Dana climbed onto her bed instead of the floor this time, propped against the headboard, and scooped Gizmo back up the second she was settled, the dog resettling across her lap like he'd never been interrupted at all.

"Okay." She got comfortable, actually comfortable, for the first time since the school bell. "Go ahead. Ask your question."

Why do you steal.

Dana's hand stilled on Gizmo's ear. "That's rude."

Didn't say it was a bad thing. Asked why.

"I don't know. It's not like I need the money." She picked the thread back up, scratching, not looking at anything in particular. "It's just, I don't know, easy. Nobody ever thinks it's gonna be me. I'm the good kid, or I used to be, whatever that means. Nobody checks the good kid's pockets."

Yeah. Something in the single word had more weight riding on it than the word itself could carry. Trust me, I know exactly how that works. Different reasons, probably. Same math.

"What were your reasons."

Wanting something and not having a single other way to get it that didn't involve asking someone who'd already said no. A beat. Or doing something I'd rather not do.

"Oh, honey," Helen said, quiet, mostly to herself, whatever had been in her own face a second ago gone somewhere softer. "That's so much to be carrying around all by yourself."

Nobody in the gallery said a word.

Behind her, Xander settled into the row at her back and laid a hand on her shoulder, sharing in the exact same ache. Neither of them said anything further about it.

Another beat, like she'd surprised herself with how much of that had come out at once. Anyway. Wasn't really asking to compare notes. Just wanted to know if it was a boredom thing or a bigger thing.

"Bigger thing, I guess. I don't actually know. I've just always done it." Dana shrugged, mostly for her own benefit since there was nobody else in the room to see it. "What about the guy on the bike. You gonna ask about him too, or was that not on the list."

Wasn't gonna, but now that you're offering. Something almost like amusement threaded through it. Trent. That's a name that's really committing to a bit. A beat. Not my flavor of beefcake, though.

"Don't."

"Oh yeah?" Dana was smirking now, actively digging for more. "What exactly is your flavor."

Oh, you know. Faith didn't even hesitate. Harleys... and the guys that ride 'em.

Not judging. I dated worse. So, what, he's your whole personality, or is there more going on there.

"He's fine. He's whatever." Dana pulled her knees up, Gizmo grumbling as he resettled across her shins instead. "It's not a big deep thing. He's got a bike and he doesn't ask a lot of questions and that's kind of exactly what I need most days."

Sounds like a getaway car with a heartbeat.

"Rude. Accurate. Rude."

Both things can be true.

Dana let herself laugh, short and real, the first one that had made it all the way out since the mirror. "Okay, your turn's over. New rule, we're not doing this every single day, I need at least one conversation with you that doesn't feel like a deposition."

Deal. A pause, and then, quieter, less like a joke: For what it's worth. You're handling today better than most people would.

"You don't actually know that. You've known me for like six hours."

I know enough.

They stayed like that a while, girl and dog and the voice of a dead-tired nineteen-year-old occupying the same quiet room, none of them in any hurry to be the one who spoke next.

The front door, then, downstairs, opening and closing.

"Dana!" Helen's voice, muffled through the floor, already sounding harried. "Dana, come down here and help me with this cake!"

Dana was up before she'd finished deciding to move, Gizmo tumbling off her lap in a heap of betrayed dignity, and took the stairs two at a time, the whole afternoon's worth of impossible conversation forgotten in the ordinary, reflexive urgency of a mom's voice with an edge on it.

She hit the bottom step and skidded to a dead stop.

Helen stood in the kitchen doorway, arms full of grocery bags, hair coming loose from wherever she'd pinned it that morning, entirely, completely unremarkable.

Dana froze there a full second too long, fortunately with her mother's back half-turned toward the counter and not toward her.

Faith?

Nothing came back. Not a word.

"You okay?" Dana kept her voice down, careful not to move her lips too much with her mother three feet away, back still turned, unpacking grocery bags onto the counter.

Fine. Give me a minute.

Helen glanced up, flour already on one hand from somewhere. "Dana? You just gonna stand there, or are you gonna help me with this cake?"

"Yeah, sorry, just." Dana cast around for something, anything. "I think I left my backpack open upstairs. Everything's gonna fall out. Give me one second."

She didn't wait for permission, just turned and took the stairs at a pace that wasn't quite a run, quick enough to get herself out of the kitchen, slow enough not to look like she was fleeing anything.

You don't actually have a bag problem, Faith said, once they'd cleared the top step.

"No," Dana agreed. "But you clearly needed a minute, and I wasn't gonna get one for you standing in front of my mom."

Then, low, flat, more statement than reaction: That's the chick from the room.

"What?"

Faith didn't answer her, not really, the words that came next clearly meant for nobody but herself. "...for reasons that will become clear," my rosy white ass!

"You okay?"

Dana, standing alone in the upstairs hallway with an empty excuse and a backpack that had never actually been open, let her have the silence instead of pushing for more.

It was almost more than Faith could hold straight in her own head, let alone say out loud to a kid who'd already had enough dropped on her for one day. The face in that waiting room, the one that had told her, calm as anything, that there were reasons she'd understand soon enough, had been Dana's mother's face. Not a stranger's. Not some random borrowed look. Her actual mother, the same woman one floor below them right now, unpacking groceries and asking about a cake like the whole world hadn't just tilted sideways.

Whatever that thing in the waiting room actually was, it hadn't picked that face by accident. Nothing about that room had felt accidental. Faith just didn't have the first idea yet what it meant, only that it meant something, and that she was going to have to sit with that not-knowing for a while longer before she figured out what to do with it.

"Well... shit."
