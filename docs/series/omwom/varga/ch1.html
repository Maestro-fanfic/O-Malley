Wrong Door

Randomizing the coordinates was the point.

They could have searched methodically. Metis had spreadsheets. Dragon had standards.

That sounded useful.

It also sounded far less fun.

Taylor entered a random seed. Metis checked the power. Ianthe checked the glowing spheres holding the magic. The circular EDM door waited in the middle of the workshop, attached to no wall and currently leading nowhere.

"Ready?" Taylor asked.

"No," Metis said.

"Yes," Ianthe said at the same time.

Taylor pressed the key.

Tinker hardware, power-storage technology, Varga magic, and Dragon's code combined into one deeply unreasonable answer. A matching door extruded into another reality. White glare faded to a carpeted theater aisle.

"The connection is stable," Metis reported.

"Coordinates?" Taylor asked.

"Unknown reality. Unknown local position. Which is what happens when someone refuses to let me build an index."

"Spontaneity," Taylor corrected.

"Poor data hygiene."

Ianthe leaned between them to study the theater. "At least it's indoors."

Then air moved through the open doorway.

All three recoiled at once.

"What is that smell?" Ianthe asked.

From somewhere in the darkness beyond the door, a man said, with the dignity of someone trying to get ahead of a story that had already escaped him, "There were extenuating circumstances."

Another voice answered, "Your bladder had extenuating circumstances."

"I was under pressure!"

"Apparently so was everything else."

Metis managed skepticism without eyebrows. Ianthe managed concern. Taylor suspected Raptaur's armored muzzle was doing both.

"That isn't the same thing as safe," Ianthe replied.

"It rarely is," Varga observed inside Taylor's mind.

His tone was interested.

That decided it.

Taylor stepped through.

The carpet gave under Raptaur's weight. Rows vanished into darkness. Some held people. Some held shapes that were probably people under a generous definition. At the rear, a sign advertised PLOT-SPECIFIC SNACK. The worker beneath it wore a tag reading GENERIC CONCESSIONS WORKER.

Taylor read it twice.

The worker waved.

Metis and Ianthe followed her through. The circular frame remained standing behind them, its control cable disappearing through the open boundary toward the keyboard at home.

Their machine continued humming in the workshop on the other side, exactly as designed. The status display remained green, the return route stable. Whatever this place was, it had not interfered with the connection.

As usual, the fact that reaching it was technically their own fault did not make it less suspicious.

The audience went quiet.

Silence spread row by row. Heads turned. Popcorn lowered. The last voice belonged to a man explaining why a structural support counted as a weapon if you were motivated enough.

Taylor's senses reached past the light: hundreds of heartbeats, old carpet, cleaning chemicals, popcorn, and the unmistakable evidence of Carlos's extenuating circumstances. Yet nothing blurred together. The theater had room for every sound—and, apparently, every tail.

That was more disturbing than if it had been badly made.

Metis counted the exits, the concessions counter, and six doors Taylor hadn't noticed until Lisa did. None had signs. One occupied a wall with no room behind it.

"No cameras," Metis whispered.

Taylor found seventeen places cameras should have been and none where they were.

"Maybe they're hidden."

"My power says the room doesn't need them."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one I got."

Ianthe examined the carpet without touching it. "This isn't normal fiber."

"Please don't analyze the theater until after introductions," the mild-looking man in the teacher's jacket called from the front. "It gets defensive about peer review."

Under Ianthe's hovering claw, the carpet fibers leaned away.

She withdrew her hand.

"Noted."

In the front row, a broad-shouldered Black man in a long coat looked up at Taylor with his one visible eye.

"Well," he said. "That's... different."

Beside him, a mild-looking man in a teacher's jacket pushed his glasses up his nose. "You have a gift for understatement."

"Occupational requirement."

The one-eyed man stood.

He reached for no weapon, though Raptaur could tell he knew how. He stopped absurdly close to her claws and looked up.

Not at her muzzle.

At her eyes.

"Taylor Hebert," he said. "Welcome."

Taylor relaxed a fraction. He hadn't greeted the monster. He had looked for the Brain and spoken to her.

"Thank you," she said carefully. "I think. Who are you?"

His mouth twitched. As he opened his mouth to answer, the leather duster flowed into a black tuxedo, complete with cummerbund and bow tie.

He adjusted one cuff.

"O'Malley."

He let the beat hang.

"ROB O'Malley."

Then his attention shifted by a fraction, no farther than the space behind her own eyes.

"Varga. Welcome to you too."

Taylor went completely still.

Varga did not.

The ancient presence in her mind seemed to lean forward, not alarmed, not threatened, but sharply attentive. For one instant a memory brushed past her: a light in an impossible darkness, a decision made at a distance too vast to measure, an oath and an opening and the sense of a hand which had never been a hand moving one piece on a board larger than worlds.

Then it was gone.

"You know us," Varga said aloud through Raptaur's deeper register.

"I've been paying attention."

The man turned to Metis. "Lisa Wilbourn. Metis. Whichever is more comfortable in the room."

Metis narrowed her glowing eyes. "That answer did not contain an answer."

"You'll fit in fine."

He moved on to Ianthe. "Amy Dallon. Ianthe. Same offer."

"Where are we?" she asked.

"Gallery. Theater. Waiting room with better seating. Depends which filing cabinet you open."

"How did our door connect to it?"

"Above your clearance."

"Do you actually have clearance levels?" Metis asked.

"I have folders. Same principle."

The teacher in the front row covered a smile with one hand.

Metis had stopped smiling entirely.

Taylor knew the look. Lisa's power was assembling facts, discarding explanations, reaching for the next link, and finding something at the end of the chain which refused to be placed in words. Her pupils widened. Then narrowed. Her tail made one short, irritated movement.

"You're not Nick Fury," she said.

The one-eyed man touched his eyepatch. "Accessories say otherwise."

"You know facts you had no route to learn. This place has no consistent external dimensions. The doors aren't exits until someone needs them to be. Half the audience is behaving as if they've died, and the other half is behaving as if death was an administrative inconvenience. My power keeps returning metaphors."

"Good metaphors?"

"Annoyingly smug ones."

"Definitely working, then."

"What are you?"

For the first time, the man's amusement softened rather than sharpened.

"The person who says welcome before the lights go down. Tonight, that's enough."

It was not enough for Metis. Taylor could see that in every line of her. But Lisa also knew when an answer could not be forced without damaging whatever chance remained of receiving it later.

She looked at Taylor.

Taylor gave the smallest possible nod.

For now.

The one-eyed man finished his inspection of the three visitors, then looked back to Taylor. He cocked his head slowly to one side.

His face began to change.

His face flowed. Bone shifted. The eyepatch vanished. A dozen faces flickered past before one settled into the familiar.

Taylor knew that face.

She had never seen it in her life.

Both facts arrived with equal certainty.

Beside her, Metis inhaled sharply. Ianthe's claws clicked once against the carpet. Varga became very quiet.

The familiar stranger smiled and snapped his fingers.

The sound was tiny.

The theater moved around it.

The screen blazed white. Seats expanded around three impossible bodies. Popcorn appeared in Taylor's claws, labeled EXPOSITION BUFFER.

Their EDM door did nothing at all.

It remained open behind them, a stable window onto their own workshop. The frame, power feed, and control display showed no sign that the stranger had touched the connection. Whatever he had just done belonged entirely to the theater side of the threshold.

Taylor looked from it to him.

"What did you just do?"

His smirk was pure mischief.

"You'll find out. I promise it's not bad."

"That promise has a distressing number of interpretation branches," Metis muttered.

"Only the fun ones."

The familiar stranger looked back to Taylor.

"Well, it's about time you made it here. And hello to you too, Taylor. I hope my gift has been as helpful as I hoped."

Varga answered before Taylor could.

His voice came from Raptaur's throat, older than the shape carrying it and suddenly solemn.

"Hello, ROB. Thank you for gifting me with this. You were gifting more than you knew when you Joined us."

Taylor felt the capital letter. She always did when Varga meant the word that way.

The stranger—ROB—inclined his head.

"Oh, I knew. I always know."

Taylor looked from him to the presence sharing her life.

The memory brushed her again. Light in darkness. A hand which was not a hand. Something answering before the power which should have found her could arrive.

"Wait," she said slowly. "So this is the being that sent you to me?"

Varga's silence became suddenly eloquent.

"You knew," Taylor said.

"I suspected with considerable confidence."

"You never mentioned it."

"It did not seem urgent."

Metis made a choking sound. Ianthe stared at Varga. ROB looked openly delighted.

"What can I say?" He spread his hands. "I contain multitudes. Sometimes even I lose track of what I've done. None of us are quite as Omnipotent as we like to pretend."

"You just said you always know," Taylor pointed out.

"And for several glorious seconds, everyone believed me."

The familiar face flowed back into Fury's around the grin.

He looked at Taylor and Varga together.

"I guess you could say you started this whole train rolling. I had forgotten how much fun tinkering with the universe could be until I saw some of the things you two were getting up to."

Taylor considered the impossible seats, the screen waiting behind him, and a narrator at the microphone who had not yet realized his job was in danger.

"This is your version of tinkering?"

"One of them."

"How many versions are there?"

"I contain multitudes," he reminded her.

Then Fury gestured toward the new seats.

"Have a seat. Let's watch the movie."

Taylor remained standing for another second. Varga's attention had not left the stranger. There was recognition in it, unmistakable now, but no warning.

That mattered.

She sat.

The reinforced seat accepted Raptaur's weight without complaint. Metis and Ianthe took the two beside her, tails negotiated with armrests, and Fury returned to the front row.

The lights dimmed.

A bass note rolled through the theater. The screen filled with stars, smoke, and a title card which had clearly spent more money on itself than some governments spent on roads.

A deep male voice began.

"In a world where—"

Tap.

Tap.

Tap.

The voice stopped.

The lights came up just enough to reveal a man standing at a microphone beside the screen. Taylor had not noticed him before. Judging by his expression, he had not noticed the new arrival standing directly behind him either.

He looked over his shoulder.

His gaze met armored scales.

Then it moved up.

And up.

And up.

Raptaur was not Taylor's largest form. She was not even close. That information did not appear to help him.

The narrator made a small sound.

A dark patch spread across the front of his trousers.

Then his eyes rolled back and he folded at the knees.

The larger figure behind him caught him before his head struck the floor.

From three rows over, Carlos shot to his feet and pointed with the wild vindication of a man receiving a favorable verdict from the highest possible court.

"See? I'm not the only one!"

The entire Gallery turned toward him.

Carlos sat down.

"Still under pressure," he muttered.

"Somebody grab him and strap him in so he doesn't hurt himself," a voice called.

Two audience members strapped the narrator into a chair before he could hurt himself. Generic Concessions Worker sprayed the carpet with CONSEQUENCE REMOVER and vanished.

The larger figure beside the abandoned microphone watched all this with patient interest.

Then she looked down at the script stand.

It was empty.

She sighed.

"Okay," she said. "Now where were we? Somebody get me the script."
