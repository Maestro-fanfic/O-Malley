In a World Where

The script arrived in the hands of the Generic Concessions Worker.

Nobody saw where it came from.

It looked like twenty pages and a brass fastener. The speaker took it, lifted page one, and discovered page two was attached.

Then the third.

Then the fourth.

The pages slid over the stand, whispered down the front, reached the carpet, and continued toward her feet. The paper did not tear. It did not fold. It flowed.

The speaker watched it go past one foot.

Then the other.

It crossed the aisle, passed beneath the first row, emerged under the second, and kept going into the darkness at the back of the Gallery.

Somewhere far away, an audience member yelped as the leading edge went over his shoes.

"That," Metis said, "is not a script. That's a land-use problem."

The speaker peered at the first page. Her eyes tracked down several lines. Whatever she found there made the tip of her tail twitch.

She reached into empty air and pulled out a green accountant's visor.

Across its band, in neat white letters, was printed TACHAT™.

Taylor leaned forward. "Did that say—"

"Yes," Ianthe said.

"Is that one of ours?"

"I have no idea."

Varga sounded delighted. "It should be."

The speaker put on the visor, adjusted it with great care, and bent over the stand. A red pencil appeared in one hand. She worked her way down the page in grave silence.

"No," she muttered.

The pencil scratched.

"No. Too obvious. No."

Another mark.

"We're not making that joke."

Three rows back, someone whispered, "Coward."

The speaker looked up.

The whisperer sank below the seat in front of him.

She returned to the script.

"No. No. Okay, that's actually funny. Keep that." Scratch. "Cut this. Move that down. Who wrote four consecutive paragraphs about breakfast?"

Taylor raised one claw halfway, then slowly lowered it.

"Eight paragraphs," Metis murmured. "If it's the morning I'm thinking of."

"Nine," Ianthe said. "You forgot the discussion about the coffee machine."

The speaker followed the script down the aisle. "No. No. Weather report? No. Shipping-container pun? Fine. Complete sandwich order?"

She read the next page.

"Relevant sixty thousand words later. Keep it."

Fury waited with the serenity of a man whose schedule could not be harmed by time.

Mr. Gladly took off his glasses and cleaned them.

Carlos examined the ceiling with great concentration.

At last the speaker stopped.

"There."

She drew a thick red line across the paper, rolled her shoulders, and returned to the microphone. The visor stayed on. It somehow made the size of her more absurd.

She looked at the screen.

When she spoke again, her voice was deep enough to rattle Taylor's teeth inside Raptaur's jaw.

"Okay, now..."

The lights trembled.

"IN A WORLD WHERE THE FANFIC IS FAR TOO LONG FOR US TO FIT IT ALL INTO A SINGLE TRAILER..."

The paper script continued unspooling behind her.

"MARVEL as an author writes a fic bigger than War and Peace... makes the whole thing read like a minute-by-minute depiction of everyday life and an entire city's reactions to it... and yet somehow manages to keep it interesting."

The screen showed a sunrise over Brockton Bay.

The shot held.

And held.

A boat crossed the bay. A dockworker unlocked a gate. A PRT analyst poured coffee. Somewhere, someone began a forum post about a lizard nobody believed in.

The sun had moved perhaps an inch.

"Minute by minute," the narrator repeated.

On-screen, the dockworker discovered he had brought the wrong key.

The Gallery laughed.

The image cut to a school hallway.

The laughter ended.

The trailer did not show the inside of the locker. It showed the closed metal door, the deserted corridor, and the last daylight fading from the high windows. That was enough. Taylor's claws tightened around the bag of EXPOSITION BUFFER.

Ianthe's hand settled on her forearm.

Metis's tail curled around the base of Taylor's seat.

The narrator's register changed. It remained impossibly deep, but the joke stepped aside.

"When one bullied girl cries out for help, the answer comes from very far away, very long ago... and with far more teeth than expected."

The black screen split.

On one side waited another path: insects, a dark costume, a city burning by installments, and choices narrowing until survival and victory meant the same thing.

On the other, a point of light opened in a darkness no camera could have filmed. Something ancient turned its attention toward a dying girl.

The point of light expanded into memory.

Luna wasn't another passenger in Taylor's head. She was a life already over, preserved in Varga: instincts, balance, battles, and the memory of a kingdom long gone. Her hands flashed on-screen once, then the memory became Taylor's motion.

"Memories," Taylor said quietly.

The narrator paused.

The screen corrected itself. A second silhouette which had begun to form beside Taylor dissolved into light and folded into the older recollection where it belonged.

"Memories," the narrator agreed.

Varga's approval moved through Taylor like distant thunder.

Mr. Gladly glanced from the screen to her. "Does it usually listen to corrections?"

"It had better," Fury said.

The endless script rustled across the floor. Several lines crossed themselves out.

"WATCH as Taylor Hebert's life takes a hard left from bugs... into INFINITELY VARIABLE DINOSAUR."

The second half of the screen exploded into scales.

Saurial grinned at a camera. Raptaur unfolded. Umihebi became a maritime zoning dispute. Kaiju made the word Brute look underfunded.

The Gallery erupted.

"That's not a power classification," someone shouted. "That's a municipal budget!"

"Multiple municipal budgets," another voice corrected.

"Federal matching funds."

On-screen, a PRT analyst opened a fresh assessment form, looked at the previous one, and placed both hands over her face.

Taylor laughed despite herself.

The trailer cut back to Winslow.

Taylor never woke up, because she had never been unconscious. She experienced the bond, Varga, and the inherited memories as they happened. When it was over, she was still at school—healed, altered, and entirely aware that she now had several feet of reptilian tail to account for.

She cleaned herself up in the locker-room showers. Then, instead of fleeing, she found a school camera and documented the locker: the filth, the bloody handprints, and the trail she had left behind. She removed the memory card and kept it.

Only then did she use the telephone in the secretary's office.

"Dad?"

Danny's frightened questions tumbled over one another.

Taylor's composure finally cracked. "Can you come and pick me up, please?"

"I'm leaving now."

"WATCH as Taylor responds to a life-changing ordeal," the narrator intoned, "by collecting evidence, calling her father, and arranging her own ride home."

Danny arrived. Varga's concealment let him—and only him—see Taylor. He drove her home wrapped in a blue cloak, his fear hardening into purpose by the mile.

The look on his face ended the jokes.

Taylor wrestled her brand-new tail onto the sofa without breaking anything. Danny returned with two hot chocolates and the expression of a father holding himself together by the handle.

"Tell me," he said.

"EXPERIENCE the most unrealistic event in the entire Worm multiverse," the narrator said more softly. "A traumatized teenage cape tells a responsible adult what happened... and the adult listens."

Mr. Gladly winced.

Fury glanced sideways at him. "You planning to object?"

"No."

"Good plan."

"I was going to say the standard is depressingly low."

"Better plan."

The music stopped.

Not faded. Stopped.

The screen held on the Hebert living room. Taylor sat curled into one end of the sofa while Danny listened.

She told him about the ruined schoolwork. The stolen clothes. The insults and the shoves and the little humiliations designed to be invisible whenever an adult happened to look their way. She told him how long it had been going on.

Danny's face lost all color.

He did not interrupt. He did not ask why she had kept it from him. He simply sat and listened with his hands locked together so tightly that the knuckles stood out white.

Then Taylor reached the locker.

She described the smell. The depth of the filth around her shoes. The things floating in it. She haltingly explained how she had hammered on the metal and screamed for someone—anyone—to let her out.

Danny raised one trembling hand.

"Excuse me for a moment."

His voice was very quiet.

He stood, crossed the hall, opened the basement door, went down the steps, and closed the door softly behind him.

The screen held on Taylor.

One second passed.

Two.

Three.

The roar from under the floor made half the Gallery jump.

"GODDAMN VICIOUS BASTARDS! I'M GOING TO KILL THEM ALL!"

Something wooden broke with a tremendous crack.

Another impact followed, then language that made Steve Rogers' eyebrows climb. The basement lost an argument with Danny.

The trailer narrator spoke over the muffled destruction in a reverent bass.

"WITNESS the mild-mannered dockworker take one short, responsible break to process his emotions."

CRASH.

"Very responsibly," Metis observed.

Taylor had both hands over her face. "I had forgotten how loud that was."

"You heard it through a floor," Ianthe said. "We have theater acoustics."

From below came one final, distant thump. Then silence.

After another long pause, the basement door opened.

Danny emerged with his hair slightly disordered and the beginnings of damage across the knuckles of his right hand. He closed the door, returned to his chair, and sat down with immense dignity.

"I'm sorry, Taylor. Please go on."

The Gallery stared.

On-screen Taylor stared too. Then she nodded and, with the careful expression of someone making a major tactical decision, said, "It gets kind of weird for the next part."

Fury turned slowly toward the enormous reptilian girl sitting among them.

Taylor lowered her hands just enough to peer back at him. "In context, it did."

"Kid, your father just tried to beat the basement to death. I think the context had already left the building."

Varga's laughter rolled through her mind. I liked him immediately.

Danny read Taylor's notes. The basement threatened an encore. Then he called Principal Blackwell, who discovered that interrupting a furious union negotiator was an advanced-level mistake.

He kept Taylor home. He set the meeting date. He mentioned the Dockworkers Association's many friends in local government. Blackwell's confidence began to disintegrate in real time.

Records appeared. Contradictions accumulated. A hidden camera captured words three girls had assumed no one else would ever hear. The machinery which had protected them found itself asked to explain, in writing, how it had known facts Danny had never supplied.

"Cheer as the terrifying dinosaur demon deploys its most devastating weapon."

A beat.

"Documentation."

The Gallery applauded.

Taylor looked down at her popcorn. "This is strange."

"Watching it?" Ianthe asked.

"Watching them watch it."

Metis glanced across the rows. "They're not watching you like a threat."

"Some are."

"Yes, but not only that."

On-screen, Saurial stuck a tiny camera to her headband and adjusted the angle in a mirror. The scene cut to Raptaur doing the same, then to an evidence technician receiving a memory card with the reverence normally reserved for a confession signed in triplicate.

"Follow a heroine whose answer to secret-identity complications is to film everything, provide copies to law enforcement, and make lying about the ten-foot reptile with the forehead camera a significantly worse legal strategy than admitting what you did."

"Objective evidence," Fury said approvingly.

"It saves time," Taylor replied.

"It creates time," Metis said. "Usually several hours of it, all spent answering questions about why the camera footage includes something that wasn't supposed to be physically possible."

The narrator heard her.

"And settle in for a story where every impossible event receives the one thing no cosmic horror has ever survived intact—"

The screen filled with desks. Analysts. Whiteboards. Video enhancement. Interdepartmental calls. A physicist staring at a measurement and quietly erasing the unit.

"—REACTION SHOTS."

Police reacted to the Family. The PRT reacted to the police. Washington reacted to the PRT. Foreign agencies reacted to Washington. PHO beat them all to it with one blurry photograph.

The dockworkers built something useful.

The same fishing boat from sunrise finally returned to harbor.

Its captain looked at an eighty-five-foot reptile standing in the bay, took out his phone, and began recording.

"Because in Brockton Bay," the narrator said, "terror is temporary... but content is forever."

The title card slammed onto the screen.

TAYLOR VARGA

The letters acquired a tail.

Then the tail knocked the title sideways.

Taylor stared at it.

Varga chuckled inside her mind.

"I like this movie," he said.
