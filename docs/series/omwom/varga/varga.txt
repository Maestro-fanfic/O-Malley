Chapter 1: Wrong Door



Randomizing the coordinates was the point.

They could have searched methodically. Metis had spreadsheets. Dragon had standards.

That sounded useful.

It also sounded far less fun.

Taylor entered a random seed. Metis checked the power. Ianthe checked the glowing spheres holding the magic. The circular EDM door waited in the middle of the workshop, attached to no wall and currently leading nowhere.

"Ready?" Taylor asked.

"No," Metis said.

"Yes," Ianthe said at the same time.

Taylor pressed the key.

Tinker hardware, power-storage technology, Varga magic, and Dragon's code combined into one deeply unreasonable answer. A matching door extruded into another reality. White glare faded to a carpeted theater aisle.

"The connection is stable," Metis reported.

"Coordinates?" Taylor asked.

"Unknown reality. Unknown local position. Which is what happens when someone refuses to let me build an index."

"Spontaneity," Taylor corrected.

"Poor data hygiene."

Ianthe leaned between them to study the theater. "At least it's indoors."

Then air moved through the open doorway.

All three recoiled at once.

"What is that smell?" Ianthe asked.

From somewhere in the darkness beyond the door, a man said, with the dignity of someone trying to get ahead of a story that had already escaped him, "There were extenuating circumstances."

Another voice answered, "Your bladder had extenuating circumstances."

"I was under pressure!"

"Apparently so was everything else."

Metis managed skepticism without eyebrows. Ianthe managed concern. Taylor suspected Raptaur's armored muzzle was doing both.

"That isn't the same thing as safe," Ianthe replied.

"It rarely is," Varga observed inside Taylor's mind.

His tone was interested.

That decided it.

Taylor stepped through.

The carpet gave under Raptaur's weight. Rows vanished into darkness. Some held people. Some held shapes that were probably people under a generous definition. At the rear, a sign advertised PLOT-SPECIFIC SNACK. The worker beneath it wore a tag reading GENERIC CONCESSIONS WORKER.

Taylor read it twice.

The worker waved.

Metis and Ianthe followed her through. The circular frame remained standing behind them, its control cable disappearing through the open boundary toward the keyboard at home.

Their machine continued humming in the workshop on the other side, exactly as designed. The status display remained green, the return route stable. Whatever this place was, it had not interfered with the connection.

As usual, the fact that reaching it was technically their own fault did not make it less suspicious.

The audience went quiet.

Silence spread row by row. Heads turned. Popcorn lowered. The last voice belonged to a man explaining why a structural support counted as a weapon if you were motivated enough.

Taylor's senses reached past the light: hundreds of heartbeats, old carpet, cleaning chemicals, popcorn, and the unmistakable evidence of Carlos's extenuating circumstances. Yet nothing blurred together. The theater had room for every sound—and, apparently, every tail.

That was more disturbing than if it had been badly made.

Metis counted the exits, the concessions counter, and six doors Taylor hadn't noticed until Lisa did. None had signs. One occupied a wall with no room behind it.

"No cameras," Metis whispered.

Taylor found seventeen places cameras should have been and none where they were.

"Maybe they're hidden."

"My power says the room doesn't need them."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one I got."

Ianthe examined the carpet without touching it. "This isn't normal fiber."

"Please don't analyze the theater until after introductions," the mild-looking man in the teacher's jacket called from the front. "It gets defensive about peer review."

Under Ianthe's hovering claw, the carpet fibers leaned away.

She withdrew her hand.

"Noted."

In the front row, a broad-shouldered Black man in a long coat looked up at Taylor with his one visible eye.

"Well," he said. "That's... different."

Beside him, a mild-looking man in a teacher's jacket pushed his glasses up his nose. "You have a gift for understatement."

"Occupational requirement."

The one-eyed man stood.

He reached for no weapon, though Raptaur could tell he knew how. He stopped absurdly close to her claws and looked up.

Not at her muzzle.

At her eyes.

"Taylor Hebert," he said. "Welcome."

Taylor relaxed a fraction. He hadn't greeted the monster. He had looked for the Brain and spoken to her.

"Thank you," she said carefully. "I think. Who are you?"

His mouth twitched. As he opened his mouth to answer, the leather duster flowed into a black tuxedo, complete with cummerbund and bow tie.

He adjusted one cuff.

"O'Malley."

He let the beat hang.

"ROB O'Malley."

Then his attention shifted by a fraction, no farther than the space behind her own eyes.

"Varga. Welcome to you too."

Taylor went completely still.

Varga did not.

The ancient presence in her mind seemed to lean forward, not alarmed, not threatened, but sharply attentive. For one instant a memory brushed past her: a light in an impossible darkness, a decision made at a distance too vast to measure, an oath and an opening and the sense of a hand which had never been a hand moving one piece on a board larger than worlds.

Then it was gone.

"You know us," Varga said aloud through Raptaur's deeper register.

"I've been paying attention."

The man turned to Metis. "Lisa Wilbourn. Metis. Whichever is more comfortable in the room."

Metis narrowed her glowing eyes. "That answer did not contain an answer."

"You'll fit in fine."

He moved on to Ianthe. "Amy Dallon. Ianthe. Same offer."

"Where are we?" she asked.

"Gallery. Theater. Waiting room with better seating. Depends which filing cabinet you open."

"How did our door connect to it?"

"Above your clearance."

"Do you actually have clearance levels?" Metis asked.

"I have folders. Same principle."

The teacher in the front row covered a smile with one hand.

Metis had stopped smiling entirely.

Taylor knew the look. Lisa's power was assembling facts, discarding explanations, reaching for the next link, and finding something at the end of the chain which refused to be placed in words. Her pupils widened. Then narrowed. Her tail made one short, irritated movement.

"You're not Nick Fury," she said.

The one-eyed man touched his eyepatch. "Accessories say otherwise."

"You know facts you had no route to learn. This place has no consistent external dimensions. The doors aren't exits until someone needs them to be. Half the audience is behaving as if they've died, and the other half is behaving as if death was an administrative inconvenience. My power keeps returning metaphors."

"Good metaphors?"

"Annoyingly smug ones."

"Definitely working, then."

"What are you?"

For the first time, the man's amusement softened rather than sharpened.

"The person who says welcome before the lights go down. Tonight, that's enough."

It was not enough for Metis. Taylor could see that in every line of her. But Lisa also knew when an answer could not be forced without damaging whatever chance remained of receiving it later.

She looked at Taylor.

Taylor gave the smallest possible nod.

For now.

The one-eyed man finished his inspection of the three visitors, then looked back to Taylor. He cocked his head slowly to one side.

His face began to change.

His face flowed. Bone shifted. The eyepatch vanished. A dozen faces flickered past before one settled into the familiar.

Taylor knew that face.

She had never seen it in her life.

Both facts arrived with equal certainty.

Beside her, Metis inhaled sharply. Ianthe's claws clicked once against the carpet. Varga became very quiet.

The familiar stranger smiled and snapped his fingers.

The sound was tiny.

The theater moved around it.

The screen blazed white. Seats expanded around three impossible bodies. Popcorn appeared in Taylor's claws, labeled EXPOSITION BUFFER.

Their EDM door did nothing at all.

It remained open behind them, a stable window onto their own workshop. The frame, power feed, and control display showed no sign that the stranger had touched the connection. Whatever he had just done belonged entirely to the theater side of the threshold.

Taylor looked from it to him.

"What did you just do?"

His smirk was pure mischief.

"You'll find out. I promise it's not bad."

"That promise has a distressing number of interpretation branches," Metis muttered.

"Only the fun ones."

The familiar stranger looked back to Taylor.

"Well, it's about time you made it here. And hello to you too, Taylor. I hope my gift has been as helpful as I hoped."

Varga answered before Taylor could.

His voice came from Raptaur's throat, older than the shape carrying it and suddenly solemn.

"Hello, ROB. Thank you for gifting *me* with this. You were gifting more than you knew when you Joined us."

Taylor felt the capital letter. She always did when Varga meant the word that way.

The stranger—ROB—inclined his head.

"Oh, I knew. I always know."

Taylor looked from him to the presence sharing her life.

The memory brushed her again. Light in darkness. A hand which was not a hand. Something answering before the power which should have found her could arrive.

"Wait," she said slowly. "So this is the being that sent you to me?"

Varga's silence became suddenly eloquent.

"You knew," Taylor said.

"I suspected with considerable confidence."

"You never mentioned it."

"It did not seem urgent."

Metis made a choking sound. Ianthe stared at Varga. ROB looked openly delighted.

"What can I say?" He spread his hands. "I contain multitudes. Sometimes even I lose track of what I've done. None of us are quite as *Omnipotent* as we like to pretend."

"You just said you always know," Taylor pointed out.

"And for several glorious seconds, everyone believed me."

The familiar face flowed back into Fury's around the grin.

He looked at Taylor and Varga together.

"I guess you could say you started this whole train rolling. I had forgotten how much fun tinkering with the universe could be until I saw some of the things you two were getting up to."

Taylor considered the impossible seats, the screen waiting behind him, and a narrator at the microphone who had not yet realized his job was in danger.

"This is your version of tinkering?"

"One of them."

"How many versions are there?"

"I contain multitudes," he reminded her.

Then Fury gestured toward the new seats.

"Have a seat. Let's watch the movie."

Taylor remained standing for another second. Varga's attention had not left the stranger. There was recognition in it, unmistakable now, but no warning.

That mattered.

She sat.

The reinforced seat accepted Raptaur's weight without complaint. Metis and Ianthe took the two beside her, tails negotiated with armrests, and Fury returned to the front row.

The lights dimmed.

A bass note rolled through the theater. The screen filled with stars, smoke, and a title card which had clearly spent more money on itself than some governments spent on roads.

A deep male voice began.

"In a world where—"

Tap.

Tap.

Tap.

The voice stopped.

The lights came up just enough to reveal a man standing at a microphone beside the screen. Taylor had not noticed him before. Judging by his expression, he had not noticed the new arrival standing directly behind him either.

He looked over his shoulder.

His gaze met armored scales.

Then it moved up.

And up.

And up.

Raptaur was not Taylor's largest form. She was not even close. That information did not appear to help him.

The narrator made a small sound.

A dark patch spread across the front of his trousers.

Then his eyes rolled back and he folded at the knees.

The larger figure behind him caught him before his head struck the floor.

From three rows over, Carlos shot to his feet and pointed with the wild vindication of a man receiving a favorable verdict from the highest possible court.

"See? I'm not the only one!"

The entire Gallery turned toward him.

Carlos sat down.

"Still under pressure," he muttered.

"Somebody grab him and strap him in so he doesn't hurt himself," a voice called.

Two audience members strapped the narrator into a chair before he could hurt himself. Generic Concessions Worker sprayed the carpet with CONSEQUENCE REMOVER and vanished.

The larger figure beside the abandoned microphone watched all this with patient interest.

Then she looked down at the script stand.

It was empty.

She sighed.

"Okay," she said. "Now where were we? Somebody get me the script."



Chapter 2: In a World Where



The script arrived in the hands of the Generic Concessions Worker.

Nobody saw where it came from.

It looked like twenty pages and a brass fastener. The speaker took it, lifted page one, and discovered page two was attached.

Then the third.

Then the fourth.

The pages slid over the stand, whispered down the front, reached the carpet, and continued toward her feet. The paper did not tear. It did not fold. It flowed.

The speaker watched it go past one foot.

Then the other.

It crossed the aisle, passed beneath the first row, emerged under the second, and kept going into the darkness at the back of the Gallery.

Somewhere far away, an audience member yelped as the leading edge went over his shoes.

"That," Metis said, "is not a script. That's a land-use problem."

The speaker peered at the first page. Her eyes tracked down several lines. Whatever she found there made the tip of her tail twitch.

She reached into empty air and pulled out a green accountant's visor.

Across its band, in neat white letters, was printed TACHAT™.

Taylor leaned forward. "Did that say—"

"Yes," Ianthe said.

"Is that one of ours?"

"I have no idea."

Varga sounded delighted. "It should be."

The speaker put on the visor, adjusted it with great care, and bent over the stand. A red pencil appeared in one hand. She worked her way down the page in grave silence.

"No," she muttered.

The pencil scratched.

"No. Too obvious. No."

Another mark.

"We're not making that joke."

Three rows back, someone whispered, "Coward."

The speaker looked up.

The whisperer sank below the seat in front of him.

She returned to the script.

"No. No. Okay, that's actually funny. Keep that." Scratch. "Cut this. Move that down. Who wrote four consecutive paragraphs about breakfast?"

Taylor raised one claw halfway, then slowly lowered it.

"Eight paragraphs," Metis murmured. "If it's the morning I'm thinking of."

"Nine," Ianthe said. "You forgot the discussion about the coffee machine."

The speaker followed the script down the aisle. "No. No. Weather report? No. Shipping-container pun? Fine. Complete sandwich order?"

She read the next page.

"Relevant sixty thousand words later. Keep it."

Fury waited with the serenity of a man whose schedule could not be harmed by time.

Mr. Gladly took off his glasses and cleaned them.

Carlos examined the ceiling with great concentration.

At last the speaker stopped.

"There."

She drew a thick red line across the paper, rolled her shoulders, and returned to the microphone. The visor stayed on. It somehow made the size of her more absurd.

She looked at the screen.

When she spoke again, her voice was deep enough to rattle Taylor's teeth inside Raptaur's jaw.

"Okay, now..."

The lights trembled.

"IN A WORLD WHERE THE FANFIC IS FAR TOO LONG FOR US TO FIT IT ALL INTO A SINGLE TRAILER..."

The paper script continued unspooling behind her.

"MARVEL as an author writes a fic bigger than *War and Peace*... makes the whole thing read like a minute-by-minute depiction of everyday life and an entire city's reactions to it... and yet somehow manages to keep it interesting."

The screen showed a sunrise over Brockton Bay.

The shot held.

And held.

A boat crossed the bay. A dockworker unlocked a gate. A PRT analyst poured coffee. Somewhere, someone began a forum post about a lizard nobody believed in.

The sun had moved perhaps an inch.

"Minute by minute," the narrator repeated.

On-screen, the dockworker discovered he had brought the wrong key.

The Gallery laughed.

The image cut to a school hallway.

The laughter ended.

The trailer did not show the inside of the locker. It showed the closed metal door, the deserted corridor, and the last daylight fading from the high windows. That was enough. Taylor's claws tightened around the bag of EXPOSITION BUFFER.

Ianthe's hand settled on her forearm.

Metis's tail curled around the base of Taylor's seat.

The narrator's register changed. It remained impossibly deep, but the joke stepped aside.

"When one bullied girl cries out for help, the answer comes from very far away, very long ago... and with far more teeth than expected."

The black screen split.

On one side waited another path: insects, a dark costume, a city burning by installments, and choices narrowing until survival and victory meant the same thing.

On the other, a point of light opened in a darkness no camera could have filmed. Something ancient turned its attention toward a dying girl.

The point of light expanded into memory.

Luna wasn't another passenger in Taylor's head. She was a life already over, preserved in Varga: instincts, balance, battles, and the memory of a kingdom long gone. Her hands flashed on-screen once, then the memory became Taylor's motion.

"Memories," Taylor said quietly.

The narrator paused.

The screen corrected itself. A second silhouette which had begun to form beside Taylor dissolved into light and folded into the older recollection where it belonged.

"Memories," the narrator agreed.

Varga's approval moved through Taylor like distant thunder.

Mr. Gladly glanced from the screen to her. "Does it usually listen to corrections?"

"It had better," Fury said.

The endless script rustled across the floor. Several lines crossed themselves out.

"WATCH as Taylor Hebert's life takes a hard left from bugs... into INFINITELY VARIABLE DINOSAUR."

The second half of the screen exploded into scales.

Saurial grinned at a camera. Raptaur unfolded. Umihebi became a maritime zoning dispute. Kaiju made the word *Brute* look underfunded.

The Gallery erupted.

"That's not a power classification," someone shouted. "That's a municipal budget!"

"Multiple municipal budgets," another voice corrected.

"Federal matching funds."

On-screen, a PRT analyst opened a fresh assessment form, looked at the previous one, and placed both hands over her face.

Taylor laughed despite herself.

The trailer cut back to Winslow.

Taylor never woke up, because she had never been unconscious. She experienced the bond, Varga, and the inherited memories as they happened. When it was over, she was still at school—healed, altered, and entirely aware that she now had several feet of reptilian tail to account for.

She cleaned herself up in the locker-room showers. Then, instead of fleeing, she found a school camera and documented the locker: the filth, the bloody handprints, and the trail she had left behind. She removed the memory card and kept it.

Only then did she use the telephone in the secretary's office.

"Dad?"

Danny's frightened questions tumbled over one another.

Taylor's composure finally cracked. "Can you come and pick me up, please?"

"I'm leaving now."

"WATCH as Taylor responds to a life-changing ordeal," the narrator intoned, "by collecting evidence, calling her father, and arranging her own ride home."

Danny arrived. Varga's concealment let him—and only him—see Taylor. He drove her home wrapped in a blue cloak, his fear hardening into purpose by the mile.

The look on his face ended the jokes.

Taylor wrestled her brand-new tail onto the sofa without breaking anything. Danny returned with two hot chocolates and the expression of a father holding himself together by the handle.

"Tell me," he said.

"EXPERIENCE the most unrealistic event in the entire *Worm* multiverse," the narrator said more softly. "A traumatized teenage cape tells a responsible adult what happened... and the adult listens."

Mr. Gladly winced.

Fury glanced sideways at him. "You planning to object?"

"No."

"Good plan."

"I was going to say the standard is depressingly low."

"Better plan."

The music stopped.

Not faded. Stopped.

The screen held on the Hebert living room. Taylor sat curled into one end of the sofa while Danny listened.

She told him about the ruined schoolwork. The stolen clothes. The insults and the shoves and the little humiliations designed to be invisible whenever an adult happened to look their way. She told him how long it had been going on.

Danny's face lost all color.

He did not interrupt. He did not ask why she had kept it from him. He simply sat and listened with his hands locked together so tightly that the knuckles stood out white.

Then Taylor reached the locker.

She described the smell. The depth of the filth around her shoes. The things floating in it. She haltingly explained how she had hammered on the metal and screamed for someone—anyone—to let her out.

Danny raised one trembling hand.

"Excuse me for a moment."

His voice was very quiet.

He stood, crossed the hall, opened the basement door, went down the steps, and closed the door softly behind him.

The screen held on Taylor.

One second passed.

Two.

Three.

The roar from under the floor made half the Gallery jump.

"GODDAMN VICIOUS BASTARDS! I'M GOING TO KILL THEM ALL!"

Something wooden broke with a tremendous crack.

Another impact followed, then language that made Steve Rogers' eyebrows climb. The basement lost an argument with Danny.

The trailer narrator spoke over the muffled destruction in a reverent bass.

"WITNESS the mild-mannered dockworker take one short, responsible break to process his emotions."

CRASH.

"Very responsibly," Metis observed.

Taylor had both hands over her face. "I had forgotten how loud that was."

"You heard it through a floor," Ianthe said. "We have theater acoustics."

From below came one final, distant thump. Then silence.

After another long pause, the basement door opened.

Danny emerged with his hair slightly disordered and the beginnings of damage across the knuckles of his right hand. He closed the door, returned to his chair, and sat down with immense dignity.

"I'm sorry, Taylor. Please go on."

The Gallery stared.

On-screen Taylor stared too. Then she nodded and, with the careful expression of someone making a major tactical decision, said, "It gets kind of weird for the next part."

Fury turned slowly toward the enormous reptilian girl sitting among them.

Taylor lowered her hands just enough to peer back at him. "In context, it did."

"Kid, your father just tried to beat the basement to death. I think the context had already left the building."

Varga's laughter rolled through her mind. *I liked him immediately.*

Danny read Taylor's notes. The basement threatened an encore. Then he called Principal Blackwell, who discovered that interrupting a furious union negotiator was an advanced-level mistake.

He kept Taylor home. He set the meeting date. He mentioned the Dockworkers Association's many friends in local government. Blackwell's confidence began to disintegrate in real time.

Records appeared. Contradictions accumulated. A hidden camera captured words three girls had assumed no one else would ever hear. The machinery which had protected them found itself asked to explain, in writing, how it had known facts Danny had never supplied.

"Cheer as the terrifying dinosaur demon deploys its most devastating weapon."

A beat.

"Documentation."

The Gallery applauded.

Taylor looked down at her popcorn. "This is strange."

"Watching it?" Ianthe asked.

"Watching them watch it."

Metis glanced across the rows. "They're not watching you like a threat."

"Some are."

"Yes, but not only that."

On-screen, Saurial stuck a tiny camera to her headband and adjusted the angle in a mirror. The scene cut to Raptaur doing the same, then to an evidence technician receiving a memory card with the reverence normally reserved for a confession signed in triplicate.

"Follow a heroine whose answer to secret-identity complications is to film everything, provide copies to law enforcement, and make lying about the ten-foot reptile with the forehead camera a significantly worse legal strategy than admitting what you did."

"Objective evidence," Fury said approvingly.

"It saves time," Taylor replied.

"It creates time," Metis said. "Usually several hours of it, all spent answering questions about why the camera footage includes something that wasn't supposed to be physically possible."

The narrator heard her.

"And settle in for a story where every impossible event receives the one thing no cosmic horror has ever survived intact—"

The screen filled with desks. Analysts. Whiteboards. Video enhancement. Interdepartmental calls. A physicist staring at a measurement and quietly erasing the unit.

"—REACTION SHOTS."

Police reacted to the Family. The PRT reacted to the police. Washington reacted to the PRT. Foreign agencies reacted to Washington. PHO beat them all to it with one blurry photograph.

The dockworkers built something useful.

The same fishing boat from sunrise finally returned to harbor.

Its captain looked at an eighty-five-foot reptile standing in the bay, took out his phone, and began recording.

"Because in Brockton Bay," the narrator said, "terror is temporary... but content is forever."

The title card slammed onto the screen.

TAYLOR VARGA

The letters acquired a tail.

Then the tail knocked the title sideways.

Taylor stared at it.

Varga chuckled inside her mind.

"I like this movie," he said.



Chapter 3: The Hebert Family Business



The title card tipped back into place.

Before it settled, a steel-toed work boot stepped down on one corner and held it there.

Steel-toed boot. Worn jeans. DWU jacket. Danny Hebert held a clipboard, pinned a phone to his shoulder, and somehow followed three conversations over a reversing forklift.

"LAUGH ALONG WITH US," the narrator announced, "as an ordinary dockworker gets mistaken by every intelligence agency on the planet for some sort of mafioso."

Danny ended the call.

Six thousand miles away, an analyst froze the image on the exact frame where his eyes passed over the camera.

"Subject appears aware of surveillance," a subtitle reported.

In the Gallery, Taylor made a strangled noise.

"Dad was looking at a gull."

The trailer zoomed in on the gull.

It had stolen half a sandwich and was watching Danny with criminal intensity.

"Known courier remains unidentified," the subtitle added.

Metis folded forward against the seat in helpless laughter.

The screen split twelve ways. From Washington to Beijing—and several offices that officially didn't exist—Danny's photograph appeared on secure monitors. Lines connected him to unions, police, politicians, shipping firms, and increasingly large reptiles.

The lines multiplied.

One chart ran out of room and continued onto a second display.

The theories grew according to national temperament.

One agency made Danny the civilian head of a parahuman logistics network. Another made the DWU a weapons-research front. A third capitalized *Family* and built a Mafia org chart from coffee-break seating.

A British analyst suggested, cautiously, that the organization might simply be a labor union with unusually capable friends.

His supervisor asked whether he had been compromised.

The Russians called his silence professional discipline. The Chinese decided his lack of bodyguards meant the whole city was his bodyguard. An American committee argued jurisdiction for three hours, then classified the minutes to hide that everyone had brought a secret Danny file.

"Every intelligence agency on the planet," the narrator repeated, while the screen added PLEASE HOLD FOR INTERPRETER beneath a video conference with too many windows.

"That's an exaggeration," Mr. Gladly said.

A thirteenth window opened.

An intelligence service belonging to a country with fewer people than the Dockworkers Association had prepared a seventeen-page assessment of Danny's handshake.

Mr. Gladly took off his glasses. "I withdraw the objection."

"Meet Danny Hebert," the narrator said. "Hiring manager. Lead negotiator. Widowed father. Man who knows a guy."

Each phrase appeared beneath a different image.

Hiring manager: Danny shook hands with a new crane operator.

Lead negotiator: Danny sat across from a city official who had arrived confident and left agreeing to six points he had intended to reject.

Widowed father: Danny stood in a kitchen at two in the morning, waiting for Taylor to come home, pretending the coffee in front of him was the reason he was awake.

Man who knows a guy: Danny greeted a police sergeant by name, asked after his knee, and received a favor which was neither illegal nor especially dramatic.

The intelligence chart flashed red.

"CONTACT CONFIRMED," someone had typed.

"Oh, come on," Taylor protested.

"Your father has an excellent poker face," Fury said.

"He was asking about physical therapy."

"Best cover stories are boring."

"It wasn't a cover story."

"Even better."

On-screen, the Dockworkers Association began to recover.

Contracts multiplied. Old equipment woke up. Warehouses stopped collecting pigeons and started collecting paychecks. At the center stood Danny, armed with lists and the frightening ability to get the right machine to a crew before it admitted needing one.
