Halfway There

"Momentum is its own kind of proof. When the work starts moving faster than expected, that's not luck. That's the work being right."
— Murphy's Laws of Combat

Wednesday, December 17, 1997: Chestnut Street Office, Sunnydale,
Evening 8:00pm —

Dawn had learned, over six weeks of this work, that the hardest names were never the ones with the most information attached to them. The hardest names were the thin ones — a single newspaper clipping, a missing-persons flyer curled at the edges in some evidence box nobody had opened since 1974, a mother's name and nothing else.

Tonight's was thin.

MARGARET ANNE COYLE, the file read. Age 16. Last seen leaving Sunnydale High School, October 9, 1974. Case classified: runaway, presumed relocated with unknown party. Case closed, 1976.

"There's nothing else," Dawn said, quiet, turning the single page over as if a second side might materialize if she looked hard enough. "No family statement. No follow-up. They just — closed it. Two years, and they just decided she'd relocated with somebody and closed it."

Angel went very still, the way he did when a name pulled at something specific rather than general.

"I remember her," he said, finally. "Margaret Coyle. I didn't know her name was in any file at all. I assumed, the way you assume about most of them, that nobody had ever bothered writing it down properly in the first place." He was quiet a moment longer. "She used to sit on the library steps at lunch. Always alone. I noticed her because Angelus noticed loneliness the way a shark notices blood — not out of any feeling, just as an opportunity. He took his time with her. Weeks, actually, which was unusual for him. He liked that nobody would come looking."

"Nobody did," Dawn said, and it came out smaller than she meant it to. "Not for two years. And then they just — closed it. Runaway, presumed relocated. That's it. That's her whole file."

Sheila set down the folder she'd been working, and Zabuto set down his, and for a moment nobody in the small office said anything at all, because the silence a name like this required didn't have a script yet, even six weeks and forty-some names into learning how to hold this kind of grief.

"She has a name now," Zabuto said, finally, quiet. "Properly recorded. Not 'presumed relocated.' Margaret Anne Coyle, sixteen years old, murdered in Sunnydale on or shortly after October ninth, nineteen seventy-four, by the vampire this file will now correctly identify rather than omit." He looked at Angel. "I'll need your account, properly written, for the record. Whatever you're willing to give it."

"All of it," Angel said, and there was something in his voice that hadn't been there for the other names — a specific, deliberate weight, as though this one mattered to him in a way the general accounting hadn't quite managed to. "She deserves all of it. I've spent two hundred years letting myself think of what he did as a blur, a hundred years of undifferentiated cruelty I wasn't required to examine individually. That's its own kind of convenient forgetting, and I don't think I'm entitled to it any more than this town was entitled to closing her file."

Dawn wrote the name at the top of a fresh page — MARGARET ANNE COYLE, 1958–1974 — and found her hand wasn't quite steady doing it, and let it not be steady, because Sheila had taught her, weeks ago, that steady wasn't actually required for this kind of honesty.

"Ninety-four," she said, quiet, once the entry was finished.

"Ninety-four," Zabuto agreed. "Council records account for perhaps sixty of the full count independently. The remainder has come from Angel's memory and the town's own quietly buried archives, name by name, the way this entire project has had to work from the very beginning."

"We're maybe two-thirds through the full century," Angel said. "There's more. I know there's more. Some of it I only half-remember, from periods where I wasn't myself, and I don't know yet how to give you a name for a face I can't fully place."

"We'll take what you can give us," Dawn said, gentler now, some of the earlier unsteadiness settling into simple resolve instead. "Nobody's asking for perfection. Just honesty, as complete as we can actually make it, one name at a time, for as long as it takes."

Willow arrived partway through, having just finished another diagnostic sweep, and the quality of her expression told the room the news before she said anything.

"It's still improving," she said. "Faster than last time, actually. Whatever we're doing, it's working better the further in we get." She looked at the growing ledger, at Margaret Coyle's name freshly added at the top of tonight's page. "I think acknowledgment compounds. Each name makes the next one matter more, somehow, instead of less."

Sheila looked at the ledger a long moment — at ninety-four names now, properly honored after decades of convenient silence, at a girl who'd sat alone on library steps for weeks while something patient and cruel decided she was worth the wait precisely because nobody would come looking — and felt something that had been unsettled in her own long unlife since Halloween finally, slowly, settle a little further.

"Ninety-four down," she said, dry, standing to stretch a back that hadn't actually needed stretching in a hundred and thirty years but did it anyway, out of habit, the small ritual she used to mark the end of a name properly finished. "God knows how many to go. Let's keep going. Somebody's gotta finish what we started, and I'm not about to let it be some other poor bastard doing this work a hundred years from now, wondering why nobody got to it sooner."
