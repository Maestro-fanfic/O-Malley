First Sergeant, Age Eleven, Bedtime Negotiable

"The heaviest thing a soldier carries isn't equipment. It's the moment they realize the numbers on the page were people." — Tim O'Brien, The Things They Carried, paraphrased

Monday, November 3, 1997: Sheridan Residence, Rosewood Court, Sunnydale, Evening 7:40pm —

Beverly Teresa Sheridan — First Sergeant Benjy Sheridan to the seventeen kids currently under her operational command, Bev to her parents, Benjy to everyone else since the second grade for reasons nobody fully remembered anymore — was sitting on her bedroom floor with a shoebox full of maintenance logs for the amusement park's electrical grid, cross-referencing generator output against the number of working Christmas lights on the Ferris wheel, because someone needed to and apparently that someone was her.

She was eleven.

She kept having to remind herself of that fact lately, the way you'd remind yourself of a phone number you didn't call often enough to have memorized. Eleven. Fifth grade. Two more years until middle school, assuming middle school was still a thing that applied to her, which she was increasingly unsure about.

"You've redone that math three times," Misty said, from the beanbag chair by the window, where she was field-stripping a wrist rocket with the unhurried competence of somebody who'd done it forty times this week alone and intended to be able to do it in the dark, blindfolded, with her hands half-frozen, because that was the kind of contingency Misty had started planning for without being asked.

"It keeps not coming out right."

"It's come out the same wrong three times in a row, Bev. That's not a math problem anymore." Misty still didn't look up from the wrist rocket. "That's you needing something to hold that isn't actually about the generators."

"Because it keeps not adding up." Benjy set down the shoebox. Looked at her hands, which were, she noticed with the distant clinical interest that had become a habit since Halloween, not shaking. They didn't shake anymore. They used to shake, a little, before tests, before anything that mattered. They'd stopped shaking somewhere around the point where she'd had to tell four kids to move left instead of right in a hallway full of things with too many teeth, and the four kids had lived because they'd listened, and she'd had approximately one second to be relieved about that before the next thing needed deciding.

"Bev."

"I'm fine."

"You said the math wasn't adding up like it's a personal insult." Misty set the wrist rocket parts down in the order she always set them down in — order mattered, Misty had explained once, with the seriousness of someone stating doctrine, because a rushed reassembly under pressure was how people lost fingers, and Misty Pantine, who three days ago had been the kind of girl who couldn't remember her locker combination, now thought in these terms constantly and without apparent effort. "It's a Ferris wheel generator. It's allowed to just be bad math."

"I know."

"So what's the actual thing."

Benjy looked at the shoebox. At the maintenance logs. At the eleven years of her life that had, up until October 31st, mostly consisted of soccer practice and a moderate ongoing rivalry with a girl named Cassidy over who got picked first in gym class, and which now consisted of this — logistics, casualty math she didn't want to be doing, a very specific and very permanent weight sitting somewhere behind her sternum that she'd started thinking of, privately, as the thing.

"Halloween," she said.

Misty went still. Not scared-still. Careful-still. The stillness of somebody who'd learned, fast, that when Benjy said the word Halloween in that specific flat voice, the next few minutes required actual attention rather than the reflexive kind.

"What about it."

"I keep running it back," Benjy said. "The hallway. The four of them. I told them left." She looked at her hands again. Still not shaking. "What if I'd told them right."

"You didn't."

"That's not the point—"

"It's exactly the point," Misty said, setting the wrist rocket aside now, giving this her full attention in a way that would have been completely uncharacteristic of the girl she'd been two weeks ago, and was now simply how she operated. "You told them left because left was correct. You'd mapped the hallway. You knew where the thing behind us was faster than any of us did, because you'd been watching it the whole fight instead of just fighting it, which is a thing exactly none of us knew how to do before Wednesday."

"I got lucky."

"You got right," Misty said. "Those aren't the same word, and you know they're not the same word, and I think you know it and you're choosing the scarier word anyway because the scarier word means you get to keep worrying, and worrying feels like doing something, even when it's not."

Benjy looked at her.

Eleven years old. Both of them. Misty Pantine, who six months ago had been unable to find her way to the front office without a hall pass and a friend to walk with, now sitting cross-legged on a beanbag chair explaining the psychology of command anxiety with the calm precision of somebody twenty years older, because that was apparently also a thing Halloween had done to her, quieter than the wrist rockets, easy to miss if you weren't looking for it.

"When did you get smart," Benjy said. It came out more raw than she meant it to.

"I was always smart," Misty said. "I was just spending it all on figuring out how to seem like I wasn't, because seeming smart in fifth grade gets you eaten alive in a different way than the literal kind." She picked the wrist rocket back up. Started reassembling it, unhurried. "Now there's actual things trying to eat me the literal way, so. Reprioritized."

Benjy almost laughed. It came out as something closer to a real laugh than she'd managed in three days, small and surprised and genuine.

"The meeting's at eight," she said.

"I know."

"I have to give Captain Reeves the casualty numbers. The actual ones. Not the version where I say we're fine."

"You are fine," Misty said. "Fine doesn't mean nothing happened. It means you're still standing up afterward and figuring out the next thing." She finished the reassembly, checked the tension, set it in her lap with the satisfaction of a job done right. "You want me to come with you to the meeting."

"Yeah," Benjy said, quiet. "Yeah, I do."

Downstairs, Michael Sheridan called up that dinner was ready, in the careful voice both her parents had started using since Halloween — not scared exactly, but working very hard not to look directly at the thing that had happened to their daughter, the way you didn't look directly at the sun, because looking at it fully might mean having to say something neither of them had found the words for yet.

Benjy stood. Picked up the shoebox.

Her father was at the table when she came down, and something in the set of his shoulders was different tonight — less careful, more direct, the shift she'd learned to read over eleven years as the difference between Dad-who-worries and the other one, the one who used to come back from things he never fully talked about and would, for a day or two afterward, move through the house checking the locks twice without seeming to notice he was doing it.

"Casualty numbers," he said, without preamble, once Michelle had gone to get the bread. "You're taking them to the meeting tonight."

"Yeah."

"Real ones. Not the soft version."

"Yeah."

Michael Sheridan nodded once, and stood up from the table instead of sitting back down to it, which wasn't what Benjy had expected at all.

"Come here a second," he said. "Bring the shoebox."

He cleared a space on the kitchen counter and had her lay the maintenance logs out flat, the way she'd have laid out a map, and started going through them with her — not talking about fear, not talking about the meeting at all, just working the actual numbers alongside her, checking her math the way he'd have checked a rigger's inspection before a jump, silent and thorough.

"You know what they told us, first jump school," he said, eventually, still checking a column of figures. "Nobody in that plane is less scared than you are. The guy who looks calm just learned to keep his hands busy while he's scared. That's the whole trick. Not being unscared. Just not letting the fear be the one doing your job for you." He tapped the shoebox. "This is you keeping your hands busy, Bev. Numbers, logs, a job to do instead of just standing at the door waiting to jump. Nothing wrong with that. I did the same thing my whole career and so did every good jumpmaster I ever served under."

"Did it get easier?"

"No," he said, flat, honest, the way she'd learned this year that flat honest was worth more than careful. "You just get more practice doing it scared. Static line doesn't care how you feel about the door. You clip in, you check the man in front of you, and when the light goes green, you go. That's the whole job, every time, forever. Feeling ready's got nothing to do with it."

He closed the last log, satisfied the numbers actually held, and set the shoebox back in her hands.

"Numbers are good," he said. "Go eat something before you present them. Command decisions land better on a full stomach — learned that one the hard way, more than once."

"Thanks, Dad."

"Anytime." He didn't hug her, didn't make it a moment — just clapped her once on the shoulder, the same brief, solid contact he'd have given a soldier heading out the door, and went back to his coffee like the whole exchange had been nothing more than checking a chute.

"Hey," Misty said, at the door. "For what it's worth. Left was right."

Benjy looked at her for a second.

"I know," she said, and found, a little to her own surprise, that this time she actually believed it.
