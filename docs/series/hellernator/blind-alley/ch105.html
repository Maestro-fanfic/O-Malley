What the Weight Actually Feels Like

"The weight doesn't get lighter. You just get stronger arms." — attributed to Lt. Col. Michael Sheridan, U.S. Army, 82nd Airborne, Ret.

Wednesday, November 5, 1997: 1630 Revello Drive, Sunnydale,
Evening 8:00pm —

Cordelia had been home two days, and she'd spent most of them doing the performance of normalcy that came easily to someone who'd spent a lifetime performing composure in front of an audience that expected it — showering properly for the first time in what felt like a decade, eating real food in careful, deliberate portions because Ramesh had warned her stomach would need reintroducing to actual meals slowly, sitting through the board meeting and contributing sharp, useful opinions about patrol scheduling exactly the way old Cordelia would have.

She had not yet cried. She had not yet talked about the office, the desk, the cold coffee, the sound the room had gone quiet with afterward.

Xander found her on the Summers' back porch a little after eight, sitting alone in the dark with her knees pulled up, and he didn't say anything at first, just sat down beside her, close enough to be present, far enough to not crowd whatever she needed the space for.

"You don't have to talk about it," he said, eventually. "I just didn't want you sitting out here by yourself."

"I keep waiting to feel bad about it," Cordelia said, quiet, staring at the dark yard. "Guilty. Something. Everyone always says the first time costs you something, that you carry it, that it changes you." She looked at her own hands. "I don't feel guilty, Xander. I feel — relieved. Steady. Like I did the math and the math came out clean, and that scares me more than guilt would, because what does it say about me that it came out clean?"

Xander was quiet for a moment, choosing his words with the care he'd learned, over the last week, mattered more than easy reassurance.

"I don't think it says anything bad about you," he said, finally. "I think it says you spent four days deciding exactly what that woman had earned, with a clear head, not a scared one, and you were right. That's not the same as not caring, Cordelia. That's caring enough to actually look at it clearly instead of flinching from the decision." He took her hand. "The guilt you're waiting for — maybe it's not coming because there isn't actually anything there to feel guilty about. Maybe that's allowed to just be true."

Cordelia looked at him for a long moment, something in her chest that had been braced and waiting finally, slowly, permitting itself to unclench.

"It's not actually the killing part that I keep going back to," she said, slower now, working toward something she hadn't quite let herself say plainly yet. "It's the four days before it. The way she talked to me. Never once raised her voice. Never once looked scared of me, even when I gave her every reason to be. She called me 'Subject Chase' like it was my actual name, like the whole rest of me — the part with a name and a father and an actual life — was just paperwork she hadn't bothered filing yet." Her hands tightened, briefly, in her lap. "She kept me without clothes for two days as a control measure. Said it herself, plain as anything, like she was describing a thermostat setting. Not cruelty, exactly — I don't even think she enjoyed it, particularly. That's the part I can't get out of my head. She wasn't cruel because she hated me. She was cruel because I was data, and data doesn't get to keep its dignity if the dignity's inconvenient to the study."

"That's not something you did wrong," Xander said, quiet, fierce underneath the quiet. "That's something she did to you."

"I know that. I actually do know that, logically." Cordelia looked at her hands again. "But logically knowing it and having it stop being the thing my brain replays at three in the morning are two different problems, and I don't currently have a plan for the second one. I keep catching myself checking whether people are looking at me like I'm still a case file. Ramesh, mostly — nothing he's done, just the reflex, the flinch that shows up before the thought does." She shook her head, short, frustrated with herself, though the frustration had something more deliberate underneath it than simple self-pity. "I know exactly why I do that, by the way. I'm not confused about the mechanism. She spent four days training me to expect to be looked at that way, so now some part of me checks for it automatically, the same way you check for a step that isn't actually there anymore after you've tripped on it enough times. I know why the sarcasm got sharper too, back in that room — I watched myself do it in real time, decided being funny and awful was safer than being scared and quiet, because scared and quiet was what she actually wanted from me. I wasn't being brave. I was managing her, the only tool I had left once she'd taken everything else." She looked at Xander directly. "I'm telling you all this so you understand I'm not just having feelings happen to me, Xander. I'm watching myself have them, cataloguing them, same as I catalogue everything else. I just haven't figured out yet whether knowing the mechanism actually helps switch it off, or whether that's a second, separate skill I have to build from scratch." She almost laughed, short and humorless. "I killed her clean, and I stand by it completely. I just didn't expect the actual wound to be the four days before that, instead of the moment itself."

"I keep thinking about the sound of the room afterward," she added, quieter. "How quiet it got. I think that's the part I'm actually going to carry. Not what I did. Just — how quiet everything got right after."

"That makes sense," Xander said, gently. "That's not guilt. That's just what a big moment sounds like, after. The world doesn't make noise about it for you. You have to make your own peace with the silence." He was quiet a moment, then added, careful, "And the other part — the Subject Chase part. I don't think that one gets a clean answer the way the killing did. I think that one's just going to take longer, and I think you get to take exactly as long as it takes, without anybody, including you, deciding there's a deadline on it."

Cordelia leaned into him, finally, the last of the carefully held composure softening into something real.

"I'm glad you're the one who came out here," she said, quiet.

"Always going to be me," Xander said, simple and certain, and they sat together in the dark porch quiet, four days finally, genuinely, starting to become something she could actually put down rather than just carry.

Thursday, November 6, 1997; the Chase house, Sunnydale; Afternoon 4:00pm —

Aura had organized it without asking permission, which was, Cordelia reflected, watching her friends arrange themselves across the good living room furniture like they still owned the place, exactly the correct call, because if Aura had asked, Cordelia would have found four perfectly reasonable excuses to say not yet.

"We brought the bad movies," Aura said, dropping a stack of videos on the coffee table with the ceremony of someone delivering medical supplies. "The truly terrible ones. The ones we watch specifically to make fun of, because I've decided that's what this afternoon requires and nobody's allowed to argue with the woman holding the logistics notebook."

"I wasn't going to argue," Cordelia said, and found, to her own mild surprise, that she meant it.

The doorbell went before the movie even started, and Cordelia found, on the porch, a face she hadn't expected but should have — Sergeant Burroughs, CHP, still built like a doorway with a man crammed into it, out of uniform for once, holding a grocery bag with the awkward care of a large man handling something he was worried about crushing.

"Miss Chase." He looked her over, once, thorough, the same assessing once-over he'd have given a suspect or a crime scene, and seemed satisfied with whatever he found. "Heard you were back. Figured I'd see for myself instead of taking some detective's word for it." He held out the bag. "My wife made soup. Told her you probably had plenty of people feeding you already, and she told me to bring it anyway, because apparently that's not how soup works in her family."

"Thank you, Sergeant."

"Burroughs is fine. You're not in my custody anymore, God willing, so we can drop the rank." Something almost like a smile crossed his broad face. "Last time I saw you, you were telling a detective a giant robot from the future tried to kill you, deadpan as you please, like you were reporting a parking violation. I figured, given everything since, you were probably due for somebody showing up who believed you this time, instead of writing you up for a psych eval."

"You believed me back then too. Eventually."

"Eventually," Burroughs agreed, gruff. "Took me longer than it should have. I've thought about that some, since." He set the bag down properly in her hands. "Anyway. Wasn't gonna stay for the movie, whatever you kids are watching. Just wanted eyes on you myself. Man likes to finish what he starts, and I started looking out for you and Harris back in that station, whether either of you asked me to or not."

"We noticed," Cordelia said, something moved under the dry delivery. "For what it's worth."

"Good." He nodded once, satisfied, already turning to go. "Eat the soup. Tell that Harris boy I said he's still an idiot for running toward the gunfire instead of away from it, and that I'm still glad he did."

Detective Paul Stein arrived twenty minutes later, off-duty, in the ugly Hawaiian shirt, carrying a pizza he hadn't been asked to bring and clearly had no intention of being talked out of.

"Don't start," he said, before anyone could comment on the shirt, setting the box down and dropping into the armchair like he owned it, because he basically did. "I've been staring at paperwork all week. A man's allowed one crime against fashion on his day off." He looked at Cordelia, and the easy grumbling dropped out of his voice for exactly one sentence. "You eating actual food these days, or still doing the thing where you forget you're allowed to."

"I'm eating, Paul."

"Uh-huh." He didn't push it further than that, because eighteen years of knowing her had taught him precisely how far a direct question got him with a Chase, which was to say, about this far and no further. "Your old man used to say the same thing about you when you were six. 'She's fine, Paul, stop hovering.' Man was wrong about a lot of things at the end there. Wasn't wrong about that." He grabbed a slice before anyone else could. "Play the movie. I want to see the one where the shark's obviously a rubber prop on a fishing line, I've been told that's the whole appeal."

The afternoon unspooled easy after that — bad dialogue mocked in unison, Aura keeping a running tally of plot holes in the same notebook she used for everything else, Paul falling asleep in the armchair for twenty minutes and denying it with the flat conviction of a cop who'd lie to your face about smaller things too if the situation called for it — and Cordelia, watching her actual life reassemble itself around her in real time, felt something she hadn't let herself feel yet since Halloween: the ordinary relief of being surrounded by people who'd known her before any of this, and would, apparently, keep right on knowing her after.
