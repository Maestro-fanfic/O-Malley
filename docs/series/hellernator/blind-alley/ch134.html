One Hundred and Sixty-Seven Names, Spoken Aloud

"A name spoken aloud is a small resurrection." — attributed to Willow Rosenberg

Tuesday, February 17, 1998: Restfield Cemetery, Old Chapel,
Sunnydale, Evening 9:00pm —

The old chapel at Restfield hadn't held a proper service in decades, and Giles had spent the better part of an afternoon simply getting the dust and disuse cleared enough that the space felt respectful rather than merely available, sweeping the stone floor himself while Dawn arranged candles along the sills in a pattern she'd sketched out three days earlier and refused to explain until it was finished.

"It's supposed to look like the notebook pages," Dawn said, finally, when Giles asked. "The way I organize the names. I wanted the light to hold the same shape."

Giles looked at the candles — a careful, deliberate arrangement that did, in fact, mirror the cross-indexed structure of Dawn's ledger — and felt something in his chest tighten with quiet, genuine pride.

"It's exactly right," he said.

The Board arrived in a slow, respectful trickle rather than all at once — Buffy and Faith together, Kendra and Zabuto formal and solemn, Blahd and Veronique having closed the Threshold early specifically for this, Jonathan and Tamara, Warren and Seven, Cordelia and Xander, Rona standing quietly beside Michaela, still new enough to this community that she watched everyone else for the correct posture before settling into her own. The TX came last, escorted by Giles personally, standing near the back with the careful stillness it brought to anything it considered significant.

Sheila stood at the front beside Angel, the two of them holding the master list between them — one hundred and sixty-seven names, researched across ten weeks of patient, difficult work, finally ready to be spoken aloud in full.

"We don't do this because a ritual demands it," Giles said, opening the ceremony, his voice carrying easily in the old stone room. "We do this because it's true, and because this town has spent a century pretending it wasn't. Tonight, we stop pretending."

Sheila read first — the earliest names, decades old, drawn from newspaper archives and half-remembered rumor, each one spoken slowly, given its own full weight before moving to the next. Angel followed, drawing from his own two centuries of memory, his voice steady but carrying something raw underneath the steadiness, because several of these names he'd known personally, in ways the rest of the room hadn't.

"Sarah Blankenship," he said, quiet. "Nineteen years old. 1962. I remember her. She used to bring flowers to the cemetery for her grandmother, every Sunday, and one Sunday she simply didn't come home." He paused, the weight of the memory audible. "The town filed her as a runaway. She wasn't a runaway."

Zabuto read the names the Council's archives had independently confirmed, his formal bearing cracking slightly with each one, sixty years of institutional distance finally giving way to something more human. Dawn read the most recent names — the ones closest to living memory, closest to families who might still, somewhere, be quietly wondering — and her young voice, steady despite the weight, carried a specific gravity that made the whole room go very still.

When the last name was spoken — a boy named Michael Ostrander, seventeen, 1994, filed as a transfer to a school that had, upon Angel's careful checking, never actually received him — the chapel held a long, complete silence.

Willow stepped forward last, closing her eyes, extending her attention toward the boundary beneath the school the way she'd practiced for weeks now, careful and precise.

"It's settling," she said, quiet, eyes still closed. "I can feel it — not gone, but genuinely, properly settling, the way a debt actually settles when it's paid honestly instead of avoided." She opened her eyes, something like tears standing in them. "We did it. Properly. Not a trick."

The room exhaled together, one hundred and sixty-seven names finally spoken, finally honored, and Giles, looking around at the Board he'd watched assemble itself out of one impossible Halloween, felt something settle in his own chest that had been carrying weight since November.

"Thank you," he said, to the room, to the names, to all of it. "All of you. This was never going to be easy, and none of you flinched from doing it properly."

They stayed a while longer, quiet, respectful, the candles slowly burning down in their careful notebook-page arrangement, before filing out into the cool February night, the boundary beneath their town finally, genuinely, at peace.
