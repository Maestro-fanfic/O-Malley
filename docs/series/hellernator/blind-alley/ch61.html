Naked Under Fluorescent Light, Which Is Somehow Worse

Monday, November 3, 1997: Sunnydale MHMR, Topanga Street, Sunnydale, Evening 7:12pm —

Okay. So. Here's where we're at.

One paper gown that ties in the back, which — whoever designed this thing, I hope you step on a Lego, every day, for the rest of your natural life, because this is not clothing, this is a dare. One gray blanket that smells like bleach and somebody's worst week. Zero shoes, because apparently the geniuses running this facility decided the real threat to national security was Cordelia Chase's cute flats, and okay, fine, credit where it's due, that's actually not the dumbest security call anyone's made this month, but I am going to be doing this barefoot on linoleum that has personally insulted my feet forty different times since Thursday and I want that on the record.

Camera on the ceiling. Fourteen-second cycle. I know this the way I used to know which lunch table not to sit at. Some skills you don't ask for, you just wake up one day fluent.

I'm cold. I'm going to be honest about that for exactly one sentence and then I'm never bringing it up again, because if I start listing everything that's wrong with tonight we will be here till 2004.

Randall Chase's daughter. That's who's lying on this bed being a good quiet non-threatening little psych patient. Not the girl who cried in the bathroom because Harmony said something bitchy about her hair sophomore year. My father walks into a room and in thirty seconds he has the exits mapped, the weak points flagged, and the names of the three people who matter. He taught me that before I taught myself literally anything else useful. Never once said "I love you" like a normal dad. Said "always know where the door is." Same thing, apparently, in Chase family.

I am going to have so many things to say to that man someday —

No. Not someday. There's no someday for that conversation, there hasn't been one since I was fourteen, and my brain just tried to schedule an argument with a dead man like he's going to walk through that door and take a number.

Great. Excellent brain. Really pulling its weight tonight.

Fine. Not tonight. Tonight I've got a bracket. Lower left crossbar. Somebody repaired it at some point with a fastener that is not up to code for a facility that specializes in, oh, I don't know, keeping people who might want to leave from leaving. Behind it, three inches of spring steel that isn't a knife and isn't a gun and is absolutely going to be something before this is over, because that's the whole plan — you use what you've got, not what you wish you had, which is a sentence I got from a JROTC assembly I slept through sophomore year and did not expect to become my personal religion.

Camera left.

I hold still. I have gotten disgustingly good at holding still. If someone handed me a medal for Olympic-level Not Moving I would wear it to prom.

Two rotations left before the fastener clears. I've been counting in my head like some kind of unhinged metronome since Thursday, one- Mississippi, two-Mississippi, and if you'd told me in September that by November I'd have a security camera's sweep pattern memorized better than my class schedule I would've had you committed, ha, very funny, very timely, save that one, somebody's going to appreciate the psych-ward joke from inside the actual psych ward eventually. Probably not tonight. Tonight the audience is me and a camera and God, and God's not saying much.

Walsh's people have been in twice since the escape attempt. Not for "sessions." For verification. Is the subject still horizontal. Is the subject still non-threatening. The subject has gotten extremely good at non-threatening in under two days, which either means I have a future in community theater or it means Walsh's people are not paying close enough attention, and I am rooting hard for door number two.

Pooka's late.

She's not late. She's exactly on schedule and I know that, I built the schedule, and I am still lying here with my heart trying to relocate to my throat every single time that vent doesn't light up violet on the first possible second it could.

She's a seven-inch Marine with two swords and more actual discipline than half the adults I've met this week. She's fine. Say it again. She's fine.

Camera left. Right on time. Story of my life, the camera's more reliable than everybody I used to trust.

Vent's dark.

Okay, don't. Don't do the thing where you fast-forward straight to the worst version. You don't have the seconds for it. You've got a bracket and a girl the size of my thumb somewhere inside a building full of guys with guns and a boss who's already gotten at least one person killed by a Terminator this month, so — no. Not doing it. Counting instead. Left. Right. Left.

Violet light.

I nearly cried from relief and I would like that struck from the record too, right next to the cold thing.

"Hi," she whispers. The careful whisper. The not-hysterical-anymore whisper of someone who found out exactly what hysterical costs you on Halloween and has decided never to pay that bill again if she can help it.

"Hi," I breathe back, eyes at half-mast, face doing its best sedated-and-boring impression. "You okay?"

"Uh huh. You okay?"

"Working on it." Camera's sweeping toward the door. Six seconds. "Xander—"

"He's okay." Fast. Like she already knew that was going to be question one, because of course it was, because it's always question one. "Hurt but okay. Captain Chief Mickey says Harris doesn't know how to be anything else."

God. Of course he doesn't. Nobody ever taught that boy the concept of lying down and staying down, and if I get him alone for ten consecutive minutes when this is over I am going to tell him exactly what I think of that, at length, possibly while checking his pulse with my own two hands just to be sure.

Camera's on the door. Eight seconds.

"Tell them I have a second plan," I say, fast, low, spending every word like it costs money I don't have. "First plan got me information. Tell them don't move before oh-six-hundred. I need till then. You got all that?"

"Duh," Pooka says, with the offended dignity of a decorated combat veteran being asked whether she can remember a sentence, and honestly? Fair. I deserved that.

I almost laugh. Actually laugh, out loud, in a psych ward, because apparently I am fiercely, stupidly fond of a pixie with two swords and zero patience for my nonsense, and if that's the last normal feeling I have room for tonight, fine, I'll take it.

"Tell them Walsh has a backup archive," I say instead of laughing. "Not Lowell House. Here. I've got the code." Camera's sweeping back. Four seconds. "That comes out with me. Tell Michaela specifically."

"What else."

Camera's on the bed. Eyes closed the rest of the way. Sedated. Resting. Excellent, boring, uninteresting data point, nothing to see here.

"Tell Xander," I say, in the half-second the mic's only picking up breathing, "I'll be home by morning."

The pause that comes back is longer than the others.

"He's gonna worry," Pooka says, and there's nothing tactical in those four words at all, just a very small person who watched a boy try to run on a bullet wound to get to this building, telling me the truth about what that means.

"Yeah," I say. "Tell him anyway."

The light goes out.

And I lie there in my twelve-by-nine box, in my paper gown, freezing, furious, doing math on a bolt instead of falling apart, thinking about a boy who apparently does hardware checks with his left hand and doesn't even know he's doing it anymore, and I think: I have a list. Seventeen things. I'm going to say every single one of them to his stupid, brave, currently-shot-twice face, and he is going to sit there and take it, because he owes me that much for making me plan an escape in a paper gown.

Camera left. Two rotations. One.

I close my hand slow around the edge of the blanket, where no camera on earth can see what my fingers are doing, and I start working that last bolt free.

Somewhere over my head, through eleven feet of ceiling and forty-eight hours of planning, a girl the size of my thumb is flying back through a vent to blow up a meeting that hasn't even started yet.

I don't know that part. I just know the bolt's almost loose, morning's still ten hours and forty-three minutes off, and I intend to spend every single one of them getting ready to walk out that door on my own two bare, ungrateful, freezing feet.

Camera right.

I smile in the dark, where that can't see it either.
