Sixteen Years Doesn't Prepare You for a Gorilla, But It Helps

"Sixteen years will teach you plenty. A gorilla will teach you the rest in about four minutes." — attributed to Buffy Summers

Monday, November 3, 1997: Fondren High School, Gymnasium (temporary shelter),
Sunnydale, Evening 9:05pm —

Elena Diaz had left Los Angeles County EMS in 1994 for the deliberate reason that she wanted to spend the back half of her career somewhere quiet, and she had chosen Sunnydale, of all the small California towns that might have offered that, because the housing was affordable, the commute to her sister's place in Oxnard was reasonable, and the crime statistics — official ones, the kind that made it into the packet the county gave her — had looked, on paper, remarkably unremarkable.

She had been in this town three years now. She had stopped, sometime around year two, being surprised by the unremarkable statistics not quite matching what she actually saw on shift, and had developed, the way you develop calluses, a personal policy of simply not looking too hard at the gap between the two.

That policy had lasted until last Friday night, when the gap had stopped being a gap and become, instead, an entire town's worth of impossible things happening in the open, all at once, and Elena Diaz — sixteen years an EMT and paramedic, four hundred plus documented calls a year for over a decade and a half, a woman who had believed there was no category of emergency scene left that could still surprise her — had spent the single strangest overnight shift of her professional life triaging patients for injuries that made no earthly sense, in a town that had apparently decided, collectively, not to discuss any of it afterward.

She had not discussed it either. Not with her sister. Not with her supervisor, who had handed her the Fondren High shelter assignment three days ago with a specific careful blankness in his face that told her, more clearly than any briefing could have, that this was a need-to-know situation and she had, apparently, been quietly moved onto the need-to-know list without anyone formally telling her so.

She had decided, around hour six of that first impossible night, that the only way through this particular professional chapter was the same way she'd gotten through every other hard chapter in sixteen years — one patient at a time, one set of vitals at a time, and a personal, ironclad refusal to let the size of the mystery distract her from the size of the job actually in front of her.

Tonight the job in front of her included: seven minor lacerations, two sprained ankles, one dislocated shoulder that had gone back in clean, a low-grade fever in a nine-year-old that was almost certainly just an ordinary nine-year-old's virus and not, thank God, anything stranger, and one very large, very gentle, real gorilla who required, apparently, the same basic monitoring she'd give any other unusually calm large mammal patient — weight, temperature, appetite, general demeanor.

She sat now on the low bleacher a few feet from where Joel Garrity and the girl he called Amy had been having what was, without exaggeration, one of the more thoughtful conversations about the nature of borrowed memory she'd overheard in her entire career, and found herself, for a moment, simply watching them — two teenagers, seventeen at most, discussing inherited trauma and the philosophy of consent regarding knowledge you never chose to carry, with the calm fluency of people twice their age, while a gorilla snored contentedly six feet away.

She'd stopped being surprised by scenes like this three days ago. She had not, however, stopped finding them remarkable, which felt, to her, like an important distinction to maintain — the difference between numbness and simple professional composure. Numbness scared her. She'd watched colleagues burn out into it over the years, watched them stop being able to feel the weight of what they were actually witnessing, and she had promised herself, a long time ago, back in her rookie year in East LA, that she would rather feel the full weight of every strange and terrible thing she saw than protect herself by going numb to it.

So she felt it. All of it. The strangeness. A quiet grief underneath it — kids who'd had entire other lives grafted into their heads overnight, kids managing weight no seventeen-year-old should have to carry, a town's worth of families quietly, carefully, not discussing what had actually happened to their children. She felt all of it, every shift, and she coped the only way she'd ever known how to cope with anything too large to look at directly: she did the next concrete task in front of her, and then the one after that, and she trusted that the accumulation of small, specific, competent actions would eventually add up to something that resembled getting through it.

"Sorry," Diaz said, not sounding especially sorry, catching Joel and Amy both glancing her way. "It's just — you two are having a genuinely fascinating philosophical conversation next to a sleeping four-hundred-pound gorilla, and I want you to know I clocked that, and I'm choosing not to ask any follow-up questions, because I've been working this shelter for three days and I've decided that's the healthiest way to get through a shift."

"Smart," Amy said.

"Sixteen years in emergency medicine," Diaz said, checking something on her clipboard without urgency. "You learn the questions that help the patient and the questions that just help your own nerves, and you learn to only ask the first kind. Guy shows up with a gorilla he's apparently fostering with his witch friend, that's a second-kind question, every time." She nodded at Magilla, still snoring contentedly against the gym mats. "He's due for another checkup tomorrow, by the way. Dr. Osei wants weight and temperature again, said his numbers looked good Thursday but she likes a pattern before she stops worrying."

"You've been checking on him personally," Joel said.

"Somebody's got to, and frankly he's the easiest patient in this entire building," Diaz said, with the dry affection of someone who'd clearly become fonder of the arrangement than she was strictly willing to admit. "He doesn't argue about his vitals. He doesn't try to leave against medical advice. World's best patient, honestly, present company held to a much lower standard." She looked pointedly at Joel's staff, at the faint singed patch on his sleeve he hadn't yet explained to anyone. "Speaking of which — that burn on your forearm. When were you going to mention that."

"It's minor."

"That's also a second-kind answer," Diaz said, already reaching for her bag. "Sit still. Gorilla whisperer or not, you're still on my list tonight."

Her radio crackled. A minor call — a scraped knee, two bunks over, a kid who'd fallen off a cot trying to reach a granola bar on a high shelf, the mundane injury that felt, some nights, like the kindest thing in the entire building.

She finished with Joel's burn first, quick and efficient, then stood, gathered her kit, glanced once more at the sleeping gorilla, and headed toward the scraped knee with the same steady, unhurried competence she brought to everything, because that competence was, she'd decided three years ago and confirmed three days ago, the only thing in this entire strange profession that had never once let her down.

"Diaz," she said, quietly, to herself, the way she sometimes did on hard shifts, a small private ritual of naming herself back into her own body when the job got large enough to blur the edges. "You're still here. Still doing the job. That's enough for tonight."

It was enough. It had to be. She had four more hours on shift, a gorilla due for vitals, and, somewhere across town, she suspected, though nobody had told her directly, a great deal more of this strange, impossible, quietly beautiful mess still unfolding, one concrete task at a time.

She got to work.
