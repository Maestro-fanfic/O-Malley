The Sub-Basement, Finally Investigated

"Some rooms get quietly erased from the map precisely because someone needed them forgotten." — attributed to a Sunnydale High School facilities memo, redacted

Sunday, April 19, 1998: Sunnydale City Hall, Sub-Basement,
Night 1:00am —

Angel, Sheila, and Nancy Doyle went in together, the hour chosen carefully around the night janitorial staff's rotation, moving through City Hall's ordinary basement toward the access point Dawn's research had identified — a maintenance door, unremarkable, that the 1898 permits confirmed led somewhere the last ninety-seven years of official records insisted didn't exist.

Nancy had been the obvious choice the moment Giles mentioned a sealed chamber nobody living had properly documented in a century — she'd developed, since Halloween, the unshakeable competence of someone who apparently now knew, bone-deep, exactly how old structures wanted to be read, how a door that looked original rarely was, how the dust pattern on a hinge told you more than any permit ever would.

"This lock's been serviced within the year," she said, crouching to examine it, one gloved finger tracing the mechanism with careful, practiced curiosity. "Whoever's using this door isn't worried about anyone official finding it. They're worried about anyone competent finding it. There's a difference, and it tells you something about how confident they are."

The door opened easily, old but well-maintained, someone clearly still tending it despite its official erasure.

"This has been used recently," Sheila said, quiet, examining the hinges. "Regularly, in fact."

The stairs descended further than either of them expected, and Nancy went first without being asked, moving with the unhurried economy of someone who'd apparently done this exact kind of descent countless times in a life she'd never actually lived, testing each step's weight before committing to it, watching for the pressure plates and tripwires that a century of careful preparation would absolutely have included somewhere in this stairwell.

"Clear," she called back, quiet. "Whoever built this wanted it defended, but they built the defenses for someone in a hurry. We're not in a hurry."

The room, when they finally reached it, was larger than the stairwell had any right to suggest — a low, wide chamber that had clearly been carved out well before City Hall itself existed, older stone underneath newer stone, the layered geology of a place that had been important to someone long before Sunnydale had a name.

The walls were covered floor to ceiling in symbols cut directly into the rock, not painted, not chalked — carved, deep and deliberate, in a script Angel recognized in pieces without being able to place the whole. Some of it looked older than Latin. Some of it looked older than writing had any business looking. Whoever had carved it had done so slowly, over what must have been years, because the strokes never wavered, never corrected themselves, the unhurried confidence of someone who'd had a century to get every line exactly right and had used every year of it.

At the room's center, a raised dais of dark, unmarked stone sat empty, roughly circular, its surface worn smooth in a pattern that suggested repeated, ritual weight rather than accident — something had stood there, again and again, for a very long time, always in the same spot, always facing the same direction.

The air smelled of cold stone and something underneath it, faint and sweetish and wrong, the smell Angel had learned, across two centuries, to associate with places where something significant had died slowly rather than quickly.

In the far corner, half in shadow, sat the box — old wood, iron-strapped, roughly the size of a steamer trunk, its surface free of the dust that coated everything else in the room. Someone had moved it recently. Someone dusted around it, carefully, the way you'd dust around something you visited often enough to have a habit about it.

"The box Trask mentioned," Angel said, quiet, recognizing the weight of the moment. "Whatever arrived on April first."

Nancy crouched beside it, studying the seal without touching it, the patient stillness of someone who'd learned, the hard way apparently, exactly what happened to people who opened old sealed things before they'd finished reading them properly. "Don't touch that yet," she said. "See the dust displacement around the edge? Somebody's opened this recently and resealed it. That means either it's been checked on a schedule, or something inside needed adjusting. Either way, I'd want to know which before anyone's hands go near it."

He didn't open it. Two centuries of careful instinct told him some things were better documented than disturbed, and between his own caution and Nancy's, they photographed everything instead, careful and thorough — the carved walls, the worn dais, the box in its careful nest of undisturbed dust — before they retreated the way they'd come.

"We need to show Giles this immediately," Sheila said, once they were safely back on the street.

"And Willow," Angel agreed. "If there's a ritual component sitting in that box, she needs to see it before graduation, not after."

"For what it's worth," Nancy added, "whoever's maintaining that room is patient, methodical, and doesn't expect to be found. That's not a description of somebody planning to improvise. That's a description of somebody who's had a hundred years to get this exactly right." She said it with the dry satisfaction of someone who enjoyed being the one to read a dangerous room correctly, rather than the one caught off guard by it.

They walked back toward Chestnut Street together, the grim satisfaction of confirmed intelligence settling over all three of them, a century-old plan finally, fully visible in all its careful, patient architecture.
