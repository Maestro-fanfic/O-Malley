The Gap Column

"Intelligence work is ninety percent noticing what's ordinary and ten percent noticing what isn't. The ratio never changes. Only the stakes do." — Murphy's Laws of Combat

Monday, November 3, 1997: Levinson Residence, Paden Street, Sunnydale, Evening 7:50pm —

The thing nobody warned you about, when a war hero's entire combat memory got installed in your head via a Halloween costume malfunction, was the quality of quiet that came afterward. Not peace. Jonathan Levinson had stopped expecting peace somewhere around day two. Just quiet — the kind where you sat very still and watched the exits without deciding to, because some part of you that wasn't entirely you anymore had decided the exits mattered more than whatever conversation was happening in the room.

"You're doing it again," Tamara said, not looking up from the legal pad.

"Exits," Jonathan said, which had become shorthand between them for the whole phenomenon, three syllables standing in for sorry, that wasn't me, or it was me, I can't always tell anymore.

"Specifically the window this time, or the door?"

"Both. Window's got a fire escape landing. Door's got a straight shot to the stairs." He heard himself say it in the flat cataloguing voice that wasn't his voice, not originally, and made himself stop. Made his shoulders drop half an inch, which Tamara had taught him was the tell — when his shoulders came up, the residue was driving, and when he noticed and dropped them, he was back.

Tamara had learned all his tells in about four days flat, which said something about her that Jonathan hadn't fully worked out how to categorize yet, except that it was good, whatever it was, and he was grateful for it in a way that embarrassed him slightly every time he thought about saying so.

"Gap column," she said, tapping the legal pad. Three columns: TX BEHAVIOR. BEHAVIORAL BASELINE (Harmony). GAP. The gap column was the interesting one. The gap column was where Jonathan's actual brain — the part that was just his, unaugmented, Sunnydale-High-social-nothing Jonathan Levinson — did work that the borrowed combat instincts couldn't touch.

"The TX is running Harmony's social architecture," he said. "Competently. Better than competently — it's had three days and full access to everything Harmony ever knew, every calibration, every reflex." He tapped the second column. "But there's a lag. A processing lag before it executes anything socially calibrated. Harmony didn't have that lag. Harmony was mean the way some people are cold — it wasn't a decision, it just happened before she'd thought about it."

"And the TX thinks first."

"Every time." Jonathan looked at the gap column, at the pattern he'd been building for three days now, cross-referenced against Giles's nightly notes. "It's not faking human. It's approximating human, competently, from the outside in. That's not the same thing as being human, and I don't think it thinks it's the same thing either."

Tamara wrote that down. Her handwriting was better than his — looped, confident, the handwriting of somebody who'd never once doubted a thought was worth committing to paper, which Jonathan found, honestly, kind of remarkable, given that six months ago she'd have said the same thing about him and been catastrophically wrong.

"You found something else today," she said. Not a question. She'd learned to read him almost as well as she'd learned to read the TX reports.

"Sightlines." He flipped back two pages in his own notebook, found the sketch he'd made that afternoon, the rough floor plan of Sunnydale High with three positions marked in red. "I walked the building at lunch. There are exactly three spots where you can see both the library entrance and the main hallway doors at once. I checked, and Harmony never used any of them — she didn't think in sightlines, she thought in social geography, who's-watching-me-and-do-they-matter."

"And the TX has been standing in them."

"According to Giles's notes, yes. Repeatedly." Jonathan looked at the third column. GAP. "That's not social behavior. That's tactical behavior. Which means whatever's running behind Harmony's face has been doing threat assessment on its own containment environment for three days straight, which either means—"

"It's planning an escape," Tamara said.

"Or it's protecting something," Jonathan said. "And I don't know which, and I think that not-knowing is the actual thing I need to bring to Giles tonight, because everybody keeps asking me for a conclusion and the honest answer is I've got a gap and I don't have enough data to close it yet."

Tamara was quiet for a moment, looking at the sketch, at the three red marks, at the accumulated evidence of a Terminator quietly, patiently, thinking about a school library for three days straight.

"Audie Murphy never got an after," she said, carefully, like she was testing the shape of the thought before committing to saying the rest of it. "Whatever's in your head stops on a burning tank destroyer with half his platoon down and Germans still coming. No homecoming. No years afterward learning to stop flinching at doors, because that memory doesn't include ever making it out the other side." She looked up. "What if that's what you're actually looking at, in the TX. Not a machine building an escape route. Something that also doesn't have an 'after' to reference — that ran a mission, and the mission stopped, and it has no template at all for what happens once you're just supposed to be safe. So it keeps watching exits. Not because it's planning to use one. Because it doesn't know what standing down is even supposed to feel like."

Jonathan sat with that for a long moment.

The residue in him — the part that was Audie Murphy's, the part that had come home decorated and broken and had spent thirty years trying to be a person again in a body that kept flinching at doors — recognized something in what she'd just said with a clarity that had nothing to do with tactics at all.

"You're better at this than me," he said, quiet, meaning it entirely. "The reading-people part. I've got the tactical read down cold. You've got the actual person underneath it, every time, and I don't know how you do it."

"Practice," Tamara said, and something in her expression shifted, warmer, less about the gap column now. "Four days of learning your tells, remember. Turns out once you've figured out somebody's shoulders, the rest of them gets considerably easier to read too."

"Is that a compliment or a warning."

"Depends how you behave the rest of the evening," Tamara said, mild, not looking up from the legal pad, though the corner of her mouth was doing something that suggested she knew exactly what she'd just done. Jonathan felt his own ears go warm and elected, wisely, not to comment on it.

"That's not in the reports," he said, quiet.

"No," Tamara agreed. "It's not data. It's just — a guess. About what it's like to be a thing that used to only know how to be dangerous, and is trying to figure out how to be something else, in a room everyone's watching."

Jonathan looked at the gap column. At the space between what the TX did and what Harmony would have done, which had felt, until thirty seconds ago, like a tactical puzzle and now felt like something considerably more like a mirror.

"I need to write that down," he said. "Not for the report. For — I don't know. For me."

"Write it down for the report too," Tamara said, gently. "Giles should hear it. Michaela's people should hear it. Everybody keeps treating the TX like a math problem, and maybe it is one, but maybe it's also just—" she shrugged, a small, careful gesture, "—somebody who came back from something, the way you did, and is trying to figure out who that makes them now."

Jonathan looked at her.

"When did you get good at this," he said.

"I've always been good at this," Tamara said, echoing something she couldn't have known Misty had said in almost the same words three blocks away, in the way that this town's kids had all apparently gotten quietly, terrifyingly competent in the same three-day window, each in their own direction. "I just didn't have anything worth being good at it for, before."

Jonathan closed the sketch notebook. Checked his watch. Forty minutes to the meeting.

"Eight o'clock," he said.

"Eight o'clock," Tamara agreed, already gathering the legal pad, the sketches, the whole careful architecture of the gap column, ready to carry it into a room full of adults who were, Jonathan had started to suspect, going to need this specific insight a great deal more than they currently understood.
