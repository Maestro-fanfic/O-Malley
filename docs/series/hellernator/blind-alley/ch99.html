Old Money, New Paperwork

"New paperwork is simply old power wearing a disguise it hasn't fully learned to wear yet." — attributed to Veronique de Alençon

Wednesday, November 5, 1997: Squisher's Basement, Sunnydale,
Night 11:15pm —

Squisher's basement smelled the way it had smelled for as long as anyone currently drawing breath in Sunnydale could remember, which was to say it smelled like old stone, older secrets, and whatever the current management was using to mask both, and Veronique de Alençon found, three days into actually doing business here, that she'd developed a genuine fondness for the consistency of the smell, the way one grows fond of any environment that reliably tells the truth about itself.

"The terms," said the creature across the table — old, formless in the way that made assigning it a gender or even a species feel presumptuous, known to the room only as the Ledger, because it kept Squisher's actual books and had done so, by all accounts, since before the building had walls — "are seven percent, compounded at the old rate, against the manor's eventual revenue. Standard terms, for old blood re-establishing a house."

"Seven percent at the old rate is closer to eleven by modern compounding conventions," Veronique said, without heat, simply stating the correction the way one corrects a minor arithmetic error. "I am aware what the old rate actually compounds to, Ledger. I have paid old rates before. Several times, in several centuries."

Something that might have been amusement rippled through the Ledger's formless bulk. "You are better informed than most who walk in seeking credit."

"I have eight hundred years of informing myself," Veronique said. "I intend to continue the habit."

Blahd, beside her, said nothing, content to let her run this negotiation, because they'd agreed early — this was her specific expertise, financial architecture across centuries, in a way his own considerable strengths didn't especially cover.

"Six percent," Veronique said. "At the old rate. Compounded annually, not monthly, given the manor's renovation timeline requires patience before it produces revenue at all."

The Ledger considered this with the unhurried patience of something for whom negotiation was simply another form of conversation, unbounded by mortal urgency.

"Six and a half," it said. "And information sharing, as previously discussed. Trask's movements. The formation's — apologies, the Board's — activities, as you observe them."

"We will share what serves mutual interest," Veronique said. "Not everything, unconditionally. I did not survive eight centuries by handing over unlimited intelligence to every creditor who asked for it."

"Fair," the Ledger allowed. "Six and a half, then. Information as it serves us both."

Veronique extended a hand — an old gesture, from a older century, that the Ledger apparently still recognized, because it extended something that might have been a hand in return, and the deal was sealed with the ceremonial weight that old things brought to agreements they intended to honor.

"The manor renovation begins in earnest tomorrow," Blahd said, once they'd stepped back out into the cool November night. "The plasterers arrive at eight."

"Good," Veronique said, and something in her voice held real satisfaction, the pleasure of a problem solved cleanly rather than merely survived. "I confess I had not expected to feel quite this invested in a nightclub renovation, given everything else currently occupying this town's attention."

"You've built six residences across four centuries," Blahd said. "I imagine the habit simply persists, regardless of context."

"Perhaps." Veronique looked up at the stars, at the ordinary November sky over a town that had, this week alone, hosted a Terminator's redemption, a federal black site's collapse, and a pixie-brokered alliance with the Lost Boys. "Or perhaps I simply find myself, for the first time in some centuries, curious what happens next. That's not nothing, Savros. That's rather the opposite of the exhaustion I've been carrying since roughly the Restoration."

Blahd looked at her, something old and considering in his expression.

"No," he agreed, quietly. "It's rather the opposite indeed."

They walked back toward Crawford Street together, nine and eight centuries of accumulated weariness both, unexpectedly, feeling just slightly lighter than they had in longer than either of them cared to calculate.
