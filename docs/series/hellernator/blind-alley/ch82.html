What the Ghost Roads Sound Like When You're Not Using Them

"The dead don't actually rest easy. They just stop being in a hurry." — Murphy's Laws of Combat

Monday, November 3, 1997: Rosenberg Residence, Sunnydale, Evening 9:00pm —

Willow Rosenberg's bedroom had, over the past three days, developed a very specific problem, which was that she could no longer sit in it after dark without being fully aware of exactly how many walls stood between her and the outside, because for one entire night on October 31st she had known, personally and completely, what it was like to have no walls stand between her and anything at all.

She'd tried explaining this to her parents. She had not succeeded. Sheila and Ira Rosenberg had returned from their conference in Pasadena two days after Halloween to a daughter who insisted, calmly and specifically, that she was fine, and who had also rearranged her entire bedroom so that her bed no longer faced the closet, for reasons she had not been able to articulate in any way that didn't sound insane.

She was not fine. She was working on it. There was a difference, and she'd started, over the last three days, to actually believe there was a path between the two.

"You've been looking at that closet for a while now," Jenny Calendar said, from the desk chair, where she'd been sitting for the better part of an hour going through a stack of books that Giles had loaned her with the caveat that several of them were extremely old and she should handle the bindings gently.

"Sorry. Zoned out."

"You went very quiet and looked at it like it had said something to you," Jenny said, not unkindly. "I've been a teacher for three years, Willow. I notice when a student's somewhere else."

Willow looked at the closet. It hadn't said anything. It never said anything. That was, itself, the strange comfort of it — closets were supposed to be silent, doors were supposed to be solid, walls were supposed to actually stop things, and for one night none of that had been true for her, and some part of her was apparently still checking, every few minutes, whether it had gone back to being true.

"I keep remembering things I never learned," Willow said, finally, because Jenny had earned the honesty by simply sitting there, patient, not pushing. "Not spell components. Not incantations, though I have plenty of those too, suddenly, filling up space in my head like books on a shelf I never bought. It's — smaller things. A specific way an old woman's hands moved when she was teaching me — teaching someone, teaching whoever I was, a lifetime ago, in a life I never lived — how to bind a wound. The smell of a kind of candle smoke. A whole childhood of being afraid of my own power and slowly, over years, learning not to be." She looked at her own hands. "I have a witch's entire life in my head, Jenny. A whole life, start to finish, layered underneath my own like — like wallpaper somebody put up over older wallpaper, and now I can see both patterns at once whenever I look too closely."

Jenny set the book down, carefully, at the angle that meant she was giving this her full attention now.

"And the other thing," she said. Gently. Not pushing, exactly, but not letting it go unnamed either. "The ghost."

Willow was quiet for a long moment.

"That's different," she said. "That's not memory. That's — I was there. I did it. Me, this me, the me sitting in this chair right now — I walked through the Kellerman's living room wall on Wednesday, and I know exactly what that felt like, because it happened to my actual body, whatever my actual body was for those two hours." She wrapped her arms around herself, an old habit from being cold that didn't quite apply anymore, because the cold she remembered from that night hadn't been a temperature. It had been an absence. "There's no boundary between things, when you're that. No wall means anything. No door is really shut. You just — go, wherever the intention takes you, and the only thing keeping you anywhere is deciding to stay."

"That sounds terrifying," Jenny said, quietly.

"It should have been," Willow said. "That's the part I haven't figured out how to say to anyone yet. It wasn't terrifying. It was — quiet. Genuinely, deeply quiet, in a way nothing in my actual life has ever been, because I've always been the girl who worries about everything, all the time, all at once, and for two hours none of that mattered, because worry needs walls to bounce off of, and I didn't have any." She looked at Jenny. "I liked it. That's the thing I can't say to my parents. I liked being a ghost, Jenny. Some part of me is still homesick for it, and I don't know what that says about the actual me, the me that lives here, in this body, behind these walls, worrying about tests and boys and whether Xander's going to be okay."

Jenny was quiet for a long moment, and Willow watched her actually consider it — not flinch from it, not rush to reassure her out of a feeling that was clearly, obviously true — which was, Willow had learned over three days of increasingly strange conversations, becoming rarer and more valuable than she'd have guessed a week ago.

"I don't think that makes you broken," Jenny said, finally. "I think it means you got a very brief, very real look at a kind of freedom most people never get to see, and it's going to take you longer than three days to figure out how that fits next to the rest of your life. That's not a flaw, Willow. That's just — a lot, arriving all at once, the way everything's arrived all at once for everyone this week." She paused. "Does the — knowledge, the witch's knowledge — feel like it's working with the ghost thing, or against it?"

"Wait," Willow said, something occurring to her. "What were you? That night. Everybody keeps talking about their own thing, and I never actually asked you."

Something amused and unbothered crossed Jenny's face. "A gladiatress. Real one, apparently — not just a costume-shop archetype. I came out of it speaking actual Latin, Willow. Accented Vulgate Latin, the kind a linguist would've had opinions about, and at one point rather a lot of Celtic or Iberian, I still don't know which, cursing at a young man who very much deserved it. I was carrying a real sword by the end of the night — a spatha, I've since looked it up — and I swung it at someone's throat without a single second's hesitation, and I would have taken his head clean off if it had actually connected." She said it plainly, no drama in the delivery, the ease of someone who'd had seven months to make her peace with the fact. "The strangest part isn't that I did it. It's who I did it for. I called Rupert 'Scholar' the entire night, like it was the only name that mattered, and near as I can work out, whoever she was, she'd decided he was hers to protect, immediately and completely, before either of us had said a proper word to each other in this life. I didn't get a name for her. No memories of an actual life the way you've got, layered under my own. Just the sword-work, the languages, and that one specific loyalty, which I'll admit I've thought about rather more than the Latin." She shrugged, easy about it, comfortable in a way that made clear she'd settled this before tonight. "Rupert finds the whole thing more interesting than I let on that I do. I mostly just appreciate not flinching anymore, and I'll admit I've never once minded that she picked him specifically."

"That sounds like it's been easier for you than this has been for me."

"It has been," Jenny agreed, plainly, no false modesty about it. "I didn't inherit somebody else's grief or somebody else's fear, Willow. I inherited somebody else's steadiness. That's a much simpler gift to carry than the one you got handed. I'd trade you if I could, if it would actually make this easier on you — but I don't think it works that way, and I think you already know that too."

Willow considered this seriously, the way she considered most things, methodically, out loud.

"With it, I think," she said, slowly, working it out as she said it. "Whoever she was — the witch, the life I'm remembering — she knew things about the space between the living and the dead. Not casually. Seriously, the way you know something you've studied your whole life. Ghost roads, she called them, in the memory, though I don't think that's a term anyone actually taught her, more like something she arrived at on her own after enough years of walking close to that boundary professionally." Willow looked up. "And now I have her knowledge of that boundary, plus my own actual, physical memory of what it's like on the other side of it. I don't think anyone alive is supposed to have both of those at once."

"What are you going to do with it?"

Willow was quiet for a long moment, looking at the closet, at the door, at all the ordinary solid things in her ordinary solid room that she was, slowly, over three days, starting to trust again.

"I don't know yet," she said, honestly. "But I think it's going to matter. Not today. But someday, when something's happened that needs someone who actually knows what's on the other side of the wall, instead of just guessing." She looked at Jenny, steadier now than she'd been at the start of the conversation. "I think that's going to be me. I think I'm supposed to figure out how to be ready for that, instead of just being scared of what I already am."

Jenny reached over and squeezed her hand, briefly, warm and solid and entirely, reassuringly present.

"Then let's start figuring it out together," she said. "Properly. Not just borrowed memory and instinct — actual training, actual understanding, so that when the day comes that you need it, you're not improvising with somebody else's half-remembered life. You're working with your own, fully yours, built on top of what you were given rather than just haunted by it."

Willow looked at her, and something in her chest — something that had been coiled tight and watchful since Wednesday night — loosened, just slightly, for the first time in three days.

"Yeah," she said, quiet. "I'd like that."

Outside, the November evening went on being ordinary, and somewhere across town a meeting was still filling a conference room with people who were all, in their own ways, carrying exactly this same kind of impossible weight, and finding, slowly, that carrying it together made it something closer to bearable.
