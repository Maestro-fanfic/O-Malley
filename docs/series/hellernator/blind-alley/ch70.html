All the King's Men

"All the king's horses and all the king's men couldn't put Humpty together again — but they could, at least, all show up to try." — traditional nursery rhyme, adapted

Monday, November 3, 1997: South Marion Drive, Sunnydale Medical Complex, Conference Room B, Sunnydale, Evening 7:58pm —

The conference room smelled like industrial solvent and fluorescent futility, and no amount of Joyce Summers's garlic bread was going to fully fix that, though it was, Michaela had to admit, putting up a genuinely heroic fight.

She'd counted heads twice already, out of habit more than necessity. Giles and Zabuto, arrived nine minutes ago, Zabuto going around the room in one slow professional sweep the moment he walked in, cataloguing exits and threats with a Watcher's trained thoroughness that Michaela recognized because she did the same thing herself, just faster and with better posture. Joyce, setting the garlic bread down next to Aura's covered dish with the calm of a woman who'd stopped being surprised by anything three days ago and had decided the only sane response left was to keep feeding people. Dawn, one step behind, notebook already open, pen already moving.

Brockhurst had found coffee somewhere in a building that had no business producing coffee this good, and handed her a cup without ceremony. "Don't get used to it, Army," he said, mild. "Zoomie hospitality's a limited-time offer."

"Careful, Colonel," Michaela said, taking it anyway. "Keep being useful and people might start mistaking you for competent."

"Wound me," Brockhurst said, unwounded, and went back to his tablet.

Cheng had the far corner, laptop open, running comms checks on the extraction team's frequencies with the economy of a man who'd done this in worse rooms than a hospital conference space, against stranger things than whatever tonight turned out to require, and had stopped, some years back, seeing much daylight between the two categories.

"Boss," Buckley's voice came in low over her earpiece, flat, no wasted syllables, from the mobile command post two blocks out. "Sitrep."

"Full house, minus the two currently extracting," Michaela said, thumb on the mic. "Cheng's got comms."

"Copy. We clear on the vampire and the exchange student who isn't a vampire."

"We're clear."

"And the eleven-year-old running her own casualty reports like a company clerk."

"Also clear."

A pause, exactly as long as it took a man who'd personally walked out of two things that shouldn't have been survivable to decide whether a joke was worth the airtime. "Copy. Wouldn't have made it into the brief if I hadn't seen weirder. Buckley out."

Cheng, without looking up from the laptop, allowed himself the smallest possible smile — the rationed amusement of a man who'd learned, a long time ago, exactly how much humor a comms channel could afford before it started costing you something.

"Disposition," she said, to the room.

"Moseby's in the corridor. Merrill's doing a final sweep of the loading dock." Brockhurst checked his tablet. "Bell's with the MPs, coordinating something she's being cagey about."

"Cagey how."

"Cagey in the way that means she's already done the thing and is waiting for the right moment to mention it," Brockhurst said, mildly, and Michaela decided she didn't have the bandwidth tonight to chase that thread down before the meeting started.

The door opened. Buffy came in first, dark blue, steadier than Michaela had seen her look in the two brief encounters they'd had so far, and behind her Jonathan Levinson and a tall girl with a legal pad Michaela didn't yet have a name for, both of them moving with the focused urgency of people who'd found something and needed it heard before anyone else's agenda ate the room's attention.

"Levinson," Michaela said. "You look like you've got something."

"I might," Jonathan said, and there was a flatness in it that Michaela had learned, over three days, to take seriously — the flatness of somebody quoting a much older, much more tired voice than his own. "It's not conclusive. I want to say that up front so nobody treats it like conclusions."

"Noted. Sit."

He sat. The tall girl sat next to him, notebook already angled so they could both read it, and Michaela filed that too — whoever she was, she mattered to how Jonathan Levinson processed information now, which meant she mattered to the room.

Warren Mears and a girl Michaela's briefing materials identified as Kimberly Williams — no. Not Williams. Seven. Just Seven, apparently, the briefing had been very specific that using anything else got you a correction, delivered politely but without deviation — came in ninety seconds later, exactly as advertised by nobody except themselves, and took seats with the economy of two people who'd already had every conversation they needed to have before walking in.

"Captain Reeves," Seven said, by way of greeting, precise, formal, without small talk. Michaela decided she appreciated that more than she'd expected to.

Giles cleared his throat. "Before we begin properly — Jonathan, you said you had something on the TX's behavior."

"The gap column," Jonathan said, and walked the room through it — the sightlines, the three positions overlooking both the library entrance and the main hall, the processing lag between what Harmony would have done and what the TX actually did. Michaela watched the room's attention sharpen as he laid it out, watched Giles go very still in the way he went still when something confirmed a suspicion he hadn't wanted confirmed.

"And your read," Giles said, when Jonathan finished, "is not escape."

"My read," Jonathan said, and here his voice did something Michaela hadn't heard from him yet, something quieter and more careful, "is that it might just be watching exits because it learned to and doesn't know how to stop, the way people do, after. Not because it's planning anything. Because it can't turn it off." A pause. "I'd know something about that."

Nobody said anything for a moment. Giles looked at Jonathan with an expression that had gone, briefly, soft.

"That's — extremely useful, Jonathan," Giles said. "Thank you."

The door opened again. Benjy Sheridan came in with Misty Pantine a half-step behind her, and Michaela watched an eleven-year-old First Sergeant walk to the front of the room, set down a folded sheet of paper with actual casualty numbers on it — not the softened version, the real one — and deliver it standing at something that was, unmistakably, parade rest, before anyone had even asked her to report.

"Casualty and status report from the Irregulars, ma'am," Benjy said. "Full numbers. I didn't round anything down."

Michaela took the paper. Read it. Looked at the girl in front of her, eleven years old, steady, having clearly decided sometime in the last three days that steady was a thing you built rather than a thing you waited to feel.

"Thank you, First Sergeant," Michaela said, and meant every syllable of the rank.

The room kept filling. Aura with her notebook, relaying an update from Xander's room that she delivered in the flat precise register of someone who'd made logging into a discipline rather than a hobby. A runner from the school confirming the TX's status — unchanged, still, watching. Zabuto asking Giles a low quiet question about Kendra that Michaela didn't catch and didn't try to.

At two minutes to eight, the door opened one more time, and the room's temperature changed.

Michaela clocked it before she consciously understood why — the shift in air pressure that happened when something old walked into a space, the kind of old that made ordinary predators go quiet out of instinct rather than calculation. Two teenagers stood in the doorway. A boy, maybe fifteen, with the stillness of something that had learned to hold very still for centuries and kept the habit out of preference rather than necessity. A girl beside him, sixteen maybe, moving like she'd spent a very long time in spaces where the geometry itself had been hostile, and had simply stopped noticing.

Giles went the kind of still that meant something had just become more complicated.

"Baroness," he said.

"Watcher," the girl said, and something passed between them that had centuries stacked on one side of the conversation and considerable hard-won experience stacked on the other.

"You know them," Michaela said.

"Not personally," Giles said, still watching the girl with the careful voice he used for things that required precision. "By reputation. The Count, whose current era name is—"

"Blahdivastri," the boy said. "Savros Hieronymus. Your records will show Iverson. Freddy Iverson." A pause that did several things at once. "That era is concluded."

"And you," Giles said, to the girl.

"Veronique de Alençon," she said. "Currently Veronica Daley, although similarly — concluded." She looked around the room, taking in Buffy, Benjy, Jonathan, all of it, cataloguing with the efficiency of someone who'd already decided what she needed to say and was simply confirming her audience before saying it. "We are also, at present, the only entities in this city who know the complete floor plan of Squisher's basement, the identity of the individual managing the Mayor's supernatural intelligence network, the location of four of the seven illegal safe houses your surveillance has not yet found, and the prior cache location the Terminator used before it adopted the Kendall girl's body." Her gaze settled on Michaela. "We did not come here to be a threat. We came because we have information relevant to your current situation, and we have an interest in how it resolves."

The room held its breath in the way rooms do right before somebody senior decides which way this goes.

Michaela looked at Giles. Giles looked back with an expression that said, clearly, your call, my read is cautiously proceed, and this is Sunnydale, Captain, act accordingly.

She looked at Brockhurst.

He looked at the two teenagers in the doorway with the mild expression he reserved for things that were not remotely mild.

"You said you know who manages the Mayor's network," Brockhurst said.

"Trask," Veronique said. "Older than he appears. Considerably more capable than his manner suggests. And currently reporting to City Hall about the contents of this exact meeting, which he became aware of approximately forty minutes ago."

The room's quality changed again, sharper this time.

"Sit down," Michaela said. "Both of you." She glanced at Joyce, who was already reaching for the bread. "Mrs. Summers — does anyone want garlic bread."

"Of course," Joyce said, with the perfect, unshakeable calm of a woman who had made her peace with this town some time ago and intended to keep everyone fed through whatever came next regardless.

The Board, Michaela thought, watching the room settle — Buffy and Jonathan and Warren and Seven and Benjy and Misty and now two vampires older than most nations, all of it converging into one overcrowded conference room that smelled like solvent and garlic bread — was, against every reasonable expectation, actually assembling.

"All right," she said, and the room quieted for her. "Let's start with what everyone's found."
