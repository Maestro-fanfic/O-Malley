A Door That Stays Open

"An open door costs nothing to build and everything to maintain." — attributed to Rupert Giles

Wednesday, November 12, 1997: Chestnut Street Office, Sunnydale,
Evening 6:00pm —

Marcus Okafor had spent three days working up the nerve to walk into an office he'd only heard about secondhand, and Sheila Martini looked up from the maintenance ledger with the mild curiosity of someone who'd learned, over the last two weeks, not to be surprised by who walked through that door anymore.

"Can I help you?"

"My name's Marcus Okafor," he said. "I fell through a door in the Never-After four days ago. I've been talking to Admiral Landlorn. I think you need to hear what he told me."

Sheila's expression sharpened considerably. "You'd better sit down."

Giles arrived within ten minutes of Sheila's call, and sat across from Marcus with the patient attention he'd learned to bring to anyone who looked like they'd been carrying something too large for one person to hold alone.

"Start from the beginning," he said. "However long it takes."

"I was walking home from the library," Marcus said, still visibly working to make his own memory of it sound like something that had actually happened rather than something he'd dreamed. "There was a — a seam, I guess, in the air, right where the old Hendricks lot used to be before they tore the building down. I didn't even really decide to walk through it. My foot was just already moving before the rest of me caught up." He rubbed his hands together, an old nervous habit apparently still very much intact. "And then I was standing on a ship. An actual ship, wooden, cannons, the whole thing, except the sky over it didn't have a sun in it. Just a kind of gray light that came from everywhere at once."

"The Never-After," Giles said, quiet.

"That's what he called it. Landlorn." Marcus's voice steadied slightly, finding its footing in the telling. "He's — I don't have good words for what he is. Old. Older than old. He didn't threaten me, which I think surprised both of us. Just sat me down and asked me questions for what felt like hours, real careful questions, like he was trying to figure out exactly how I'd gotten there before he decided what to do about it."

"And what did he conclude?"

"That the wall between his place and ours is thinner than it's been in a long time. Thin enough that I fell straight through it by accident, which he said basically never happens anymore, not since some kind of settlement a long time back set the boundary properly." Marcus glanced between Giles and Sheila. "He's not happy about the thinness. Not angry, exactly. More like — worried, the way somebody worries about a fence that used to be sturdy suddenly not holding weight it used to hold. He kept asking me what was different about Sunnydale lately. What's changed. I told him about Halloween, the machine, the vampires, all of it, best I could explain it."

"And his response to that."

"He said, and I think I've got this close to word for word, 'A town that concentrates that much unusual power in one place eventually finds the walls between places grow thin around it, the way water finds the weakest point in a dam.' He offered to keep the channel open. Said he'd rather know what's happening on this side than be surprised by it from his."

Giles rubbed his eyes, the weariness of a man adding one more genuinely enormous variable to a board already carrying more than any single corkboard should reasonably hold. "You're telling me," he said, slowly, "that whatever we're building here is thin enough now to be bleeding through into other planes entirely."

"That's what he told me," Marcus said. "And he wasn't lying. I've gotten pretty good at reading when ancient things are lying to me, the last few days. He wasn't."

"Well," Giles said, finally. "I suppose we'd better find out what's causing it, before Admiral Landlorn's curiosity turns into something less patient."

"He said the door stays open," Marcus said. "Whenever you're ready to use it."

Sheila looked at Giles. Giles looked at the board, mentally, already composing the new entry this would require.

"Thank you, Marcus," Giles said. "This may turn out to be the single most important thing anyone's told us all month."

Marcus, exhausted, relieved, and more informed about the actual shape of reality than any seventeen-year-old had any right to be, allowed himself, for the first time in four days, to simply sit down and let someone else carry the weight of what came next.
