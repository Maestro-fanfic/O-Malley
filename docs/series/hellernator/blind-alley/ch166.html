Two Hundred Heartbeats

"When the window finally opens, you don't get to hesitate. You get to have already decided, months ago, exactly what you're going to do the instant it does." — Murphy's Laws of Combat

Tuesday, June 16, 1998; Sunnydale High School, Football Field; Afternoon 1:05pm —

The vulnerability window did not open the way a real door opened. It opened the way a held breath released — all at once, and only for exactly as long as the lungs behind it could sustain the effort.

"Now!" Willow called, and felt the window catch, felt it want immediately to snap shut the way every unstable thing wanted to return to its rest state, and held it anyway, both hands out, sweat already breaking cold across her back despite the June heat. "It's open — I've got it, but not for long, go, go now—"

She could not watch the fight. That was the cruelest part of the job she'd built for herself — holding the window meant keeping her attention entirely on a shape only she could see, ancient and vast and straining against her grip, and trusting completely that everyone else was doing their part in a fight she couldn't spare a glance toward.

"Target acquired," Seven reported, beside the weapon platform, voice flat, hands steady. "Discharge in three. Two. One."

The weapon fired. The bolt crossed the field in less time than either she or Warren could consciously register, and struck exactly where Willow's window had promised the vast, still-stabilizing shape would be weakest — a soft, unarmored joint just beneath what had, until ninety seconds ago, been Richard Wilkins III's left shoulder — and the impact staggered the whole enormous mass of it, a visible ripple running through flesh that shouldn't have had anything so ordinary as flesh at all.

"Confirmed hit," Seven said. "No degradation. Recharging."

"It worked," Warren said, disbelieving despite four hundred rehearsals having promised him it would.

On the east perimeter, three of Wilkins's remaining loyalists broke from the tree line, and Kendra met the first of them without needing to look, trusting Zabuto completely to cover the ground she couldn't watch herself.

"Behind you," Zabuto called, and she pivoted, the third attacker's blow meeting empty air where she'd stood a half-second earlier.

"They're thinning," Zabuto called, breathing hard now but steady. "Whatever's happening at the stage is drawing their attention. Press it."

At the north perimeter, the demonic reinforcements came up out of the sub-basement in a ragged wave, more numerous than Trask's reconnaissance had suggested, and Blahd met the first of them with the unhurried efficiency of nine hundred years' practice.

"There are more than we planned for," Veronique said, her blade already wet.

"Which I find, oddly, reassuring," Blahd said. "I intend to reward the assessment that made him spend this much."

Near the evacuation lanes, Benjy checked her timer against the chaos. "Gate one's moving. Gate two, report."

"Moving," Devila's voice came back, flat, unbothered.

"Clear on gate three," Chessie called from somewhere nobody could see her. "Cordelia's reroute is holding exactly like she said it would."

Kato said nothing over the radio, because Kato never had much use for radio chatter, but her progress could be tracked by the wake of calm that followed her — two frightened children gathered up before their own parents had processed the danger, an elderly man steered gently away from debris. Michael Sheridan moved through the crowd near gate one without any radio at all, thirty years of a jumpmaster's instinct for keeping a stick moving finding new purpose on an otherwise ordinary Tuesday.

"Ninety seconds," Benjy called. "We're inside the window. Keep them moving."

Near the library doors, the TX saw the four students an instant before the vast, still-staggering shape's momentum would have carried a glancing blow directly into them — two younger siblings wandered from the family section, two seniors frozen at the sight of what their principal had become — and it did not calculate the optimal outcome first, the way it once would have. It simply moved, placing itself between the students and the blow, servos in its left side screaming a diagnostic warning it chose, deliberately, to ignore.

"Move," it said, flat, urgent. "Now. This way."

They moved. Kato's small, competent hands gathered the youngest of them the instant they reached the evacuation lane.

Forty yards back and eleven feet up, Cordelia cycled the bolt on the Griffin & Howe, breathing even, the stillness her father had drilled into her over a decade of dove fields and skeet ranges finally earning its keep on a day worse than any of those had been. Her first shot had gone in clean. Her second had gone wide by inches, a correction made instantly, professionally, without letting herself feel the half-second of cold panic that came with a miss on a day like today.

"Angel," she said into the comm. "Tell me the second the joint opens again. I won't get a fourth chance at this angle once Buffy closes the distance."

"Three seconds," Angel called back, tracking the shifting mass with two centuries of demon-hierarchy knowledge. "Two. Cordelia, now—"

She fired. The round found the same soft joint her second shot had missed, and this time it held — the vast mass staggering hard enough that Buffy's opening strike, arriving a half-second later, found less resistance than it would have otherwise.

Buffy felt the shift the instant it happened, and moved, Faith already moving beside her, closing the last several yards together with the fluid coordination of eight months' rehearsal finally collapsing into pure instinct.

"Now," Faith said, unnecessary, because Buffy was already there, already swinging, her strike landing exactly where Angel's guidance and Willow's impossibly held-open window had promised. Faith's follow-through arrived a half-second later, finishing what Buffy's opening had started.

The ancient shape that had been Richard Wilkins III for a hundred careful, patient years collapsed. Completely. The two-hundred-heartbeat window closing not with stabilization but with total, genuine defeat.

The field went suddenly, completely silent.

"That's it?" Faith said, breathing hard, disbelieving. "That's actually it?"

"That's it," Buffy said, and felt her own knees, briefly, consider not holding her weight, and made them hold it anyway. "Willow — you can let go now. It's over."

A pause, and then Willow's voice, exhausted, shaking, relieved: "Letting go. Oh, thank God, letting go—"

"Report," Michaela called, into the settling quiet. "Casualties, injuries, status—"

"All civilians clear," Benjy reported, breathless, triumphant. "Full evacuation successful, zero casualties among the families."

"TX took a hit protecting students," Giles called, already moving toward it. "Status?"

The TX straightened, damaged but functional. "I am functional. The students are safe. I do not regret the damage. I would make the same choice again, with full knowledge of the cost."

Smoke settled slowly over a graduation ceremony that had, in the span of one impossible minute, tested every single thing eight months of careful, coordinated preparation had built, and the whole field, disbelieving, began, slowly, to breathe again.
