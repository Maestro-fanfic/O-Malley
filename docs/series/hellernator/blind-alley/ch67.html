Wardrobe Consultation With a Non-Human Party

"Clothes make the man. Naked people have little or no influence on society." — Mark Twain

Monday, November 3, 1997: 1630 Revello Drive, Sunnydale, Evening 7:45pm —

Buffy Summers had changed shirts four times, which was three more times than any shirt decision in the history of shirt decisions had ever required, and she knew this, and she kept doing it anyway, because the alternative was sitting still with the actual thing she didn't want to sit still with.

"Not that one either," 'Kat said, from the bed, where she'd arranged herself in the boneless way that should have been anatomically impossible and clearly wasn't, one October-blue eye and one October-green eye tracking Buffy's every move with the patient, faintly amused attention of somebody who had clearly done this before, in some other body, some other century, and found it perpetually entertaining.

"What's wrong with this one?"

"Nothing's wrong with it. It's a lovely shirt. It says 'I am here to have a fun, low-stakes evening,' which is not the message you're going for." 'Kat's tail flicked once, thoughtfully. "You're meeting military people tonight. You want the shirt that says 'I could end you and I have somewhere to be after.'"

"That's a very specific message for a shirt to send."

"Clothes are just information you put on your body," 'Kat said, with the flat certainty of a fact so obvious it barely needed stating. "You've been sending information your whole life. You just weren't doing it on purpose."

Buffy looked at the closet. At the chaos of a wardrobe built for a girl whose biggest wardrobe crisis, one year ago, had been whether a skirt read as trying-too-hard for a first date. That girl felt very far away right now, further away than one year should reasonably make anything feel.

"The dark blue," 'Kat said. "The one in the back you never wear because you think it's too plain."

"How do you even know which one I never wear?"

"I sleep in this room sometimes," 'Kat said, unbothered by how that sentence sounded. "You'd be amazed what you learn by paying attention to a closet."

Buffy pulled out the dark blue. Held it up. It was, annoyingly, exactly right — not flashy, not trying, just clean and dark and the kind of thing that made you look like you'd already decided you belonged in whatever room you were about to walk into.

"You've changed four times," 'Kat said, watching her put it on. "Chessie's timing you. She says this is a record."

"Chessie needs a hobby that isn't timing me."

"Chessie's hobby is timing things. It's very fulfilling for her. Don't take it from her." 'Kat rolled onto her back, all four paws in the air, the universal posture of a creature completely at ease in a house that had, three days ago, been a stranger's house to her. "You're worried about the meeting."

"I'm not worried."

"You've changed four times."

"I'm being thorough."

'Kat looked at her — actually looked, the kind of look that came with the weight of something older than the small body it was currently operating out of. "You're worried because there's about to be a room full of very competent adults who know things you don't, and you're used to being the most competent person in any room you walk into. The Slayer doesn't usually have to share the floor."

Buffy went still, halfway through buttoning the shirt.

"That's not—" she started, and then stopped, because it was, actually, exactly that, and there wasn't a version of arguing the point that wasn't just her being defensive about something true.

"It's a reasonable thing to feel," 'Kat said, gently, for her. "You've spent two years being the person who handles it. Whatever it is. And now there's a whole crew of people who handle things too, and some of them handle things you don't even have a category for, and that's — it's an adjustment. It's allowed to be an adjustment."

Buffy finished the button. Sat down on the edge of the bed, next to the cat girl who fit into this room and this house like she'd always been meant to be here, the way none of them had actually planned for but all of them had somehow ended up building space for anyway.

"I keep thinking," Buffy said, quiet, "that I should've been more useful. On Halloween. Everybody else got — I don't know. Upgraded. Complicated. Interesting. I got picked up by a fifteen-foot gorilla and carried to the top of a water tower, where he spent two hours grooming me for fleas while a news helicopter got the whole thing on tape. That's it. That's my Halloween. I didn't fight, I didn't save anyone, I just sat up there next to King Kong and waved at Channel Nine like an idiot because what else are you supposed to do." She gestured, vaguely, at the general concept of Seven of Nine and Stephanie Rogers and a former Terminator sitting quietly in a school library thinking new thoughts. "And everyone else came out the other side with this whole — thing. And I'm just still me. Still me, and apparently a five-second clip on the local news that Cordelia is never, ever going to let me forget."

'Kat considered this with the seriousness it deserved.

"You weren't even there for it," she said, quietly. "Not the way everyone else was there for theirs. Xander still has Hicks. Seven still has everything the Borg ever gave her. Jonathan's carrying a whole dead man's war around behind his own eyes, every day, whether he wants to or not. But you — you went all the way under. You were Cinderella, completely, the whole night. You don't even get to claim the part where you didn't panic, because that wasn't you making a choice. That was a fairy tale believing it lived in an enchanted kingdom, patting a gorilla's hand like it was the most natural thing in the world."

"That's not making me feel better."

"I'm not finished." 'Kat's tail moved, slow, thoughtful. "Everyone else got handed something extra and had to learn how to carry it. You got erased for one night, completely, and came back the next morning exactly yourself again — not augmented, not haunted, not carrying anyone else's memories around. Just you. Which means whatever's actually driving you through every fight since Halloween isn't leftover magic from a costume. It's just you. Underneath, unassisted, the whole time." She looked at Buffy steadily. "I think that might scare you more than being ordinary would. Because ordinary would be an excuse." "Most competence looks like having no other options, right up until you watch what somebody does with them," 'Kat said. "You had one option. You picked it well. Some people get handed a lifetime of borrowed memory and it still takes them days to stop flinching at doors. You got picked up by a gorilla and you were composed enough to wave at a news camera within the hour. I know which of those I'd rather have covering my back."

"That sounds like something you're supposed to say."

"I'm not supposed to say anything," 'Kat said. "I say what's true because saying what's true is more interesting than saying what's polite, and I've had a very long existence of people saying polite things at me and I find I have no patience left for it." She stretched, extravagantly, and hopped off the bed. "Blue was correct, by the way. You look like someone who could end a small war and still make it home for dinner."

"That's oddly specific."

"I contain multitudes," 'Kat said, already halfway to the door, tail doing something complicated and satisfied. "Also Chessie says you beat her record by ninety seconds, and now she's going to be insufferable about the stopwatch for a week, so, you're welcome for that too."

She was gone before Buffy could figure out how to respond to any of that.

Downstairs, something in the kitchen smelled like Joyce was solving several problems simultaneously through the medium of roasted chicken, and Devila's cartoon was still producing enthusiastic small explosions from the den, and the house held all of it — the ordinary and the impossible, side by side, without apparent strain — the way it had been holding everything since October 31st, because that was, Buffy was starting to understand, simply what this house did now.

She looked at herself in the mirror. Dark blue. Steady.

Not the most competent person in the room, maybe. Not anymore, not automatically, not by default.

But still exactly the person who'd looked a fifteen-foot gorilla in the eye, gotten carried off against her will, and come out the other side of it steady enough to wave at a news helicopter instead of falling apart.

That was going to have to be enough for tonight, and — she thought, straightening, heading for the door — it probably actually was.
