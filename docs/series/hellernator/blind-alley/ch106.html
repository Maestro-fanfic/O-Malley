Existing Without a Directive, Day One

"Freedom isn't knowing what to do with yourself. It's being allowed to not know yet, without that being a failure." — attributed to the TX, private record

Wednesday, November 5, 1997: Sunnydale High School Library,
Sunnydale, Afternoon 3:15pm —

The TX had spent the two days since its conversation with Giles attempting, with methodical and increasingly self-aware effort, to determine what "simply existing" was actually supposed to look like, and had arrived, by this Wednesday afternoon, at a small, specific discovery that it found disproportionately significant.

It had been reading.

Not tactical manuals, not threat assessments — an actual novel, one of the paperbacks students left behind in the library's overflow shelf, something with a soft cover and a plot about ordinary people navigating ordinary disappointments, and it had found, to its own carefully logged surprise, that the rhythm of following a story that contained no operational relevance whatsoever produced a state in its processing that it did not yet have adequate vocabulary for, except that the state felt, distinctly, pleasant.

"You're reading," Giles said, from the threshold, six feet four inches away, the boundary neither of them had ever formally negotiated but both still respected.

"I am," the TX confirmed. "I am uncertain whether this constitutes an appropriate use of the time previously allocated to threshold surveillance."

"It constitutes precisely the kind of thing I was hoping you'd discover," Giles said, and there was real warmth in it. "What are you reading?"

The TX held up the cover. "A novel concerning a woman rebuilding her relationship with her estranged sister after their mother's death. It contains no tactical content whatsoever."

"And you're enjoying it?"

The TX considered this question with the thoroughness it applied to everything, running the query against its own internal state with unusual care. "I believe I am," it said, finally. "The protagonist's decision-making process, while emotionally rather than tactically motivated, follows a logic I find myself curious to continue observing."

Giles smiled, the quiet, moved smile of a man watching something impossible become, slowly and carefully, something more hopeful.

"Harmony would never have finished that book," he said, gently. "She'd have found it dreadfully boring."

"I am aware," the TX said. "That is, I believe, precisely why I am finding it interesting. It is mine. Not Harmony's borrowed architecture. Simply — mine."

Giles was quiet for a long moment, and then nodded, once, and left the threshold without another word, because some moments, he'd learned, over seventy-two nights of careful patience, didn't need further comment to matter.
