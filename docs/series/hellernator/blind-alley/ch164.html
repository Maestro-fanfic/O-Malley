Names Called, Diplomas Given

"The last ordinary moment before the extraordinary one is often the hardest to sit through patiently." — attributed to Jonathan Levinson

Tuesday, June 16, 1998; Sunnydale High School, Football Field; Morning 9:30am —

The ceremony began at nine, exactly on schedule, Wilkins himself presiding with his usual pleasant, unhurried warmth, utterly unconcerned that every single person watching him from the Board's carefully positioned vantage points understood, precisely, what he actually was.

Names called in alphabetical order. Diplomas handed across the stage. Proud parents photographing every handshake with the oblivious joy of people who had no idea their children's graduating class contained two Slayers, a vampire hunter's honorary Tech-Sergeant, and a reformed war machine currently stationed by the library doors in a rented cap and gown of its own, because Giles had insisted the TX walk the stage too, when its turn came, and the TX had found, to its own quiet surprise, that it wanted to.

Buffy sat in her assigned seat among the graduating seniors, cap and gown identical to everyone else's, counting down the alphabet toward her own name with a specific tension that had nothing to do with academic pride.

"You okay?" Faith murmured, seated two rows back among the crowd, close enough to be heard, far enough to look unremarkable.

"Counting names," Buffy murmured back. "We're maybe forty minutes out, based on the pace."

Jonathan sat near the stage's edge, ostensibly waiting his own turn, actually watching Wilkins's every gesture with the analytical focus that had, eight months ago, first cracked open the mystery of the TX's behavior. "No visible change yet," he murmured to Tamara, seated beside him, her hand finding his without either of them needing to discuss it. "Still fully human-presenting. Whatever the trigger point is, it hasn't happened yet."

"You'll know it when it happens," Tamara said, quiet. "You've read the file on this more than anyone. Trust the read."

Dawn watched from the family section beside Joyce, notebook tucked discreetly in her lap despite Joyce's gentle insistence that today, of all days, she simply be a sister watching her sister graduate. She wrote nothing yet. She simply watched, the weight of eight months' careful preparation sitting heavy and real in her chest.

Michael Sheridan sat two rows behind Joyce, in a suit that fit him the way old uniforms sometimes still did, watching the field with the flat scanning attention of a man who had done this kind of watching professionally once and had never put the habit down. Beside him, Michelle Sheridan held his hand, tighter than either of them would have admitted to, watching their eleven-year-old daughter stand at parade rest near gate one like it was the most natural posture in the world for a fifth grader to hold.

Willow, positioned near the field's edge with careful, unobtrusive stillness, kept her attention extended toward the boundary, waiting, patient, for the first sign of the shift Landlorn had warned them to expect.

The alphabet moved steadily onward — Anderson, Ashworth, Barnes — and Wilkins's pleasant voice carried across the field, warm and paternal and utterly, chillingly unbothered, exactly as it had been for a hundred patient years leading toward this single, carefully chosen morning.
