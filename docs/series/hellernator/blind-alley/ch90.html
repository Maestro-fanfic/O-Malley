Everyone's Version of Not Sleeping

"Insomnia is just the mind refusing to file the day away as finished." — attributed to a Sunnydale Memorial intake form

Monday into Tuesday, November 3–4, 1997: South Marion Drive, Sunnydale Medical Complex, Conference Room B,
Sunnydale, Night 1:15am —

The meeting had technically ended hours ago, in the sense that people had stopped sitting in chairs around the table, but Michaela Reeves had learned enough about this town's specific rhythms in three days to know that a meeting like tonight's didn't actually end so much as it slowly redistributed itself into smaller, quieter versions of itself scattered across a building that never fully went dark.

She was on her fourth cup of coffee, which Brockhurst had stopped commenting on around cup three, on the theory, he'd said, that a woman running an extraction in four hours forty-five minutes had earned the right to poison herself with caffeine on her own recognizance.

"Final positions," she said, laying the annotated floor plan across the table one more time, the paper soft now at the creases from repeated folding. "Bell's confirmed the loading dock blind spot holds through oh-six-thirty. Moseby and Merrill take the perimeter, north and south. You and I take the extraction point itself."

"And the writs," Brockhurst said.

"Serve at oh-eight-hundred, exactly on schedule, whether Chase is out by then or not." Michaela traced the timeline with one finger. "We want her clear at least ninety minutes before service, so Walsh's legal scramble doesn't have any incentive to accelerate a search of her own facility before the paperwork actually lands."

"And if the second plan doesn't hold. If Chase can't move at oh-six-hundred for whatever reason."

"Then we go to the writs alone, and we get her out through the front door with federal paper instead of a vent," Michaela said. "Slower. Messier. But not nothing." She looked up. "I don't love that version. I trust her more than I trust paperwork with a recused judge's replacement I haven't vetted yet."

Brockhurst was quiet for a moment, looking at the floor plan, at the small annotated X that marked the cell block, at the four hours and forty-five minutes standing between now and the version of tonight where this either worked cleanly or didn't.

"You're pushing hard on this one," he said, not quite a question.

"I'm pushing hard on all of them." Michaela didn't look up from the plan. "But yeah. This one specifically." She was quiet a beat, deciding, apparently, that four days and four cups of coffee had earned him the honest version. "I've got two of my own, back with their father, because active duty and custody don't mix and I made my peace with that a long time ago. Doesn't mean I don't think about it, every single time one of these extractions has an actual kid's name attached to it instead of a case number. Chase is eighteen and dangerous in her own right, and I'm still going to move heaven and earth to get her out clean, because somewhere back in Colorado I've got a girl about to turn eleven who I'd want somebody moving heaven and earth for, if it were her instead."

"Didn't figure you for kids," Brockhurst said, careful, not unkind.

"Most people don't. I don't exactly advertise it." She tapped the timeline, done with the subject as abruptly as she'd opened it. "Oh-five-thirty. Let's not be late."

"You've been running this op for three days on maybe six hours of actual sleep," he said.

"I'm aware."

"I'm not telling you to stop," he said. "I'm telling you I've watched good operators lose the extra half-second that matters because they convinced themselves exhaustion was the same as discipline." He set his own cup down. "Take twenty minutes. Actual eyes-closed twenty minutes, not just sitting quietly pretending. I'll hold the board."

Michaela looked at him for a long moment — at the steady competence that had, over three days, become something she genuinely relied on rather than merely tolerated, which was, for a woman who'd built her entire career on relying on precisely no one she hadn't personally vetted twice, its own small quiet data point worth noting.

"Twenty minutes," she said. "Wake me if anything moves."

"Nothing's going to move for twenty minutes," Brockhurst said. "That's rather the point of picking this specific twenty minutes."

She let herself sit back in the chair, close her eyes, and found, to her genuine surprise, that sleep actually came, thin and fast and useful, the soldier's sleep that didn't ask permission and didn't overstay it either.

Monday into Tuesday, November 3–4, 1997: Chestnut Street Office,
Sunnydale, Night 1:20am —

Sheila Martini did not sleep, which was, on nights like this, a genuine professional advantage rather than the quiet loneliness it usually felt like, because it meant she was awake at 1:20am when Giles came through the door looking like a man who'd been arguing with his own conscience for several hours and had finally lost.

"You should be resting," she said.

"So should you, in whatever sense applies," Giles said, dropping into the chair across from her with the weariness of a man carrying more than tonight's immediate crisis. "I keep thinking about the TX."

"The conversation."

"Yes." He rubbed his eyes. "I told it, essentially, to simply exist without a directive attached. I'm not at all certain that was responsible advice to give something with that much raw capability, Sheila. What if 'simply existing' produces something none of us can anticipate?"

Sheila considered this, turning it over with the patience of someone who'd had a great many decades to develop patience as a discipline rather than merely a personality trait.

"I existed for over a century without a directive," she said, finally. "Nobody built me a mission. I simply survived, and did what survival required, until Halloween gave me the chance to ask what I actually wanted instead of what continued existence demanded." She looked at him steadily. "It was terrifying. It was also the first time in a hundred and thirty years I felt like a person rather than a process. I don't think you did something reckless tonight, Giles. I think you did something kinder than reckless, and kindness is always a small risk, because you can't fully control what someone does with the freedom you hand them."

Giles was quiet for a long moment.

"You're better at this than you give yourself credit for," he said. "The reassurance. The perspective."

"I've had more practice being uncertain about my own nature than most people," Sheila said, dry. "It turns out that's transferable."

Outside, the November night held its quiet, four hours and forty minutes from a deadline that mattered to nearly everyone in this town, whether they knew it yet or not.

Monday into Tuesday, November 3–4, 1997: Rory Harris's property, above Ojai; Night 1:00am —

Rory sat on his own porch steps with a rifle across his knees he had no present use for, cleaning it anyway, because his hands needed something to do and the alternative was sitting still with what his property currently looked like under the yard lights.

Four of Michaela's people hadn't walked away from his land tonight. He'd known exactly none of their names before this evening and would carry every one of them now regardless, because that was simply how it worked, he'd learned that lesson younger than he'd have liked and had never once managed to unlearn it since. Some of Walsh's people hadn't walked away either, and he found, turning that over, that he couldn't locate even the beginning of regret about that half of the count.

He'd fired his own weapon tonight. Actually fired it, at an actual thing that had actually needed stopping, for the first time in longer than he generally let himself think about. His hands weren't shaking now. They'd shaken plenty an hour ago, in the delayed way hands did, and he'd let them, alone on the porch, because there hadn't been an audience worth performing steadiness for.

The phone had rung twice already. Xander's voice, tight and careful, checking on him with the formality of a boy trying very hard not to sound like he needed the checking-on to go the other direction too. Rory had told him he was fine. Mostly true. True enough for tonight, anyhow.

He thought about the last time his hands had shaken like that. A jungle, a considerable number of years and a considerable number of pounds ago, a much younger man in a body that used to bounce back from things overnight instead of aching about them for a week. He'd buried that ghost decades back, mostly, the way you buried things — not gone, just quiet, filed somewhere you didn't visit on purpose.

Tonight had gone and dug the whole plot up without asking permission.

"Should've stayed retired," he said, to nobody, to the dark yard, to four strangers whose names he intended to learn properly by morning and never once forget after. "Should've just stuck to fixing up cars and complaining about my knees like a normal old man."

The yard didn't answer. Yards generally didn't.

He kept cleaning the rifle anyway, methodical, unhurried, the old discipline of a man who'd learned a very long time ago that grief and vigilance could occupy the same set of hands at the same time, and that neither one required the other's permission to be real.

Around two, headlights came up the long drive — Michaela, back for a follow-up sweep she'd insisted on doing herself rather than delegating — and Rory set the rifle aside, stood, and went to meet her, because whatever tonight had cost either of them, there was still a morning coming that both of them intended to be standing upright for.

"You should be resting," Michaela said, taking in the rifle, the cleaning kit, the state of a man who clearly hadn't slept and had no present intention of starting.

"Physician, heal thyself, Chief Warrant Officer," Rory said, dry, and something passed between them that didn't need further words attached to it — two people who'd both just watched people die on the same patch of ground, deciding, separately and simultaneously, that the way through it was simply to keep standing, and to make sure the kids who still had a morning ahead of them got to have one.

Monday into Tuesday, November 3–4, 1997: South Marion Drive, Sunnydale Medical Complex, Room 412,
Sunnydale, Night 1:30am —

"You should sleep," Aura said, for the fourth time in an hour, not looking up from the notebook.

"I'm resting my eyes," Xander said, eyes very much open, fixed on the water stain.

"That's not a real category of rest. I checked with Ramesh. He specifically said 'resting your eyes' is not a recognized medical phase of recovery, it's a phrase people use to avoid admitting they're not actually resting."

"Ramesh talks a lot for a man who's supposed to be off shift."

"Ramesh cares about you in a way he finds professionally inconvenient," Aura said, which was, Xander reflected, probably the single most accurate description of Dr. Ramesh's entire relationship to this patient that anyone had produced all week. "Four hours and thirty minutes."

"I know the countdown, Aura."

"I know you know it. I'm just confirming we're both still doing math on the same clock." She set the notebook down, finally, and looked at him directly, something unguarded in it that she allowed herself rarely and always, Xander had noticed, precisely when it mattered most. "She's going to be fine, Xander."

"I know."

"You keep saying that like you're trying to convince the water stain."

Xander almost laughed, and it came out closer to real than he'd have predicted an hour ago. "Maybe I am. It's a good listener. Very patient. Never interrupts."

"Low bar," Aura said, and there was real warmth in it now, unguarded, the warmth of someone who'd spent the last several days discovering that caring about people out loud cost less than she'd always assumed it would. "Four hours and twenty-nine minutes."

"I heard you the first time."

"I know," Aura said. "I'm just going to keep saying it, every few minutes, until it's zero, because apparently that's what I do now instead of sleeping. Logging. Watching. Counting down." She picked the notebook back up. "It's in the methodology."

Xander looked at the ceiling, at the water stain, at the quiet weight of four hours and twenty-nine minutes standing between tonight and whatever came next, and found, for the first time in days, that the waiting felt less like helplessness and more like the last patient stretch of a plan that was, against every reasonable expectation, actually working.
