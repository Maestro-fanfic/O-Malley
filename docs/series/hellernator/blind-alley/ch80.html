A Lifetime of Knowing Things You Never Studied

"Some knowledge you earn. Some knowledge simply arrives, uninvited, and asks to be lived with." — attributed to Jonathan Levinson, private notebook

Monday, November 3, 1997: Fondren High School, Gymnasium (temporary shelter), Sunnydale, Evening 8:50pm —

Fondren High's gymnasium had been converted, over the past three days, into the kind of makeshift operation that happened whenever a town quietly needed somewhere to put people and things it wasn't ready to explain to the outside world — cots along one wall, a folding table with a coffee urn that never seemed to run empty, and, in the far corner past the bleachers, three hundred and eighty pounds of extremely content silverback gorilla, currently asleep on a stack of gym mats with his knuckles curled under his chin like a housecat twelve times too large for the metaphor.

Joel Garrity sat cross-legged on the floor a few feet from him, staff across his lap, doing the kind of stillness that wasn't meditation exactly but wasn't not meditation either — the stillness of a man learning to listen to something that had opened up inside his head three days ago and hadn't fully stopped talking since.

"He's snoring again," Amy Madison said, from the folding chair she'd pulled up beside him, not looking up from the paperback she wasn't really reading.

"He does that when he's happy," Joel said. "I looked it up. Well — Dresden looked it up, once, a long time ago, for reasons I don't have access to. Gorillas snore more when they're relaxed. It's a good sign."

"You keep saying that. 'Dresden looked it up.' Like there's a filing cabinet in there and you just walk over and open a drawer."

"It's not that clean," Joel said, honestly. "It's more like — I'll be thinking about something, anything, warding the door, and then I'll just know a thing I never learned. Not remember learning it. Just know it, the way you know your own name." He looked at his hands. "I know forty-three different variants of a basic ward circle. I've never drawn one before Friday. I know what silver nitrate smells like when it's been properly consecrated versus when someone's cutting corners. I have never touched silver nitrate in my life."

Amy was quiet for a moment, and Joel recognized the quiet, because he'd started recognizing it in her over the last three days — the quality of someone about to say something she'd been carrying alone and had finally decided was safe to put down.

"It's not just knowledge, for me," she said. "That's the part nobody's asked about yet, because everyone's very focused on whether I can throw a ward or curse somebody's shoes, which, yes, I can, but that's not the actual — " She stopped. Started again. "I have memories, Joel. Not facts. Memories. A whole life of being a witch that I never lived. Someone's coven. Someone's teacher, an old woman with a bad hip who used to make her climb actual stairs for actual reasons before she'd teach her actual anything. A specific rainy Tuesday in a specific kitchen that smelled like burnt sage, learning to bind a wound closed with nothing but intention and a very kind of patient stubbornness. I remember being afraid of my own power once, a long time ago, in a life I never had, and I remember getting over that fear, slowly, the hard way, over years I didn't live through."

"That's not the same as gaining a skill," Joel said, quiet.

"No," Amy said. "It's not. Jonathan got a soldier's instincts and half his nightmares. I got somebody's entire childhood, entire adulthood, an entire relationship with power that took her a lifetime to build — and I got it all at once, on a Friday, standing in my kitchen in a bad Halloween costume, and none of it ever happened to me." She looked at her own hands, turning them over slowly, the way someone examines hands that suddenly feel like they might not belong to them. "Sometimes I reach for a spell and I don't know if I'm remembering how to do it, or if I'm remembering someone else remembering how to do it. I don't know if there's a difference anymore. I don't know if there's supposed to be."

Joel didn't say anything for a moment. In the corner, Magilla shifted, resettled, exhaled a long low rumbling breath that vibrated faintly through the gym floor.

"I think," Joel said, slowly, "that maybe it doesn't matter where the water came from, once it's actually in the well. You can use it. It's yours to use, regardless of the source, because you're the one standing here deciding what to do with it." He glanced at her. "That's not me being glib. Dresden thought about this a lot, apparently — inherited power, family curses, that whole complicated inheritance thing wizards deal with. The knowledge isn't the danger. What you choose to build with it is."

Amy considered that. "That's annoyingly reasonable."

"I contain several lifetimes of annoyingly reasonable, apparently," Joel said. "It's a whole thing."

A soft snort came from the corner, not from Magilla — the gorilla was still fully and completely asleep — but from further along the bleachers, where a paramedic on her dinner break had been quietly listening to more of this conversation than either of them had noticed.

"Sorry," the paramedic said, not sounding especially sorry. "It's just — you two are having a fascinating philosophical conversation next to a sleeping four-hundred-pound gorilla, and I want you to know I clocked that, and I'm choosing not to ask any follow-up questions, because I've been working this shelter for three days and I've decided that's the healthiest way to get through a shift."

"Wise," Amy said.

"He's very sweet, by the way," the paramedic added, nodding at Magilla. "Ate an entire box of granola bars earlier and then let three different kids braid things into his fur. Never once complained."

"He likes kids," Joel said. "We figured that out day one. He gets agitated if he thinks one of them's upset and can't immediately identify why. It's very specific and very sweet and also occasionally alarming when a four-hundred-pound primate decides he needs to personally investigate why somebody's crying in line for the bathroom."

"Buffy's mom stopped by yesterday," Amy said. "Wanted to see him. He recognized her instantly — got right up, walked over, did the whole gentle-grooming thing he used to do with Buffy. Started checking Joyce's hair for nits like it was the most natural greeting in the world."

"How'd she take that?"

"Cried a little, actually," Amy said, quiet. "Said it was the first time since Friday that something about that night made her laugh instead of just scared her. Then she brought him a fruit basket the next morning, because apparently that's just what Joyce Summers does when something in her life needs feeding."

Joel looked over at the sleeping gorilla, at the sheer domestic absurdity of the whole arrangement — a nine-hundred-year-old vampire aristocracy renovating a manor three miles away, a Terminator meditating in a school library, and, here, in a converted gymnasium, a real silverback gorilla who'd apparently decided his life's calling now involved gentle grooming duties and granola bar appreciation.

"What happens to him," Joel said, "once things settle down. Assuming things settle down."

Amy was quiet for a moment.

"I don't know," she said, honestly. "The Sunnydale Zoo doesn't have anywhere near the setup for a gorilla his size. Sending him to some accredited sanctuary feels — wrong, somehow, given he's not actually feral, he's not actually dangerous, he's basically just an enormous, extremely gentle guy who got picked to carry the weight of somebody's Halloween costume and ended up, I don't know. Real. Whatever real means for something like that." She looked over at him, sleeping peacefully, utterly unbothered by the strangeness of his own continued existence. "I think, for now, he stays with us. With me and Joel. Somebody's got to, and honestly — " she smiled, small and a little surprised at herself, " — I like having him around. He doesn't ask complicated questions about inherited power and borrowed memory. He just wants granola bars and somebody's hair to check for nits."

"Lower bar," Joel agreed. "Healthier, in some ways."

Magilla, as if sensing himself being discussed, shifted, cracked one eye open, surveyed the two of them with the mild, unbothered curiosity of a creature entirely at peace with a life he never could have predicted, and then closed his eye again and went back to snoring.

"Yeah," Amy said, watching him settle. "That's the whole philosophy, right there."
