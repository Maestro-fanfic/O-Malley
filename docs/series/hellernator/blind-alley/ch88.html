Threshold Analysis

"The most dangerous enemy is the one who's stopped being sure which side it's actually on."
— Murphy's Laws of Combat

Monday, November 3, 1997: Sunnydale High School Library,
Sunnydale, Night 11:30pm —

The library was quiet in the way the TX had come to associate with the hours between eleven and four, a quiet it had catalogued across seventy-two consecutive nights now, cross-referenced against ambient temperature, foot traffic in the adjoining hallway, and the creak pattern of a building settling as it cooled.

It sat in the position it had determined, on its third night, offered the optimal sightline — library entrance and main hallway doors simultaneously visible, a configuration Harmony Kendall's stored social architecture had never once required, because Harmony Kendall had never needed to watch two exits at once. Harmony Kendall had needed to watch which table Cordelia Chase was sitting at, and whether her hair looked acceptable in the cafeteria's specific lighting, and whether anyone had noticed her new shoes.

The TX had access to all of that. Every calibration. Every social variable Harmony had ever weighed, filed, acted upon. It could produce Harmony's laugh with ninety-one percent fidelity, Harmony's specific dismissive hand gesture with ninety-six, Harmony's particular vocal inflection when delivering an insult with eighty-eight, the gap accounted for by processing lag that Harmony herself had never experienced, because Harmony had never needed to process before executing. Harmony's cruelty had been reflexive. The TX's approximation of it required calculation, every time, a beat of latency that no amount of refined modeling had yet eliminated.

It had run threat assessment on this discrepancy four hundred and twelve times since the containment ritual completed. It had not yet resolved a satisfactory explanation for why the discrepancy troubled it, given that troubled was not, technically, a state its base architecture should have been capable of experiencing.

Outside, in the hallway, footsteps approached — Giles, based on gait pattern and the tempo of a man who had walked this corridor at this hour enough times now that the TX had a full biomechanical profile logged. He stopped at the library's threshold, same distance he always stopped at, six feet four inches from the doorframe, the exact edge of a boundary neither of them had ever formally negotiated but both, apparently, respected.

"Good evening," Giles said.

The TX considered several possible responses, weighted by the social calculus Harmony's architecture provided, and selected the option that produced the lowest variance from expected behavior. "Good evening, Mr. Giles."

"May I ask you something," Giles said, "that isn't part of tonight's assessment protocol?"

The TX ran this request against its threat model. Found no vector for harm in it. "You may."

Giles was quiet for a moment, the quiet of a man choosing his words with unusual care. "Do you know why you keep watching the exits? Both of them, simultaneously, every night, for seventy-two nights running?"

The TX processed the question through several available frameworks — tactical necessity, containment escape planning, residual Harmony-derived social vigilance — and found that none of them, run individually, produced a complete answer. It ran them in combination. Found a partial fit. Found the fit itself troubling, in the unresolved sense it had been unable to name across four hundred and twelve prior assessments.

"I do not have complete data on that question," it said, finally, which was, for the TX, an unusually direct admission of an information gap.

"Would it help," Giles said, carefully, "if I offered a hypothesis?"

"Proceed."

"Jonathan Levinson believes," Giles said, "that you may be doing something more human than tactical planning. He believes you may simply be a thing that ran a mission — the extraction, Harmony's takeover, the completion of your directive — and the mission ended, and you have no template at all for what comes after a mission ends. So you keep doing the only thing you still know how to do. You keep watching for a threat that isn't coming, because standing down isn't a state your architecture was ever actually built to occupy."

The TX ran this hypothesis against its own internal state with a thoroughness it rarely applied to input from an external source, because the hypothesis, unlike most external input, produced an immediate and significant reduction in unresolved variance across multiple prior assessments simultaneously.

"That is," it said, after a pause longer than any it had previously produced in conversation with Giles, "a more complete explanatory model than any I had independently generated."

Giles's expression did something the TX's Harmony-derived social architecture read as cautious hope, cross-referenced against seventy-two nights of accumulated data on what Giles's face did under various conditions. "Does that change anything? Now that you have the model?"

The TX considered this for four hundred and thirteenth time, in a sense, though the framing was new.

"I do not require an active mission to justify continued function," it said, slowly, working through the logic as it stated it, a process that felt, in whatever the TX's version of feeling was, unusually exposed. "This is a new determination. I had previously operated under an unexamined assumption that continued function required active justification through ongoing threat assessment. I am uncertain how long I have held that assumption without examining it."

"Since the mission ended, presumably," Giles said, gently.

"Presumably," the TX agreed.

They stood in the quiet that followed, six feet four inches of respected boundary between them, and the TX ran one more assessment — quieter this time, less urgent, closer to something it did not yet have adequate vocabulary to name.

"Mr. Giles," it said, finally. "If I no longer require an active threat to justify my continued operation, what would you suggest I do instead, during the hours I have previously allocated to threshold surveillance?"

Giles was quiet for a moment, and something in his expression shifted, gentle and moved. "Perhaps," he said, "you might simply try existing for a while, without a directive attached to it. See what that produces. It's worked reasonably well for the rest of us, on the whole."

The TX processed this suggestion for a long moment, running it against every available framework, and found, for the first time in seventy-two nights, that it did not immediately return to threshold analysis afterward.

It remained in its position — the optimal sightline, library and hallway both visible — not because the mission required it, but because it had not yet decided what else, precisely, to do with the quiet of eleven-thirty at night, and found, to its own mild and carefully logged surprise, that the not-deciding didn't trouble it nearly as much as it might once have expected.

"Thank you, Mr. Giles," it said.

"You're welcome," Giles said, and after a moment, turned and walked back down the corridor, leaving the TX alone with the library's quiet and, for the first time in seventy-two nights, no active threat assessment running underneath it at all.
