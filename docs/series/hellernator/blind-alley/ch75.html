A Century and Change of Deciding Otherwise

"A century is a long apprenticeship in patience, and patience is the only weapon that never runs out of ammunition." — attributed to a Watcher's Council training manual

Monday, November 3, 1997: Chestnut Street Office, Sunnydale, Evening 8:30pm —

The office on Chestnut Street had been, until three days ago, an empty storefront that had cycled through a nail salon, a failed video rental place, and eight months of nothing at all, and it now held four desks, a whiteboard nobody had gotten around to labeling properly yet, and Sheila Martini, who was standing at the window with her arms crossed, watching the street outside with the stillness of someone who had spent a very long time learning to be unnoticed and had never quite unlearned it.

"You don't have to stand guard," Giles said, from the desk where he was going through a box of files Zabuto had brought over. "Joel warded the whole building this afternoon. Threefold, he said, whatever that means precisely."

"Old habits." Sheila didn't turn from the window. Her voice had the faint, careful accent of somewhere that had stopped existing as a place two centuries ago, worn smooth at the edges by a hundred years of living in places that weren't home. "Hundred and thirty years of watching doors doesn't just switch off 'cause some kid drew a rune on a threshold. Trust me, I've tried turning it off. Doesn't work. Believe me, I'd love to just sit down and relax like a normal person for five fucking minutes."

Giles set the file down. Looked at her properly for the first time all evening, past the ordinary shorthand they'd fallen into over these three days.

"May I ask you something," he said, "that isn't operational."

Sheila's shoulders did something small and guarded. "You may ask. No promises I'll answer without being an ass about it first."

"What was it like," Giles said, carefully, "being in Spike's court, before. Not the details, if you'd rather not — just. What made you the one who chose differently, when the others didn't."

Sheila was quiet for a long moment, still watching the street, though Giles suspected she wasn't seeing it anymore.

"Turned in 1867," she said, finally. "By something a hell of a lot less charming than Spike, in a century that gave a woman exactly two options if she wanted anything resembling power, and both of 'em involved a man deciding to hand some to her." A pause. "Spent the first forty years exactly the way you'd expect. Not gonna dress that up for either of our comfort."

"I wasn't asking you to."

"I know." She glanced at him, briefly, something almost like gratitude in it. "Spike's court took me in around 1930. Liked collecting things with a bit of history, a bit of style. I fit the bill." Her mouth did something that wasn't quite a smile. "Got real good at being whatever a room needed. It's a survival skill. Stops bein' a choice somewhere around year sixty and just becomes the whole act, top to bottom."

"And Halloween."

"Halloween was the first night in a hundred and thirty years somethin' handed me the chance to be somebody else entirely, and turns out — surprise, apparently — the actual me underneath all that performing had opinions the whole damn time. Just never once got permission to open her mouth about 'em." She finally turned from the window, something raw in her face Giles hadn't seen there before. "Chose different because for one night, different was actually on the table, and I found out real fast I couldn't go back to pretending it wasn't, after."

Giles was quiet for a moment, absorbing that.

"That took considerable courage," he said. "Whatever the spell's mechanics were. The choice underneath it was still yours."

"Was it, though." Real uncertainty in it now, the kind that had clearly been sitting under the surface for three days with nowhere safe to land. "Or did the spell just hand me permission I'd never have granted myself in a hundred and thirty years, and I've just been runnin' on borrowed permission ever since, waitin' for somebody to notice I'm still exactly the same sorry piece of work underneath it."

"I don't believe that," Giles said, simply.

"You don't know me well enough yet to believe or disbelieve shit about me, Mr. Giles."

"Perhaps not." Giles looked at her steadily. "But I've spent a great many years around Watchers who believed a person's nature got fixed the moment they were turned, and I've found that belief wrong nearly every single time. What you do with a hundred and thirty years counts for a great deal more than what happened in the first one. You've spent three days doing logistics, warding schedules, keeping this office running while everyone else gets credit for the flashier parts of what we're building. That's not the behavior of somebody waiting around to revert."

Sheila looked at him for a long moment, something working behind her eyes she clearly hadn't expected to be examining tonight, over a box of files.

"You're awful direct, for a librarian," she said, finally, warmth creeping back into it, careful but genuine.

"I've been told that's a recent development," Giles said, something in his own expression suggesting he knew exactly which recent events had caused it.

The bell over the door chimed. Joel Garrity came in, staff in hand, looking pleasantly surprised none of an entire afternoon's worth of Latin had gone wrong.

"Wards are holding," he reported, to no one in particular, then, catching the quality of the room's silence, glanced between Giles and Sheila. "Am I interrupting something."

"Nah," Sheila said, and this time the almost-smile made it all the way to something real. "Not interrupting a damn thing. Good work on the wards, kid."

Joel looked, briefly, unconvinced, decided not to pursue it, and set his staff by the door with the care of a man who treated his tools like they mattered, because in his case, increasingly, they did.

Giles went back to the file box. Sheila returned to the window, though something in her posture had shifted, slightly, the guard down a fraction it hadn't been down before.

Outside, Sunnydale went on being ordinary, oblivious, and the office on Chestnut Street held its quiet work a little longer into the November evening.
