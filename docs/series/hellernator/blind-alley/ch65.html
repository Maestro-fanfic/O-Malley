The Never-After Doesn't Keep Time the Way You Think It Does

"Time is what keeps everything from happening at once." — attributed to Ray Cummings

Somewhere in the Never-After, aboard the Windover, No time that translates cleanly —

The Windover didn't creak the way a ship built of ordinary wood was supposed to creak. It groaned lower, longer, in a register that lived somewhere under the water instead of above it, and Elise Nakamura had stopped flinching at the sound around day four, or what she'd decided to call day four, because there wasn't a sun in the Never-After to measure days by, only the ship's bell and the watches and the quality of exhaustion that told your body it was time to sleep whether or not anything outside your body agreed.

She was on deck-swab duty, which was the good duty, the duty you wanted, because deck-swab duty happened in the open air instead of belowdecks, and belowdecks was where the things that weren't duty happened.

Captain Ezekiel Hook — Chad Everette, before, though nobody on this ship used the before-name, including, increasingly, Chad Everette himself — walked the quarterdeck in the unhurried way he walked everything, a man who had all the time in a place that didn't measure time and had made his peace with that in a manner Elise found more frightening than any amount of shouting would have been.

He stopped near her. Didn't say anything at first. That was its own kind of pressure — the not-saying, the waiting to see what you'd fill the silence with, because filling it wrong cost you.

Elise kept swabbing.

"You're quick," Hook said, eventually. "Quicker than the Petrova girl was, this time last week."

Elise didn't ask what had happened to the Petrova girl. She'd learned that too, around day four. Some questions bought you information you couldn't afford to have.

"Admiral Landlorn's fleet took two prizes last night," Hook said, conversational, the way another man might discuss weather. "A trawler out of somewhere that doesn't exist anymore and something stranger that I'm told used to be a Spanish galleon before it wasn't anything at all for about three hundred years. Crew's being sorted." A pause, precisely timed. "Some of the sorted crew will come aboard here."

Elise kept her eyes on the deck. On the grain of wood under the brush.

"You'd know some of them, perhaps," Hook said. "This being where Sunnydale's overflow tends to wash up, one way or another. Funny thing about a town on top of a Hellmouth. Things fall through the cracks here that wouldn't fall through cracks anywhere else."

"I don't know anyone who's missing," Elise said. It came out steadier than she felt, which she counted as a small private victory.

"No," Hook agreed. "You wouldn't, would you. You've been keeping your head down very admirably." He crouched, unexpectedly, to her level, the hook that had replaced his hand catching the ship's lantern light in a way that made the shadow of it stretch long and wrong across the deck. "That's a skill. Keeping your head down. I respect it in a crew member. It's the ones who don't learn it who end up interesting to Lady Joy, and interesting to Lady Joy is not a condition I'd wish on you."

He stood. Walked on. Left the sentence sitting there the way he left most sentences — unfinished on purpose, so the finishing happened in your own head, where it did more work than anything he could have said outright.

Elise finished the section of deck she was on. Moved to the next. Did not look toward the companionway that led belowdecks, where Lady Joy kept the private quarters that everyone on the Windover knew about and nobody discussed above a whisper, because Lady Joy's interests ran to specific things that Elise had decided, around day two, she was never, under any circumstance, going to think about directly if she could help it. Thinking about it directly made it real in a way that surviving it required her not to let it be, at least not yet, not while there was still a version of this where she got out.

Because there was going to be a version where she got out.

She held onto that the way you held onto anything on a ship that groaned like something drowning — carefully, with both hands, not looking down.

Somewhere else in the Never-After, the flagship Retribution, Same non-time —

Admiral Landlorn did not visit the lower decks often. It wasn't beneath him, precisely — he'd been beneath worse things in worse centuries — but the lower decks of his own fleet held a specific smell of fear-sweat and salt and bilge that he found, at this stage of a very long unlife, simply tiresome rather than satisfying.

Tonight he made an exception, because tonight's haul included something his lieutenant had described, with the carefulness of a man reporting something he didn't fully understand, as not from anywhere we've charted.

The something was currently chained in the forward hold, and it did not look afraid, which was itself the interesting part. Landlorn had seen a great many prisoners over a great many centuries and fear was, invariably, the baseline. This one — young, human-shaped, dressed in clothes that belonged to no era Landlorn immediately recognized — was watching him with the calculating stillness of somebody doing math, the way a navigator does math, the way somebody plans a thing three moves ahead while their face stays perfectly, deliberately blank.

"You're not from the trawler," Landlorn said.

"No," the prisoner agreed. Male voice. Young. Unbothered in a way that was, Landlorn noted, either very brave or very stupid, and he'd learned the difference between those two things mattered less than people assumed. "I fell through something. About four days ago, your time, I think, though your time and my time don't seem to be the same thing at all."

"A door."

"A door," the prisoner confirmed. "One of yours. It opened somewhere it shouldn't have, and I was standing in the wrong place at the wrong moment, and here I am." He looked around the hold with the interest of somebody cataloguing an environment rather than fearing it. "This is Never-After. I've heard of it. Didn't believe in it, exactly, until about four days ago."

"You know what this place is and you're not afraid of it."

"I'm plenty afraid," the prisoner said. "I'm just also from a town where being afraid and doing the math anyway is basically the local hobby." A beat. "Sunnydale. You've probably heard of it, if doors keep opening near it."

Something in Landlorn's long-still chest did the thing that passed, in him, for genuine interest — a rare enough sensation after several centuries that he noted it specifically, filed it, decided it mattered.

"Sunnydale," he repeated.

"You've heard of it," the prisoner said. Not a question. Something that might, in a face less carefully arranged, have been the beginning of a plan settling into place.

"I have heard," Landlorn said slowly, "that there is presently a great deal of interesting activity in Sunnydale. More than usual, even for a Hellmouth." He crouched, the way Hook had crouched on the Windover in another part of the Never-After that did not know it shared this exact posture at this exact non-moment. "What's your name, boy."

"Marcus," the prisoner said. "Marcus Okafor." A pause, precisely as timed as anything Hook had ever managed. "And before you decide what I'm worth to you, Admiral — I should tell you I know things about that town's current situation that I don't think you've heard yet. And I'm the kind of person who trades information for things I want."

Landlorn looked at him for a long moment.

Then, for the first time in longer than he cared to calculate, he laughed — a low, delighted sound that hadn't come easily to him in centuries.

"Chain him somewhere with a chair," Landlorn told the guard. "And bring him something to eat. I find I'm curious."
