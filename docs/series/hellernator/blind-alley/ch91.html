Four-Fifty-Five

"The hour before dawn asks more of a person than any hour that follows it." — attributed to a night-watch tradition, origin disputed

Tuesday, November 4, 1997: Sunnydale MHMR, Cell Block B,
Sunnydale, Early Morning 4:47am —

Creed had been out of his own cell for eleven minutes before anyone in the building had a reason to notice, which was exactly as long as he'd planned for, because eleven minutes was how long the overnight guard's coffee break ran, clocked and confirmed across three separate nights of listening, and Victor Creed did not build plans around guesses when actual data was available for the taking.

He'd gone up through the ceiling panel at 4:36, same route he'd used every night to talk through the ductwork, except tonight he kept moving past cell three instead of stopping there, because tonight's first stop wasn't Barkley. Tonight's first stop was the weapons locker in the secondary security station, because a plan that ended in getting three unarmed people out of a federal facility was a plan built on hope, and Creed had exactly no professional respect for hope as a tactical resource.

The secondary station had one guard posted overnight — a man named Delgado, according to the badge Creed had read through a gap in the door three nights running, mid-thirties, competent enough by ordinary civilian security standards and unprepared for what actually shared this building with him.

Creed dropped down from the ceiling access directly behind him.

Delgado got exactly as far as turning his head before Creed had a hand across his throat and the man's own sidearm out of its holster, the whole exchange lasting under four seconds, controlled and precise and without the theatrical cruelty Creed could have brought to it and chose, deliberately, not to. Delgado was unconscious and zip-tied to his own chair inside another ten seconds, breathing, alive, out of the equation for at least the next several hours, which was all Creed actually needed from him.

He took the sidearm, a second weapon from the locker, and two spare magazines, working the combination Barkley had walked him through blind, over three nights, purely from memory of facilities she'd worked in that used the same manufacturer's standard lock pattern.

Four-fifty-five. Right on schedule.

He was back at cell three's ceiling panel in under four minutes, dropping through, and found Barkley already standing, already braced against the wall favoring her left side, ready in the way of a woman who'd spent four days assuming tonight might be the night and had refused to let herself fully relax on any of the others.

"Got you somethin'," Creed said, low, handing her the second sidearm.

Barkley checked it in the dark by feel alone, chambered a round with the economical motion of someone who'd done this ten thousand times before whatever had put her in this building, and nodded once. "Good stock. You didn't have to clear the whole locker for this."

"Wasn't gonna leave a professional soldier unarmed on my watch." Something almost prim about the phrasing, entirely undercut by the low growl underneath it. "Seemed like poor manners."

"You have manners now."

"Got priorities." Something like a low, satisfied rumble. "Manners is just what priorities look like from a distance."

Getting her through the crawlspace was the part he'd actually worried about, four nights running, because the duct system wasn't built for two people moving at once, and Barkley's left side wasn't taking weight the way the rest of her clearly wanted it to. She didn't complain. She moved the way soldiers moved through terrain that hurt them — economical, controlled, converting pain into a scheduling problem rather than an obstacle, exactly the way Creed had read her from the very first conversation through the vent.

He half-carried her the last stretch above corridor B, one arm doing the work her leg currently couldn't, and set her down gently at the junction above cell seven with the care of a man who'd decided, four days ago, that this particular cargo mattered more than his own reputation for not caring about anything.

Cordelia was already awake below them — he could hear it in the quality of her stillness through the grille, the stillness of someone listening rather than sleeping — and Creed didn't have a clean way to talk to her the way he'd talked to Barkley all week, because sound carried differently this close to the monitored section of the block, close enough to the corridor camera's audio pickup that a conversation risked exactly the kind of attention none of them needed in the next six minutes.

He eased the grille aside just far enough to catch the sliver of light from her cell, and found her already looking up at the gap, waiting.

She mouthed the words instead of speaking them, slow and deliberate, shaping each one fully so he could read it off her lips in the thin light. Guard's gone?

Creed answered the only way he had available — one slow, deliberate shift of the grille, tilted a fraction to the left. Yes.

Barkley? she mouthed.

He shifted the grille again, same slow tilt. Yes.

She held his eyes for a second, reading the rest of it off his face rather than needing more from the vent, and mouthed the next thing carefully enough that there was no mistaking it. Walsh. First.

Creed didn't move the grille at all this time. He just held it steady, dead still, which had become, over four days of this exact silent conversation, its own kind of answer — the stillness that meant already decided, no argument needed.

Cordelia's mouth curved, just slightly, in the dark.

Then let's go, she mouthed, and reached up to work her own loosened panel free.

She came up through the gap under her own power, steady on her bare feet despite four days of no real food and no real sleep, and the three of them stood together in the cramped crawlspace junction for one silent moment — four days of separate, patient, individually built plans finally converging into a single shared direction.

Creed handed her nothing, because she hadn't asked for a weapon, and something in the set of her jaw told him she had her own plan for Walsh that didn't especially require one.

They dropped back down into the corridor together, quiet, and moved.

The east stairwell door had the telltale creak Cordelia had catalogued days ago — the one that meant actual foot traffic rather than the building settling — and tonight, at four-fifty-nine in the morning, it stayed silent, because nobody was using these stairs except the three of them.

They went up, not down, past the ground floor and the loading dock and the clean, quiet exit that Pooka had confirmed twice over the last hour, because clean and quiet wasn't where any of them were actually headed first.

Walsh's private office was on the second floor, east wing, and the two staff Cordelia had catalogued as regularly posted near it at this hour — a night nurse doing rounds and one contract security guard who preferred the break room's coffee to actual patrolling — were both, per four days of careful observation, reliably absent from this specific corridor between four-fifty and five-twenty.

The door was locked. Barkley had it open in under fifteen seconds, favoring her left hand, using a pick set Creed didn't ask where she'd acquired, because some questions between professionals didn't need asking.

Walsh was at her desk.

She'd been at her desk, it was clear, for most of the night — security readouts still open on the monitor, a legal pad covered in her own tight, decisive handwriting, a cup of coffee gone cold beside it — and she looked up at the three of them coming through her door at five-oh-two in the morning without so much as a change in her breathing, the same unhurried, cataloguing attention she'd have given any other unscheduled variable.

"Well," Walsh said. "That's rather earlier than I planned for."

"We noticed you'd adjusted your own timeline too," Cordelia said, stepping fully into the room, Creed and Barkley flanking her without needing to be told where to stand. "New brackets. Extra loading dock coverage. You've been busy tonight, Doctor."

"I run a tight program." There was, extraordinarily, still something like professional pride in it, even now, even with two armed intruders and an escaped subject standing in her office at five in the morning. "You should be flattered, Miss Chase. Very few subjects have ever made me revise a security protocol mid-containment."

"I'm not here for flattery," Cordelia said. "I'm here for the backup archive. The access code. And a very specific accounting of exactly how many other people you've done this to, because I don't think Rona Williams is the only name in that file, and I intend to know every single one of them before this morning is over."

"You don't understand what I've actually built," Walsh said, slowly, without heat, as though correcting a promising but mistaken student. "The things that come up out of that Hellmouth won't wait for your formation to finish getting its footing. Someone has to be ready before the politeness runs out. I built the cage sturdy enough to matter, and every single person currently horrified by my methods is going to be extremely grateful for them the first time—"

"Save it," Cordelia said, flat and cold in a way that four days in a paper gown had built in her, brick by brick, patient hour by patient hour. "I've had four days to think about every justification you were going to give me, and none of them change what's actually in that archive."

Creed shifted his weight, almost imperceptibly, the readiness of something that had already decided, several nights ago through a ventilation duct, exactly how this conversation was likely to end — and exactly whose decision it actually was to make.

Walsh looked at him. Studied the flat, patient violence sitting behind his eyes with the same detached interest she'd have given any other specimen, and filed it, visibly, as data rather than threat.

"You brought the mutation," she observed, mild, cataloguing rather than afraid. "Sabretooth. I'd wondered whether the designation stuck, or whether it had reverted to something more ordinary once the initial adrenaline wore off. Apparently it stuck."

"Brought a professional." Something low and rumbling moved under Creed's voice. "Careful what you call things, Doctor. Words like that cost people, sometimes."

Barkley moved to the door, checking the corridor, professional and unhurried. "Clock's still running," she said, quiet, to Cordelia. "We've got maybe six minutes before the next patrol sweep."

"The access code," Cordelia said. "Now."

Walsh looked at the three of them — at a girl who'd out-planned her for four straight days, at a soldier who'd disarmed her own facility from the inside, at something with Creed's flat patience standing between her and the door — and made, with the same clinical efficiency she brought to every decision in her career, the calculation that survival tonight required cooperation rather than defiance.

She gave them the code.

Creed looked at Cordelia, waiting, the patient stillness of something that had already decided this wasn't its decision to make for her.

Cordelia looked at Walsh — at the woman who'd stripped her down to a paper gown and called four days of careful, patient survival a flattering compliment to her own methods — and felt the last of whatever hesitation might once have lived in that decision simply fail to arrive at all.

"You said it yourself," Cordelia said, quiet. "Someone has to be ready before the politeness runs out. I spent four days getting ready, Doctor."

"I see that you did." Walsh said it plainly, without fear, without any visible shift at all from the same composed, assessing register she'd used since the moment they walked in — the final courtesy of a woman who had, evidently, already run this scenario in her own head at some earlier point and found herself, at the actual end of it, more curious than afraid. "For what it's worth, Miss Chase — and I recognize you may not want it — you have been, without exception, the single most instructive four days of my career. I would have liked to see what you became, given time."

"You don't get more time," Cordelia said. "That's the actual point."

"Yes," Walsh agreed, simply. "I imagine it is."

Cordelia's hand found the spring-steel shim in her waistband, the same worked bracket she'd spent four days freeing bolt by patient bolt, and crossed the last of the distance between them without hurrying, because there was nothing left in the room worth hurrying for. Walsh did not flinch, did not raise a hand, did not so much as widen her eyes — simply watched, to the very last available second, with the same detached, cataloguing attention she'd given every hour of the preceding four days, as though even this were, to her, primarily an opportunity to observe something she hadn't previously had data on.

It took four seconds. Cordelia drove the shim in once, low and hard, beneath the sternum and up, the way four days of careful, silent rehearsal against a folded blanket had taught her hands to do it without needing her mind's permission in the moment itself, and Walsh's breath left her in a single long exhale that was not quite a word, and her hands, which had never once moved to defend herself, simply opened over the desk and went still.

There was no triumph in it. Cordelia felt none arrive, then or after, no righteous surge, nothing that resembled the ending a different kind of story might have handed her. It was simply a necessary, unpleasant task, finished efficiently because efficiency was the only mercy either of them had left available, done the way you'd clear a nest of something dangerous out of a house you had to keep living in — carefully, completely, without ceremony, and without pretending afterward that it had been anything other than exactly that.

Creed didn't move. Didn't need to. He simply watched, the way he'd told Barkley he watched everything — filing it, respecting it, offering nothing except the fact that nobody in that room was going to stop her.

Barkley didn't look away either, because she'd decided, somewhere in the last four days, that whatever this girl had earned the right to do, she'd earned the right to have it witnessed rather than done quietly in a corner where it wouldn't count.

Cordelia stood over the desk a long moment, breathing, feeling nothing she could honestly call victory, the cold coffee cup still sitting undisturbed beside the legal pad, which had, apparently, mattered to Maggie Walsh right up until the final second exactly as much as the person standing in front of her had.

"Clock's still running," Barkley said, after a moment, low, steady, the professional calm of a soldier moving everyone's attention forward before it could snag on what had just happened. "Six minutes, maybe less now."

Cordelia looked down at the woman who would not be filing any more writs, running any more programs, building any more cages out of other people's stolen lives.

"Get the archive drive," she said, quiet, and her voice held steady. "We're taking everything."

They were out through the loading dock at five-eleven, the blind spot holding exactly as promised, three shapes moving fast and quiet across the dark lot toward the tree line where Merrill's team was waiting with a vehicle and more relief on their faces than any of them had expected to feel this morning.

Cordelia didn't look back at the building. She'd spent four days memorizing every inch of it from the inside, and she had no further use for the view from outside, and no need to see it again in any capacity at all.

"Oh-six-hundred," Creed said, checking the sky, still dark, still nearly an hour off from the deadline they'd all been building toward. "We're early."

"We had good information," Barkley said, favoring her side, already moving toward the waiting vehicle with the relief of a woman who'd just kept a promise to herself about getting home to someone.

Cordelia stopped at the tree line for one moment, breathing the actual outside air for the first time in four days, cold and real and nothing at all like the recycled hospital air she'd been living in, and thought, with the fierce clarity of someone who'd just walked out of the worst four days of her life under her own power, about a boy in a hospital bed running hardware checks he didn't know he was running, and a corkboard on a dining room wall she'd never seen but was, finally, about to.

"Let's go home," she said, and the three of them moved together into the tree line, four days of separate patient work finally, completely, done.
