An Offer, Properly Delivered

"An offer, properly made, costs the offerer more than the one receiving it ever fully knows." — attributed to Peter Pan

Tuesday, November 4, 1997: 1630 Revello Drive, Sunnydale,
Early Morning 6:45am —

Joyce Summers had a very firm policy about who was allowed to knock on her door before seven in the morning, and that policy did not, under any previously established circumstance, include a boy in green who appeared to be somewhere between fourteen and several centuries old, standing on her porch with a girl beside him and a small, extremely serious expression that suggested he'd rehearsed this greeting more than once.

"Mrs. Summers," Peter said. "I'm sorry for the hour. I wanted to come before the day got started, when things might be — busier."

Joyce looked at him. At the girl beside him, Wendy, who had the composed patience of someone who'd talked her friend out of at least two worse opening lines on the walk over. At the general, undeniable strangeness of two Lost Boys standing politely on her porch at quarter to seven in the morning, one of them holding what appeared to be a slightly bruised gift basket of wild blackberries.

"You're the boy from the amusement park," Joyce said. Not unkindly. Simply establishing facts, the way she'd learned to do with almost everything since October 31st.

"Peter Pan," Peter said. "Formerly Danny Osbourne. Currently — this, I suppose." He held out the blackberries, slightly crushed from the walk. "I brought these. Wendy said arriving somewhere important without bringing anything was poor manners, and she's usually right about that sort of thing."

"He wanted to bring a whole pie," Wendy said, dryly. "I explained that pies don't survive a two-mile walk through brush very well."

Joyce looked at the blackberries. At the two teenagers holding them out with the earnest formality of people who'd clearly practiced this exact moment and were now discovering that practicing a thing and doing it were different exercises entirely.

"Well," she said, "you'd better come in, then. I was just about to start coffee."

Buffy came downstairs twelve minutes later, hair still damp, and stopped dead in the kitchen doorway at the sight of Peter Pan sitting at her mother's kitchen table, drinking orange juice out of a glass with a cartoon sun on it, while Joyce cut up the blackberries into a bowl with the unbothered efficiency of a woman who had stopped being surprised by anything that walked through her front door.

"You're up early," Buffy said, to Peter, because it was the only sentence available that didn't require immediately unpacking the fact that this was happening at all.

"I wanted to catch you before the day started," Peter said. "Before things got — busy. I understand your Board has a lot happening right now."

Buffy's eyebrows went up slightly at "the Board," which had apparently made it out of last night's conference room and into general circulation faster than she'd expected. "You've been talking to Pooka."

"She found me first, actually," Peter said. "Very direct. Asked if we had food before she asked anything else, which I found reassuring — anyone whose first priority is snacks generally turns out to be trustworthy, in my experience." He set the orange juice down. "I want to offer something. Properly, this time. Not the way I offered it after your people beat us at the park — that offer had a lot of wounded pride mixed into it, and I don't think it landed the way I meant it to."

"What's the offer," Buffy said, sitting down across from him, Wendy settling beside Peter with the watchful patience of someone ready to step in if the negotiation needed steadying.

"We know this town's underside better than anyone your Board currently has access to," Peter said. "Four days, and we've already mapped things you haven't found yet — places where the things that come up out of the dark actually go, tunnels under the old part of town nobody's used in decades, a whole network of spaces too small for adults to move through comfortably but perfectly sized for us." He paused. "We're fast. We're small. Half my crew can go places your soldiers can't fit. I'm not offering fealty — I did enough of that as somebody's idea of a story, I'm not doing it again — but I'm offering cooperation. Real cooperation, on our own terms, in exchange for something."

"In exchange for what."

"Legitimacy," Peter said, and something in his voice went quieter, more honest than the earlier rehearsed formality had allowed. "We're forty runaway kids living in a dead amusement park. I don't want fealty and I don't want charity. I want us to stop being a rumor the adults in this town are frightened of, and start being something that actually matters. A crew people can call on, instead of a story people tell each other to explain why the carnival's still fenced off."

Buffy looked at him for a long moment — at a boy who'd been handed exactly the shape of who he'd always secretly wanted to be, and was now discovering, the same as everyone else in this town since Halloween, that wanting a thing and living inside it forever were different weights entirely.

"There's a meeting every Monday," she said. "A whole board, actually, on my dining room wall, with everybody's business on it — patrol logs, an action column, all of it." She glanced at Joyce, who was still cutting blackberries with the serene patience of a woman who fed anyone who walked through her door regardless of species or century. "I think you should come to the next one. Not to ask permission. To actually be part of building it."

Peter was quiet for a moment, something moving behind his eyes that looked, for the first time since he'd walked through the door, less like careful diplomatic strategy and more like genuine hope.

"I'd like that," he said.

Wendy, beside him, allowed herself the smallest smile, the quiet satisfaction of someone who'd talked her friend into doing the brave, uncertain thing and watched it land better than either of them had dared plan for.

Joyce set the bowl of blackberries down in the middle of the table.

"Well," she said, "in the meantime, you're both welcome to breakfast. I made too much anyway. I always make too much lately." She looked at Peter with the warm, unbothered practicality that had, over the last four days, become the single most reliable constant in this entire strange new world. "You look like you could use a real meal, dear. Being fourteen and also several centuries old is probably exhausting on the appetite."

"You have no idea," Peter said, and reached for the toast.
