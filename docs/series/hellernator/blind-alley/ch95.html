Home By Morning

"The mission's not actually over when the objective's secured. It's over when the people who were waiting stop counting."
— Murphy's Laws of Combat

Tuesday, November 4, 1997: Rendezvous Point, Tree Line off Topanga Street,
Sunnydale, Early Morning 5:19am —

Merrill had been staring at the tree line for eleven straight minutes without blinking more than the job strictly required, and Moseby, beside him in the parked van, had noticed, and had decided, with her usual pale-eyed patience, not to comment on it, because some things a person needed to do without being teased about doing them.

"Movement," Merrill said, quiet, the second he had it.

Three shapes came out of the dark tree line at a fast, controlled pace — not running, not quite, but moving with the economical urgency of people who'd spent four days learning exactly how much haste a situation actually required and no more.

Cordelia Chase came through first, barefoot on the cold ground, wrapped in improvised layers that had clearly been assembled from whatever four days of patient hoarding could produce, and Merrill was out of the van and moving toward her before he'd fully registered making the decision.

"Miss Chase," he said, and found, to his own mild surprise, that his voice wasn't steady. "Welcome back."

"Thank you," Cordelia said, and there was something in her face — steady, changed, older than four days should have made anyone, but steady — that told Merrill this conversation was going to need considerably more care than a simple welcome, later, when there was time for it.

Creed came through next, unhurried, unbothered by the cold or the hour or anything else, and Barkley behind him, favoring her left side, and the moment Barkley cleared the tree line Moseby was out of the van at a dead run, all pale-eyed professional patience gone in an instant.

"Keisha," Moseby said, and there wasn't anything careful or controlled left in her voice at all.

"Jan." Barkley's own voice cracked, just slightly, four days of controlled soldier's composure finally, briefly, allowed to slip. "Told you I'd—"

She didn't finish the sentence, because Moseby had already closed the distance and had both arms around her, careful of the injured side, and for a moment neither of them said anything else at all, just held on, four days of separation collapsing into a single held breath.

Merrill looked away, giving them the moment, and found Cordelia watching the same scene with an expression that held something complicated — genuine warmth for two people whose reunion she'd apparently helped make possible, tangled up with something else, something Merrill recognized from his own combat tours as the weight of a person who'd just done something that morning she hadn't fully processed yet. He didn't say anything about it directly. Twenty years in this line of work had taught him that the gentlest thing a man built like him could do was not draw attention to a moment by commenting on it.

"C'mon, kid," he said instead, gruff, understated, the words landing with more warmth than the delivery let on. "Doc's got a room ready. And there's a boy been on the phone with the Captain most of the night who isn't gonna believe you're actually standing here till he sees it with his own two eyes." He fell into step beside her, matching his stride to hers without seeming to notice he was doing it, moving with the quiet economy of a very large man who'd spent a career learning not to announce himself.

Something in Cordelia's face shifted at that — the complicated weight easing, just slightly, replaced by something that looked, for the first time all morning, uncomplicatedly like relief.

"Yeah," she said. "Let's go."

Tuesday, November 4, 1997: South Marion Drive, Sunnydale Medical Complex, Room 412,
Sunnydale, Early Morning 5:35am —

Xander had not slept, despite Aura's repeated and increasingly creative attempts to convince him otherwise, and he was staring at the water stain with the fixed intensity of a man doing math on a clock that had, twenty-five minutes ago, dropped below thirty minutes remaining and had started, in his head, to feel like the longest thirty minutes of his entire life.

"You should—"

"Don't say sleep, Aura."

"I was going to say breathe," Aura said, mildly, though the quality of her own stillness suggested she was doing exactly as much counting as he was. "You've been holding it for the last four minutes."

The phone on the side table rang.

Xander had it against his ear before the second ring finished.

"Harris."

"She's clear." Michaela's voice, and there was something in it — controlled, professional, but with a warmth underneath the control that Xander had learned, over four days, to recognize as genuine — that told him everything before she'd finished the sentence. "Chase, Carmichael, and Barkley are all out and accounted for. Extraction team confirms no injuries requiring immediate concern beyond what Barkley was already carrying. They're en route to you now."

Xander sat very still for a moment, the water stain blurring slightly at the edges in a way that had nothing to do with the ceiling.

"She's okay," he said, not quite a question, needing to hear it said plainly one more time.

"She's okay, Xander," Michaela said, gentler now. "And from what I'm hearing secondhand, more than okay. She'll tell you the rest herself. ETA fifteen minutes."

He hung up. Sat with it for a moment, the enormous relief of four days finally, actually ending, and found Aura watching him with her notebook forgotten in her lap for the first time since November first.

"She's clear," he said.

"I heard your half of it," Aura said, and something in her carefully composed face cracked, just slightly, into something unguarded and glad. "Fifteen minutes."

"Fifteen minutes," Xander agreed, and looked at the door, and found that for the first time in four days, the water stain had absolutely nothing left to offer him that mattered more than the door actually opening.

Tuesday, November 4, 1997: South Marion Drive, Sunnydale Medical Complex, Room 412,
Sunnydale, Early Morning 5:52am —

The door opened at 5:52am, seventeen minutes later than Michaela's estimate, and Cordelia Chase stood in the doorway in borrowed layers and bare feet, hair a mess, four days etched into her face in ways that would take longer than a morning to fully soften — and looked, for one long, suspended moment, exactly like every single thing Xander had spent four days telling himself she'd be, and something more besides, something steadier and harder-won than he'd let himself fully imagine.

"Hi," she said.

"Hi," Xander said, and found he couldn't manage anything more sophisticated than that single word, because everything else he'd been saving up — the seventeen things, the nineteen things, all of it — had evaporated on contact with the simple, overwhelming fact of her actually standing there.

She crossed the room. He was already moving too, despite four days of careful medical instruction about not straining anything, and they met somewhere in the middle, and for a long moment neither of them said anything at all, just held on, the way you hold onto something you'd genuinely, quietly feared you might not get to hold onto again.

"You're home by morning," he said, finally, into her hair.

"Told you I would be," Cordelia said, muffled against his shoulder, and there was something fierce and grateful and entirely herself in it. "You should've believed me faster."

"I believed you the whole time," Xander said. "I just also had contingency plans, because you'd have been furious with me if I hadn't."

"I would have been," Cordelia agreed, and pulled back just far enough to look at him properly, at the careful bandaging, at the exhausted steadiness in his face that matched, precisely, the exhausted steadiness she suspected was on her own. "You look terrible."

"You look amazing, considering."

"I look like I've been living in a paper gown for four days."

"You look like someone who got herself and two other people out of a federal black site," Xander said, quiet, serious now. "That's not amazing, Cordelia. That's extraordinary. I need you to actually hear that, not just deflect it."

Cordelia looked at him for a long moment, something working behind her eyes — the weight of what the last four days had actually cost her, of what she'd done in that office at five in the morning, of things she wasn't ready to say out loud yet and might not be for a while.

"I will," she said, quiet. "Just — not this second. This second I just want to sit down somewhere that isn't a cell, and eat something that isn't hospital food, and hear about this corkboard everyone apparently decided to name a whole organization after while I was gone."

"The Board," Xander said, and something warm and genuine broke through the exhaustion in his voice. "You're going to have opinions."

"I already have opinions," Cordelia said, settling carefully onto the edge of his bed, close, steady, four days finally, completely behind her. "I've had four days to develop them. Buckle up."

Aura, quietly, in the corner, opened her notebook and wrote down the time — 5:52am, the exact moment the log finally, mercifully, had good news to record — and let the two of them have the rest of the morning without further comment, because some things, even for a woman who'd made logging into a discipline, simply didn't need writing down to matter.
