Nine Hundred Years Is a Long Time to Not Know What a Shell Company Is

"Money has no memory, and no particular respect for how many centuries you've accumulated." — Mark Twain, attributed

Monday, November 3, 1997: Crawford Street, Sunnydale, Evening 7:24pm —

The ballroom ceiling had once been something. Frescoes, probably. California damp had been unkind to whatever it used to be, and what was left now was mostly the ghost of composition — shapes suggesting themselves in the mold the way a body suggests itself under a sheet.

Count Savros Hieronymus Blahdivastri stood in the middle of the ruined floor with his hands folded behind his back, looking up at it, and thought, not for the first time since Tuesday, that he had genuinely seen better ceilings.

He'd had a castle once. Actual castle. Actual frescoes, done by a man who understood proportion, back when proportion had been the entire point of a fresco and not something people apologized for wanting.

That castle had also, admittedly, had significantly less indoor plumbing, so. Trades.

"The plaster's coming down," Veronique said, from the doorway. She had her hands behind her back too, in the exact mirror of his own posture, which he'd have found irritating in anyone else and found, in her, oddly steadying. Eight hundred and forty-four years of practice, standing in ruins and deciding what came next. She was good at it. She'd always been good at it.

"Obviously," he said. "What's beneath it may be salvageable."

"It won't be." She walked further into the room, her footsteps exactly as loud as she'd chosen to make them, which tonight was almost silent. "I checked Tuesday night. Water damage goes down to the lath in most of the east half. We're not restoring a fresco, Savros. We're painting a new ceiling and telling people it's authentic, and in four hundred years somebody will believe us, because that is, historically, how this works."

He allowed himself the sound that passed, in this body, for a laugh. It came out smaller than it should have. He was still getting used to that — the insult of an eight-hundred-and-something-year-old sense of humor filtered through a sixteen-year-old's diaphragm.

"You've done this before," he said. It wasn't a question. He knew she had. He'd heard the stories, the ones she told and the ones other things told about her.

"I have restored six residences across four centuries," Veronique said. "None of them were Californian. All of them required convincing mortal authorities that a very old thing was, on paper, a very new thing." She turned in place, reading the room the way she read everything — assessing, filing, already three moves ahead. "The principle transfers. The specific instruments do not."

"Meaning?"

"Meaning," she said, "in 1887 I established residence in Prague using a marriage contract, a forged baptismal record, and the cooperation of a notary who owed my sire a debt he did not survive long enough to regret. None of those tools exist here. What exists here instead is something called a limited liability company, which Sunnydale's public library was extremely willing to explain to me in considerable and tedious detail this afternoon."

Blahd looked at her. "You went to a library."

"I went to a library," she confirmed, "and sat at a small table reading a pamphlet titled Starting Your Small Business: A Guide, while a librarian named Mrs. Castellan asked if I needed help finding anything, and I said no, thank you, I believe I have it, and she smiled at me the way adults smile at children who are pretending to understand something advanced, and I let her, because it was easier than the alternative conversation."

"The alternative conversation being."

"'No, madam, I do not require assistance, I have simply not needed to know what a limited liability company is because the last time I established a residence the primary legal instrument was a wax seal and a threat.'" She said it entirely flat, dry, and something in Blahd's chest that had gone quiet a very long time ago did the thing it did increasingly often around her lately, which he had also not fully worked out how to categorize.

"We require capital," he said, because that was the actual problem and the ceiling was not going to solve it by being looked at.

"We require capital," she agreed. "My accounts are, as of Tuesday, inaccessible. Two centuries of holdings, and I cannot produce so much as a document proving Veronica Daley has ever opened a bank account, because Veronica Daley was sixteen and had, as far as I can determine, an allowance and a library card."

"Mine are worse," Blahd said. "Freddy Iverson's proxy documentation named me — named Freddy — as executor of certain instruments that no longer exist in any accessible form, because the attorney who arranged them died in 1974 and his firm dissolved with him."

They stood in the ruined ballroom for a moment, two people who between them had personally witnessed the fall of at least one empire and the rise of several others, stymied by the concept of opening a checking account.

"There is," Veronique said slowly, "a sufficiency of old money in this city that does not answer to banks at all."

"Squisher's."

"Squisher's," she agreed. "And several individuals who owe debts of the sort that predate currency as we currently understand it. I spent Thursday evening there. A sufficiency of the patrons recognized what we are despite these—" a small, precise gesture at her own body, the sixteen-year-old shape of it, the school uniform she'd taken to wearing because it was, infuriatingly, the correct camouflage for this era — "despite this."

"And?"

"And several were extremely interested in the idea of old blood taking an interest in this town's affairs again," she said. "There has not been an established house here in decades. Just — appetite. Wandering appetite, with no structure, which is why this town is, forgive the observation, an embarrassment by any civilized vampiric standard."

Blahd looked at the ceiling again. At the ghost of the frescoes.

"A nightclub," he said, "requires capital before it produces capital. We require the former to obtain the latter."

"Squisher's would extend credit," Veronique said. "At interest. At very old interest, calculated the way interest used to be calculated, before usury laws made everyone soft about it."

"And in exchange for the credit?"

"Cooperation," she said. "Information sharing. A seat, eventually, at whatever passes in this era for the table where Sunnydale's supernatural economy actually gets discussed. Which brings us," she said, turning to look at him directly, "to the meeting."

"We have discussed the meeting."

"We have discussed attending the meeting. We have not discussed what we intend to say when we arrive." She folded her arms. "The Watcher will recognize us. He is old enough in his training, if not his years, to know precisely what we are the moment we walk through the door."

"And the American Captain."

"Michaela Reeves," Veronique said, "runs Black Company field operations with a ruthlessness I have observed and found impressive, which I do not say lightly. She will not be charmed. She will assess."

"So we give her something to assess."

"We give her everything," Veronique said. "Trask. The safe houses. The floor plan of Squisher's basement. All of it, freely, before she asks — because the alternative is being treated as a threat to be managed rather than a resource to be used, and I did not survive eight hundred years by choosing to be managed."

Blahd considered this. Considered her. The sixteen-year-old face with the nine-century weight sitting behind it, at ease with a decision most things twice her apparent age would have hedged on for a month.

"You want to see whether they're the kind of people who can be told the truth and use it correctly," he said.

"I have not frequently encountered such people," she said. "But I have encountered them. And when I have—"

"You remained."

"I remained." She looked at the door, at the ruined foyer beyond it, at the city outside doing the thing cities did after dark, ordinary and humming and entirely unaware of what was standing in this house deciding its future. "The meeting is at eight. We should not be late. First impressions, Savros."

"We were not invited."

"We were not not invited," Veronique said, already moving toward the door. "The distinction is going to matter a great deal, I suspect, before this century is out."

He looked at the ceiling one last time — at nine hundred years of better ones, filed away somewhere he no longer had access to — and followed her out into the dark.
