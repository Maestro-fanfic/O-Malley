The Historian Has Feelings About This

"The historian's job isn't to make the record comfortable. It's to make it true." — attributed to Dawn Summers, private notebook

Tuesday, November 4, 1997: 1630 Revello Drive, Sunnydale,
Early Morning 6:50am —

Dawn Summers had eleven notebooks now, which was ten more than she'd started with three days ago, and she'd organized them with a cross-indexing system of her own invention that involved colored tabs, a master index in the front cover of notebook one, and a set of abbreviations that made perfect sense to her and absolutely none to anyone who tried to read them without her personal decoder key, which she had decided, privately, was probably the point.

She was awake at six-fifty in the morning, which her mother did not yet know, because Joyce was downstairs discovering that a boy named Peter Pan apparently liked his orange juice served in the cup with the cartoon sun on it, a discovery Dawn had made from the top of the stairs and filed away without comment, because not everything needed a notebook entry, and some things just needed to be enjoyed quietly from a safe eavesdropping distance.

She had a different notebook open now — not the operational one, not the one with patrol schedules and the MHMR floor plan sketch and the training rotation. This one was smaller, plainer, kept separate from the others in a way she hadn't fully admitted to herself was deliberate until just this moment, sitting cross-legged on her bedroom floor in the pale early light, pen hovering over a blank page.

Things I actually feel about all of this, she'd written, three days ago, at the top of the first page, and had not added anything else since, because writing the header had used up whatever courage she'd had available that particular night.

She looked at it now.

The thing nobody quite understood about being the record-keeper — and she'd started to suspect, over the last three days, that even Buffy didn't fully understand it, despite loving her, despite checking in on her constantly in the careful way of an older sister trying very hard to do right by a situation she hadn't asked for either — was that keeping the records meant she saw everything. All of it. Every casualty number Benjy reported honestly instead of rounding down. Every gap in Jonathan's column. Every quiet, private moment of fear that got mentioned once, in passing, and then filed, and then never discussed again because everyone had somewhere urgent to be.

She was eleven years old, and she had, in the last three days, transcribed more genuine terror than most adults encountered in a lifetime, and she had done it calmly, competently, with good handwriting, because that was her job, and she was good at her job, and being good at her job meant nobody worried about whether the job was costing her anything.

She picked up the pen.

I'm scared all the time now, she wrote, slowly, the words coming easier once the first one landed on the page. Not the regular scared. Not homework scared or Buffy's-going-to-be-late scared. The other kind. The kind where I write down that four kids in the hallway almost died and I have to keep my handwriting neat while I do it, because if my handwriting gets messy someone might notice, and then they'll ask if I'm okay, and I don't actually know how to answer that question honestly without everyone deciding I'm too young to keep doing this, and I don't want them to decide that, because this is the one thing I'm actually good at, the one thing that's mine, and I don't want to lose it just because I'm also scared.

She stopped. Read it back. Felt something in her chest loosen slightly, the way it sometimes did after she cried, except she hadn't cried, she'd just written the true thing down instead, which felt, unexpectedly, like almost the same kind of relief.

There was a soft knock at her door.

"It's me," Stephanie said, from the hallway — Cap, mostly, to everyone else, but Stephanie to Dawn, because Dawn had decided early on that she wasn't going to call an eleven-year-old her own age by a superhero nickname just because the rest of the household had settled into it. "Can I come in?"

"Yeah."

Stephanie came in, sat down cross-legged across from her with the careful economy of someone who'd learned, seventy years ago in a body she hadn't yet grown into, to always check a room before fully entering it. Her eyes went, briefly, to the notebook, to the private one, and then, deliberately, away from it, respecting the boundary without needing to be asked.

"You're up early," Stephanie said.

"So are you."

"I don't really sleep the normal amount," Stephanie said. "Never have, since. It's a whole thing." She looked at Dawn steadily. "You've been carrying a lot of other people's hard days in that notebook system of yours. I've been meaning to ask if you've got anywhere to put your own."

Dawn looked at the small plain notebook in her lap, the one with exactly one honest paragraph in it.

"I'm starting one," she said, quiet. "It's not very good yet. I don't really know how to write about myself the way I write about everyone else. I know how to write down what Benjy did in the hallway. I don't really know how to write down what it's like being the person who has to hear about it calmly."

"That's allowed to take practice," Stephanie said. "I've had forty years' worth of memory about how to hold a line under fire. I did not come with any matching memory about how to be an eleven-year-old girl adopted into a house full of magic. Nobody hands you a manual for the second kind." She reached over, gently, and tapped the edge of the small notebook. "For what it's worth — the fact that you're scared and still doing the job perfectly doesn't mean the job isn't costing you something. It just means you're handling the cost quietly, the way people who are actually good at hard things tend to. That's not nothing, Dawn. That's not something to hide so people don't worry."

"But if I tell people, they might—"

"They might worry," Stephanie agreed. "And then they'll probably still let you keep doing the job, because you're the only person in this house who can do it the way you do it, and everyone here has finally figured out, this week especially, that treating people like they're too fragile for the truth doesn't actually protect them. It just leaves them carrying it alone." She looked at Dawn steadily. "You don't have to carry this one alone either. Even the historian's allowed a witness."

Dawn looked at her notebook — at the one true paragraph, at the blank page waiting underneath it — and picked the pen back up.

Stephanie says even the historian's allowed a witness, she wrote, carefully, her handwriting steady again. I think I believe her. I'm going to try believing her, anyway. That feels like a start.

Downstairs, faintly, she could hear Peter Pan asking Joyce whether it would be terribly rude to have a second helping of blackberries, and her mother's warm, unbothered laugh answering him, and Dawn sat with her small private notebook and her one true page and felt, for the first time in three days, like the fear and the job might actually be able to sit in the same room together without either one having to lose.
