A Phone Ringing in the Middle of Everything Is Never Good News, Except When It Is

"The most dangerous moment in any operation is the one where everyone in the room agrees on something. Check twice." — Murphy's Laws of Combat

Monday, November 3, 1997: South Marion Drive, Sunnydale Medical Complex, Conference Room B, Sunnydale, Evening 8:09pm —

Michaela's phone buzzed against the table in the rattling way that meant she'd left it on vibrate and forgotten to also silence it, which was a small operational failure she filed away to correct later, and which currently had the entire effect of stopping Veronique de Alençon mid-sentence about Squisher's basement floor plan.

"Reeves," Michaela said, answering it without leaving the table, because in a room this crowded stepping out for a phone call felt like conceding ground she wasn't sure she'd get back.

"It's Xander," the voice said, thin through the small speaker but unmistakably itself — steady, direct, the voice of somebody who'd learned very recently and very completely how to sound calm regardless of what his actual heart rate was doing. "I've got something for the meeting. It's about the Kendalls."

Michaela put the call on speaker without asking anyone's permission, because the room had earned the right to hear this together, and because she'd learned, over three days, that half of what made this Board work was that nobody in it hoarded information as leverage.

"Go ahead," she said. "You're on with everyone."

There was a small pause — Michaela pictured him one floor up, phone against his ear, MPs outside the door pretending very hard not to notice — and then he said it, plainly, the way he apparently said everything now: "I want them brought all the way in. Not managed at arm's length. Not the operational picture, just — they should know their daughter's being looked after by people who actually give a damn about her, not just about what she is now. That's not a security risk. That's the whole point of what we're building."

Giles, across the table, had gone quiet in the way he went quiet when something landed exactly where it needed to. Dawn's pen had stopped moving.

"They lost their daughter to something they don't have a framework for," Xander went on, "and instead of grieving her they've been managed, politely, at a distance, by adults who kept telling them it was for their own good. I think that's backwards. I think grief that gets handled at arm's length just curdles into something worse. I think you bring people in, and you tell them the truth they can handle, and you let them decide what to do with it, same as we did with Rona Williams."

"Rona Williams hasn't been introduced to this room yet," Brockhurst said, mildly, to no one in particular, and Michaela made a note to fix that oversight before the night was out.

"The Kendalls deserve the same offer," Xander said. "Come to a Monday meeting. See the board. Meet the people. Decide for themselves whether they want in, and if they do, they're in — not as liability management, as family."

Veronique, across the table, was watching the phone with an expression Michaela couldn't fully read — old, assessing, and something else underneath it that might, in a face eight hundred years less practiced at concealment, have been approval.

"That is," Veronique said, quietly, to Blahd, "precisely the kind of thing I was hoping to hear from this room."

Giles cleared his throat. "Xander. I concur completely. I should have proposed it myself days ago, and I— I didn't, because I let myself believe managing the Kendalls' access was protecting them, when in fact it was simply easier for me." A pause, and something that might have been genuine self-reproach moved across his face. "Thank you for saying it plainly."

"Somebody had to," Xander said. "Also, quick unrelated thing — Pooka's debrief just came through. Cordelia's working a second plan. Nobody moves before oh-six-hundred. She says she'll be home by morning."

The room went very still.

Michaela looked at the faces around the table — Buffy, Benjy, Jonathan, Warren and Seven, two vampires who'd walked in ten minutes ago carrying centuries of leverage and had somehow ended up, in the space of that single sentence, looking like everyone else in the room: quietly, fiercely relieved.

"Copy that," Michaela said, into the phone, keeping her own voice level with the ease of long practice. "Oh-six-hundred. Tell her — tell her the Board's holding."

"I'll tell her," Xander said, and there was something in the way he said it, something that had nothing to do with tactics or timelines, that made even Zabuto, at the far end of the table, look briefly, unexpectedly moved.

"Get some rest, Harris," Michaela added.

"Working on it," Xander said, in the exact tone of somebody who had absolutely no intention of resting, and hung up.

Michaela set the phone down. Looked around the table — at Giles, already making a note to contact the Kendalls in the morning; at Dawn, adding a new line to whatever page the notebook was currently on; at Blahd and Veronique, settling into the room in a way that suggested they'd just decided, definitively, that this was a table worth sitting at.

"All right," she said. "The Kendalls join the Board, effective this week. I want that handled with care, not procedure." She looked at the clock on the wall, at the eleven hours standing between now and oh-six-hundred, at the weight of every single thread in this room converging toward the same quiet, unbearable countdown. "Now. Where were we."

"Squisher's basement," Veronique said, and the meeting went on.
