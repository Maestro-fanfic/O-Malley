The Bolt Comes Free

"Freedom rarely arrives all at once. Mostly it arrives one stubborn bolt at a time." — attributed to Cordelia Chase, private notebook

Monday, November 3, 1997: Sunnydale MHMR, Topanga Street, Sunnydale, Night 11:52pm —

The bolt came free at 11:52pm, which Cordelia noted with the flat satisfaction of someone who had been expecting the moment for so long that its actual arrival felt almost anticlimactic, three inches of spring steel dropping into her waiting palm with a small, undramatic click that nonetheless felt, in her chest, like something enormous.

She lay very still for a full minute afterward, just breathing, just holding it, because four days of careful patience had taught her that the moment you finally got the thing you wanted was exactly the moment you were most likely to get sloppy about keeping it.

The camera swept left.

She tucked the steel into the waistband of the paper gown, where it sat cold against her hip, and thought, with the dry humor she'd been rationing herself in careful doses since Thursday: well. That's a start.

Six hours and eight minutes until oh-six-hundred.

She had spent the last four days building a mental map of this facility more thorough than anything she'd have believed herself capable of a month ago, back when her biggest logistical challenge had been coordinating which Cordettes wore which colors to which game. Now she had shift changes memorized. She had the fourteen-second camera cycle memorized. She had the creak of the third floor's east stairwell door memorized, distinct from the ordinary building settling sounds, because Pooka had confirmed on Tuesday that particular creak meant someone was actually using those stairs rather than the elevator, and elevator use meant staff, and staff use at odd hours meant something worth knowing about.

She did not have Walsh's exact location memorized, because Walsh moved like a woman who understood that predictability got you killed, and Cordelia had spent enough of the last four days respecting Walsh's competence, even while hating her with a specificity that had crystallized somewhere around day two into something closer to a professional grudge than ordinary fear.

The vent grille didn't glow. It wasn't supposed to, not yet. Pooka's next check-in wasn't until 1am, per the schedule they'd built together, the schedule that ran on fourteen-second increments and shift patterns and the rhythm of a building that thought it knew everything happening inside it and didn't.

Cordelia lay in the dark, spring steel warm now against her skin, and ran the plan forward one more time, the way she'd run it forward maybe two hundred times since Thursday: bolt free, steel in hand, wait for Pooka's next signal, confirm Creed's position, confirm Barkley's condition, move at oh-five-thirty to allow buffer before the true oh-six-hundred window, exit via the east stairwell during the shift change, rally point at the loading dock with no camera coverage since February's broken bracket that nobody had filed the budget to fix.

Six hours and seven minutes.

She thought about Xander's hands, doing hardware checks he didn't know he was doing. She thought about the seventeen things, which had, sometime in the last four days, quietly become nineteen things, because apparently trauma made her more articulate rather than less, which was either a personality flaw or a professional asset depending on how you chose to frame it.

She thought, briefly and with real fondness, about a corkboard on a dining room wall that she had never personally seen but had heard described in exhaustive detail by Pooka, a board with a parking lot and an action column and Dawn's careful handwriting, and found herself, absurdly, looking forward to seeing it in person — to standing in front of it and adding her own items to it, in her own handwriting, as someone who had actually earned a place on it rather than simply dating into proximity of it.

The Board, Pooka had said, delighted, during the 9pm check-in, relaying the whole naming story in a rush of details Cordelia had made her repeat twice because it was, genuinely, the first thing all week that had made her laugh out loud in a room designed to make her feel like laughing was a symptom.

The Board, Cordelia thought now, testing the shape of it the way Giles had tested it in the hallway, and found she liked it more than "the formation," which had always sounded, to her ear, like something out of a bad military thriller rather than what it actually was — a corkboard, a parking lot, an action column, and people who kept showing up for each other because showing up was simply what they'd decided to do.

She was going to be on that board by morning. Not as an item in the parking lot. As someone who'd gotten herself and two other captives out of a federal black site using a bolt, a piece of spring steel, and four days of very careful patience.

The camera swept right.

She smiled in the dark, where it couldn't see, and settled in to wait out the remaining six hours and six minutes with the stillness of someone who had, somewhere in the last four days, stopped being a girl waiting to be rescued and started being one of the people other people could rely on to do the rescuing herself.
