A Vampire's Idea of Staying Out of the Way

"A vampire who has survived long enough learns that the safest place to stand is usually just out of the story's way." — attributed to Veronique de Alençon

Monday, November 3, 1997: Crestwood Apartments, Sunnydale, Evening 9:10pm —

Angel had been sitting in the same chair for forty minutes, not moving, which was not in itself unusual — he'd had a hundred years to get comfortable with stillness in a way most people never had to develop — except that tonight the stillness wasn't peaceful. Tonight it was the deliberate stillness of a man holding himself in place by main force, because every instinct he had was telling him to be somewhere else, doing something, and he had made the decision, three days ago, that the most useful thing he could do for this town right now was exactly nothing.

It was not going well.

He'd been to the hospital twice. Both times he'd stood in the parking lot, looked up at the fourth floor, and turned around, because Xander Harris did not need a vampire looming at the foot of his bed while he recovered from two gunshot wounds, and Angel had learned, the hard way, more than once, that his presence tended to complicate things that were already complicated enough without him.

He'd been to the school once. Stood outside the library for ten minutes in the dark, close enough to feel the Hellmouth's low hum under his feet, and had not gone in, because Giles was managing something delicate with the TX and Angel's specific history — a vampire with a soul, cursed, unpredictable by the standards of anyone who didn't already know him — was not a variable that conversation currently needed.

He had not been anywhere near Cordelia Chase's location, though he knew, from Aura's careful, deliberately roundabout phone updates, roughly where she was and roughly what was happening to her, and every part of him that had spent a century learning to fight monsters wanted to go do something about that specific problem right now, tonight, with his hands.

He didn't. Because Xander would go, because Xander had a plan already moving, because inserting a vampire — however well-intentioned — into a delicate extraction with a fourteen-second camera cycle and a six a.m. deadline was exactly the kind of variable that got people killed.

So he sat in his apartment, in the dark, not moving, and did the thing he was actually good at, which was thinking very carefully about what he was for, now, in a town that had built something without him.

There was a knock at the door.

Angel was across the room and had it open before the second knock landed, old reflexes, old habits, and found Buffy Summers standing in the hallway in a dark blue shirt he hadn't seen before, looking steadier than he'd seen her look since Halloween.

"Hi," she said.

"Hi." He stepped back, let her in, watched her take in the apartment the way she always did, cataloguing without meaning to, the habit of someone who'd learned to notice exits even in places she trusted.

"You weren't at the meeting," she said. Not an accusation. Just an observation, offered plainly.

"I wasn't invited."

"Nobody invited Blahd and Veronique either," Buffy said. "They came anyway."

Angel was quiet for a moment. "That's different."

"Is it?"

"They walked in with something to offer," Angel said, carefully. "Information. Leverage. A reason for the room to want them there despite what they are." He looked at his hands. "I don't have that. I have a soul that could theoretically stop working if certain things happen, and a history of being a considerable threat to everyone in that room under exactly the wrong circumstances. That's not a contribution. That's a liability with good intentions."

Buffy was quiet for a moment, looking at him with an expression he recognized — the look she got when she was deciding whether to let something slide or push on it.

"You've been staying away on purpose," she said. "Since Halloween. Not just tonight. The whole three days."

"I've been trying to be useful without being in the way," Angel said. "There's a difference, and I've been trying very hard to land on the right side of it."

"By disappearing."

"By not making myself the thing everyone else has to manage while they're already managing a Terminator and a federal cover-up and a psychiatric hospital escape plan," Angel said, and there was real weight in it, the exhaustion of someone who'd spent three days convincing himself this was the responsible choice and was only now, with her standing in his apartment, hearing how it sounded out loud.

Buffy sat down on the arm of his couch, uninvited, the way she did most things when she'd decided a conversation mattered more than the formality around it.

"You know what I did on Halloween," she said. "The gorilla thing."

"I heard."

"'Kat told me something tonight," Buffy said. "About how I wasn't even really there for it — I was Cinderella, completely, the whole time, and whatever calm I had wasn't me choosing anything, it was just a fairy tale not knowing enough to be scared." She looked at him steadily. "And I've been thinking about that, the whole walk over here, because I think you're doing the opposite version of the same mistake."

"Which is."

"You're so busy deciding what you're allowed to be — soulless monster, cursed vampire, dangerous variable — that you've stopped actually being anything at all. At least I had an excuse. I wasn't in the driver's seat that night. You've had three days of being completely, yourself, and you've spent every single one of them deciding the safest thing to do with that self was nothing."

Angel didn't say anything for a long moment.

"I've hurt people," he said, finally, quiet. "Not this soul. The other one. But it's the same face, the same hands, and everyone in that room tonight has read a file with my name in it somewhere, one way or another, and I don't think it's unreasonable for them to want to keep some distance."

"Nobody's asked you to keep distance," Buffy said. "You decided that part yourself. Same as Blahd and Veronique could've decided to stay hidden in their manor and let this town figure itself out without them, and instead they walked into a room full of strangers and said, here's what we know, use it or don't." She stood, crossed to him, close enough that he had to actually look at her instead of past her. "You keep treating what you are like it's a threat everyone has to be protected from. Nobody in that room tonight is asking to be protected from you, Angel. They're asking what you're going to bring to the table, same as they asked everyone else."

"And if I don't have anything to bring."

"You've fought things in this town for two hundred years that nobody else in that room has ever heard of," Buffy said. "You know the demon hierarchies. You know which old things sleep and which ones are just waiting. You know Angelus's old contacts, his old debts, the things that move in this town's dark that nothing in Giles's books has ever properly catalogued." She looked at him steadily. "That's not nothing, Angel. That's not a liability. That's the exact same kind of thing Blahd and Veronique walked in offering, except you've been sitting on it for three days because you decided your own history disqualified you before anyone else got the chance to decide that for themselves."

Angel looked at her for a long moment, something shifting behind his eyes, old and careful and slowly, reluctantly, reconsidering.

"There's a meeting every Monday," Buffy said, quieter now. "The next one's not for a week. That gives you a week to decide whether you're going to keep hiding in this apartment being noble about it, or whether you're going to walk in and actually be part of the thing we're all building." She headed for the door, paused, hand on the frame, echoing without quite meaning to the exact gesture she'd used leaving Faith's motel room hours earlier. "I hope it's the second one. I think you'd be good at this, if you let yourself try."

She left him standing in the dark apartment, alone with a century of careful self-judgment and, for the first time in three days, a genuine question about whether any of it had actually been protecting anyone, or just protecting himself from the risk of being told no.
