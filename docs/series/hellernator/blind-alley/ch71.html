The Water Stain Has Views

"A man watching the ceiling is a man who has run out of better places to put his fear." — attributed to a Sunnydale Memorial night-shift nurse

Monday, November 3, 1997: South Marion Drive, Sunnydale Medical Complex, Room 412, Sunnydale, Evening 8:04pm —

The water stain on the ceiling had, over the past four days, become the single most reliable fixed point in Xander Harris's entire world, which said something uncomfortable about the current state of his world that he'd decided not to examine too closely.

It had a shape. Not a good shape — not a rabbit or a state he recognized or anything satisfying like that — just an irregular brown continent of old water damage that he'd memorized down to the smaller peninsula near the light fixture, because memorizing it gave his brain something to do that wasn't running the seventeen things or the seven contingency plans or the quiet dread that lived under both of those, the one he wasn't naming, the one that showed up whenever he let himself think too long about a girl in a paper gown eleven miles away doing math on a piece of spring steel.

"You're doing the hand thing," Aura said, not looking up from the notebook.

Xander looked down. His left hand was, in fact, doing the thing — trigger assembly check, thumb and forefinger where a selector switch would be if he were holding something that had a selector switch, which he was not, because he was holding a hospital blanket, which did not require safety checks of any kind.

He didn't know exactly when the hand had learned to do that on its own. Didn't let himself trace it back further than "sometime this week," because tracing it back further meant a specific house on a specific night, and a specific reason his hands had needed something to check and recheck that had nothing to do with any weapon at all, and that was a hallway he did not currently have the floor space to walk down, not with eleven miles and a countdown clock between him and the one person who'd have understood exactly why without him having to explain it.

He made it stop.

"Eleven times today," Aura said, writing the number down with the same flat precision she applied to everything since November first. "Down from nineteen yesterday. Ramesh would probably call that progress if I told him, which I'm not going to, because he'd want to know why I'm counting in the first place, and 'methodology' only covers so much conversational ground with a licensed physician."

"You could just tell him the truth."

"The truth is I started logging your hardware checks because it was something concrete I could do while everyone else was doing things I couldn't do, and it turned out to actually be useful, and now I can't stop, because stopping feels like admitting the logs were never really about the checks." She looked up, finally, and there was something unguarded in it that Aura Breckenridge — former social lieutenant, professional performer of not-noticing — allowed herself increasingly rarely. "They were about knowing something was still working. About you specifically. In a system where a lot of things weren't."

Xander looked at her for a moment.

"Thank you," he said. "For the logs. All of it."

"It's in the methodology," Aura said, which had become, over four days, the phrase that meant something else entirely, and they both let it sit there without either of them pushing on it further.

His phone — the one Michaela's people had left him, against Ramesh's specific and vocal objections, on the theory that a seventeen-year-old who'd already survived two gunshot wounds and a Terminator engagement this month probably deserved operational awareness more than he deserved bed rest — buzzed once on the side table.

Aura picked it up, read it, and her expression did something complicated.

"Pooka," she said. "Full debrief just came in through Moseby. Chase is working a second plan. Don't move before oh-six-hundred. There's something about a backup archive and an access code, and—" she paused, and Xander watched her decide, deliberately, to say the rest of it without softening it. "She said to tell you she'll be home by morning."

Xander looked at the water stain. At the peninsula near the light fixture that he now knew better than his own bedroom ceiling.

He believed her. That part came easy, came without having to work for it — Cordelia Chase did not say things she didn't intend to make true, had never once in the two years he'd properly known her, said a thing lightly that she meant heavily.

He was also, in the same breath, running the contingency for if she wasn't, because she would be the very first person to tell him that belief wasn't a plan, and being right about people was one of the seventeen things on the growing list of reasons he wasn't currently allowed to lose her.

"The meeting's happening right now," he said. Not a question.

"One floor down. Started four minutes ago." Aura checked her watch, the precise gesture of someone tracking multiple timelines simultaneously and keeping them all straight without apparent effort. "You're not invited, on account of Ramesh's extremely detailed instructions to the MPs about you staying in this bed."

"Ramesh didn't say anything about the phone."

"No," Aura agreed, a faint, careful smile finding the corner of her mouth. "He did not."

Xander picked up the phone. Looked at the screen. At Michaela's number, already saved, already trusted in a way that would have seemed insane to him two weeks ago and now just felt like Tuesday.

"The Kendalls," he said, before he dialed. "I want them in the Board. Actually in it. Not managed from a distance."

Aura's pen paused. "Why now, specifically."

"Because Cordelia's coming home tomorrow morning," Xander said, "and when she does, the first thing she's going to ask about is whether we did the actual work while she was gone, or just held the fort. And bringing the Kendalls in properly — not the operational picture, just knowing their kid's being looked after by people who give a damn — that's actual work. That's not holding anything. That's building something."

Aura looked at him for a long moment. Wrote something down.

"That's Monday meeting material," she said.

"Everything's Monday meeting material."

"That's why there's a Monday meeting," Aura said, and there was real warmth in it now, unguarded, the kind she'd have performed not having six months ago and simply had, plainly, tonight.

Xander dialed. Waited for Michaela to pick up, one floor below, in a room full of people who were, without his help, without Cordelia's help, building the exact thing the two of them had started without ever quite meaning to.

The water stain watched him do it, the way it had watched everything for four days, patient, brown, unbothered by any of it.

He was, he thought, going to have a lot to say to that ceiling someday, if he ever fully explained this month to anyone who hadn't lived it.

The line connected.

"Reeves."

"It's Xander," he said. "I've got something for the meeting. It's about the Kendalls."
