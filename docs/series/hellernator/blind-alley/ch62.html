Seven and a Half Inches of Pure Attitude

Monday, November 3, 1997: Sunnydale MHMR, Topanga Street, Sunnydale, Evening 7:19pm —

Ventilation systems, Pooka Bell had decided somewhere around Wednesday, were the single greatest human invention nobody appreciated properly. Better than pizza. Almost. She'd have to think about that ranking more later, when she wasn't currently flying flat-out through eleven feet of galvanized steel duct at a speed that would've made her old drill instructor either extremely proud or extremely concerned, possibly both, he'd had a face that did both at once a lot.

The vent grille behind her was still humming from where she'd popped through it, which meant she had, generously, six seconds before the next camera sweep hit that section of hallway ceiling, and she intended to use every single one of those seconds being somewhere else entirely.

Left at the junction. Then down. Then the stretch that ran over the nurses' station, which was the fun part, because it was also the part where if she went too fast her wings buzzed loud enough that Nurse Denny — the one with the perpetually offended eyebrows, like the whole building was constantly letting her down personally — might glance up.

Pooka slowed down for that part. Barely. Just enough.

She's fine, she thought, meaning Cordelia, meaning the whole reason any of this mattered tonight. She's fine and she's got a plan and plans are good, plans are the best, Sergeant Michaela is gonna eat that plan up like it's got sprinkles on it.

She cleared the nurses' station stretch. Buzzed a hard right into the service shaft, which dropped four feet before it leveled out again, and enjoyed exactly one half-second of freefall the way she'd enjoy a good sneeze, sharp and satisfying and over too fast.

There was a hawk that lived somewhere near the parking structure. She knew this because it had tried to eat her on Thursday, which, rude, extremely rude, she was a Marine on a mission, not a snack, and she'd had Words about it at the time — mostly hand gestures, since talking takes longer than gestures and also the hawk didn't speak English — and every time she cut past that section of duct now she checked, out of what she considered professional vigilance and what a less generous observer might call a grudge.

No hawk tonight. Good. She had places to be.

The thing about the mission — the actual mission, not the fun parts, though the fun parts were also real, because both things were just true at the same time and always had been — was that Lady Cordelia had looked so still back there. Not scared-still. Not exactly. Working-still. The kind of still Pooka herself did right before a breach, where your whole body goes quiet because everything that matters is happening somewhere else, in your head, where nobody can see it.

She'd looked cold, too. Pooka had noticed that and hadn't said anything, because there wasn't a version of I noticed you're cold that helped anybody get out of that room faster, and helping was the whole job.

She thought about Xander lying in that hospital bed doing his own version of working-still, and she thought, not for the first time, that these two absolutely deserved each other, in the way that meant good, actually, the way you meant it about two people who were both going to drive each other completely up the wall for the rest of their natural lives and be delighted about it the entire time.

The main duct opened up ahead — the big one, the one that ran the length of the east wing and let her actually get some speed going instead of squeezing through joints built for airflow and not for seven-and-a-half-inch Marines with somewhere urgent to be.

She hit it at full throttle. Wings a blur. Two tiny swords riding quiet against her back where she'd strapped them, because you didn't fly a covert extraction with unsecured gear banging around, that was Basic, Chapter One, everybody knew that.

Don't move before oh-six-hundred, she recited to herself, locking it down the way she'd locked down every briefing she'd ever gotten, word for word, no wiggle room. She's got a second plan. First plan got her information. Access code. Backup archive. Not Lowell House — here. Tell Michaela specifically.

And the other part. The part that wasn't tactical at all, that she was going to carry the whole way back like something breakable held carefully in both hands.

Tell Xander I'll be home by morning.

Pooka had said he's gonna worry before she could stop herself, and Lady Cordelia had said tell him anyway, and something about the way she'd said it — steady, sure, like she'd already made her peace with the worrying and just needed him to have the information anyway — had made Pooka's chest do something complicated that she didn't have a tactical term for yet.

She cleared the last junction and dropped through the return vent into the stairwell shaft, which put her three floors and one very inconvenient elevator shaft away from the parking structure exit where Merrill was supposedly waiting with the van, assuming Merrill hadn't gotten Views about the ethics of waiting in a van outside a psychiatric facility, which, given his face lately, was a genuine possibility she was prepared to argue him out of.

Focus. Mission. Message. Then pizza.

Priorities. A soldier without priorities was just a person running around a duct system for no reason, and Pooka Bell was never, under any circumstances, a person running around for no reason.

She hit the exit vent going fast enough that the grille rattled, popped it with her shoulder, and dropped out into the cool November dark over the parking structure in a controlled little tumble that ended, as these things generally did lately, with both feet planted and her chin up like she'd meant to do that the whole time.

Somewhere above, a shape moved against the streetlight glow. Wings. Not hers.

Pooka looked up, found the hawk sitting smug on the light pole like it owned the whole parking structure, and gave it both hands, very deliberately, very seriously, the exact gesture she'd have gotten written up for back home and did not currently care even slightly about.

The hawk did not appear impressed.

"Yeah, well," Pooka muttered, already airborne again, angling toward the van, "you started it."

She had a message to deliver, a meeting to interrupt, and, God willing and if Joyce Summers had made any extra, a pizza waiting on the other end of all of it.

Mission first. But she was absolutely thinking about the pizza.
