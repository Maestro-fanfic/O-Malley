On the Board

"A board is just a table until enough people decide to sit at it honestly." — attributed to Rupert Giles, private correspondence

Monday, November 3, 1997: South Marion Drive, Sunnydale Medical Complex, outside Conference Room B, Sunnydale, Evening 9:40pm —

The meeting broke the way meetings broke when everyone in the room had somewhere else they urgently needed to be — not all at once, but in a slow uneven spill into the hallway, people peeling off in twos and threes, still finishing sentences they'd started at the table.

Zabuto was one of the last ones through the door, and he stopped Michaela with a small, formal incline of the head that she'd learned, over three days, meant he had a question he'd been holding back out of politeness and had finally decided the politeness cost more than it saved.

"Captain Reeves," he said. "Forgive me — I've been in this town three days and I confess I still don't have a name for what I'm looking at." He gestured, mildly, at the general spill of people into the corridor — Blahd and Veronique deep in quiet conversation with Giles, Benjy and Misty comparing notes with Dawn's notebook open between them, Jonathan and Tamara already halfway down the hall still arguing gap columns. "This. All of it. What is it called?"

Michaela opened her mouth to answer and found, for the first time in longer than she cared to admit, that she didn't have a ready answer. Black Company had a name. AFSOC had a name. This did not have a name, because nobody had ever needed one before tonight, when a hundred-and-something-year-old Watcher-in-training had asked the question plainly enough that the gap became impossible to ignore.

"It's not an operation," she said, slowly, working it out loud. "It's not a unit."

"It's not anything the Council has a category for either," Zabuto said. "Which is, I confess, part of why I'm asking."

"We're not an organization," Dawn said, from a few feet away, not looking up from the notebook, in the flat matter-of-fact tone of an eleven-year-old contributing to an adult conversation without asking permission first. "We're a corkboard with opinions."

That got a laugh out of Brockhurst — a short, genuine one, rare enough from him that several heads turned.

"She's not wrong," Joyce said, coming out of the room last with the empty garlic bread tray under one arm. "There is, very literally, a board. On my dining room wall. With a parking lot and an action column and Dawn's handwriting all over it."

"So what does that make us," Veronique said, with the dry precision of someone who found the whole exercise mildly amusing and was prepared to be entertained by wherever it landed. "The Parking Lot?"

"The Action Column," Blahd offered, deadpan, and Michaela caught the corner of his mouth doing something that, on a face nine hundred years old, passed for genuine delight.

"We could just be the Board," Xander said.

Everyone looked at the phone propped against Aura's ear, because that was where the voice had come from — Xander, still one floor up, still on the call he'd apparently never actually ended, listening in on speaker the whole time.

"You're still on the line," Michaela said.

"I've been on the line the whole meeting," Xander said, entirely unbothered. "Ramesh didn't say anything about staying on a call. Anyway. The Board. There's a board. We're the people who show up to it. Simple. Accurate. Doesn't require anybody to explain anything to anybody new, because you just point at the wall."

There was a pause in the hallway — the pause that happens right before a room decides, collectively and without quite meaning to, that something has landed.

"The Board," Giles repeated, testing the shape of it. "It's rather understated, for what we're actually doing."

"That's the point," Dawn said, still not looking up, already writing something in the notebook. "Nobody asks follow-up questions about a board. Everybody asks follow-up questions about a 'formation.'"

"I like it," Benjy said, with the decisiveness of a First Sergeant rendering a verdict. "It's not trying to sound like more than it is. That's — that's actually good tactics."

"High praise, coming from her," Misty said.

"I've never met a board I didn't respect," Veronique said, and something about the sheer incongruity of a woman who'd been alive since before printing presses existed weighing in seriously on corkboard nomenclature broke the last of the tension in the hallway into something warmer.

"The Board," Zabuto said again, quieter this time, like he was filing it away properly. "Very well. Thank you. That's precisely what I needed."

"Anytime, Mr. Zabuto," Xander's voice said, tinny through the phone speaker, and then, after a beat: "Somebody tell Cordelia when she's home. She's going to have opinions about whether she got a vote."

"She'll have opinions regardless of whether she got a vote," Aura said, already writing the exchange down in her own notebook, because apparently this, too, was going in the logs. "That's never stopped her before."

The hallway laughed, quietly, all of it — vampires and Watchers and pixies and eleven-year-old First Sergeants and a phone call from a hospital bed — and somewhere in that laugh, without anyone formally declaring it, the thing that had needed a name for three days finally, quietly, had one.

The Board.

It stuck immediately, the way the good ones always did — not because anyone had planned it, but because it was true, and because it was funny, and because, as Dawn pointed out later while updating the actual literal board with a new header that just read THE BOARD in her small precise handwriting, nobody ever asks follow-up questions about a corkboard.
