Chapter 1: Summer, Which Is a Word That Used to Mean Something Simpler

"Everything you are not stops you from becoming everything you might be." — attributed to Anaïs Nin

Wednesday, June 17, 1998; Revello Drive, Sunnydale; Morning 9:40am —

Nobody had told the sun it was supposed to feel different this morning, so it didn't. It came in through Buffy's window at the same slant it had used all spring, and generally behaved like a summer that had won something wasn't owed a different kind of light.

Buffy lay on her back and did the inventory she'd made a habit of since March. Am I hurt. No, not really. Is anyone screaming. No. Do I remember what today is supposed to be about. And for the first time in eight months, the answer to that last one was: nothing. Today was just Wednesday.

Downstairs, coffee was already crawling up the stairwell the way it always did, and Dawn's voice was going at the pitch of someone narrating to somebody who hadn't asked.

"—and Chapter One Hundred Sixty-Seven's the last one, obviously, because it's Book Three, and Giles says the Board decides what Book Four even is, which, no offense to Giles, is the kind of thing an adult says when he means 'we have no idea, we're making this up same as always'—"

"Dawn." Joyce's voice, patient, amused. "Cereal first. Historiography after."

"It's not historiography, it's a record, there's a difference—"

Buffy got up, got dressed, went down. Dawn was at the table with a new notebook, thicker than the last one — the math of an ongoing family apparently budgeted for. Joyce handed Buffy a mug she hadn't asked for.

"How do you feel?"

Not rhetorical. Hadn't been for eight months.

"Weird," Buffy said, honestly. "Good-weird. Like your ears after a concert — everything's quiet, but the wrong kind of quiet."

"I stole that from Xander," she added. "He said it about the Hellmouth thing in April."

"He's not wrong very often," Joyce said, and there was something underneath it — eight months of watching a boy become someone who ran logistics for a supernatural task force, and not quite having language yet for what that did to how you looked at him.

Dawn looked up. "Want to know what I wrote for yesterday?"

"You're going to tell me either way."

"June 16th, 1998. We won." She said it like she'd practiced how it should sound out loud. "And then what Giles said, about the board staying on the wall. And then I left half a page blank. Book Four needs its own first page."

Buffy looked at her sister — twelve, keeper of the record by her own appointment — and felt something adjacent to pride and adjacent to grief for the version of Dawn who'd never had to learn this, sitting right next to each other, the way everything did these days.

"Leave it blank a little longer," Buffy said. "I think we've got some summer to have first."

"That's not historiography. That's just hope."

"Write that part down too."

Chapter 2: Diplomatic Immunity, Crawford Street Division

"A great fortune is a great slavery." — Seneca

Wednesday, June 17, 1998; Crawford Street Manor, Sunnydale; Late Morning 11:15am —

"The realtor called again," Veronique said, not looking up from correspondence that had survived three centuries and one ill-advised 1920s renovation, and was not, this morning, going to survive Blahd's boots on the good end of the table.

"Tell her we're not selling."

"I've told her four times. She believes this is a negotiating position."

"Do we want more money?"

"We have more money than the county has ever audited. The issue is that a house owned continuously since 1887, by an entity that doesn't age, doesn't appear on any census, and now receives mail addressed to 'The Crawford Street Household — Attn: Board Liaison,' is going to attract exactly the attention we've spent a century not attracting."

Blahd took his boots off the table. Not because she'd asked. Because she was right, and enjoying it less than usual.

"We could bite the realtor."

"We are not biting the realtor."

"I said could."

"And has the distinction ever once stopped you?"

"...Somewhat," he allowed, and she almost smiled — didn't, quite, on principle, but he'd had nine hundred years to learn to read the almost.

The bell at the gate went — the actual 1887 bell, Blahd having refused every proposed replacement — and Veronique rose to answer it, because whoever answered the door first got to set the tone.

It was Pooka. Parade rest, the gravity of someone about to deliver a briefing rather than a visit.

"Ma'am. Board business. Got a minute?"

"For the Board," Veronique said, stepping back, "we have several centuries of minutes, largely unspent. Come in."

Chapter 3: What a Bound Thing Notices About a Free Morning

"The unexamined life is not worth living." — Socrates, as recorded by Plato

Wednesday, June 17, 1998; Giles's apartment, Sunnydale; Morning 8:50am —

The TX had catalogued, over eight months, four hundred and eleven instances of what it privately called ordinary continuation. This morning made four hundred and twelve.

It sat in the chair by the window — nobody's assigned chair, understood as its chair regardless — and watched Giles read a novel. Not a demonology text. A novel. Giles had started reading novels again in April, filed under recovery, ongoing, promising.

"You're cataloguing me again," Giles said, not looking up.

"I am cataloguing."

"Why."

The TX considered this with care, because Giles's simple questions rarely were. "Eight months ago you did not read novels. You read what the work required. This is not required. I find I want to understand what causes a person to do a thing that isn't."

Giles looked up, kept his place with one finger. "That's not a small question, coming from you."

"No. Most of mine have stopped being small ones."

"Would you like a theory?"

"I would find one useful."

"I think you've stopped needing everything to be required," Giles said. "Eight months ago you acted because the mission required it. Now you sit in that chair and catalogue me reading a novel because you want to understand something, not because it serves a function. That's rather the largest thing a person can do — decide to want to know something for no reason but wanting."

The TX was quiet eleven seconds. "I don't have a function for this conversation."

"No. I shouldn't think you do."

"I would still like to continue it."

"Then we shall," Giles said, and found his page. "Welcome to it."

Chapter 4: Regeneration Modules and Other Forms of Repair

"Any sufficiently advanced technology is indistinguishable from magic." — Arthur C. Clarke

Wednesday, June 17, 1998; Sunnydale MHMR sub-basement, "the Lab"; Midday 12:30pm —

Seven had reorganized the workbench four times since April, and Warren had learned, incrementally, that it wasn't inefficiency. It was Seven doing the thing she did instead of saying she was thinking about something.

"You're rearranging again," he said.

"I am optimizing workspace."

"You optimized it Monday."

She stopped. Set down the third-generation regeneration module — smaller than the first prototype by a factor of six — and looked at him with the directness eight months hadn't softened.

"I am uncertain what Book Four requires of me," she said. "Book Three required efficiency under crisis. I do not have a category for a Wednesday that requires nothing — except, apparently, this." She glanced pointedly at how close he'd pulled the stool.

"You know you don't actually have to invent a reason every time you want me standing this close," Warren said, grinning.

"I am aware proximity does not require justification. I continue providing one regardless. I find I enjoy watching you notice that I've provided one."

"That's the single most Seven way I've ever heard anybody admit to flirting."

"I am not opposed to being told that," Seven said, something wicked flickering behind her usual precision.

Warren pulled a stool closer, took her actual hand. "You know what I think," he said. "Dr. Soong built Data to want to be human. Not efficient. Human. I think the secret is being human's mostly just tolerating the Wednesdays that don't require anything. Getting good at those is the hard part." He leaned in slightly. "Getting good at this part seems to be coming pretty naturally, though."

"I do not currently tolerate them well."

"No. But you held my hand six seconds before you noticed you were doing it. That's new."

She looked down at their hands, verified this was true, and found she did not particularly want to let go.

"I will continue attempting to tolerate Wednesdays," she said.

"That's basically the whole assignment, Seven. That's Book Four."

"Yes," Seven agreed, something almost like a smile finding the corner of her mouth. "I have noticed considerable natural aptitude. In both of us."

Chapter 5: Seven Notices Something She Wasn't Looking For

"The most dangerous readings are the ones you almost don't bother taking." — attributed to Seven of Nine, private log

Thursday, June 18, 1998; 1630 Revello Drive, Sunnydale; Afternoon 3:00pm —

Seven had made a habit, since the crisis ended, of running a basic wellness scan on anyone who sat still long enough near her modified tricorder unit — Warren's own tinkering, adapted from salvaged components neither of them had originally built with this purpose in mind — because a genuinely quiet summer had, for the first time, left her with actual leisure to be thorough about the household's health rather than merely its survival. Most days the readings told her nothing she didn't already expect: Dawn's growth curves, Xander's old scar tissue, the ordinary business of human bodies doing what human bodies did.

Joyce's readings, today, told her something she hadn't expected at all.

"Would you mind holding still one moment longer," Seven said, careful to keep her voice entirely level, recalibrating the scan and running it again, because a single anomalous reading was, by her own standards, not yet evidence of anything beyond a need for confirmation.

"Everything okay?" Joyce asked, still smiling, unaware that anything about the last ninety seconds had been unusual.

"I am uncertain yet," Seven said, honest, because she had learned, this year, that honesty served people better than false reassurance, even when the honesty was incomplete. "There is a reading I do not have sufficient context to interpret confidently. It is very likely nothing. I would like your permission to have Warren cross-reference the scan against actual medical equipment, simply to be thorough."

Something in Joyce's easy expression sobered, carefully, the controlled worry of a mother who had spent a year learning not to panic first and ask questions second. "What kind of reading."

"A minor density variance," Seven said, choosing her words with real precision, "in tissue I would not expect to show one, absent a specific cause. I want to stress, clearly, that minor anomalies of this kind are very often benign, artifacts of equipment calibration, or simply within the range of normal variation my database has incomplete data on for a fully human physiology. I do not want you unduly alarmed by an uncertainty I have not yet resolved into an actual finding."

"But you want me to get it checked."

"Yes," Seven said, simply. "I would rather we confirm this is nothing, properly, with actual imaging, than assume it is nothing and be wrong."

Joyce was quiet a moment, something working behind her steady expression. "Where, exactly."

Seven told her, precisely, clinically, because vagueness would have served neither of them, and watched Joyce absorb it with the same careful composure she brought to most things that frightened her.

"Ramesh," Joyce said, finally, decisive. "I'll call him myself, today. Better to know early, whatever it is."

"I concur entirely," Seven said. "I would like to accompany you, if you're amenable. Not to manage the appointment. Simply to ask the questions a physician might not think to explain fully to a layperson, and to translate the answers back into terms that are actually useful rather than merely accurate."

"I'd like that," Joyce said, and reached out, briefly, to squeeze Seven's hand. "Thank you for not waiting to mention it. I know it would've been easier to just assume it was nothing."

"I do not believe in that kind of easier," Seven said. "I believe in the kind of easier that comes from actually knowing, rather than the kind that comes from choosing not to look."

Joyce made the appointment for Monday. Neither of them told anyone else yet, by unspoken mutual agreement — not out of secrecy, but because a reading without a diagnosis attached to it was not yet a fact worth handing to a household that would worry considerably before there was anything concrete to worry about.

Chapter 6: Pooka's Report, Delivered to an Audience of Vampires Who Did Not Ask But Are Absolutely Going to Get One

"Never send a pixie to do a Marine's job, unless the pixie insists, loudly, that she already is one." — attributed to Pooka Bell

Wednesday, June 17, 1998; Crawford Street Manor, Sunnydale; Late Morning 11:20am —

"Board meeting's not till Friday!" Pooka said, standing on the mahogany table itself, invited up by Veronique with the ceremony of a hostess who understood a seven-inch pixie deserved better sightlines than the tablecloth. "But got a message now! Lost Boys sent a runner! Peter Pan wants talk! Big talk! Ambassador talk!" She held up both hands, counting off on fingers that didn't quite land on the right number. "And he wants it somewhere with — um — 'proper old magic and good manners,' that's what the runner said, I don't 'zactly know what that means but I know it means here."

Veronique's eyebrows rose, fractionally. "He wishes to negotiate with us specifically."

"Uh huh! That's the whole message!"

"We have tried getting that insufferable flying child to sit still for eight months," Veronique said.

"He watched the big fight!" Pooka said, bouncing slightly where she stood, pleased to be delivering good news. "The graduation one! Said it was real good fighting, all lined up, everybody knowing their job! I dunno what else he said, First Scout wasn't there, just heard from the runner!"

Veronique considered this a long moment. "Graduation changed his calculus, I expect," she said, slowly, to Blahd rather than to Pooka. "Two hundred heartbeats, a coordinated line holding against something that size, out on the lawn where any reconnaissance could see it. I imagine he watched that and concluded, for the first time in three centuries, that organized beats magic, long-term."

"He's not wrong," Veronique said, quiet.

"Very well," Blahd said. "Crawford Street will host. Full old-world courtesies. And tell him we remember refusing to grow up because growing up looked, from outside, like nothing but loss. Tell him we're prepared to discuss what it looks like from the inside, once he's done it anyway."

Pooka looked at him a second longer than the message required. "That's a good line. I'll deliver it word for word."

"Nine hundred years," Veronique said, warm now, "and you still manage one worth repeating verbatim."

Chapter 7: The Board, Friday, First Meeting of the Summer

"The board stays on the wall." — attributed to Rupert Giles

Friday, June 19, 1998; Giles's apartment, Sunnydale; Evening 7:00pm —

Everyone came — Buffy and Angel on the couch, Faith with her boots on the armchair, Xander and Cordelia by the bookshelf, Willow with a spell book she was holding more than reading, Seven and Warren by the window, Kendra and Zabuto near the door, Joyce with tea, Blahd and Veronique attending a Board meeting in person for the first time, Pooka on the mantel as self-appointed sergeant-at-arms, and Dawn on the floor with her notebook.

"Right," Giles said, and there was something different in how he said it — eight months of right meaning here is the crisis, now meaning, for the first time, here is simply the gathering. "Book Four."

"Already named it," Dawn said. "It's in the notebook."

"Very well." Giles cleaned his glasses. "First matter — Peter Pan's requested formal talks, Crawford Street hosting, Friday next."

"Since when do we do ambassador to ambassador," Faith said.

"Since eight months ago, when a robot from 2033 asked for the same thing at that table," Veronique said, "and it proved the best decision this house made in nine centuries."

"Second matter." Giles looked to Zabuto. "Perhaps you'd best tell them yourselves."

Zabuto wheeled himself forward slightly, the letter — heavier stock than Council correspondence usually ran — in his hand. "Kendra wrote the Council last month, honestly, about doctrine. I've had a reply. The eighteen-month pilot review has been suspended, pending what they call 'further evaluation of irregular field conditions.'" He paused. "Quentin Travers — the Council's actual head — will arrive in Sunnydale personally, within the fortnight, to conduct that evaluation himself."

The room went quiet in the particular way it did when a name landed that not everyone recognized but everyone could tell mattered.

"He'll want to see everything," Zabuto said. "The Board. The Slayers working together rather than isolated. The vampires we've allied with. The machine we integrated rather than destroyed." He set the letter down. "I don't believe he's coming to be persuaded. I believe he's coming to find evidence confirming what he already fears. But we will meet him with nothing but the truth. That is the only response I intend to offer him."

"Then that's what we give him," Buffy said. "The whole complicated thing, not a highlight reel."

Giles looked around the room. "Friday meeting adjourned. Everyone think on it over the weekend. We'll decide, properly, Thursday, how we wish to be seen."

Dawn wrote it down. June 19th, 1998. Somebody's coming to grade us. I think Book Four's first real chapter starts with him.

Chapter 8: Faith Has Opinions About Being Judged

"Trust takes years to build, seconds to break, and forever to repair." — attributed to unknown

Saturday, June 20, 1998; the Bronze, Sunnydale; Night 9:40pm —

"So some old British dude's flying in to grade our homework," Faith said, around a mouthful of fries, irreverence deployed the way she always deployed it over something that actually worried her.

"That's a reduction," Buffy said, "but not inaccurate."

"I know his type before I ever met you people," Faith said, harder now. "Guys with a clipboard who decide, real quick, whether you're worth the paperwork. Four years of foster care before the Council ever found me, B. I know how fast that decision gets made. Usually before you've said a fucking word in your own defense."

"This is different."

"Maybe. Or he looks at me and Kendra alive and doing okay, looks at a robot reading novels, looks at vampires running a legit nightclub, and decides the whole thing's exactly the unsupervised chaos his doctrine exists to shut down."

"Then we make him see it clearly," Buffy said. "Not perform it. See it."

Faith pushed her fries around, some of the hardness easing into something more tired. "Hope you're right. First time in maybe ever I can say I like my life, B. Don't love some guy with a clipboard getting a vote on whether I keep it."

"You keep it," Buffy said, steady. "Whatever he decides, that part isn't his to take back. You were never on probation with us."

Faith looked at her a long moment, something working through before settling into gratitude she wasn't ready to say aloud. "Yeah," she said instead. "Still bringing my knives to the meeting though. Old habits." She smirked, some of her usual edge coming back now that the hard part of the conversation was over. "Also gonna wear the good jeans. If English's gonna judge me, he can judge me looking hot while he does it."

"That's not really how Council evaluations work, Faith."

"Everything works better if you look hot doing it, B. That's just science."

"Faith."

"Kidding. Mostly."

Chapter 10: What the TX Understands About Being Evaluated

"To be tested by someone who has already decided the outcome is not evaluation. It is theater." — attributed to a Tech-Comm field manual, 2031

Sunday, June 21, 1998; Giles's apartment, Sunnydale; Evening 8:15pm —

Giles told it plainly, and the TX received it very still, in the register that had stopped being purely tactical months ago.

"He will want to evaluate me specifically."

"Almost certainly," Giles said. "You're the single most unprecedented element he'll find here."

"I have catalogued four hundred and twelve instances of ordinary continuation since November. I do not know how to present that as evidence to a man whose framework may have no category for it."

Giles removed his glasses — the tell the TX had catalogued precisely. "I won't pretend there's no risk. But I told Zabuto this afternoon what I'll tell you now. We show him precisely what eight months actually built, honestly, and trust the truth stands on its own merits better than any performance."

"And if it doesn't."

The TX was silent eleven seconds. "I would like to meet him honestly. I have grown unwilling to perform something smaller than what I actually am."

"Around February, I should think," Giles said. "Roughly when you started reading over my shoulder and pretending you weren't."

"I was cataloguing your reading habits."

"Of course you were."

Chapter 11: The Word Nobody Wants to Say First

"A diagnosis is not a sentence. It is simply the first accurate sentence in what comes next." — attributed to Dr. Ramesh

Monday, June 22, 1998; Sunnydale Memorial Hospital, Sunnydale; Afternoon 2:00pm —

The imaging took the better part of an hour, and Seven sat in the waiting room unusually still, running through medical terminology in her head so she'd be ready to translate rather than simply witness whatever Ramesh eventually said.

He came out himself, rather than sending a nurse, which Joyce understood immediately as its own kind of information.

"Mrs. Summers." He sat across from her, unhurried, the careful pacing of a man who had delivered this kind of news enough times to know that rushing it never actually helped anyone receive it better. "We found something. I want to walk you through it properly, and I want you to ask every question you have, because I'd rather this conversation take an hour than have you leave with half an answer."

"Just say it," Joyce said, steady, though her hand had found Seven's without her seeming to notice she'd done it.

"A meningioma," Ramesh said. "A tumor, benign in the great majority of cases we see, growing on the tissue surrounding your brain rather than within it. Given the size we're seeing — and I want to stress, this is a small one — and given we've caught it at a stage most patients never get caught at, your prognosis, assuming it behaves the way the overwhelming majority of these do, is very good."

"Assuming."

"Assuming," Ramesh agreed, honest. "I won't promise you certainty I don't have. What I can promise you is that early detection is, without exaggeration, the single most important variable in an outcome like this, and you have that variable working entirely in your favor, which is more than most of my patients get."

Seven had been quiet through this, absorbing it with real focus, and now she spoke, careful. "What are the treatment options."

"Surgical removal, most likely, given the location and size — a resection, done properly, has a good success rate for tumors presenting this way, though any procedure at that specific location carries genuine risk I won't minimize. There may be a course of radiation afterward, precautionary, depending on what we find."

"There is another option," Seven said, slowly, something working behind her careful composure. "I would like to propose it, though I want to be honest that it has not been attempted on human tissue before, and I do not offer it lightly."

Ramesh looked at her, curious rather than dismissive, which was, Joyce had learned this past year, precisely the response that separated good doctors from merely competent ones. "I'm listening."

"Nanoprobes," Seven said. "Borg technology, adapted — the same category of technology underlying the regeneration modules I've built for the TX and, more recently, for Cassidy. Properly programmed, they are capable of identifying and breaking down abnormal cellular structures with a precision no scalpel could match, without the trauma of an incision, without the recovery period surgery requires, and without disturbing healthy tissue immediately adjacent to the growth." She was quiet a moment. "I would need to test the calibration extensively before I would consider it safe to use on Joyce specifically. I do not offer this as a shortcut around genuine caution. I offer it because I believe, given sufficient preparation, it may be safer than opening her skull."

Ramesh considered this for a long moment, visibly weighing whether to trust something outside his own training. "You're proposing an approach I have no way to independently verify."

"I understand that limitation," Seven said. "I would ask that you observe every stage of the calibration and testing process, and that we proceed only if you are satisfied the risk profile is favorable compared to surgical alternatives. I do not ask you to trust the technology blindly. I ask you to evaluate it properly, the same way you would evaluate any treatment option, and reach your own conclusion."

"How long would calibration take."

"Two to three weeks, done properly. I would not rush it for any reason, including urgency, because rushing this specific process is precisely how it becomes dangerous rather than precise."

Joyce looked between them, something steadying in her chest at the quality of the conversation — two people who both, evidently, cared more about getting this right than about being the one whose method won. "What would you actually do, Seven. Talk me through it like I don't already understand the technology, because I don't."

"The nanoprobes would be introduced through a simple injection, minimally invasive, and guided to the site using imaging Ramesh already has. Once positioned, they identify abnormal cellular structures specifically — programmed to recognize precisely the boundaries pathology has already mapped — and break them down at the cellular level, over a period of hours rather than instantly, allowing your body's own systems to process and clear the resulting material naturally. There is no incision. There is no surgical recovery. There is, so far as I can currently model it, less risk of complication than the alternative." She met Joyce's eyes directly. "I would not propose this if I were not confident in it. I have spent this past year learning the difference between confidence and hope, and I do not currently believe I am confusing the two."

"I have two daughters," Joyce said again, quieter this time.

"I know," Seven said. "That is precisely why I am being as careful as I am being, rather than simply offering the technology because it exists."

Ramesh was quiet a long moment. "I want to observe every test," he said, finally. "Every single one. And I reserve the right to insist on the conventional approach if I see anything that concerns me at any stage."

"I would expect nothing less," Seven said. "And I would welcome it."

"Then," Ramesh said, something cautiously hopeful entering his own careful bedside manner, "let's find out together whether this is as good as it sounds. Mrs. Summers, I'd like your genuine consent understood as provisional — given only once we've both watched this technology prove itself, not before."

"Understood," Joyce said, and found, to her own quiet surprise, that some of the fear in her chest had already eased, simply from watching two people take this seriously enough to disagree about method rather than pretend certainty neither of them fully had yet.

"I would like to be present for every stage of this," Seven said. "Not merely the treatment itself. The calibration, the testing, all of it. I want you to see exactly how carefully this is being done, so that whatever confidence you eventually have in it is earned rather than simply offered."

"I'd like that very much," Joyce said, and squeezed her hand properly this time. "Thank you. For not waiting. For any of it."

"I do not believe in waiting," Seven said, "when waiting is the more dangerous option. I also do not believe in rushing precision work simply because waiting is uncomfortable. I intend to hold both of those beliefs at once, for as long as this takes to do properly."

"The truth does not require your permission to be sufficient." — attributed to Rupert Giles

"Travers arrives Sunday," Giles said, once everyone had settled, the room holding a taut quiet, everyone having each, separately, arrived at the same conclusion this week. Dawn wrote it down. June 25th, 1998. Everyone decided the same thing separately before we said it out loud. I think that means it's true.

Chapter 12: Seven Runs the Numbers on Her Own Risk

"Fear is the mind-killer." — Frank Herbert, Dune

Friday, June 26, 1998; the Lab, Sunnydale MHMR sub-basement; Afternoon 3:00pm —

A man near the back rose, then, who Pemberton had been watching carefully all afternoon without appearing to — Roger Wyndam-Pryce, composed as ever, though something in the set of his jaw suggested the composure was costing him more today than usual.

"No," Travers said, dry. "Some details are better left to arrive with the actual implementation, rather than the announcement. Let Kendra Young have her unclouded moment first.
