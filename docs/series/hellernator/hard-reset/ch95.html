Xander's First Official Day

"A title does not change what you were already doing. It only ensures everyone else takes it exactly as seriously as it deserved." — attributed to Michaela Reeves

Tuesday, August 18, 1998; Black Company field office, Sunnydale; Morning 9:00am —

The office they'd given him wasn't much — a desk, a phone line, a whiteboard already half-covered in Michaela's own shorthand — but Xander stood in the doorway a moment before actually stepping inside, disbelieving, a boy who'd spent a year doing this exact job for free suddenly confronting the fact that it now had his name on the door.

"You gonna stand there all day," Michaela said, passing with a folder, "or you gonna come in and start earning that salary?"

"Just letting it sink in a second." Xander stepped inside, ran a hand along the edge of the desk. "This is real."

"It's real. Congratulations, Coordinator Harris." She set the folder down on his desk — an actual folder, actual letterhead, BOARD LOGISTICS COORDINATOR printed under his name in a font that made something in his chest go tight in a way two hundred heartbeats of an Ascension somehow hadn't quite managed. "First task. Formalize the rotation schedule for quiet protection detail on the TX's library sessions. You've been doing this informally for months. Now you get to do it with an actual budget line."

Xander sat down at the desk, pulled a legal pad toward him — habit, comfort, the same tool he'd always used — and found himself, for the first time, writing at the top of the page not just a task list but an actual title: Logistics — Week One — X. Harris, Coordinator.

"Feels different," he admitted, "writing it down with the actual title attached."

"That's kind of the whole point of a title," Michaela said, not unkindly. "It doesn't change what you were already doing. It just makes sure everybody else has to take it exactly as seriously as it already deserved."

Tuesday, August 18, 1998; Black Company field office, Sunnydale; Night 9:30pm —

The paperwork packet sat on his desk long after everyone else had gone home, and Xander had made it through the tax forms, the direct deposit authorization, and two full pages of standard employment boilerplate before he hit the line that stopped him cold.

Emergency Contact (Name, Relationship, Phone):

He picked up the pen. Set it back down. Picked it up again.

"Ha," he said, out loud, to an empty office, in the tone of someone testing whether a joke still worked if there was nobody around to laugh at it. "Guess I'll just, uh. Guess I'll put Giles. Giles is basically — Giles counts. Giles is emergency-contact-adjacent. That's a totally normal thing to write down, nothing weird about a seventeen-year-old's emergency contact being his ex-librarian, very standard."

He wrote Rupert Giles — family friend in the blank, and his handwriting came out wrong, cramped, nothing like his usual scrawl, and he stared at it for a while before he let himself actually look at why.

"It's fine," he told the empty office, louder this time, like volume might convince the room. "It's a form. It's a stupid form. Everybody's got an emergency contact, it's not a whole thing, I'm not gonna—"

He didn't finish the sentence. His throat had closed around it somewhere in the middle, without his permission, and he set the pen down again, harder this time, and pressed the heels of both hands against his eyes like that might hold whatever this was back where it belonged.

It didn't.

By the time Cordelia found him — she'd come by to walk him home, same as most nights now, and let herself in when he didn't answer the front bell — he had his forehead down against the desk, shoulders shaking in a way he was very obviously trying to fold small enough that maybe it wouldn't count, and the emergency contact line sat there in front of him, half-filled, mocking him with its own ordinary bureaucratic indifference.

"Hey." She didn't make it a production. Pulled the second chair around, sat close, put a hand flat between his shoulder blades, steady and certain, exactly how she'd learned, two weeks ago, this kind of thing wanted to be met. "Talk to me."

"It's stupid," he said, muffled, still not lifting his head. "It's a form, Cor. It's the dumbest possible thing to fall apart over. I held two hundred heartbeats of timing during an actual Ascension without losing it once, and a form is what gets me."

"Doesn't sound stupid to me. What's the form asking."

He turned the packet toward her without lifting his head the rest of the way, one finger tapping the line, and she read it, and understood immediately, and didn't say anything false or easy about it.

"There's nobody to put there," Xander said, and now that the first sentence was out, the rest came apart faster than he could organize it. "That's the whole thing. That's the stupid, tiny, whole entire thing. Everybody in this building's got a mom or a dad they can just — write down. Automatically. Doesn't even cost them a thought. And I sat here for ten minutes trying to figure out who's supposed to go on my line, and the actual honest answer is nobody, there's nobody, there hasn't been anybody since October, and I've spent this whole year being so — so fine about it, so useful, so busy running everyone else's logistics that I never had to sit still long enough to fill out my own paperwork, and tonight I finally had to, and it turns out I don't actually have an answer, I just have a blank line and Giles's name written in wrong, and I don't—"

His voice actually broke on that last word, cracked clean down the middle, and the tears that followed weren't the composed kind, weren't anything he could have performed even if some part of him had wanted to; they arrived rough and embarrassing and out of his control, the unguarded wreck of someone who had held a enormous thing together for ten months through sheer stubborn refusal to look at it directly, and had simply, finally, run out of stubbornness for tonight.

Cordelia didn't tell him it was okay. She'd learned, recently, exactly how useless that particular sentence was in a moment like this one. She just moved her chair closer, wrapped both arms around him properly, and let him fall apart against her shoulder for as long as it took, which turned out to be considerably longer than either of them would have guessed, ten months of very carefully not doing this arriving all at once, the way held things always did, eventually, when they finally got permission.

"I got you," she said, quiet, into his hair, the words less complicated than either of them wanted to explain to anyone else, ever. "Cry it out, Xander. I promise the world's not gonna end because Coordinator Harris had a rough Tuesday. I'll fill out your stupid form myself, if it comes to that."

"You can't be my emergency contact," he said, wet, half-laughing despite himself, some of the wreck already easing at the sheer absurdity of her offering. "That's — Cor, that's a whole thing, putting your girlfriend as—"

"Watch me," Cordelia said, fierce, certain. "I'd like to see the form that tells me I'm not allowed to be the person they call." She pulled back just far enough to look at him properly, wiped at his face with her sleeve without asking permission, the way you'd tend to something you had absolutely no intention of being delicate about. "Give me the pen."

He gave her the pen. She filled in the line herself, careful, deliberate, her own handwriting steady in a way his hadn't managed to be — Cordelia Chase, partner — and set it down between them like she'd just settled something larger than a form.

"There," she said. "Done. You're not walking around with a blank line anymore. Not on my watch."

Xander looked at her handwriting a long moment, ten months of very carefully not doing this finally arriving all at once, and let it.

"Thanks," he said, rough, entirely sincere. "For not making it a bigger deal than it needed to be. And also for making it exactly as big a deal as it actually was."

"That's kind of the whole job," Cordelia said, echoing, without quite meaning to, something he'd said to her two weeks ago in this same building. "Not fixing it. Not making it smaller. Just — not alone."
