Scope

Shyla's first week in the Mountain was a lesson in watching somebody recalibrate.

Not struggle — struggle was the wrong word entirely for what Shyla did, since struggle implied resistance, and Shyla's relationship with difficulty was more like a river's relationship with terrain: she just found the path through, no drama, no complaint, adjusting course whenever the course needed adjusting. What she was actually doing was processing, at speed, the way somebody does who's survived two years alone on a culled planet and learned that slow processing gets you killed.

Buffy watched her do it and recognized the whole mechanism and mostly stayed out of the way, because the other thing she'd learned about fast processors was that they needed space to work in, and company was something they chose, not something they needed.

She made herself available. That was different from hovering.

The distinction mattered.

On the third day Shyla found the gym on level nineteen at 0515, fifteen minutes after Buffy had gotten there, and stood in the doorway sizing up the space with that same evaluating look she brought to every new room.

Buffy was on the heavy bag. She kept going.

Shyla came in, found a corner, and started into a movement sequence Buffy watched out of the corner of her eye without ever quite looking straight at it, since looking straight at it would've been a kind of pressure, and Shyla didn't need pressure — she needed to move in a space where somebody else was moving too, without it becoming a whole thing.

The sequence was interesting. Not a martial art Buffy recognized, which meant it was native to P2X-416, or maybe just native to Shyla — built up over years of practical necessity instead of inherited from a tradition. Economical, the way things get economical when you can't afford to waste anything, every movement doing two or three jobs at once, carrying the same quality all physical work carried when it came from the same source Buffy's did.

The Slayer.

The Slayer in her recognized it. That was really the only way to put it. Looked at what Shyla was doing and recognized it the way you'd recognize your own reflection.

Shyla finished the sequence and stood quiet a moment. Then, without looking over: "You were watching."

"In my peripheral," Buffy said.

"Still watching."

"Yeah," Buffy said. "I wanted to see what you do."

Shyla turned. Not annoyed, more considering. "Why."

"Because I've been doing this alone a long time," Buffy said. "And I've never once seen somebody else do what I do. From the inside. It's different watching it from outside."

Shyla held her gaze. "What does it look like."

"Like what I do when I've stripped everything else away," Buffy said. "The thing underneath all the training."

Shyla looked at the space between them. "On my world," she said, "there is no name for what we are. I did not have one. I only knew I was different, and that the difference kept me alive when it should not have."

"The name doesn't change the thing," Buffy said. "The thing was always there."

"No," Shyla agreed. "But the name means there are others who understand it. That is not nothing."

"No," Buffy said. "It's really not."

Shyla looked at the heavy bag. "Show me what you do," she said.

Buffy showed her.

By the end of the first week Shyla had a routine of her own going, which Buffy noted as a good sign, since routine was infrastructure, and infrastructure was what you built when you planned to stick around.

Hammond assigned her a formal orientation, a modified version of what new personnel usually went through, adapted for somebody who wasn't from Earth, had no existing military framework, and spoke Standard as a learned language rather than a native one. Carter handled the technical side. Daniel handled the historical and anthropological context, which he approached with the thorough enthusiasm of a man who'd found his ideal subject and was going to learn everything about her world's history whether or not she'd actually asked him to.

Shyla bore all of it with total equanimity, correcting him occasionally, which he received with the delight of a man who'd just been handed primary sources.

Teal'c handled security orientation, which on paper meant teaching her the Mountain's protocols, and in practice meant two Jaffa-adjacent people of similar vintage sitting in rooms together not saying much, arriving at some kind of mutual understanding without needing a single word for it. Buffy watched that from a distance and found it satisfying, the way things are satisfying when they land somewhere you didn't expect but that makes total sense in hindsight.

O'Neill's contribution to Shyla's integration ran characteristically indirect. He showed up wherever she was, said practical things, looped her into operational briefings at whatever level she could access, and handled the mountain of paperwork with Davis with the grimace he saved for necessary administrative tasks he intended to do properly no matter how he felt about them.

On the fourth day he also had a conversation with Shyla that Buffy wasn't part of and never asked about, and afterward Shyla's posture in shared spaces shifted, just slightly, from assessed-and-provisional into something not quite settled but close.

She asked Shyla about it once, sideways.

Shyla said, "He told me my world would be remembered. That the names of the people from it would go into the record. That he had done this before, for others, and that he would do it for mine."

Buffy thought about that. About a colonel who'd lost people in ways she only knew the shape of, not the specifics — who'd apparently made a practice out of remembering them properly — and who'd extended that practice, unasked, to somebody he'd known all of four days.

"Yeah," she said. "That's him."

"He is a good commanding officer," Shyla said.

"He is," Buffy agreed.

"He is also," Shyla said, with the precision of somebody choosing words carefully, "something more than that to you."

Buffy looked at her.

Shyla held her gaze with the same equanimity she brought to just about everything. "I am not asking about it," she said. "I am observing it. There is a difference."

"There is," Buffy said.

"On my world," Shyla said, "the relationship between somebody who does what we do and the person who chooses to stand closest to them — it is considered a specific kind of thing. It has a word."

Buffy waited.

"The word translates approximately as anchor," Shyla said. "The person who keeps you attached to the world when the work tries to pull you out of it." She looked around at the corridor, at the Mountain around them. "It is considered important. Not necessarily romantic. Not necessarily familial. Its own category."

Buffy thought about Giles. About who he'd been before the Cruciamentum and imperfectly after, and what losing him had cost her. About O'Neill and Saturday dinners and a chair pulled up beside a hospital bed, a hockey stick in the corner, and that flat certain voice saying I need enough to keep you safe, that part isn't negotiable.

"Yeah," she said. "We don't have a word for it in English. But yeah."

Shyla nodded. "Good," she said. "People who do what we do need an anchor. Without one, the work takes everything eventually."

She said it plainly, no weight to it, the way she said things she'd learned firsthand instead of in theory, and Buffy took it that way too — as a fact — and carried it with her down the corridor and into the rest of her day.

The second gate address on Daniel's priority list was P3X-774, which had the advantage of sitting outside Goa'uld-controlled space and the disadvantage of being a world the SGC had already visited once, briefly, three years back, logged as inhabited and non-hostile and never followed up on, because three years ago the SGC had bigger fires burning.

The survivor anomaly in that old mission report was a single line. Local male, approximate age forty, survived a structural collapse that killed six other people. Physiological note flagged, never followed up.

Daniel cross-referenced the address and confirmed the match to the chamber notation within two days of finishing the full transcription, and produced a briefing document forty pages shorter than his P4X-639 analysis, which made it a whole lot more accessible to anyone who didn't read six languages.

Hammond approved the mission on a Thursday. They went through Friday.

~~~LELB~~~

P3X-774 was green in a way that meant abundance, not just vegetation — the kind of green that comes from water and deep soil and things growing because they wanted to, not just because conditions happened to be tolerable. The gate stood in a clearing at the edge of what looked like a managed forest, not wild but not exactly farmland either, the kind of land stewardship you only see from a civilization that's stayed put long enough to plan in generations.

Buffy came through the gate, checked her nine and three, and felt the planet.

Quieter than Earth. Not less active, not less alive — just quieter, the way places without a hellmouth were quieter, lower ambient dimensional pressure, a thicker membrane. She'd started noticing this on every gate world she'd been to, this variation in what the Slayer could feel through the wall, and she'd started building a mental taxonomy of it, something she was going to write up for Hammond once she had enough data points to actually say something useful.

P3X-774 was thick. Stable. Whatever lived here had lived here a long time without much disruption.

"Clear," she said.

"Beautiful," Daniel said, staring at the forest.

"Clear," Carter confirmed.

"Move out," O'Neill said.

The settlement sat three kilometers out from the gate, down a path clearly maintained, showing the kind of foot traffic that meant regular use. They covered it in forty minutes, a pace that let Buffy scan ahead, let Carter run her instruments, let Daniel stare at the trees, which were doing something architecturally interesting with their root systems, a fact he murmured into his recorder the whole way.

The settlement turned out bigger than she'd expected from the mission report. Several hundred people, maybe more, spread across a cluster of structures showing multiple generations of construction — older materials at the center, newer ones toward the edges, the physical archaeology of a community that had been building itself up for a long, long time.

They were met at the settlement's edge by a woman with the bearing of somebody used to being the one who met strangers — either a formal role or a personal one — who looked over the four of them with the alert attention of somebody assessing, not welcoming.

"Travelers," she said. Not Standard, but Daniel had it, which Buffy had known he would, since Daniel had most things. "From the ring."

"Yes," Daniel said. "We mean no harm. We've come looking for someone."

The woman looked at them steadily. "Who."

"A man," Daniel said. "Three years ago there was a structural collapse. He survived when others did not."

Something shifted in the woman's face. Not alarm. A different kind of recognition. She looked at Buffy, the way people sometimes looked at Buffy when the Slayer was visible in her somehow, when whatever it was the Slayer put off registered somewhere that didn't have a name for it.

"Erran," she said. "You are here for Erran."

"Yes," Buffy said.

The woman studied her another second. Then turned. "Come."

~~~LELB~~~

Erran was forty-three years old and large in the way some people are large — not just physically, but in the amount of room they take up just by being in it — and he had that quality Buffy had started calling the Slayer signature. Readiness without aggression. Awareness without anxiety. A body organized around a purpose whether or not the mind bothered naming the purpose.

He was working when they found him, doing something practical with tools she didn't need to identify, and he looked up at them with the assessing calm of somebody who'd learned to assess fast, long enough ago that the speed of it had become invisible.

He looked at Buffy.

She looked at him.

The Slayer recognized the Slayer. That was the only way to put it, and it happened in about a second, and she could tell from the way his eyes changed that it happened for him too.

"You know what you are," she said.

"I have known since I was young," he said. His Standard was better than Shyla's, more practiced, which suggested a lot more contact with gate travelers over the years. "My grandmother told me. Her grandmother told her." He held her eyes steadily. "It has always been this way in my family. One in each generation who carries it."

Buffy went still.

"One in each generation," she said.

"Sometimes skipping a generation," he said. "But always present. Always one." He set his tools down. "You did not know this was possible."

"No," she said.

"On your world the line is different."

"One at a time," she said. "One person, worldwide, until she dies and it passes on."

Erran took that in. "Here it runs in a family line," he said. "Has, for as long as the records go back. I am the current one." He glanced at Daniel, at Carter, at O'Neill, then back to Buffy. "You have come because of something in the ring network."

"There's a record," she said. "On another world. Every place the line's been active, recorded. You're on the list."

"A record," he said. "Old?"

"Older than the Ancients."

Erran went quiet a moment. He had the quality of a man who found this significant and was letting it be exactly as significant as it deserved, rather than performing some reaction to it. "Then somebody was keeping track," he said.

"Yes," she said. "We're still working out who and why."

He nodded slowly. "What do you need from me."

"Information," she said. "How it works here. What the line protects against. What you know about its history. And I want you to know we exist. That there are others. In case it matters someday."

"It matters now," he said. "Knowing is not nothing."

"No," she said. "It's really not."

He looked at her a moment, weighing something. Then: "You have lost someone. Recently. Not recently. Both."

Not quite a question. More an observation — the kind Slayer-sense sometimes produced, not about physical threat, just about the person standing in front of you.

"Yes," she said.

"The line does that," he said, no sentimentality in it at all. "It takes people. Not just the one who carries it. The people around them too." He looked down at his tools on the workbench. "My wife understood what I was. She chose to stay anyway. She died three years ago, in the collapse." A pause. "Not in the collapse. She died getting others out. She wasn't the one with the line. She was just brave."

Buffy held onto that.

"I'm sorry," she said.

"Yes," he said. "So am I." He looked back up. "The people with you. They know what you are."

"Yes."

"They chose to stay anyway."

"Yes."

He looked over at O'Neill, who'd been standing quietly to her right since they'd walked in, doing the thing he did where he was present and unobtrusive at the same time, paying attention to everything. O'Neill looked right back at Erran with the expression of a man being sized up and declining to perform for the occasion.

"Good," Erran said. He looked back at Buffy. "Sit down. I will tell you what I know. It will take some time."

"We have time," Buffy said.

She sat, and Erran sat across from her, and he started talking, and Daniel's recorder was running, and outside the window the green world of P3X-774 went right on doing what it had been doing for a long time — accumulating itself, generation by generation, in the patient way of things that had already decided to stick around.

Four hours, and Erran talked the way he did everything else: no hurry, no waste.

He started with the founding record — a woman named Kessa, six generations back, who'd carried the line alone, the way Buffy understood it, a single vessel and nothing else. Then Kessa had died protecting the settlement from something Erran's family called only the burning, and the line hadn't ended. It had split.

"Split," Buffy said. "Not passed. Split."

"Passed to two of her children in the same hour," Erran said. "Not equally. My family's records call it a saturation." He tried the Standard word Daniel offered, discarded it, tried a different one. "Flooded. More than one vessel could hold, so it filled two."

Daniel's recorder stayed a steady red the whole time. Carter had given up on paper notes twenty minutes in and switched over to a branching diagram, half family tree, half circuit schematic.

"Does it always split at two," Carter asked.

"Sometimes three," Erran said. "Once, in my grandmother's grandmother's time, four, all in one birth season. Not all of them lived to adulthood." He said it the way he said everything difficult — flat, a fact laid down and left there, no softening. "The line does not care how many vessels it fills. Only that it is filled."

Buffy sat with that a while. One Slayer, alone, dying and passing to the next single girl, an unbroken chain running since before the Watchers even had a name for it. Here, that same weight, poured out wider. Poured onto people who actually got to know each other. Who got to share the thing instead of carrying it alone until it killed them and the cycle started over.

"That doesn't sound worse," she said finally.

"It is not worse," Erran agreed. "It is not better either. Ask my cousin Petra how it felt, waking up at eleven years old with the line already inside her. No choosing. No growing into it the way I did." His face didn't move. "Ask my brother, who never got it at all, and spent forty years certain the line had chosen wrong."

That landed exactly the way he'd meant it to.

They kept going. Erran walked them through three more generations of the family record — who'd carried it, who'd died, who'd lived long enough to teach the next one — and somewhere in the second hour Buffy stopped hearing it as data and started hearing it for what it actually was: an entire family's history, organized around the one thing that kept killing some of them and saving the rest, handed over to strangers because those strangers had shown up with proof it hadn't all been random after all.

By the fourth hour Erran's voice had gone rough at the edges, Daniel had refilled the water pitcher twice, and the light on P3X-774 outside had slid from full gold afternoon into the long slant of evening.

"That is what I know," Erran said finally, both hands wrapped around a cup he hadn't touched in an hour. "The saturation. The family line. What it costs, and what it does not." He looked at Buffy, and there was something in it now that hadn't been there at the start — something earned across four hours of talking. "I have never told all of it to anyone outside the family. Not even those we trade with."

"Why now," Buffy said.

"Because you are also the line," Erran said. "And you asked as though it mattered whether I said yes."

Daniel's notes ran to sixty-three pages before he finished transcribing. Carter had three whole threads flagged as scientifically significant before they were even back through the gate, and she kept working them out loud in the alkesh the whole way home, the kind of unstoppable momentum you get from somebody just handed a stack of new data.

"That's not supposed to be possible," Buffy said, in the alkesh, still turning it over.

"According to what framework," Daniel said.

"According to everything I know about how the line works."

"Which is based entirely on how it works on Earth," Daniel said. "One world. One expression. We've now got three data points — Earth, P2X-416, P3X-774 — and they're all different." He checked his notes. "Earth: one at a time, global, dies and passes on. P2X-416: Shyla's situation, single individual, no succession mechanism we can see, she was just activated and what happens next is still an open question. P3X-774: familial transmission, potentially multiple active individuals at once, saturation origin."

"Three different expressions," Carter said. "Which suggests the underlying mechanism's a lot more flexible than one single operational mode. Like different implementations running off the same base code."

"If the base code's older than the Ancients," Daniel said, "and it's been running across the galaxy that whole time, you'd expect variation. Genetic drift. Environmental adaptation. Same way human languages branch off from a common ancestor."

Buffy looked out the viewport at the stars. At the gate address on the tablet in her lap. The next one on the list. And the next. And the next.

"Every one's going to be different," she said.

"Almost certainly," Daniel said.

"Which means we can't walk in with assumptions," she said. "Every contact's its own first contact."

"Yes," Daniel said. "Which is actually—"

"Exciting," Carter said.

"I was going to say scientifically unprecedented," Daniel said, "but sure, also exciting."

O'Neill, listening from the pilot's compartment doorway with his arms crossed, said, "It also means we need a different protocol for each one. Can't run these off one playbook."

"No sir," Buffy said.

"Which means more planning time between contacts." He looked at her. "Which you're going to be impatient about."

"I'm not impatient," she said.

"Cali."

"I'm aware of the time pressure," she said. "That's different from impatient."

"It's not that different."

"It's slightly different."

He looked at her a second. Then at Daniel. "Is it slightly different."

"Staying out of this one," Daniel said.

O'Neill looked at Carter.

"It's meaningfully different, sir," Carter said carefully, threading her way through it. "Impatient implies not enough regard for preparation. She's not proposing we skip preparation. She's proposing we compress the timeline between contacts."

"That's literally what impatient means," O'Neill said.

"It really isn't, sir," Carter said.

O'Neill looked like he was considering arguing the point and then decided the argument wasn't worth the investment. He looked back at Buffy. "We'll set a working rhythm. Contact mission, debrief and documentation, minimum two weeks of analysis before the next one. That's the baseline. If there's real time pressure on a specific address, we revisit."

"Yes sir," she said.

"And you tell me if you're feeling time pressure on a specific address."

"I will."

He headed back to the cockpit. Carter gave Buffy the look she used when she found something funny and had decided not to fully show it.

"Slightly different," Buffy said.

"Meaningfully," Carter said, and went back to her data.

The debrief on P3X-774 produced a document Hammond read overnight, which resulted in a senior staff meeting the next morning — the first one Buffy had been included in as a matter of course instead of by special invitation, which she noticed, and filed accordingly.

The meeting was Hammond, O'Neill, Carter, Daniel, Teal'c, Shyla, and Buffy — an unusual mix, one Hammond had clearly thought through before calling, since the mix itself said something about how he was categorizing what they'd brought back from P3X-774.

"Dr. Jackson's documentation of the P3X-774 contact," Hammond said, "combined with the existing data from P4X-639 and the Shyla situation, suggests we're looking at something a good deal larger than an individual asset with unusual capabilities." He looked around the table. "What we appear to be looking at is a galactic-scale phenomenon with a history predating our most advanced known civilization, and an operational scope we haven't mapped yet. I want to be precise about what this means for this command."

He looked at Buffy.

"Cadet Summers came to this command because Colonel O'Neill identified her as an exceptional officer candidate with relevant capabilities for our operational environment," Hammond said. "That assessment was correct, as far as it went. What we know now is that it didn't go far enough." He held her eyes. "You're not an asset with unusual capabilities, Cadet. You're Earth's expression of something that's been operating in this galaxy since before the gate network even existed. That has real implications for how this command treats what you carry, and what we ask you to do with it."

Buffy waited.

"Specifically," Hammond said, "I'm not treating these contact missions as a side project to your regular SG-1 duties. I'm reclassifying them as a primary mission objective for this command, same operational priority as the Goa'uld threat." He looked at O'Neill. "I want a dedicated mission planning structure. Not ad hoc. A formal framework."

"Already drafted," O'Neill said. "Davis can help on the inter-agency side. The Tok'ra need briefing on what we're doing, since at least two of the remaining addresses fall in their operational territory."

"I'll handle the Tok'ra brief myself," Hammond said. He looked at Shyla. "Your integration status. How are you finding the Mountain?"

Shyla had that quality she got in formal settings, applying precision to her words because the words mattered. "It is not my world," she said. "But it is not hostile. The people here have been—" she paused, choosing the word — "correct. In how they have treated what I am."

Hammond nodded. "I want to formalize your status. You can't be a civilian consultant in the conventional sense, since the conventional sense doesn't account for your situation. I'm proposing a special advisor designation — full access, compensation, protection under this command's classification structure." He looked at her. "It requires your agreement."

Shyla looked at the table a moment. Then at Buffy, which Buffy took as a question and answered with a small nod.

"Yes," Shyla said. "I agree."

Hammond nodded. "One more item." He looked around the room. "The second organization's leadership is in federal custody. The immediate institutional threat to this program is resolved. What's not resolved is the bigger question — what happens when this program's existence becomes impossible to keep contained." He looked at Buffy. "You've been operating in a classified environment. The line's been classified by institutional default for decades. At some point the scale of what we're doing is going to make that containment impractical."

"I know," Buffy said.

"I want your thoughts on the right disclosure framework. Not now. Over the next month. This is a decision we make together." He looked at O'Neill. "The three of us. And Dr. Jackson and Major Carter, since their judgment matters here too."

"And Shyla," Buffy said.

Hammond looked at her.

"Shyla's the other expression of the line we've got direct access to," Buffy said. "Her read on what disclosure means is exactly as relevant as mine."

Hammond looked at Shyla. Shyla looked back with that equanimity she brought to everything.

"Agreed," Hammond said. "Dismissed. Colonel, a moment."

The room cleared out. Buffy went last, pulled the door most of the way shut behind her, and caught Hammond say, quietly, "The Cadet's judgment continues to impress," and O'Neill say, "Yes sir, it does," in the tone he used when he meant a lot more than just the words.

She headed down the corridor toward the commissary, since it was 1100 and she needed food before the afternoon's planning session, and Shyla fell in beside her with that easy familiarity they'd built up over a week of moving through the Mountain together.

"The general is serious about the disclosure question," Shyla said.

"Yes," Buffy said.

"What is the right answer."

"I don't know yet," Buffy said. "That's why he gave us a month."

Shyla walked in silence a moment. "On my world the line was not secret," she said. "Everyone knew there was one person, each generation, who was different. That knowledge harmed no one. It may have helped."

"How."

"Because when people knew someone was specifically tasked with protecting them, they were more willing to help with the protecting," Shyla said. "Less willing to look away from threats. More willing to say what they had seen. Secrecy produces isolation. The one doing the protecting has no one to protect them, because no one even knows protection is needed."

Buffy thought about Sunnydale. About years of people not knowing, the whole Sunnydale effect, everyone looking away and looking away and looking away while she fought alone in the dark.

"Yeah," she said. "That's been my experience."

"Then perhaps the question isn't whether to disclose," Shyla said, "but how to disclose in a way that produces the outcome you actually want, instead of chaos."

"That's the hard part," Buffy said.

"Most of it is," Shyla said, with that equanimity she brought to most things, and pushed open the door to the commissary.

The month Hammond had given them felt long enough and nowhere near long enough at the same time, which is basically what every important question feels like when it means changing something that's been one particular way for a long, long time.

Buffy wrote her thoughts down, because writing was how she organized things too big to hold in her head all at once. She wrote them in O'Neill's office, evenings, which had by now just become the place she did her real thinking, and he read them once she was done, because that was how they worked now — she wrote, he read, and then they talked it through with the same directness they'd been building since Georgia.

What she wrote was this.

The line had stayed secret on Earth for a long time. The Council enforced that secrecy with all the conviction of an institution that believed it knew best, and it had produced exactly what Shyla described — isolation, loss, Slayers dying young because nobody around them even knew they needed protecting.

She was honest enough to write that the secrecy had also protected the line from some of what Simmons and Mayfield had tried — weaponizing it. Known things got weaponized. That was just a fact about institutions and power and how certain people reacted to capability they couldn't control.

But the scale had changed. The line wasn't just one person on one planet anymore, or not only. It was spread across the galaxy in forms she was only just starting to map. Keeping it a secret at that scale wasn't a real long-term option. It was going to get discovered eventually. The only real question was whether that discovery happened managed or by accident, and an accidental discovery in a galaxy with the Goa'uld in it was a strictly worse outcome than a managed disclosure to the people already fighting them.

She wrote: the question isn't if, it's how, and who first.

She thought about the Tok'ra. About the Asgard, whose name had come up enough times in Daniel's briefings that she'd read everything the SGC had on them, and had gotten the sense they'd matter to this question eventually. About the free Jaffa movement, which Teal'c described with that particular quality he used for things he cared about a lot and talked about very little.

She wrote: the allies first. Then a broader framework. Staged disclosure with oversight at each stage. Not the Council's secrecy, and not the opposite of it either. Something in between that protects what needs protecting and shares what needs sharing.

She finished writing at 2200 and slid the pages across the desk.

O'Neill read them with the same focused attention he gave anything she wrote — something she'd noticed early on, and taken as a kind of respect, the respect of somebody taking your thinking seriously and not just your conclusions. He read them twice, which he did whenever the first read didn't settle, then set them down and looked at the wall.

"The Tok'ra first," he said.

"Yes," she said.

"They already know about Shyla. Knew the survivor anomaly existed before we even understood what it was. They'll want more."

"Probably," she said. "Which is fine. Whatever they know, they'll use carefully. The Tok'ra aren't the problem."

"The problem's the next one," he said. "The next Simmons. The one who finds out with the wrong framework already in his head."

"Yes."

"If we do staged disclosure," he said, "we control the framework before they find out. They get the right picture before they get the chance to build a wrong one."

"That's the theory," she said.

He looked back at the pages. At her phrasing in the margin — the Council's secrecy and not the opposite, something in between. "You've been thinking about this since Fort Benning," he said.

"Since Sunnydale," she said. "Just didn't have the right context for it until now."

He was quiet a moment. "The disclosure question goes to Hammond tomorrow?"

"He asked for our thoughts by end of month."

"We're two weeks out," he said. "I want to sit with this more."

"So do I," she said.

He nodded, looked at the pages one more time, then at her, and his face carried the look it sometimes got at the end of these evening conversations — the one she'd filed in the same category as little lion and the grip on her shoulder on the Georgia parade ground and I need enough to keep you safe. The category she didn't have a word for in English, but that Shyla's world had called anchor.

"You've come a long way," he said. "From the kid who showed up in my commissary six months ago."

"I wasn't a kid," she said.

"You were younger than you are now."

"Everybody's younger six months ago than they are now," she said. "That's just how time works."

"You know what I mean," he said.

She did know. She looked at the pages on his desk, at the hockey stick in the corner, at the window that showed the mountain, since the office happened to sit inside the mountain, and she thought about how far she'd come, and where she was going, and the eleven addresses, and the phone call to Joyce she needed to make tomorrow, and the Tok'ra brief, and the disclosure framework, and all of it, the whole enormous scope of it.

"Yeah," she said. "I do."

She gathered up the pages and stood, and the evening wound down the way it always did — her heading out, him staying a while longer with whatever he was staying with, the Mountain humming its constant frequency around the both of them.

She went to bed.

She slept, which came a lot easier now than it used to, which was itself a kind of data point about how much had changed since the girl who'd sat crying in a graveyard doorway in Sunnydale in the rain until she was completely empty.

That girl was still in here somewhere. Buffy didn't believe in leaving parts of yourself behind — she'd learned the hard way that the parts you tried to leave always came back anyway, usually at the worst possible moment. But that girl wasn't the only thing in here anymore. There was the cadet, and the officer, and the member of SG-1, and the person who'd sat across from Erran and listened to four hours of everything he knew, who'd felt the Slayer recognize the Slayer across a table on a planet that wasn't hers.

There was the person who'd finally found, after a very long time, what it felt like to not be alone in any of the ways that actually mattered.

She slept.

Above her the Mountain sat on Colorado earth, and the stars did what stars did, and somewhere out in the galaxy eight more gate addresses waited with whatever they held, whatever expressions of something very old were out there managing on their own, on worlds she hadn't gotten to yet.

She'd get there.

That was the plan.
