Chapter 1: Not Alone

Planning for P2X-416 took eleven days.

Faster than it should've been, and slower than Buffy wanted — which put it squarely in the category of most things involving operational necessity fighting the institutional machinery of a classified military command. Hammond approved the mission on day three. Intel-gathering ate up six more days: Carter's UAV data from a previous MALP deployment, plus two intelligence summaries from Tok'ra contacts, which Buffy was still getting used to as an entire category of ally — the notion of a benevolent symbiote being the sort of thing that took a little internal reorganizing to fully accept.

On day ten she sat in the planning room with SG-1, looking at the topographical display of P2X-416, thinking about Shyla.

She'd read everything the SGC had on her, which wasn't much. The after-action from the culling operation was thin, since the team had been under fire for most of it, and the survivor notation was exactly that — a notation, three lines buried in a twenty-page report full of bigger concerns. Woman, approximate age late twenties, humanoid, P2X-416 native, found in the rubble at the settlement's outer perimeter. Staff weapon discharge, left side, close range. Walking and coherent by the time the team evacuated her. Physiological anomaly flagged. No follow-up.

She'd resettled at a Tok'ra-adjacent safe location, which was the only reason she was still findable two years later. The Tok'ra file on her wasn't much more detailed than the SGC's, except for one added note — she'd turned down Tok'ra help twice and seemed to be managing on her own, a detail that hit Buffy in some familiar spot lodged right between recognition and grief.

Managing on her own. Because that's what you did when you were the only one of whatever you were, and nobody had told you yet that you didn't have to be.

"The approach vector," O'Neill was saying, and she pulled her attention back to the display. "We come in here, forty klicks from the gate, using the Tok'ra's alkesh. Teal'c flies, so we don't need an outside pilot. We land here, tree coverage on the eastern ridge, and cover the rest on foot."

"Ten klicks through the ridge," Carter said. "Manageable. But Heru'ur's got a Jaffa patrol hitting the northern pass about every six hours. We time the approach to clear that window."

"Window opens 0340 local," O'Neill said. "We're through the pass by 0500. Gives us four hours before the next cycle."

"And extraction," Daniel said.

"Same route, same window, twelve hours later." O'Neill looked at the display. "So four hours on the ground before we need to be heading back to the alkesh."

"Is four hours enough," Buffy said.

Everyone looked at her.

"To convince somebody who's spent two years managing entirely on her own that she should trust a bunch of strangers who show up in the middle of the night claiming to know what she is," she said. "Four hours isn't a lot of time for that conversation."

O'Neill looked at the display, then at her. "How long do you need."

"I don't know," she said honestly. "I know what it took me to trust somebody with the truth of what I am. I know how long it took me to tell you, and I'd already been building up to it for two years." A pause. "I'm not saying four hours isn't enough. I'm saying we need a plan in case it isn't."

"We don't leave her there," O'Neill said.

"Agreed," she said. "So what's the contingency."

Carter was already pulling up the patrol map. "We miss the extraction window, we hold on the eastern ridge and wait for the next cycle — eighteen hours out. Puts us on the ground almost twenty-four hours total."

"Doable," O'Neill said. "Uncomfortable, but doable." He looked at Buffy. "You're lead on the contact. Once we're on the ground and we've found her, you're out front. Your read on the situation drives the timeline."

"Yes sir," she said.

She looked at the display, at the location marker where the Tok'ra said Shyla had been living — a structure in the foothills, on the far side of the settlement ruins, alone. Two years alone, on a planet Heru'ur periodically culled for slaves, surviving things that should've killed her, no idea why.

Buffy knew that exact shape of alone. She'd been living inside it since she was fifteen, and she recognized every contour of it the way you recognize a room you've spent a lot of time in.

She was going to go get her.

They went through the gate at 0200 Mountain time, which put them squarely in the middle of the night on P2X-416, and the alkesh was already waiting on the other side of the wormhole, pre-positioned by the Tok'ra, and Teal'c settled into the pilot's seat with the ease of a man returning to something familiar, and had them in the air inside four minutes.

The planet below was dark and heavily forested, broken up by the geometric shapes of farmland that had been abandoned long enough to start reclaiming itself. The culling had happened two years back. Whatever civilization used to live here didn't anymore.

Buffy sat in the forward compartment, checked her gear, and worked hard at not thinking about Sunnydale — which meant she was thinking about Sunnydale, the way your mind always drags itself right toward the thing you told it not to. The empty streets. The reclaimed lots. The particular look a place gets when the people are ripped out of it too fast, before the world's had time to finish absorbing the absence.

She put it away. Filed it. Turned her attention forward.

O'Neill sat beside her, working through the patrol map on a tablet. He didn't look up when she shifted, but he registered it — she could tell by the slight adjustment in his posture, the way he'd always registered her attention without ever making a production out of it.

"Twenty minutes to the ridge LZ," he said.

"I know," she said.

"You've been somewhere else the last ten minutes."

"I came back," she said.

He looked at her then. Set the tablet down. He had the expression that meant he was deciding whether to say something he'd been sitting on, that slight tightening around his eyes that always came before something he'd actually weighed.

"The survivor on this planet," he said. "It's personal."

"Everything about this is personal," she said. "But yeah. That part especially."

"You know what she doesn't know about herself."

"I know what it felt like to be her," she said. "Before somebody explained it to me. Before Giles." She looked down at her hands. "I fought vampires for six months before anyone gave me the full picture. Six months of things trying to kill me and me fighting back, no clue what I was or why or what it meant for the rest of my life. Didn't even know why I was stronger than I should've been. Just that I was, and that there were things out there in the dark, and apparently I was the one who had to deal with them. Shyla's been doing that for two years, minimum. Whatever the line does on her planet, she's done it without a Watcher, without a Giles, without anyone to tell her you're not alone, here's why this is happening to you."

O'Neill was quiet.

"I want to be that for her," Buffy said. "The person who shows up and says, here's what you are, here's why, and you're not alone. Because nobody should have to do what we do without somebody telling them that."

O'Neill looked out the forward viewport, at the dark terrain sliding by underneath.

"Somebody told me once," he said, in the tone he used when he was going somewhere he didn't often go, "that the worst part of this job isn't the danger. It's watching people go through it alone when they don't have to. I've thought about that a lot."

She looked at him. "Who told you."

"Doesn't matter," he said, which meant it mattered a great deal and was not up for discussion. She let that stand, the way she let all his closed doors stand, without pushing — because he extended her the exact same courtesy, and that was the whole foundation of how they worked.

"Two minutes to LZ," Teal'c said from the cockpit.

O'Neill picked his tablet back up. Back to business. "Stick to the plan till the plan stops working," he said. "Then we improvise."

"The Sunnydale approach," she said.

"The what."

"That's what we used to call it," she said. "When the plan stopped working and you had to make it up as you went. The Sunnydale approach."

He looked at her. "How often did the plan stop working."

"Most of the time," she said.

"Huh." And then, because he was O'Neill: "We're gonna get along fine."

The alkesh set down in the eastern ridge tree cover with Teal'c's usual precision — the precision of a man who'd landed things in worse conditions in the dark plenty of times — and SG-1 was on the ground and moving within ninety seconds of touchdown. The air was cold up at altitude, thick with the smell of pine-adjacent vegetation, close enough to Earth's to feel familiar and different enough to remind her, the way the air on every gate world reminded her, that she wasn't home.

She checked her nine and three. Treeline, treeline, ridge above, drop below. No Jaffa signatures inside her Slayer range, which stretched wider than Carter's scanner and read faster, even if it wasn't quite as precise. She'd told O'Neill about it three weeks into her time at the SGC, and he'd folded it into their operational protocol without any ceremony at all, which she'd appreciated more than she'd said.

"Clear," she said quietly.

"Clear," Carter confirmed off the scanner.

"Move out," O'Neill said.

Ten kilometers through ridge terrain in the dark was the kind of physical work that soaked up most people's full attention. It didn't soak up hers, which left her mind running two tracks at once — one on terrain and patrol windows and the structural awareness of Teal'c at point, Carter on her left, Daniel just behind, O'Neill at her right shoulder — and the other on what exactly she was going to say to Shyla.

She'd thought about it for eleven days and never once written it down, because the things that mattered most to say weren't the kind of things that improved with a script. Giles hadn't scripted it, the day he'd told her what she was. He'd just said it, plain, in that direct way he had, and the directness itself had been half of what made it land. She'd been angry and disbelieving and scared, and the directness was the one thing she'd had to hold onto while everything else moved too fast.

She was going to be direct.

She was also going to be ready for Shyla to try to kill her, because if the positions were reversed — a bunch of strangers showing up in the middle of the night, already knowing things about her nobody should know — her own first response wouldn't have been a welcome mat either.

~~~LELB~~~

The settlement ruins looked exactly like the UAV data had shown — comprehensively destroyed, the way Goa'uld cullings always were, leaving nothing but structural shells and reclaiming vegetation and the heavy quiet of a place that used to hold a whole community and now held only the absence of one.

Shyla's structure sat on the far side of the ruins, which meant walking through them first. Buffy took point through the ruins by mutual agreement, since her Slayer senses were the fastest early warning system they had, and she moved through the shells of buildings with her P90 up and her senses stretched wide open.

What she felt was grief. Not threat. Just grief, the kind that clings to places people were taken from, and she recognized it easily enough, having walked through plenty of those before.

Clear, she signaled, and they moved through.

Shyla's structure turned out to be a modified version of one of the settlement's surviving outbuildings, reinforced with scavenged material in ways that showed real practical intelligence — the kind of construction sense you only get from necessity, not training. The whole thing prioritized defensibility. Good sightlines from the one window. A single entrance, which was both a weakness and a choice — whoever built it had decided being visible at the door mattered more than having an escape route.

Also a choice Buffy understood completely.

She signaled the team to hold at the treeline and went forward alone, exactly as planned, which O'Neill had accepted with visible reluctance and a list of conditions that included keeping her in his line of sight, which she fully intended to honor.

She reached the entrance and stopped.

"Shyla," she said, in a normal speaking voice. Not loud, not aggressive, just clear enough to carry. "My name's Buffy Summers. I know what you are. I'm one too. I'm here to talk."

Silence.

She waited.

The silence had texture to it. Not empty — occupied. The kind of silence that means somebody's on the other side of the door making decisions.

"I came through the gate," Buffy said. "From Earth. I know that probably doesn't mean much to you. I know you've got no reason to trust me, and I'm not going to pretend that you do. I know you survived something two years ago that should've killed you. I know it wasn't the first time. I know you don't know why, and I know what it's like carrying that around, and I know you've been carrying it alone."

Another silence, shorter this time.

Then the door opened.

~~~LELB~~~

Shyla stood a good six inches taller than Buffy, which wasn't unusual, and carried herself with the coiled readiness of somebody who'd been the last line of defense for two straight years and had built her whole body around that fact. Dark eyes doing the exact same thing Buffy's did in a new situation — checking everything at once, prioritizing threats — and something in her right hand that was recognizably a weapon, even if Buffy couldn't have named the model.

She looked at Buffy.

Buffy looked back.

"You're small," Shyla said. Her Standard was accented, learned rather than native — the Tok'ra contact, probably.

"I get that a lot," Buffy said. "Can I come in."

Shyla's eyes flicked past her, out to the treeline where SG-1 was holding. They narrowed.

"My team," Buffy said. "They stay out here unless I call them. You have my word."

"Your word," Shyla said, in the tone of somebody who'd learned the value of words through hard experience rather than optimism.

"I know," Buffy said. "Stranger's word. Not exactly a strong opening bid. So how about this — you keep the weapon, I keep my hands where you can see them, and we find out together if I'm worth a few minutes."

Another silence. Then Shyla stepped back from the door.

Buffy went in.

~~~LELB~~~

The inside was every bit as defensible as the outside suggested. Single room, sightlines to the entrance and window, exits Buffy clocked automatically because that's just what she did, sleeping area off to one side with the bare spareness of somebody who wouldn't let herself get comfortable, because comfortable people got slow. A worktable, tools, repaired gear — the practical infrastructure of someone who'd been keeping herself operational alone for two straight years.

Shyla stood between Buffy and the door. Weapon still in hand.

"Talk," she said.

Buffy talked.

She gave her the version she'd been putting together for eleven days. Not all of it — not the cosmological scale of the device on P4X-639, not the pre-Ancient origin, not the gate addresses carved into the chamber wall. That was way too much for a first conversation with somebody still deciding whether or not to shoot her. She gave her the working version. The version Giles had given her.

There's a line. One person at a time. Always someone chosen, activated, given capabilities way past normal parameters, put in place to stand between the people who can't defend themselves and the things that want to hurt them. You're the current expression of that line on this world. You've been doing the job without ever knowing it was a job. You're not crazy. You're not broken. And you're not alone.

Shyla listened without interrupting once, which Buffy took as a good sign, and her expression moved through several different states Buffy tracked without commenting on — anger, recognition, and something that might've been relief, all fighting for control of her face.

When Buffy finally stopped talking, Shyla went quiet a moment.

"The staff weapon," she said. "Two years ago."

"Should've killed you," Buffy said. "Didn't. Because of what you are."

"I did not understand it."

"You couldn't have. Not without somebody telling you." Buffy held her gaze. "Somebody told me. Changed everything."

Shyla looked down at the weapon in her hand. Then back at Buffy. "You're not just here to tell me what I am."

"No," Buffy said. "I'm here because there are people trying to build a picture of what the line is and what it can do. Human people, from my world. People with resources, and the wrong kind of intentions. I need you to know that picture exists before they find you."

"Find me," Shyla said. Flat.

"They're not there yet," Buffy said. "But they will be. And I'd rather you hear it coming than get blindsided. I'd also rather you weren't alone when it gets here. If that's something you want."

Shyla looked at her a long time. Buffy waited the way she'd learned to wait — not filling the silence, because some silences were doing real work, and interrupting them would only get in the way.

"The people with you," Shyla said.

"My team. They know what I am. They're good people."

"Military."

"Yes."

"Military people have come here before," Shyla said. "After the culling. They were also good people. They helped with the evacuation. They could not help with what came after." She held Buffy's eyes. "I do not doubt your intentions. I doubt that good intentions are enough."

"They're not," Buffy said. "Not by themselves. But they're where it starts." A pause. "I'm not going to tell you this is going to be simple. I'm not going to promise it all works out. I've been doing this since I was fifteen, and things haven't always worked out." She thought about a library. About red hair, and a boy with terrible taste in shirts, and a man in tweed who'd died fifteen feet from a detonator he'd chosen to push himself. "But I'm still here. And I'm not alone anymore. And that matters."

Shyla was quiet.

Then she set the weapon down on the worktable.

"Tell me about your team," she said.

Buffy signaled O'Neill at the forty-five minute mark, and he came in with Carter and Daniel, leaving Teal'c on the perimeter, since four strangers all at once was one too many — O'Neill had read the whole situation right off that one signal without needing it spelled out, which was one of the things she valued most about working with him.

Daniel immediately and completely failed to hide his interest in the structure and everything Shyla had done with the scavenged materials, which was so entirely him that something loosened in Buffy's chest — the relief of people being exactly who they always were.

Shyla watched Daniel with an expression somewhere between amusement and wariness, which was also a perfectly reasonable response to Daniel.

Carter did a better job holding herself back, though it clearly took real effort, since the equipment on Shyla's worktable was interesting to Sam the way any technology was interesting to Sam — which was to say, completely, no reservations whatsoever. She managed to limit herself to one question, which counted as a real personal achievement.

O'Neill introduced himself with the same economy he used whenever the situation called for building trust fast — the economy of a man who'd done it a hundred times and had learned that the most effective move was just being exactly what you were, no performance necessary.

Shyla looked him over a moment after he introduced himself. "You're in command," she said.

"Of the team," he said. "Buffy runs her own operation."

"But you're responsible for her."

"Yes," he said, no hesitation at all.

Shyla looked between the two of them. Buffy could tell she was reading the relationship with that specific sharpness of somebody who'd learned to read people fast, because reading people fast was a survival skill.

"That's not a military relationship," Shyla said.

"No," O'Neill said. "It's not."

Shyla nodded slowly, like this confirmed something she'd already been working out. She looked at Buffy. "He came with you because he was worried."

"He comes with me on most missions," Buffy said. "It's his team."

"He came because he was worried about this specific one," Shyla said. Not a question.

Buffy glanced at O'Neill, who was doing the thing where his face was neutral in a way that didn't quite manage to actually be neutral.

"Yeah," Buffy said. "Probably."

Shyla seemed satisfied with that, for reasons she didn't explain. She looked at the worktable, at the whole space around her, at the structure she'd built from salvage on a dead planet. Two years alone in this room, keeping herself alive, doing the job without ever knowing it was a job, nobody ever worried about a mission, because there'd been nobody to worry.

"I will come with you," she said. "For now. I reserve the right to change my mind."

"Fair," Buffy said.

"What I am, on this world," Shyla said. "The job. If I leave, what happens to it."

The question landed and just sat there in the room. Buffy glanced at O'Neill, who glanced right back, and she could tell he wasn't answering because this was her territory and he knew it.

"The job continues," Buffy said carefully. "The line doesn't stop just because one person leaves. It finds someone else. I left my world. My specific territory. The work keeps going there. It never depended on me being the only one."

Shyla took that in. "And the new one. Do they know."

"Not yet," Buffy said. "But I'm working on it."

Shyla nodded — the nod of somebody filing a concern away rather than resolving it, which was honest, and which Buffy respected.

"Let's go," Shyla said.

~~~LELB~~~

They made the extraction window with forty minutes to spare, which gave Teal'c plenty of time to get the alkesh in the air and clear of the ridge before the patrol cycle came back around, and Shyla sat in the forward compartment and watched the planet fall away beneath them with the exact expression of somebody leaving a place they weren't entirely sure they should be leaving, and doing it anyway because the alternative was staying.

Buffy had worn that same expression once herself, standing at the Sunnydale bus station with a duffel bag and a one-way ticket, Joyce's voice on the phone saying I'll see you when I see you, because neither of them had been able to manage a real goodbye.

She didn't say any of that to Shyla. Just sat nearby and let the silence be exactly what it was — the silence of somebody processing something huge, no need to fill it.

O'Neill was up in the cockpit with Teal'c, which was where he went whenever he wanted to give people room he figured they needed, using "checking on the flight" as cover for the consideration underneath it — one of those things she'd learned to recognize as his language for caring about someone.

Daniel had fallen asleep in his seat with his notebook still open in his lap, a trick he apparently could pull off in any atmospheric conditions whatsoever.

Carter worked her tablet, but she glanced up once, across the compartment, and the look said are you okay, and Buffy nodded, and Carter nodded back and went right back to her data.

The planet fell away and the stars came up, and Shyla watched them with dark eyes doing some calculation Buffy didn't bother trying to read, and the alkesh headed for the gate and the address that led home.

Robert Mayfield's office in McLean looked out over a parking lot, which he'd picked on purpose — a good view was a distraction, and Mayfield did not believe in distractions. He believed in fundamentals. Assets, liabilities, timelines, and the discipline it took to tell the difference between a problem you solved right now and one you let mature on its own.

Simmons had been a problem that matured badly. Mayfield kept the Fort Benning file in a drawer he rarely opened, less as a working reference than a reminder of what failure looked like — a man who'd had a genuinely rare asset sitting right in front of him and answered his own uncertainty with elimination, the way a scared animal lashes out at anything it doesn't understand. Simmons had confused decisiveness with intelligence. Tried to fix the girl the way you fix a leak — plug it, move on — and learned, at real professional cost, that some things aren't leaks. Some things are tides.

Mayfield had no intention of repeating that mistake.

He'd read every page Simmons's operation had produced before it collapsed, and what he'd taken away from it wasn't a threat profile. It was a resource assessment. A double chute failure, calibrated for a fatal outcome, had produced a hospital stay and a set of jump wings six weeks later. That wasn't survivability. That was a category error in what Simmons thought he was dealing with, and Mayfield wasn't going to make it twice.

The file on his desk this morning was thinner than he liked, which told him something all by itself. Cadet Buffy Anne Summers had entered a command structure that had, over just a few months, gone quiet around her in that particular way that means something's being actively protected rather than just happening naturally. Clearance elevations that never showed up in any system he had access to. A liaison relationship with Colonel Jack O'Neill that had drifted from professional into something his sources cautiously described as familial. A pattern of temporary reassignments to an Air Force facility in Colorado that lined up, once he cross-checked satellite tasking requests, with a command he'd spent eleven years trying and failing to get any real visibility into.

Cheyenne Mountain kept its secrets better than anywhere else in the government. That fact alone told him the asset was significant.

He had a contact — thin, unreliable, useful mostly because they had no idea how useful they were — with something adjacent to the Tok'ra trade network, the kind of fringe intelligence most of Washington wrote off as noise. The contact had mentioned, almost in passing, a survivor anomaly on a culled world. A woman who should've died to a close-range energy weapon and hadn't. Mayfield had filed it as a curiosity, right up until the Fort Benning data landed and gave him a second data point with the exact same shape.

Two wasn't a coincidence. Two was a category.

He picked up the phone.

"I want a team ready to move on the address the contact gave us," he said. "Quietly. I want to know what's on that planet before anybody else does." He listened to the question on the other end. "No," he said. "We are not eliminating anything. Simmons eliminated things, and Simmons is currently explaining himself to people I have zero interest in meeting. We identify. We approach. We get cooperation before we get anything else. The girl's the priority, but she's inside a command structure I currently can't get into. The other one might not be."

He set the phone down and looked back over the file. At the photo from the wings ceremony his source in public affairs had pulled off a local paper's unused submissions — Summers in dress uniform, a woman beside her he'd tentatively pegged as the mother, and a colonel at the edge of the frame whose expression the analyst's notes described as "not neutral."

Simmons had looked at this exact file and seen a threat.

Mayfield looked at it and saw the future arriving slightly ahead of schedule, which was how it usually showed up for people who actually paid attention, and he intended to be standing in the right place when it did.

He was, he thought, being responsible. Somebody had to be.

Davis called O'Neill two days after they got back from P2X-416.

He had a name.

Buffy was in Daniel's office when O'Neill showed up in the doorway, which was becoming a pretty reliable pattern by now, and she could tell from his face before he even said a word that the two weeks had just gotten a lot shorter.

"Mayfield," O'Neill said. "Robert Mayfield. Former NRO, currently running a private intelligence advisory firm in McLean that's the second organization's public face." He looked at her. "Davis has enough. Ready to move in the morning."

"What does Mayfield know," she said.

"More than Simmons did. Been building his framework longer. He's got the Fort Benning data, the SGC surveillance data, and—" O'Neill paused. "He's got a contact in the Tok'ra network who told him about Shyla."

Buffy went very still.

"Before we even got there," O'Neill said. "He knew about the survivor anomaly. Not what it meant, not what she was — just that she was worth acquiring." He held her eyes. "That's why Davis is moving in the morning. Mayfield's got a team deploying to P2X-416, and we need him in custody before they make it through the gate."

She thought about that empty structure on the far side of the ruins. About Shyla's worktable, her defensible sightlines, two years of alone that were over now because she'd made a different choice.

"Shyla's here," she said. "She's safe."

"She's in the Mountain. Yes." O'Neill paused. "But Mayfield doesn't know that. And once his team hits P2X-416 and finds her gone, he's going to start asking a whole different set of questions. Gives Davis about eighteen hours before Mayfield goes dark."

"Then Davis needs to move tonight," Buffy said.

"Davis agrees. He's making the calls right now." O'Neill looked at her. "I'll be in the ops center when it happens. Hammond wants you there too."

"I'll be there," she said.

Daniel had looked up from his transcription work. He hadn't bothered pretending not to listen, which was one of the things she'd come to like about him — he never performed inattention. "It's ending," he said.

"This part of it," Buffy said.

He nodded, catching the distinction. "The gate addresses," he said. "The others out there. That part isn't ending."

"No," she said. "That part's just getting started."

Daniel looked down at his transcription pages, at the gate addresses in their pre-Ancient notation, weeks of work now, each one a world, each one a possibility. "We're going to need a plan," he said. "How to approach each one. What to say. Who makes contact."

"I should make contact," Buffy said. "Same as with Shyla. Somebody who is the thing you're trying to explain is a lot more convincing than somebody who can only describe it."

"That's eleven different planets," Daniel said.

"Eventually," she said. "Not all at once. Some of those addresses might not even be active anymore. Some might be places we can't safely reach. Some might need the kind of planning P2X-416 needed." She looked over the pages. "We work through it the same way we're working through everything else. Methodically."

Daniel smiled — the real one, the one that meant he was working alongside somebody who thought the way he thought. "O'Neill's been good for you," he said.

She looked at him. "Meaning what."

"Three months ago," Daniel said, "my read on you was somebody very good at operating alone and a lot less practiced at operating as part of something bigger. Now my read's flipped completely." He glanced down at his pages. "Good change."

She thought about that. About Saturday dinners and the office with the hockey stick, about Teal'c's careful adequates and Sam's contained enthusiasm and Daniel falling asleep in alkesh cockpits with his notebook still open. About Hammond reading fifty-two page reports before breakfast, and Abel making a memo disappear, and Joyce with her four batches of cookies.

"Yeah," she said. "It is."

She got up and went to find O'Neill.

The operations center at 0200 ran on that quiet intensity of important work happening in the middle of the night — overhead lighting at two-thirds, console screens the brightest things in the whole room. Nothing had ever happened here, because there'd never been anything worth happening for.

That was about to change.

Buffy sat in the forward compartment and felt the planet rise up beneath them the same way she felt every planet — that particular Slayer trick of reading dimensional pressure straight through the hull, like a hand pressed through water. P5C-353 was thick. Thicker than Earth, thicker than P3X-774, thicker than anything she'd felt through a gate or an alkesh hull since she'd started paying attention to the variation at all.

This planet had been Edjet a very, very long time.

"Thirty seconds to LZ," Teal'c said.

She checked her gear. Checked it again. Looked across at Shyla, doing exactly the same thing with that baseline economy of hers — everything necessary, nothing extra.

Shyla caught her eye. Ready.

O'Neill stood at the cockpit doorway. Caught her look, gave her the nod.

The alkesh set down in a mountain meadow the UAV data had confirmed sat outside the settlement's sightlines, and SG-1 plus Shyla was on the ground within ninety seconds, and the alkesh lifted immediately on Teal'c's remote and moved off to the holding position forty kilometers northeast, ready to wait.

The air hit her first.

She'd expected the pressure — the thickness she'd already felt through the hull. She hadn't expected it to feel like standing inside something instead of next to it, the way the Hellmouth felt on its worst nights, minus the malevolence, minus the darkness underneath. Just pressure. Dense, old, layered, the way soil gets layered, every stratum holding its own accumulated record of everything that ever happened above it.

She stood in the meadow a moment.

"You feel it," Shyla said beside her. Not a question.

"Yeah," Buffy said.

"I felt it from the ship," Shyla said. "It is different here."

"Different how," O'Neill said.

"More," Shyla said simply.

O'Neill looked at Buffy.

"She's right," Buffy said. "Whatever's been going on here's been going on a long time, and it's in the land." She looked at the treeline at the meadow's edge, at the mountains rising past it, at the valley the UAV data said held a settlement of two hundred people who'd been the Edjet so long they didn't remember not being it. "We go carefully."

"Always the plan," O'Neill said. "Move out."

The settlement announced itself before they ever saw it.

On Earth she'd never been able to feel another Slayer at a distance. The settlement's edge came out of the pre-dawn dark as a line of structures that had grown out of the mountain valley the way things grow when they've been there long enough to belong — stone and timber, the built and the natural blended together the way they get once a community's stayed in one spot for generations and stopped thinking of the landscape as anything separate from itself.

A few lights. Not many. A handful of windows glowing with something that was either fire or close to it.

And a person standing right at the settlement's edge.

Waiting.

Older than Buffy had expected, somewhere in her sixties, carrying that coiled readiness Buffy recognized before she recognized anything else about her — the Edjet signature, carried in a body that had held it for decades and had organized itself around the weight of it, the way old trees organize themselves around whichever way the wind usually blows.

She looked at Buffy.

Buffy stopped.

Twenty meters apart, and the woman's eyes in the pre-dawn light were doing exactly what Erran's had done across a table on P3X-774, what Shyla's had done in a doorway on P2X-416.

Recognition. The Edjet knowing the Edjet.

This was extraordinary on a human one. Twelve people around a fire, each one carrying the exact same thing she carried, each one its own expression of the same pattern in a different key, and the combined weight of all of them in one room together was something she had no framework for, because she'd never once needed a framework for it.

She'd been the only Slayer in a room her whole life.

She was not the only Slayer in this room.

O'Neill touched her elbow briefly. Just contact. Grounding. She took a breath.

She was not the only one.

She thought about what it meant to be built by something that had anticipated this exact moment, this exact challenge, this exact quality of threat, and had decided a very long time ago that the line would be enough for it.

The Builders hadn't built something they expected to fail.

"Okay," she said.

O'Neill looked at her.

"Let him watch," she said. "When he's ready to move, we're ready for him." She glanced at the sensor data one more time — the ha'tak in the outer system, small on the display, enormous in reality, Ba'al's whole patience embodied in metal and naquadah. "He's going to find out he waited too long."

O'Neill said, "You're confident about that."

"Yes sir," she said.

"Based on."

She thought about all of it. Everything built and everything survived and everything now standing at her back — spread across eleven worlds and one mountain in Colorado and the SGC's gate room, the addresses she knew by heart, the communities she'd sat with, the people now connected to each other in ways that hadn't existed before she followed the list.

She thought about the Builders and what they'd trusted the line to do.

"Based on everything," she said.

Hammond looked at the ha'tak.

"Stand by," he said. "We wait."

They waited.

The Mountain hummed at its frequency, the gate room stayed the gate room, and the ha'tak sat out in the outer system being patient, and Buffy stood in the operations center with her team around her and the network at her back and the line underneath everything, and she breathed in, and she was ready.

Whatever Ba'al had, she was ready.

Whatever the third phase turned out to be, she was ready.

Whatever came next, in whatever shape the work took from here, she was ready, and she wasn't alone, and the things that had built her were present in the structure of everything she was, and she was going to be fine.

She was going to be more than fine.

She was going to be exactly what she'd always been — the standing opposition, the permanent resistance, the thing in the world that does not yield.

The Edjet.

The Chosen One.

Lieutenant Buffy Summers, USAF.

All three. Always all three.

She squared her shoulders.

She got ready for whatever came next.
