Exactly the Right Size

The return to P4X-639 happened on a Friday.

Not because Friday carried any operational significance, just because the five days between P3Y-294 and the return had been full the way post-mission stretches get full — debrief, documentation, folding Cato and Kira and Davan and the water-world siblings and Sora and all the others into the growing shape of whatever the SGC was becoming. Something that didn't have a name yet in any file or protocol, but that Hammond was already building the administrative bones for with the same quiet, systematic attention he brought to everything that mattered.

Shyla was the one who finally named it, talking with Buffy in the gym on the third morning after they got back, mid-way through the movement work that had become their daily calibration. She said, simply, "We are becoming a community."

Not an asset pool. Not a program. A community.

Buffy thought about it two days, then wrote it up in a memo to Hammond. Hammond read it and wrote back exactly one line: That is the correct word. Adjust all documentation accordingly.

So the documentation got adjusted, the community kept getting built, and Friday morning Buffy went back through the gate to P4X-639 with O'Neill and Daniel and Shyla and stood in the clearing outside the structure, feeling the device inside doing what it had been doing since before she ever arrived, which was waiting.

"Same protocol as before," O'Neill said. "Carter's monitoring from the SGC. Teal'c's got the perimeter. Daniel, threshold only, not inside till she says."

"Understood," Daniel said.

O'Neill looked at Buffy. "You told me you've got a question."

"Yes sir."

"And you think the device has an answer."

"Yes sir."

"And you think you're ready to hear it now."

"Yes sir."

He looked at her a moment with the expression he wore right before saying something he'd been thinking about a lot longer than the current conversation. "Whatever the answer is," he said, "you come back out."

She looked at him. "Sir."

"I mean it," he said. "Whatever it tells you. Whatever it shows you. You stay tethered. To this." He gestured — the clearing, the team, the gate. "You don't go anywhere I can't follow."

She understood what he meant. Not physically. He meant the other kind of going — the kind that happens when somebody gets handed information big enough to pull them out of themselves, out of the specific weight of the life they'd built, into something abstract and enormous and completely untethered.

She thought about ascension. About the Ancients and the Ori and what it meant to leave the physical universe entirely for some higher plane. She thought about whether the third phase of the line was something like that.

She thought about Joyce in the house on Spruce Street. About the commissary table and everybody who sat around it. About Mira on P5C-353, and Davan two doors from Shyla, and Cato on his forested world, and the water-world siblings, and Sora reading gate addresses by touch.

About O'Neill in a hospital chair with a copy of Field and Stream.

"I'm not going anywhere you can't follow," she said.

He held her eyes a moment. Then he nodded.

She walked into the structure.

The device was already at full output when she crossed the threshold.

Not building from baseline the way it had the first two times. Already there, already at the level it only ever reached once she was in contact with it, like it had known she was coming and had been running at that level ever since she left P3Y-294 with the question still forming.

Maybe it had.

She walked over and put both hands on it.

Recognition came instant and complete, the enormous patient awareness settling around her the way it always did now, a thing she'd come to understand wasn't the device itself but something the device was a conduit for, something that used the device the way she used a telephone — a medium for the communication, not the communication itself.

She asked the question.

Not in words. She'd tried words the first visit and they hadn't been the right medium. What she'd learned on the visits since was that the device communicated in something a lot more like intention — you gave it intention, and what came back was understanding, not language.

She gave it the question, shaped as intention. A wanting-to-know.

Who built this.

And then, because Daniel had said the right question came in two parts:

Are you still here.

The response didn't come immediately. There was a pause she couldn't measure in seconds, because time inside the contact wasn't the same as time outside it, and in that pause she was aware of herself standing in the stone structure on P4X-639 with her hands on something very old, and aware of O'Neill outside doing whatever O'Neill did while waiting, which was stay present with total attention and zero visible anxiety, and aware of Shyla somewhere at the edge of her Slayer range, another expression of the line staying close by in case she was needed.

Then the response came.

What she came away with, after, was this.

Yes.

To both parts of the question.

The Builders had built the line. And they were still here. Not in any way SGC instruments could detect, or eyes could see, or anything could feel except the thing the line itself was made of — which was itself part of what the Builders had made, and could feel them the way water feels the shape of whatever holds it, not by looking at the container from outside but by simply being its interior.

They hadn't ascended the way the Ancients ascended — hadn't left the universe for some plane outside it. They'd done something different, something the third-phase notation described, the section she still couldn't read, and what they'd done was become the thing they'd built.

Not merge with it. Become it.

That distinction was the part she held onto longest and understood least completely, sitting on the floor of the structure with her hands off the device, O'Neill kneeling beside her with a hand on her shoulder, Daniel standing in the doorway with his recorder running, Shyla at the threshold wearing the look of somebody who could feel what had just happened even without hearing a word of it.

Become it.

Not builders who'd made the line and then stayed separate from it. The line itself, at the level of its origin, was them. The recognition the device communicated wasn't a recording of their recognition. It was their actual, present, continuous recognition, expressed through the medium of what they'd woven into the galaxy's biology.

Every time an expression of the line felt the device, they felt the Builders directly.

Every time the Edjet recognized another Edjet, the Builders were present in that recognition.

Every time the line protected something, the Builders were the protection.

Not gone. Not watching from some distance. Here, the way whatever something is made of is here in everything it makes.

She sat on the floor of the structure and understood this and just sat with it a while, not trying to do anything with it yet.

O'Neill's hand on her shoulder was warm and solid and present.

"Hey," he said quietly.

"Hey," she said.

"You okay."

"Yes," she said. "Give me a minute."

"Take what you need," he said, which was what he always said.

She took two minutes. Then looked up at him.

"The Builders are still here," she said.

He held her eyes. "Define here."

"In the line itself," she said. "They built themselves right into it. When I feel the device recognize me, that's them. When Shyla and I feel each other, that's them. When the community on P5C-353's been Edjet so long the ground itself remembers it, what the ground's remembering is them." A pause. "They didn't leave. They became what they made. And what they made's spread across the galaxy, in the biology of living things, which means they're everywhere the line is."

O'Neill sat with that.

"Which means they're here," he said. "Right now."

"Yes," she said.

He looked at the device. At the structure around them. At the alien stone and the old notation on the walls and the light the device put out, patient and even.

"Huh," he said.

Which was, she'd come to understand over three years, O'Neill's response to the most extraordinary things. Not the ones that alarmed him or challenged him or called for a tactical response. The ones that were just genuinely, purely wonderful, in the original sense of the word — the ones that produced actual wonder.

"Yeah," she said. "Huh."

He helped her up with the same practical efficiency he brought to anything physical, and she stood in the structure with the device's light around them both, and she felt what she now knew wasn't just the device but the enormous, patient awareness of the very things that had made her — present the same way the structure of her own bones was present. Not visible. Not external. Just the thing she was built out of.

She didn't feel small.

She felt, for the first time since she was fifteen years old standing over the Master's ashes in a school basement in Sunnydale, exactly the right size for what she was.

The debrief ran longer than any she'd given since the original P4X-639 disclosure, and Daniel contributed a lot to it, since he'd been listening and recording and processing simultaneously and had, by the time they got back to the SGC, already organized his understanding into something he could actually communicate, which was the single most useful thing Daniel did.

Hammond listened to all of it. Anise and Malek sat in too, since Jacob had called them, and they listened with the layered attention the Tok'ra always brought, and when it was over Selmak spoke before either of the others could.

"The Tok'ra records," Selmak said, "hold one additional notation about the Edjet that we did not share in the original brief, because we did not understand its significance at the time."

Everyone looked at Jacob.

"The earliest records," Selmak said, "from the period of the first Goa'uld encounters with the line on Earth, include a description from a symbiote later killed in a culling operation, whose memories were preserved. She described attempting to take an Edjet as a host. The blending failed. What she felt in that failure, she said, was not resistance. Not fighting." Selmak paused. "She described it as touching something that was already occupied. Not by a human consciousness. By something else. Something very large, simply present in a way that left no room."

The room went quiet.

"The Builders," Daniel said.

"Yes," Selmak said. "We believe so now. At the time, the record was filed as a failed blending with no adequate explanation. Now we understand the explanation."

"The Goa'uld can't take an Edjet as host," O'Neill said slowly, "because the space is already occupied."

"By the Builders," Selmak confirmed.

Buffy looked down at her hands.

She thought about that. About the biology of the line — what it meant to have been built as a vessel for something enormous and patient and permanent. About the Goa'uld avoiding the Edjet, not because of fighting ability, though that was real too, but because something in the biological encounter itself told them the territory was already claimed by something they had no framework for and couldn't engage.

She thought about what Dessa had said. That the Goa'uld commander who'd come to P5C-353 had looked at Aleth and his eyes were afraid. Not of her. Of the feeling.

He'd felt them.

"Ba'al," she said.

Everyone looked at her.

"Ba'al's more analytical than the other System Lords," she said. "Teal'c said he's more willing to pursue something he doesn't understand than avoid it. If he ever encounters the Edjet with enough deliberation to push past the instinctive avoidance, he's going to try to analyze it." She looked at Teal'c. "Once he analyzes it and finds out that whatever's occupying the line is something he can't identify — what does he do."

Teal'c considered it. "Ba'al's response to the unknown is not avoidance," he said. "It is acquisition. He will attempt to capture and study an expression of the line."

"Can he," O'Neill said.

"He can capture," Teal'c said. "He cannot hold. The Edjet has already demonstrated the capacity to survive conditions that should be unsurvivable. A Goa'uld prison is a known set of conditions. It is not unsurvivable."

"So he captures, can't hold, tries to study whatever he's got in the time he has it, and eventually figures out he's not going to get what he needs that way," O'Neill said.

"Yes," Teal'c said.

"So he escalates," O'Neill said.

"Yes," Teal'c said.

"To what," O'Neill said.

Teal'c went quiet a moment. "Ba'al is, above all else, strategic," he said. "When direct engagement fails, he does not repeat the attempt. He changes the method." He looked at Buffy. "If he cannot capture the line by force and he cannot understand it through study, he will attempt to eliminate the coordination. Not the individual expressions. The coordination itself."

"Because the individual expressions predate the coordination," Daniel said. "The Goa'uld have lived alongside the individual expressions for ten thousand years without treating them as an existential problem. What changed is the coordination."

"So he tries to sever it," Buffy said.

"Yes," Teal'c said.

"How," O'Neill said.

"I do not know precisely," Teal'c said. "But Ba'al's methods tend toward elegance. He does not use a larger force where a smaller one accomplishes the same result. He will identify the minimum intervention required to sever the coordination, and he will apply it precisely."

The room went quiet a moment.

"The minimum intervention," Buffy said.

She didn't say the rest. Didn't need to. Everyone in the room was already following the same thread.

The minimum intervention to sever the Edjet's coordination wasn't eliminating all twelve expressions on P5C-353, or all seven on P3Y-294, or every distributed expression across all eleven worlds.

The minimum intervention was eliminating the person who'd built the coordination in the first place.

O'Neill said, very quietly, "Hammond."

"Yes," Hammond said. "I see it."

"We need to move Summers off the visible end of this," O'Neill said. "Everything that shows her as the coordinating element, every record that puts her at the point of connection between the contacts — it needs to be classified above anything Ba'al's intelligence network can reach."

"Ba'al's intelligence network is very good," Malek said.

"I know," O'Neill said. "Which means we need to be better." He looked at Hammond. "Sir."

"Start the reclassification this afternoon," Hammond said. "Malek, I need your assessment of what Ba'al's intelligence assets can and cannot access in this sector. By morning." He looked at Teal'c. "Your assessment of his current focus. Whatever else is occupying him."

"Ba'al is currently in a political conflict with Camulus over three disputed systems," Teal'c said. "That conflict is consuming a significant share of his strategic attention. It will not last indefinitely."

"How long," Hammond said.

"Three to six months," Teal'c said. "Perhaps longer if it escalates. Perhaps shorter if one of them yields."

"Three months minimum," O'Neill said. "We use it."

"To do what specifically," Anise said.

O'Neill looked at Buffy.

Buffy looked at the community she'd built, spread across eleven worlds and the Mountain, all of it now connected the way the document on P3Y-294 had described the second phase — coordinated, aware of each other. She thought about the third phase, and what Daniel had called a natural phase transition, a consequence of the first two phases finishing.

She thought about the Builders, and what it meant that they were present in the line itself, that the recognition the device gave off wasn't a recording but an actual presence — and what that presence could do, when it was needed.

"To finish what we started," she said. "The community's built. The coordination exists. Now we make sure it survives without a single point of failure." She looked at O'Neill. "I've been the connector. I need to stop being the only one. Everyone needs to be able to reach everyone else directly. Not through me."

O'Neill nodded slowly. "Decentralize."

"Decentralize," she said. "Shyla can coordinate the contacts we've already made out in open space. Erran's family line has been running distributed communication for generations, they can anchor the P3X-774 node. Dessa's community's got records older than anyone else we've found, they become the network's historical memory instead of me carrying all of it. I stay the Earth node. But I'm not the only node. If Ba'al eliminates me, the network holds."

"That's cold-blooded thinking," O'Neill said.

"Yes sir," she said.

"I mean that as a compliment," he said.

"I know," she said.

Hammond watched the plan take shape in the room. Looked around the table. At what three years of work had actually built.

"How long to implement the decentralization," he said.

"Six weeks," Buffy said. "With Tok'ra support on the communication channels."

"You've got six weeks," Hammond said. "Malek, I'll need the Tok'ra's cooperation on the channel infrastructure."

"You have it," Malek said, in the tone of a Tok'ra who'd already decided something was worth the resources and had no intention of qualifying that decision.

"Then we have a plan," Hammond said. He looked around the table. "Dismissed. Colonel O'Neill, a moment."

The room emptied out. Buffy went last, pulled the door almost shut behind her, and caught Hammond say, "She's going to be a target regardless of the decentralization."

"I know," O'Neill said.

"Ba'al is patient. He'll wait out the conflict with Camulus, and then he'll turn his attention here."

"I know," O'Neill said.

"Your assessment of her ability to handle what that means."

A pause. "She's been a target since she was fifteen years old," O'Neill said. "Better at handling it than anyone I've ever worked with." Another pause. "She's also not alone anymore. That's different from before. The people around her are different from before."

"You specifically," Hammond said.

"Me and the team," O'Neill said.

"Jack," Hammond said, and using his first name shifted the whole register of the conversation into the one Hammond used when he was speaking as a man instead of a general. "She's going to face something significant. When Ba'al moves. Whatever form it takes."

"Yes sir," O'Neill said.

"I want to make sure she faces it with everything she needs."

"She will," O'Neill said. Flat and certain, the way he was certain about things he'd decided to make true rather than things he was just predicting.

Buffy walked down the corridor and didn't listen to the rest of it, since she'd already heard what she needed to hear — not the content, but the quality of it. The quality of two people who'd decided something was worth protecting and were fully aligned on it.

She went to find Shyla.

The six weeks ran busy the same way the previous four months had been busy — dense, purposeful, every day pushing the decentralization forward in increments she tracked against a timeline in a notebook she carried, because a notebook was lower-tech and therefore a lot less hackable than anything the SGC ran, a habit she'd picked up back in Sunnydale and never once stopped finding useful.

The Tok'ra provided the communication channel infrastructure, and Carter integrated it into the SGC's systems in a way that produced three separate redundant networks, each one capable of holding the whole coordination on its own if the others failed. Malek called it elegant, and Carter took the compliment with the particular pleasure she reserved for compliments about her actual work.

Shyla went back to seven of the eleven worlds in the first four weeks, setting up direct channels between the communities she'd visited, and came back from each trip with more and more of the quality she'd been building since her first solo contact on P6X-211 — the quality of somebody who'd found the shape of what she was for and settled fully into it.

Dessa sent a representative to the Mountain — a woman from the settlement with a records background and strong communication skills and the equanimity of somebody raised in a community that had spent a very long time carefully keeping very old things. Her name was Vela. She spent two weeks at the SGC learning the systems, talking with Daniel, and headed back to P5C-353 as the community's designated node — the network's historical anchor.

Erran sent a letter. Twelve pages, partly in his family's native language, partly Standard, partly a third language Daniel identified as an ancient trade dialect extinct three hundred years — one Erran's family had apparently kept alive in parallel this whole time, because they always had. The letter laid out the communication protocols his family had used for generations to coordinate across a settlement spread across three separate valleys, and the protocols turned out adaptable, and Daniel translated and Carter implemented, and the result was a node running with a sophistication none of them had actually designed, because it had been designed fourteen generations ago by people who'd understood this exact problem from the inside.

Mira sent a message through Dessa's channel, addressed to Buffy, short: I believe it now. You were right.

Buffy read it three times, then filed it away in the place she kept things worth coming back to.

Davan was healing the way people heal from six years of things she wasn't going to put down in writing — slowly, nonlinearly, with the kind of courage it takes to decide healing's worth doing even when the work of it is harder than what you're healing from. Teal'c had taken him to the surface twice already, out of the Mountain into actual Colorado sunlight, and Davan had stood in it for long minutes each time with an expression she couldn't fully read, except to say it was the look of somebody receiving something they'd been without so long they'd stopped believing they'd ever get it back.

The third time Teal'c took him up, Buffy went along, and the three of them stood on the surface in a Colorado afternoon, and Davan looked at the sky a while and finally said, quietly, "The job. You said there is a job."

"Yes," she said.

"When will I be ready for it."

She looked at him. At the six years still sitting in his eyes, and the thing underneath them that had been there before those six years and had survived them anyway — the same thing she'd felt in him the very first moment, in that facility on P4X-719, the thing that wasn't broken.

"You're already doing it," she said. "Right now. Standing here."

He looked back up at the sky.

"Yes," he said, eventually. "I think I understand."

She thought he probably did.

The decentralization finished on a Wednesday.

She ran the final check with Carter and Malek together, node by node, testing every redundancy, confirming every channel. It all worked. The network was live, distributed, no longer dependent on any single point — including her.

She sat in the gate room afterward, on the steps at the base of the ramp, not a spot people usually sat, but one Hammond had never once told anybody not to, and she looked at the gate and thought about three years and everything they'd built.

Shyla found her there, which wasn't surprising, since Shyla had a way of always knowing where Buffy was that she'd long since stopped questioning.

She sat down beside her on the steps.

They looked at the gate together.

"It is done," Shyla said.

"The decentralization," Buffy said. "Yes."

"Not only the decentralization."

Buffy looked at her.

"The second phase," Shyla said. "The documents described it. Coordination. Distribution. The network aware of itself. It is complete."

Buffy looked at the gate. At the symbols she could read, the addresses she had memorized, the eleven worlds she'd been to and the people on every one now connected to each other, and to here, in ways that hadn't existed six months ago.

"Yes," she said.

"Then the third phase," Shyla said.

"Happens when it happens," Buffy said. "Daniel says it's not a goal. It's a consequence."

"Yes," Shyla said. "But you are ready for it."

Buffy considered that. "I don't know if ready's the right word."

"What is the right word."

She thought about it. About what she'd felt in the structure on P4X-639, both hands on the device, the question asked, the answer coming back. About the Builders, and what it meant that they were present in the line itself, in the recognition, in everything the line was made of.

"Open," she said. "I'm open to it."

Shyla was quiet a moment. "On my world," she said, "there is a word for the moment when something very old and very large decides to show itself to the person who has been looking for it. Not the showing itself. The moment before. The moment both things know the showing is about to happen."

"What word," Buffy said.

Shyla said a word in her own language, the one that wasn't Standard, the one she used for things Standard didn't have words for.

"What does it mean," Buffy said.

"Approximately," Shyla said. "It means: the breath before."

Buffy held onto that.

The gate room hummed around them at its frequency, the gate stood in its bay with its patient symbols, and somewhere above them sat fourteen levels of granite and two hundred miles of Colorado sky, and somewhere out in the galaxy eleven worlds with their communities and their expressions of something very old that had always been here, and somewhere in the structure of things themselves, the Builders — present the way whatever something's made of is present in everything it makes.

The breath before.

"Yeah," Buffy said. "That's what it feels like."

They sat on the ramp steps, the gate right there, the work right there, and whatever came next coming at its own pace, in its own time, with the patience of something that had been building longer than the oldest thing either of them could even name.

She was ready.

She was open.

She was, for the first time since she was fifteen years old, exactly where she was supposed to be.

O'Neill found her there twenty minutes later, which she'd expected, and sat down on the step below her and looked at the gate and didn't say anything for a while, which she'd also expected, since O'Neill's silences at the gate were a specific kind she'd learned over three years working right beside him.

He was thinking about Charlie.

She'd never asked. Didn't need to. Some things you learn about a person not from what they say but from the quality of their silence, in certain places, at certain times, and she knew the quality of O'Neill's silence in the gate room the same way she knew the quality of his voice when something mattered, and the quality of his face when something was funny, and the quality of the space that had been building between them since Georgia.

She let him have the silence.

After a while he said, "Shyla was here."

"Yes."

"What'd she say."

"That the second phase is complete."

He was quiet a moment. "And the third."

"Happens when it happens."

He looked at the gate. "You're not worried about it."

"No," she said. "Are you."

He gave it real consideration, the way he considered things worth considering seriously. "I don't know what it is," he said. "Which makes it hard to know whether to worry about it."

"It's a phase transition," she said. "Natural. Built into the design. Not dangerous. Not a loss." She looked at the gate. "I think it's the opposite of a loss."

"Based on."

"Based on what the documents described and what the device communicated and what I understand about what the Builders intended. They built the line to do something at the third phase. They built themselves into it so they'd be present for it. They didn't build something they meant to lose. They built something they meant to complete."

O'Neill sat with that. "And when it completes."

"I don't know exactly," she said. "I don't think the documents could put it in terms that would mean anything to somebody who hadn't already been through the first two phases. Daniel said the notation for it was unlike anything else in the whole text. More complex. Different symbol combinations." A pause. "I think it's going to be something I can't anticipate from here. I think you have to experience it to understand it."

O'Neill was quiet.

"I'm still going to be here," she said. "Afterward. Whatever it turns out to be." She looked at him. "That's what I meant, saying it's not a loss. I'm not going anywhere."

He looked at her.

She met his eyes.

"I'm not going anywhere you can't follow," she said. "Still the deal."

Something shifted in his face. The full version of the almost-smile — and this time it stayed, and she understood it was always going to stay from here on out, that it had been the almost-smile so long only because of things that belonged to him, not her, things changing at their own pace in their own time, the same way the third phase was going to change at its own pace, and that was fine, that was right, that was exactly how the things that actually mattered were supposed to go.

"Yeah," he said. "Okay."

They sat in the gate room, the gate right there, the community right there, spread across eleven worlds and one mountain in Colorado, and the Builders right there too, in the way they were always right there — in the structure of things, in the recognition, in the line itself.

And Ba'al was out there somewhere in his own domain, tangled up in a conflict with Camulus over three disputed systems, and eventually that would end and his attention would swing back around, and when it did they'd deal with it the way they dealt with everything — together, prepared, without flinching.

But that was then.

This was now.

She sat on the ramp steps in the Mountain, O'Neill one step below her, the gate ahead, the work behind her and the next thing waiting, and she thought this was what it looked like when you'd finally done the thing you came to do without losing yourself in the doing of it.

Not finished. The work wasn't finished, and she'd stopped expecting it to finish, because finishing just wasn't the nature of the work — the work changed shape and kept going, that was what it did, that was what she did.

But arrived.

She'd arrived at the place she'd been building toward since a demon on a porch in Sunnydale back in 1999 had told her the world needed her somewhere, and the place was here, and the people were these people, and the work was this work, and she was ready for whatever came next, and the thing after that, and whatever shape the work took from here.

She breathed in.

The Mountain hummed.

The gate waited.

She was here.

Three weeks later, Ba'al's conflict with Camulus ended in a negotiated settlement Teal'c assessed as unstable, likely to collapse within a year, which meant Ba'al would have his attention back sooner rather than later.

A week after that, the SGC's long-range sensors picked up a Goa'uld ha'tak sitting out in the outer system, running silent. Not approaching. Not retreating.

Watching.

Hammond called the senior staff together. O'Neill got there first, looked at the sensor data, said nothing for a moment, then: "How long's it been there."

"Best estimate, thirty-six hours," Carter said.

"Before we finished the decentralization," O'Neill said.

"Yes sir. By about eight hours."

O'Neill studied the data. The ha'tak, watching without acting — Ba'al's whole method, sitting with something until he understood it well enough to act with precision.

"He doesn't know the decentralization's done," O'Neill said.

"No sir," Carter said. "He's watching the Mountain. Saw the last Tok'ra alkesh leave after the final channel test. He's still working out what that means."

"He thinks we're still centralizing," O'Neill said.

"Or he's watching to see if we're finished," Carter said. "Hard to say which."

O'Neill looked at Buffy.

She studied the sensor data. The ha'tak sitting out in the outer system, patient and analytical, exactly the way Ba'al always was — waiting till the picture was clear enough to act on.

"He's already too late," she said. "Whatever he's watching for, we already did it."

"He doesn't know that," O'Neill said.

"No," she said. "He doesn't."

O'Neill looked at Hammond.

Hammond looked at the ha'tak.

"We don't tip him off," Hammond said. "We let him watch, let him build whatever picture he's building, and let him make his move whenever he decides he's ready. Because when he does move, we need to be ready. And we are. So we let him come to us."

"And the network," O'Neill said.

"Is decentralized, operational, and doesn't need the Mountain to function," Hammond said. "Whatever Ba'al does here, the network holds." He looked at Buffy. "That was the whole point."

"Yes sir," she said.

She looked back at the sensor data. At the ha'tak watching from the outer system. At Ba'al's patience, turned toward her like a searchlight, patient and analytical, sooner or later bound to find something to act on.

She thought about Simmons and Mayfield, who'd tried to eliminate her and failed.

She thought about the Fort Benning contractor, whose name had finally come out during the federal proceedings as a man named Ehrlich, who'd also failed.

She thought about six years of things trying to kill her, and the one consistent result of every single attempt.

She thought about the Builders — present in the line, present in the recognition, present in that enormously patient awareness the device communicated, which she now understood wasn't the device at all, but them, directly, through the medium of what they'd built.

She thought about what it meant to be built by something that had anticipated this exact moment, this exact challenge, this exact quality of threat, and had decided a very long time ago that the line would be enough for it.

The Builders hadn't built something they expected to fail.

"Okay," she said.

O'Neill looked at her.

"Let him watch," she said. "When he's ready to move, we're ready for him." She glanced at the sensor data one more time — the ha'tak in the outer system, small on the display, enormous in reality, Ba'al's whole patience embodied in metal and naquadah. "He's going to find out he waited too long."

O'Neill said, "You're confident about that."

"Yes sir," she said.

"Based on."

She thought about all of it. Everything built and everything survived and everything now standing at her back — spread across eleven worlds and one mountain in Colorado and the SGC's gate room, the addresses she knew by heart, the communities she'd sat with, the people now connected to each other in ways that hadn't existed before she followed the list.

She thought about the Builders and what they'd trusted the line to do.

"Based on everything," she said.

Hammond looked at the ha'tak.

"Stand by," he said. "We wait."

They waited.

The Mountain hummed at its frequency, the gate room stayed the gate room, and the ha'tak sat out in the outer system being patient, and Buffy stood in the operations center with her team around her and the network at her back and the line underneath everything, and she breathed in, and she was ready.

Whatever Ba'al had, she was ready.

Whatever the third phase turned out to be, she was ready.

Whatever came next, in whatever shape the work took from here, she was ready, and she wasn't alone, and the things that had built her were present in the structure of everything she was, and she was going to be fine.

She was going to be more than fine.

She was going to be exactly what she'd always been — the standing opposition, the permanent resistance, the thing in the world that does not yield.

The Edjet.

The Chosen One.

Lieutenant Buffy Summers, USAF.

All three. Always all three.

She squared her shoulders.

She got ready for whatever came next.
