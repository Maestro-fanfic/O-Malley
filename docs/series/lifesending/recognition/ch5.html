The Builders

The Tok'ra full disclosure meeting happened on a Thursday.

Jacob came back through the gate with Selmak, plus two other Tok'ra — Anise and Malek — who arrived with the careful diplomacy of people who'd been told something significant was about to get said and had gotten themselves organized around receiving it with proper gravity. Anise had the quality of a scientist who'd decided everything was a specimen, which Buffy noted and filed. Malek had the quality of a strategist, running threat vectors before anything had even been named a threat.

Hammond had the conference room set up on level twenty-eight, the one they used for things that needed everybody looking at each other across a table instead of at a display, and SG-1 was there plus Shyla, plus Jacob and the two additional Tok'ra, plus Hammond at the head of the table with the particular gravity he brought to meetings that were about to change things.

Buffy presented.

She'd written the whole presentation out and then put the writing away, because reading off a page wasn't how this kind of information landed right. This kind of information landed best coming straight from a person, the way Giles had once spoken straight to her in a library in Sunnydale when she was fifteen and he'd sat across from her and said there is a line, and you are the current expression of it.

So she spoke straight.

She gave them everything. The device on P4X-639 and what it had told her. The gate addresses, the chamber notation. Shyla and Erran and Dessa and the settlement on P5C-353 with its twelve active expressions and its records going back fourteen generations. The Edjet, and what Selmak had told them about the Goa'uld's whole historical relationship with the line. The propagation data and what it suggested about the line's flexibility. The disclosure framework, and what it was built to accomplish.

She talked for ninety minutes without a single note.

When she finished, the room went quiet the way rooms go quiet when the information sitting inside them has just rearranged everything everybody thought they understood.

Anise spoke first. "The device communicated a directive," she said. "Find them. You have interpreted this as an operational instruction."

"Yes," Buffy said.

"What if it was not an instruction," Anise said. "What if it was a description. What if it was telling you not what to do, but what you are already in the process of doing regardless of your intentions. What if the coordination is not something you chose, but something that has been building toward inevitability for a very long time and is now entering the phase where it becomes visible."

Buffy looked at her. "That's a meaningful distinction," she said.

"It is," Anise said. "Because if it is a description and not an instruction, the timeline is not yours to control. The coordination is happening because this is the point in the line's history where coordination becomes the natural next phase. Which means the Goa'uld's eventual awareness of it is also not something you can delay by moving carefully. It will happen when it happens, because the pattern is becoming visible regardless of your pace."

"Then we should go faster," Buffy said.

"Then you should go faster," Anise agreed. "If the visibility is inevitable, the only real variable is whether you have established the coordination before or after the Goa'uld understand what they are looking at."

O'Neill looked at Buffy.

She looked back.

"She's right," Buffy said.

"I know," he said, in the tone of a man who'd known since Teal'c's assessment on the alkesh and had just been waiting for the right moment to make the argument unnecessary. "Hammond."

Hammond looked at Anise. At Jacob. At Buffy. His face carried the expression of a man who'd read the disclosure document, approved it as written, and was now getting confirmation that the implementation timeline he'd been prepared to be patient about needed to compress hard.

"How fast," he said.

"Seven contacts in four months," Buffy said. "Not a year. Four months."

"The planning alone for P5C-353 took four weeks," Carter said.

"Because we didn't have what we have now," Buffy said. "Tok'ra records. Dessa's records. The physiological assessment framework Carter built from the P5C-353 data. Erran's documentation of the familial transmission pattern." She looked at Carter. "How long's the planning take if we're not building the methodology from scratch every single time."

Carter thought about it. "Two weeks per contact. Maybe less if the Tok'ra can front-load intelligence on each address."

Malek spoke up for the first time. "The Tok'ra can provide intelligence on five of your seven remaining addresses within a week," he said. "The other two sit in sectors where our information is less current. We can have updated assessments within three weeks."

"One week for five," Buffy said. "Three weeks for the rest. Then two weeks per contact, running parallel wherever the threat profile allows it." She looked at Hammond. "Four months is achievable."

Hammond looked around the table. At the faces. At the weight of what had just been said, and the weight of the alternative to saying it.

"What does parallel contact mean operationally," he said.

"Two teams," O'Neill said. "SG-1 handles the high-risk contacts, the ones in Goa'uld territory or with complicated approach requirements. A second team, SG-3 or SG-5, handles the lower-risk contacts with Tok'ra support. Buffy coordinates both, since she's the common thread, but she doesn't need to be physically present for every single first contact if the profile's benign enough."

Buffy looked at him.

"You can't be in two places at once," he said. "And some of these addresses are going to be straightforward enough for a team with Tok'ra support to make initial contact and let you follow up. Shyla can lead the second team's contact element. She's Edjet. She's got the recognition factor."

Shyla, who'd been listening with her characteristic stillness, looked at O'Neill. "You are proposing that I lead contacts without Buffy present."

"With Tok'ra support and a full SG team," he said. "And Buffy available by comm if you need her." He held her eyes. "You're ready for it. What's your read."

Shyla was quiet a moment. "Yes," she said. "I am ready."

"Then that's the plan," O'Neill said. He looked at Hammond. "Sir."

Hammond looked over the whole plan, at the people in the room who'd just built it out in the last ten minutes off what Anise had said and what the timeline needed and what they collectively already knew how to do.

"Approved," he said. "Malek, first five intelligence assessments on my desk by Friday. Colonel O'Neill, mission planning starts Monday. Full schedule on my desk by next Wednesday." He looked at Buffy. "Cadet Summers."

"Sir."

"The commission paperwork Colonel O'Neill submitted."

She waited.

"Effective immediately," Hammond said. "You're wearing the wrong rank for this meeting. That gets fixed before Monday."

The ceremony was brief and private, exactly what she'd asked for, arranged with the same efficiency O'Neill brought to anything that mattered and that he'd decided not to complicate.

Hammond's office. Hammond, O'Neill, Carter, Daniel, Teal'c, Shyla, and Jacob, who'd asked to stay for it, and whose presence she found she was glad of. O'Neill produced a set of second lieutenant's bars from his jacket pocket with the smooth, unhurried confidence of a man who'd had them ready for some time.

Hammond looked at the bars. Then at O'Neill. "Colonel. The paperwork was approved an hour ago."

"Yes sir."

"You've had those on you since before that."

"Wanted to be prepared, sir."

"Prepared," Hammond said, in the tone of a man who had spent three years cataloguing exactly how much presumption Jack O'Neill was capable of packing into two words and had long since stopped being surprised by new personal bests. He didn't pursue it further, which was its own kind of verdict. "Get on with it, Colonel."

Hammond said the words. She repeated them. The oath of office, the same one she'd taken once before at the Academy, taken again here with a whole lot more understanding of what it actually meant than she'd had the first time, since the first time she'd been nineteen and nervous and the words had been the shape of the thing rather than the substance.

Now the substance was right there in the room. She could feel it — in the faces watching her say the words, in the weight of the bars O'Neill pinned to her collar with the same careful precision he'd brought to everything since a hospital room in Georgia.

He stepped back.

Looked at her.

The full version of the almost-smile.

"Lieutenant Summers," he said.

"Colonel O'Neill," she said.

Teal'c inclined his head. "You have earned this," he said, which was Teal'c's version of a whole speech.

Daniel said, "I have to say, from the very first time Jack told me about you I—" and then seemed to realize the story he was about to tell was going to take way longer than the occasion called for, and switched to, "Congratulations. Genuinely."

Carter hugged her, which was not protocol, and which neither of them acknowledged wasn't protocol, and which said everything Carter needed it to say.

Shyla looked at her with the equanimity she brought to most things and said, "Good," which was also Shyla's version of a whole speech.

Jacob shook her hand. "Selmak wants me to tell you," he said, "that in two thousand years of Tok'ra history, this is the first time we have been present for the commissioning of an Edjet officer. Selmak considers it an honor."

Buffy held onto that. "Tell Selmak thank you," she said. "From me."

Jacob nodded. Something in his face suggested Selmak had heard and taken it in.

Hammond said, "Dismissed. Monday 0800, mission planning. Don't be late."

People started filtering out. Buffy stood in Hammond's office a moment after most of them had gone, looking at the bars on her collar in the reflection off the window that showed the mountain.

O'Neill was still there. He waited until the others cleared out, then said, without any lead-in at all, "Your father would have been proud."

She looked at him.

"I know you didn't have a lot of time with him," he said. "I know he came to your graduation and—" he stopped. "I just think he would've been proud. That's all."

It landed somewhere she hadn't expected it to — the place where the grief for Hank Summers lived, quieter than the grief for the others, but real, the grief for a reconciliation that had only just started and never got the time to finish. She held it without flinching.

"Thank you," she said.

He nodded.

She called Joyce on the way back to her quarters, which weren't temporary in any sense anymore, and Joyce picked up on the second ring and said, "Well?" and Buffy said, "Lieutenant Summers, USAF," and Joyce made the sound she made when she was very happy and only partly succeeding at containing it, and they talked for twenty minutes about nothing significant and everything important, and when the call ended Buffy sat in her quarters with the bars still on her collar and thought about her mother, in a house in Colorado Springs, running a gallery she'd rebuilt from nothing, a whole life rebuilt from nothing, and how this had been the right choice for both of them — the hardest possible version of the right choice, made three years ago in a house in Sunnydale with a demon standing in the living room.

She thought about Whistler. Hadn't thought about him in months. Wondered where he was. Figured he was wherever the Powers That Be needed him, which was just the nature of Whistler, and figured that whatever they were doing managing the universe's affairs, they were watching this particular corner of it with something that probably wasn't satisfaction, since the Powers didn't really do satisfaction, but was in the neighborhood.

She thought about the seven remaining addresses.

She thought about Monday.

She thought about the work.

Then she got up and went to find Daniel, because Dessa's records weren't going to cross-reference themselves, and she had three days before planning started, and she meant to use every one of them.

The next four months were the busiest operational stretch of Buffy's entire life, a sentence that carried real weight given everything else her life had already contained.

She'd think about them afterward in specific moments instead of one continuous span, the way intense stretches always compress in memory down to the images carrying the most weight.

The fourth address, P2X-887, a forested world in neutral space, where the expression of the line was one elderly man named Cato, working alone forty years with the complete equanimity of somebody who'd decided forty years back this was just the shape of his life and had built everything else around it. He'd been expecting somebody to come eventually. Said as much in the first five minutes. He also fed them the best meal she'd had since Joyce's kitchen, and she told him so, and he laughed like a man who'd been alone long enough that unexpected pleasure still felt brand new, and she felt the grief of that right alongside the rightness of having finally come.

The fifth address was Shyla's first solo contact — P6X-211, a desert world, the expression there a woman named Kira, who'd spent six years believing she was cursed. Shyla sat with her four hours, Buffy staying on comm the whole time and never once needed, and when Shyla checked in from the alkesh on the way back she'd said only that it went well, and her voice had the quality of somebody who'd just understood something new about herself.

The sixth was the hardest.

P4X-719 sat in Apophis's territory, which meant an approach careful to the point of painful, a narrow extraction window, and stakes higher than anything since P5C-353. The line's expression there was a young man, barely older than Mira, taken as a slave in a Goa'uld culling operation at age twelve, who'd survived six years of that and hadn't broken and hadn't ever let himself be taken as a host and hadn't known why any of that was true.

His name was Davan.

Buffy went in with O'Neill and Teal'c, spent two hours in a facility she wasn't going to include the full details of in any report she wrote, because the full details weren't the kind of thing you documented — they were the kind you just carried. She found him. Told him what he was. He looked at her with eyes carrying six years of something she recognized without being able to name it, and said, in a flat voice that sounded like whatever was left after everything else had already been taken: "I knew there was a reason."

"Yes," she'd said. "There was a reason."

"Does it get better," he'd said.

She thought about what she'd told Mira. Wanting to believe it is where you start.

"Yes," she'd said. "It gets better."

He'd looked at her a long moment, then nodded, stood up, said, "Then let's go," and she'd taken him out.

Davan was at the Mountain now, in quarters two doors down from Shyla's, working through an orientation Hammond had shaped with the same care he brought to everything that needed it, which was most things. Teal'c had taken a particular interest in him, the quality Teal'c reserved for people who'd survived Goa'uld captivity and come out the other side still standing — a category he understood from the inside.

It was going to take time. Davan was going to take time. That was fine. Time was one thing they had.

Addresses seven through ten went pretty much to plan, the way things go to plan once you've done enough of them that the methodology's solid, the variables mostly known, and the team around you knows their jobs well enough that everybody can improvise off the same baseline.

Not without incidents. The eighth address had a complication involving a minor Goa'uld patrol wandering into the sector, which meant forty-five minutes of very careful nothing while it passed — during which Buffy and the settlement elder she was mid-conversation with simply kept talking, at a volume her Slayer hearing could pick up and no Goa'uld scanner had a hope of catching. The elder, a woman named Vastra with the quality of somebody who'd been managing impossible situations for a very long time, hadn't blinked once.

The ninth address produced a data point Carter was going to be chewing on for at least a year — the line's expression on P7X-993 came with something that had no direct match in any other expression they'd seen: a navigational quality that showed up as an ability to read gate addresses just by touch, put a hand on a DHD and know exactly where it led.

Buffy couldn't do that. She'd tried. Nothing.

The young woman on P7X-993 who could — her name was Sora — looked at Buffy's total inability to replicate it with the expression of somebody who'd assumed this was universal and was now busy recalculating. "You cannot feel them," Sora said.

"No," Buffy said.

"That is very strange," Sora said.

"My experience exactly," Daniel said from the background, and got a look from O'Neill that communicated everything necessary about the correct time for enthusiasm.

The tenth address sat on a water world, the settlement island-based, the approach requiring the alkesh to do something with water that Teal'c handled without a word of commentary and Carter filmed, since she planned on publishing something about it eventually and needed the footage. The expression there was a family, consistent with Erran's model — three siblings, Edjet on their island their whole lives, who met Buffy and Shyla with the warmth of people who'd always been just slightly outside the community's full understanding of things, and were now being told that the outside was populated.

The eldest of the three, a man named Fen, said, "We wondered if there were others." His sister said, "We argued about it." The youngest sister said, "I always said yes," with the satisfaction of somebody whose long-held position had just been officially confirmed.

Buffy liked all three of them instantly and completely.

The eleventh address was the last one.

P3Y-294 wasn't in Goa'uld territory and wasn't in neutral space either. It sat in the space the SGC's maps just marked unexplored, which meant Ba'al had never been out there, and the Tok'ra had no records on it, which meant the intelligence gap was serious and the approach needed caution well past their standard protocol.

Malek had suggested waiting. Get more data first. Build a better picture before committing.

Buffy had looked at the gate address in the chamber notation on P4X-639 — the last one, the one that had been sitting there since before the Ancients had come and gone — and said, "We go."

O'Neill had looked at her. She'd looked back. He'd said, "We go."

Malek accepted it with the grace of somebody who'd decided arguing with two people already in complete alignment wasn't an inefficiency worth engaging in.

The plan was minimal, because minimal was what you built when you had no intelligence to plan from. Alkesh insertion. Long-range survey before touchdown. Flexible contact protocol depending on what the survey showed. Full extraction readiness at every point.

Teal'c flew them in.

The planet announced itself from orbit as green and mountainous, no visible Goa'uld infrastructure, no energy signatures consistent with active technology — that particular quality of a world that had been left alone a long, long time. The survey ran three full passes. Nothing alarming. A settlement of some kind in the northern hemisphere, small, maybe fifty people, sitting in a valley that from orbit looked like every other valley in the galaxy that ever held a community — like people had found a place the land offered something and just stayed.

Teal'c set them down six kilometers out.

Buffy stepped out of the alkesh and felt the planet.

And stopped.

Because P3Y-294 felt like P5C-353, only more so, a sentence she'd have sworn was impossible after P5C-353, and it felt like P4X-639, only personal, and it felt like—

"Summers," O'Neill said.

"Give me a second," she said.

She stood in the meadow where Teal'c had set them down and opened everything she had, the full range of what the Slayer could read, and let the planet tell her what it was.

It was old. Older than any of the others. The oldest place she'd ever stood, in the sense that the accumulated weight of the line here ran older than Dessa's fourteen generations, older than anything she'd encountered across any of the other eleven addresses — old in a way she could feel running down through the ground like roots run through soil, down past any depth she could even measure.

"Buffy," Shyla said beside her. And the fact that Shyla had used her first name instead of anything else told her Shyla was feeling it too.

"I know," Buffy said.

"What is it," O'Neill said.

She looked at him. "This is where it started," she said. "I can't prove that. Not scientifically. But this is where the line started."

The team went quiet.

"The origin point," Daniel said, very quietly, the voice he used trying to hold something too big to hold. "The first place it was ever active."

"Maybe," she said. "I don't know for sure. But it's older than everything else we've found, and it's in the ground here in a way it isn't anywhere else, and—" she stopped. "And the device said the line was designed. Placed. Woven in before the Goa'uld existed. If it was placed somewhere specifically, before it propagated—"

"Then there'd be an origin," Daniel said.

"And this is it," she said.

She looked toward the valley six kilometers north, at the fifty people living there who'd been the Edjet on this world longer than she could even calculate, with no idea somebody was coming to tell them what they were.

Or maybe they did know. This place was old enough they might know it the same way Dessa's community knew — in the bone, in a record older than writing itself.

"We go carefully," O'Neill said, which was what he always said, and which here meant a whole lot more than it had on any of the other eleven.

"Yes sir," she said.

They moved.

The settlement was older than it looked, and it looked very old already.

The structures at its center had that quality of things repaired and rebuilt around an original shape so many times over that original and restoration had stopped being distinguishable at all, the way very old things absorb their own history right into their surface until the surface is the history. The people who came out to meet them as they approached the settlement's edge carried a quality Buffy had been building a vocabulary for over the last four months, and here it showed up more fully than anywhere else she'd seen it.

Seven of them stood at the settlement's edge. Seven, out of fifty.

Seven active expressions of the line in a community of fifty people.

She felt each one distinctly, and they felt her right back, and the whole exchange wasn't dramatic. It was quiet. The feeling of something that had been one way for a very long time finally completing a step it had been building toward.

The one standing at the center of the seven was a man old in a way she couldn't put a number to, carrying the posture and stillness and quality of somebody who'd held this so long it had become indistinguishable from what he simply was, and he looked at her the way the device on P4X-639 had looked at her — with attention that was both enormous and completely specific.

"You found all of them," he said. His Standard was very good, which meant this settlement had been receiving gate travelers a long time. "The ones on the list."

She looked at him. "You know about the list."

"We have records," he said. "From before the ring network was built. We know what the list is, and what it is for, and we have been waiting for someone to follow it."

Buffy held onto that. "How long have you been waiting."

The old man looked at her with the equanimity of somebody for whom the length of a wait was just a fact, not a burden. "A long time," he said. "But the right length of time. Things arrive when they are ready."

She thought about Whistler on her porch in Sunnydale. About the Powers That Be, managing timelines she couldn't see. She thought about a device on P4X-639 that had been waiting for something like her since before the Ancients, a line that had been in the biology of living things since before the Goa'uld even existed, and a girl in a library in Sunnydale who hadn't been ready and had gone anyway, because ready was a direction, not a state.

"My name is Buffy Summers," she said. "I came through the gate from Earth. I found the list. I've been following it for four months. Can we come in."

The old man looked at her, then at Shyla, then at the team behind her, then at the sky above them, then at something she couldn't see, which might have been nothing, and might have been something she just didn't have the sense to detect.

Then he smiled.

"Yes," he said. "Come in. We have been keeping a record for you."

They stayed three days.

The record was the reason. Not the kind of record Dessa's community had — fourteen generations of local history. This was something else. The settlement's elders described it as a responsibility passed down from before anybody's oldest memory — a set of documents in a script that wasn't Ancient, wasn't Goa'uld, wasn't any language Daniel had ever run into, kept in a room in the settlement's oldest building, kept there since before the current building even existed.

Daniel sat with the documents two whole days and came out of it looking the way he always looked after running into something that rearranges everything.

"It's not a language," he told the team on the evening of the second day. They sat around a fire the settlement had offered them, outside, under a sky that was almost Earth-like but not quite. "Or not only a language. It's a notation system, like what was on the chamber walls at P4X-639. The exact same notation system."

"The same," Buffy said.

"Identical," he said. "Written by the same — whatever wrote the chamber walls. Same origin." He checked his notes. "And what it notates is a continuation. The chamber on P4X-639 recorded where the line had been active up until the device was built. These documents record what the line is for. What it does at the next scale."

"What scale," O'Neill said.

"The coordination scale," Daniel said. "What happens once the expressions find each other. Once the list is followed. Once the line's distributed and aware of its own distribution." A pause. "The chamber told Buffy to find them. These documents tell her what happens after she does."

Everyone looked at Buffy.

She looked at Daniel. "Tell me," she said.

"The line as individual expressions," Daniel said, "is the first scale. The deterrent effect. Individual protection of specific places. That's what it's been doing since the very beginning." He checked his notes. "The line as coordination is the second scale. That's what the device was pointing toward. Expressions knowing each other, the pattern becoming visible, the deterrent effect turning systemic instead of local."

"And the third scale," Buffy said.

Daniel looked at her.

"There's a third scale," she said. "That's where you're going with this."

"Yes," he said. "The documents describe a third phase. The notation for it's different from the first two, more complex, symbol combinations that don't show up anywhere else in the text except the final section. I can give you an approximate translation. It won't be precise."

"Give me what you've got," she said.

Daniel looked down at his notes a second, then set them aside and spoke from what he understood instead of what he'd written, which was how he communicated the things he was surest of.

"The third phase," he said, "is described as the moment the line becomes aware of what made it. When the expressions, coordinated, build up enough collective presence to see back along their own origin. To understand not just what the line is, but where it came from, what built it, and why." He held her eyes steadily. "The documents don't describe this as something you work toward. They describe it as something that happens once coordination hits a threshold. A natural phase transition. Like water becoming steam. Not a goal. A consequence of the first two phases finishing."

The fire crackled between them.

"We're in phase two," Buffy said.

"Yes," Daniel said.

"How close to the threshold."

"I don't know," he said. "The notation doesn't give a number. It describes a quality. A felt sense of something shifting." He looked at her. "You'll know when it's happening."

She looked into the fire.

She thought about the device. About what it had felt like. About recognition, enormous patience, something very old that had been waiting for something specific.

She thought about what Anise had said. That the coordination wasn't something she'd chosen but something becoming inevitable. That the timing belonged to the line, not to her. That she was the current expression of something that had been building toward this since before the Goa'uld existed.

She thought about what that meant for the phase after this one.

"The thing that built the line," she said. "The documents. Do they say what it was."

Daniel was quiet a moment. "They use one word for it," he said. "Repeated throughout, in a position that suggests it's a proper noun, not a descriptor. I can't translate it. The notation for it doesn't appear anywhere else we've got. But the old man here, the elder, his people have a name for it in their own language. A word passed down alongside the documents."

"What word," she said.

Daniel looked at her with the expression of somebody who understood the answer was huge and had decided to just give it to her simply. "Builders," he said. "That's the closest translation. Not architect. Not creator. Builders. The ones who built what needed building, and then left, and trusted what they'd built to do the work."

Buffy sat with that.

The fire shifted between them, the alien sky turned slowly overhead, the settlement breathed around them with all its old deep weight, and she thought about Builders, about something that had looked at the shape of the future and set down exactly what would be needed exactly where it would be needed, and then trusted it to grow.

She thought about a fifteen-year-old girl in a graveyard in Sunnydale, not yet knowing she was anything in particular, angry and grieving and scared, going out to fight anyway because the alternative was not going.

She thought about what the Builders had trusted.

"Okay," she said.

O'Neill looked at her.

"It's a lot," she said. "Going to need time to sit with it. But okay."

"Take what you need," he said, which was what he said when he meant I'm here and the time is yours.

"Tomorrow," she said. "I want to talk to the elder. About what the community knows. About what they've been keeping, and why." She looked at Daniel. "And I want to read the documents."

"You can't read the notation," Daniel said.

"I could read some of it on the chamber walls," she said. "After the device. More than I could before."

Daniel looked at her. Then at his notes. "You think contact with the device changed what you can access."

"I think the line isn't a fixed thing," she said. "I think it develops. I think what I could read after the device was different from what I could read before, and I don't know what I can read now." She glanced at the documents, set carefully in the shelter of the old building's doorway for the night. "Tomorrow I want to find out."

She could read more of it.

Not all of it. Not the third-phase notation, the part Daniel had called the most complex. But the first two thirds she could read with the same partial fluency she'd had on the chamber walls — the same sense of the notation as music, a system of symbols carrying something that wasn't exactly language but was comprehensible to the right reader.

She read six hours straight, Daniel reading right alongside her, and they compared notes on what each of them understood, and what came out of the comparison was more complete than either of them alone — which Daniel said was exactly what you'd expect if the notation was built to be read by both kinds of minds together, Edjet and not, working in tandem.

What they understood, by the end of it, was this.

The Builders had made the line before the current age of the galaxy, because they'd seen what was coming and calculated what would be needed. Not the Goa'uld specifically, not Earth, not any of the individual things that had happened over the last ten thousand years. The calculation ran broader than that, at the level of galactic development cycles — the patterns by which intelligent species rise and expand and clash, and the patterns by which those clashes eventually resolve.

The line wasn't a weapon. Had never been a weapon. The chamber on P4X-639 had already shown her that — understanding the Slayer as structural, not aggressive, the thing that stays rather than the thing that strikes. The documents confirmed it at a scale that was a lot harder to hold in her head, but made total sense once she had it.

The line was a guarantor. A biological constant, built into life's development across this galaxy the way certain physical constants get built into the development of matter — making sure certain outcomes stayed possible regardless of whatever the variables did.

The outcome the line guaranteed wasn't specific. It was categorical. It guaranteed there'd always be something in the galaxy that couldn't be fully controlled. Couldn't be taken. Couldn't be turned into a tool of domination. That would persist as an expression of what the Builders had apparently considered the single most fundamental property of healthy development: the capacity to resist.

Not aggressive resistance. Not war. The capacity to resist — a different thing entirely. The standing opposition. The Edjet.

As long as the line existed and stayed active, certain kinds of total control just weren't possible. Not because the line would fight them — though it would. Because the line was what it was, fundamentally, at some level underneath fighting entirely, and what it was could not be fully overwritten.

She looked up from the documents.

Daniel was watching her.

"You understood the last section," he said. Not a question.

"Some of it," she said.

"More than yesterday."

"Yes," she said.

"The phase transition," he said.

"I don't know," she said. "Maybe. I don't feel it happening. But I understand more than I did." She looked back at the documents. "The Builders. Are they still here."

"The documents don't say," Daniel said. "The last section, the part I can't fully read, might get into that. But what I can read suggests they considered their work here finished once the line was established. What happened to them after that is—" he paused. "Unknown."

"The Ancients ascended," she said.

"Yes."

"Could the Builders have done the same thing."

Daniel was quiet a moment. "It's possible," he said carefully. "There are accounts from multiple cultures of beings who existed in the physical universe and then didn't — who shifted into some other mode of existence. The Ancients. The Nox, to a limited degree. The Ori, though theirs is—" he stopped. "It's possible. If the Builders were as old and as advanced as the documents suggest, and had finished what they came to do." He looked at her. "It's possible they're still here. Just not in any form we can detect."

She thought about that.

She thought about the device on P4X-639, waiting. About this settlement, keeping its records, waiting. About the old man who'd said we have been waiting for someone to follow the list, who'd smiled when she walked in, the smile of somebody whose wait had ended exactly the way they'd hoped.

She thought about Whistler, and the Powers That Be, running the universe's affairs from somewhere she couldn't see. She thought about higher planes and ascended beings and all the layers of the universe the SGC was only just beginning to understand even existed.

She thought about Builders who'd made something, trusted it, and left.

"Daniel," she said.

"Yes."

"When I touched the device. It felt like recognition. Like something that knew what I was. What if it wasn't just the device."

Daniel held her eyes steadily.

"What if the Builders didn't leave," she said. "Or not entirely. What if they built themselves into it. Into the recognition. Into whatever the device was doing when it acknowledged me." She looked down at her hands. At the calluses on her knuckles. At the bars on her collar. At the whole life she'd built around the line since she was fifteen. "What if the feeling I had — being known, specifically — wasn't the device knowing me. What if it was them."

Daniel didn't say anything for a long time.

Then he said, "That would mean the third phase isn't about understanding what built the line. It's about understanding that what built the line is still here. And always has been."

She held that.

Outside the settlement, the alien morning went on doing what mornings do, arriving no matter what you'd been thinking about the night before, and the old man who kept the records was probably already up, since people who'd kept things a very long time tended to rise early, and O'Neill was going to come looking for her in about thirty minutes because she'd been in this room six hours and he'd have been timing it.

She looked at the documents.

At the notation she could read.

At the section she couldn't — the third-phase section, the symbol combinations Daniel had called unlike anything else in the whole text.

She put her hand on the last page.

The symbols were still just symbols. She couldn't read them any better than yesterday. But the feeling she'd had on P4X-639 — the enormous, patient awareness, the thing that felt like recognition — sat right at the edge of her perception, faint and quiet, at the very outer limit of what she could pick up.

Not the document.

Something that had always been there, that she was only now able to feel.

She took her hand off the page.

She looked at Daniel.

"Tomorrow," she said. "When we get back to the Mountain. I want to go back to P4X-639."

"Why," he said.

"Because I have a question," she said. "And I think the device has an answer. And I think I'm ready to hear it now, in a way I wasn't ready the first time."

Daniel looked at her the way he did when he was holding excitement very carefully, both hands, so it wouldn't get away from him. "What question," he said.

She looked at the last page of the documents. At the notation she couldn't read, describing a phase transition she might already be halfway inside. At the word Builders, sitting there in its proper-noun position, over and over.

"I want to ask who built us," she said. "And I want to ask if they're still here."

Daniel let out a slow breath.

"That," he said, "is the right question."

Morning came in through the doorway, the fire had burned down to coals, and somewhere outside O'Neill was starting to go looking for her, and the work wasn't finished — the work wasn't going to be finished for a long time — but the next step was clear now, and the one after that was starting to come into view, and she was ready for both.

She stood up.

"Let's go home," she said.
