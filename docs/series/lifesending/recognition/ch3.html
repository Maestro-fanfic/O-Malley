What They Thought They Controlled

The Tok'ra brief happened on a Tuesday.

Jacob Carter came through the gate at 1400 carrying that particular energy he brought to everything — the energy of a man who'd made peace with the fact that his life had gone completely impossible and had decided to find it interesting instead of exhausting. He greeted Sam with the hug of a father who'd learned to pack a whole lot of feeling into a short embrace, since longer embraces made them both uncomfortable, the way people get who love each other and haven't always been great at showing it. He greeted O'Neill with the handshake of two people who'd been through enough together that the handshake was doing plenty of talking on its own. He greeted Buffy by stopping and looking at her a moment.

"So," Jacob said. "You're the one."

"Apparently," Buffy said.

Jacob looked at her with that double-layered quality Tok'ra hosts had — the human assessment running right alongside whatever Selmak was doing, its own separate evaluation on a frequency she could just barely feel at the edges of her Slayer sense. Not hostile. Deeply curious.

"Selmak wants me to tell you," Jacob said, "the word the Tok'ra use for what you are is very old. Older than the Tok'ra. Older than the Goa'uld. They weren't even sure it was real. It's in their oldest records as something the earliest Goa'uld encountered on Earth and reported to Egeria, who thought it significant enough to write down but couldn't confirm independently."

"What did the earliest Goa'uld report," Buffy said.

"That there was a human female who couldn't be taken as a host," Jacob said. "That the symbiote died rather than blend with her. That it happened more than once, more than one symbiote, always with a specific type of human female, always young, always the same quality the Goa'uld couldn't quite describe except to say she felt wrong from the inside." He held her eyes steadily. "Wrong for a Goa'uld, Selmak clarifies. Not wrong any other way."

Buffy took that in. "The Goa'uld know about the line."

"The oldest ones do," Jacob said. "Ra knew. Apep knew. They treated it as a curiosity, not a threat, since the phenomenon was rare and localized and the host rejection never stopped them from doing anything they actually wanted to do. There were always other hosts." A pause. "That math changes if the line's widespread."

"Which is why we're having this conversation," O'Neill said.

"Which is why we're having this conversation," Jacob agreed. He looked at Hammond. "George, what exactly are you proposing to tell us."

"Everything," Hammond said. "Staged. Starting today."

The brief took six hours.

Jacob and Selmak listened the way the Tok'ra listened — the layered attention of two intelligences running in parallel, occasionally trading vocal control to ask questions from different angles. Selmak's questions ran older and more precise, reaching straight for the cosmological framework with the instinct of a mind that had been alive two thousand years and picked up enough context along the way to know exactly which questions mattered. Jacob's questions ran sharper and more operational, going right for the threat implications and the ally implications and the what-do-we-do-with-this that was the military intelligence officer he'd been long before the symbiote, and still was after.

Daniel presented the P4X-639 data. Carter presented the physiological analysis she'd been building since Buffy's very first week at the SGC, comprehensive the way Carter's work was always comprehensive, and Jacob looked it over with the expression of a man recalibrating several things at once. Buffy talked through Erran and P3X-774 and the familial transmission and the saturation origin, and it produced a silence out of Selmak that Jacob later described as the Tok'ra equivalent of sitting down heavily.

"The implication," Selmak said, speaking directly, "is that the phenomenon is not random. Not an accident of biology. It was designed. And the designer predates our oldest records."

"That's our working hypothesis," Daniel said.

"It is not a hypothesis," Selmak said. "The Tok'ra records from the early Goa'uld period describe something the first symbiotes called the Edjet. A word in the oldest form of the Goa'uld language, no direct translation, but meaning roughly the standing opposition. The permanent resistance. The thing in the world that does not yield. The Goa'uld who encountered it on Earth reported that it predated human civilization. That it was already in humans before the Goa'uld ever arrived, woven into a specific line of descent, and had clearly been placed there by something that understood exactly what the Goa'uld were going to do before the Goa'uld had done it."

The room went quiet.

"Something anticipated the Goa'uld," Daniel said. "And built a countermeasure. Before the Goa'uld even existed as a threat."

"Before the Goa'uld existed at all," Selmak said. "The Edjet's estimated age in the early records is vague but consistent. It puts the origin before the current Goa'uld species had even formed. Which means whatever created it was working on a timescale that makes the Ancients look recent."

O'Neill looked at Buffy.

Buffy looked down at her hands. At the calluses on her knuckles, there since she was fifteen, from ten years of hitting things that needed hitting, and thought about something that had once looked at the shape of a whole galaxy and a species that hadn't even been invented yet and decided to build a standing opposition right into the biology of the creatures that would eventually need protecting from it.

"That's a lot of forethought," she said.

"Yes," Selmak said. "It is."

"Do the Tok'ra records say what created it," O'Neill said.

"No," Selmak said. "The early Goa'uld didn't know and couldn't find out. Whatever it was, it wasn't around to interrogate. There's one more piece in the records. The early Goa'uld who reported the Edjet to Egeria included a warning. Said the phenomenon was rare and localized and manageable, as long as it stayed that way. Said if it ever went widespread — if the Edjet found each other and coordinated — the Goa'uld would have a problem conventional force couldn't solve."

"Define widespread," O'Neill said.

"The record doesn't," Selmak said. "But the fact that Ra knew about it and never systematically eliminated it suggests either he couldn't, or he didn't think it worth the resources at the time."

"He couldn't," Buffy said.

Everyone looked at her.

"The Slayer can't be taken as a host," she said. "Which means a Goa'uld can't control her directly. And if what Erran described holds up — if familial transmission means multiple active expressions on some worlds — you can't remove the line by removing one person. You'd have to wipe out the whole family. And then the next generation." She looked at Selmak. "You'd be playing whack-a-mole forever against something that has more patience than you do."

"A reasonable assessment," Selmak said. "Ra apparently reached the same conclusion."

"So he left it alone," O'Neill said.

"He left it alone," Selmak confirmed. "As did most of the System Lords after him. The Edjet was an inconvenience, not an existential concern, as long as it stayed spread out and uncoordinated."

O'Neill looked back at Buffy. She could see him doing the math.

"We're coordinating it," he said. Flat. Not exactly alarmed. Just naming the thing.

"Yes," she said.

"Which changes the calculus for the Goa'uld."

"Yes," she said.

"So once the Goa'uld figure out what we're doing," he said, "we become a priority target."

"We were always a priority target," Buffy said. "Now we're a priority target they've got a specific historical reason to take seriously."

O'Neill looked at Hammond. Hammond looked at Jacob. Jacob looked at Selmak's reflection in the table, which was where he tended to look during an internal conversation.

"The Tok'ra," Jacob said after a moment, "are very interested in being part of this."

"I expected that," Hammond said.

"Not as a controlling interest," Jacob said. "As a partner. What Selmak knows, what the Tok'ra records hold about the Goa'uld's historical relationship with the Edjet — that's directly relevant to what you're doing. We can help you figure out which of your addresses fall in Goa'uld-controlled space, which System Lords know the historical record, and which ones are likely to react aggressively once they figure out what's happening."

"In exchange for," O'Neill said.

"In exchange for being told what you find," Jacob said. "And being included in planning for any contact mission that goes into contested space."

O'Neill looked at Hammond.

Hammond was quiet a second. "That's a reasonable basis for a partnership," he said.

"See, this is why I like the Tok'ra," O'Neill said. "Reasonable people. Good with a partnership. Not a lot of drama."

"Colonel," Hammond said, without looking up from his notes, in the flat tired register of a man who had absorbed six hours of galaxy-altering revelation and did not currently have the reserves for whatever O'Neill was about to build out of one sentence.

"Just an observation, sir."

"Noted," Hammond said. "Meeting's adjourned."

The meeting broke at 2100, and Buffy went to the commissary, because six hours of briefing plus the weight of everything Selmak had told them called for food and quiet in about equal measure, and the commissary at 2100 was mostly empty and the coffee was fresh, since somebody had made a pot twenty minutes back — second time that had happened, and she was starting to suspect it wasn't an accident.

Shyla was already there.

Buffy got food and sat across from her, and they ate in that comfortable silence they'd built up — the silence of two people who processed the same way and had learned the other one's processing didn't need filling.

After a while Shyla said, "The Tok'ra brief."

"You heard."

"I was in the adjacent corridor for part of it," Shyla said, without apologizing for it. "The Edjet."

"Yeah."

"My grandmother used that word," Shyla said. "Not in Standard. In her own language. She said it was the oldest word she knew for what we were. She never knew where it came from."

"From the Goa'uld," Buffy said. "Originally. It's what they called us."

Shyla took that in. "The enemy named us."

"The enemy documented us," Buffy said. "Before we ever documented ourselves."

Shyla looked down at her food. "On my world the Goa'uld culled and left. Never settled. I always assumed it was because my world had nothing they wanted. Maybe it was something else."

"Maybe," Buffy said. She thought about everything Selmak had said — the whack-a-mole, the patience, the calculation Ra had made and every System Lord after him had inherited without question. "We may never know for sure. But the pattern fits."

"If the Goa'uld avoided worlds with the line," Shyla said slowly, "then the worlds on your address list may be more intact than average. More people. More civilization. Because the Goa'uld stayed away."

Buffy hadn't thought of that. She looked at Shyla.

"The line protected them without ever knowing it," Shyla said. "Not by fighting. Just by being present. The Goa'uld stayed away because the math didn't work in their favor. That is a different kind of protection than I understood I was providing."

"Yeah," Buffy said. "It is."

She thought about what the device on P4X-639 had felt like. About that enormous, patient thing that had designed all this, whatever it was, however long ago, and had built it right into the biology of creatures that would need it before those creatures even existed. She thought about the Slayer the way she'd been thinking about it for ten years — the thing that fought, the weapon at the end of the line — and she thought about what it meant to understand it, instead, as the thing that stayed, that persisted, that made a place expensive enough to avoid entirely.

Both things. It was both things. It had always been both things, and she'd only ever seen the one.

"Shyla," she said.

"Yes."

"On P2X-416. The culling happened."

"Yes."

"Which means the line's presence there wasn't enough to deter the Goa'uld. In that case."

Shyla was quiet a moment. "I was young," she said. "The activation was recent. I had not been present long enough to be a deterrent. Heru'ur's Jaffa came under a standing order, not a strategic decision. They did not know I was there." A pause. "If they had known, I believe they would have left."

"Because the cost-benefit."

"Because the cost-benefit," she agreed.

"Then coordination changes the math both ways," Buffy said. "The Goa'uld will eventually know we're coordinating. Which makes us a bigger target. But it also means every world on that list with an active expression becomes visible to them as a world with the Edjet, which might deter future culling operations before we even make contact."

Shyla held her eyes. "You are saying our coordination is at once the thing that protects the worlds on your list, and the thing that endangers us."

"Yes," Buffy said.

"That is a difficult balance."

"Most of the important ones are," Buffy said.

They ate in silence a while. The commissary hummed on at its nighttime frequency.

Then Shyla said, "What did it feel like. The device."

Buffy looked at her. "Which part."

"All of it."

Buffy thought about how to say it. She'd already written it up for Hammond's report and produced something accurate and completely insufficient, because the accurate version had none of the actual texture.

"Like being recognized," she said. "By something very large that had been waiting a very long time. Like—" she paused. "Like the first time somebody told me what I was. Giles. My Watcher. He sat across from me and said there is a line, and you are the current expression of it, and you are not alone. And the recognition in that was the single most important thing that had happened to me up to that point, because I'd been doing the job without ever knowing it was a job, and hearing that it was a job, that it had structure and history and purpose — that changed everything." She looked down at the table. "The device felt like that, but at a scale I don't have language for. Being recognized, not by a person, but by something that built the recognition right into itself before I ever existed."

Shyla was quiet.

"Did you feel it too," Buffy said. "When you came through the gate. When you got here."

Shyla considered. "Not the same thing," she said. "But something adjacent. Being seen by people who knew what they were seeing. You. Specifically you."

Buffy held onto that.

"The anchor thing," she said. "The word your world had."

"Yes," Shyla said. "But also simply being seen by another expression of the same thing. Looking at you is like looking at—" she reached for the word — "a proof," she said. "That what I am is real, and has a shape, and I am not the only shape it takes."

"Yeah," Buffy said. "That's what it felt like. From both sides."

They sat with that a moment — two expressions of something very old, eating bad commissary food at 2130 in a mountain in Colorado, a sentence that would've sounded flat-out impossible to the girl who'd sat crying in a graveyard doorway in Sunnydale, in the rain.

Not impossible anymore.

The third contact mission took four weeks of planning.

That was because the third address on Daniel's priority list — cross-referenced against the Tok'ra records Jacob had provided the week after the brief — was P5C-353. And P5C-353 sat in Ba'al's territory.

Not one of Ba'al's controlled worlds. In his territory, which mattered operationally, since it meant the planet fell inside his sphere of influence without him ever having actively settled or regularly visited it — left alone, but not unmonitored, which meant any gate activation there was going to register somewhere in Ba'al's intelligence apparatus within hours.

That was problem one.

Problem two was that the survivor anomaly on P5C-353 wasn't a single individual. It was a whole community. The SGC's one visit there, two years ago, a routine survey logged and then deprioritized, had noted a settlement of roughly two hundred people living in a mountain valley with a level of social cohesion and physical capability the mission report called unusual, without ever explaining why. The medical officer running the health assessments had flagged four separate individuals with anomalous physiological profiles — a number that didn't make sense under any framework except the one Buffy was operating with now.

Four. On one planet. In one settlement of two hundred people.

She and Erran had talked about saturation. About familial transmission running deep enough to produce multiple active expressions. P5C-353 looked like an extreme version of that — a community where the line had been running in the founding population long enough that a real chunk of the current population carried some expression of it.

Daniel's word for it, once she explained it to him, was propagation. The line propagating through a population over generations, the way certain genetic traits do when they confer a big enough survival advantage. He wrote twelve pages about it overnight and had them in front of Hammond by breakfast.

Hammond read them, looked up at the ceiling a second, and said, "How many of them."

"Unknown," Daniel said. "The survey was brief, the medical assessments incomplete. Four confirmed anomalies. Could be a lot higher."

"Could be a lot higher," Carter said, "if the propagation's been running the full period the gate address notation suggests. Which puts the origin of the founding population on P5C-353 at approximately—"

"A very long time ago," Daniel said.

"A very long time ago," Carter confirmed.

Hammond looked at Buffy. "Your assessment."

"We have to go," she said. "Carefully, and soon, before Ba'al figures out what he's got sitting in his own territory. If that community's what Daniel thinks it is, Ba'al finding it first is a lot worse than anything else on our current threat list."

"Agreed," O'Neill said, wearing the look he got whenever something moved from problem to problem we're solving right now. "Four weeks to plan. Tok'ra support on the approach. Alkesh insertion, not the gate. We go in quiet, come out quiet, nobody in Ba'al's apparatus ever notices we were there."

"That's optimistic," Carter said.

"It's a goal," O'Neill said. "We plan for the goal and build contingencies for when the goal doesn't survive contact with reality."

"How many contingencies," Carter said.

O'Neill looked at Buffy.

"More than we had for the other two," Buffy said. "P5C-353's different. Could be a whole lot more significant. Planning needs to match that."

O'Neill nodded. "Carter, full threat assessment on Ba'al's current patrol disposition in that sector. Daniel, everything the Tok'ra records have on P5C-353's history. Teal'c, you know Ba'al's operational patterns better than anybody here — I want your read on how much time we've got before an unscheduled gate activation becomes a priority for him."

"Ba'al's intelligence apparatus is efficient," Teal'c said. "An unscheduled activation in that sector would generate a query within six hours, and an investigative response within twenty-four if the query went unanswered."

"So we're not using the gate," O'Neill said.

"We are not using the gate," Teal'c confirmed.

"Alkesh insertion point," O'Neill said to Carter.

"Working on it," Carter said, already pulling up the sector map.

O'Neill looked at Buffy. "You're going to want Shyla on this one."

"Was about to ask," Buffy said.

"I know," he said. "Answer's yes. Hammond's already approved it." He looked at the map Carter was building — the planet sitting in Ba'al's territory with its community of two hundred people and an unknown number of expressions of something very old that had been running in that population longer than anybody had records for. "Four weeks," he said. "Then we go."

The four weeks were full the way preparatory periods get full — densely, without a lot of slack, the kind of busy that leaves you tired in the evenings in a productive way, not a draining one. Carter ran threat assessments. Daniel read Tok'ra records and produced analyses that kept getting more layered the deeper the records went. Teal'c walked O'Neill through Ba'al's operational patterns with the specificity of a man who'd served under Ba'al himself and had turned that whole experience into the kind of intelligence you only get from somebody who used to be on the inside of what they're now assessing from outside.

Buffy trained with Shyla.

They'd settled into a rhythm in the gym unlike any training relationship Buffy had ever had, because it wasn't teacher and student in either direction. Two different expressions of the same underlying thing, comparing notes on how the thing worked, and what came out of it wasn't instruction, it was mutual calibration — one of them doing something, the other watching and adjusting, back and forth toward whatever synthesis emerged.

Teal'c watched them on the third morning and said, "You are both improving," which from Teal'c counted as a standing ovation.

O'Neill watched on the fifth morning and said nothing, which was his version of the same thing.

Daniel stopped by once, watched about thirty seconds, then came back with his recorder running because he'd decided he was witnessing something worth documenting. Buffy threw a towel at him and he left.

Evenings, she wrote. The disclosure framework document took shape the way important documents do — slowly, over a lot of revisions, each draft landing a little closer to precise instead of approximate. Hammond read each one and sent it back with notes that were sparse and dead accurate, the notes of a man who'd spent a career writing and reading documents that actually mattered and knew exactly the difference between a draft and the real thing.

O'Neill read each draft and sent it back with two or three words scrawled in the margin per page — fewer words than Hammond, and more useful, since O'Neill's words always found the exact spot where the logic had a gap she'd been walking around instead of through.

She fixed the gaps. The document got better.

On the last evening before the mission, she sat in O'Neill's office and read the current draft start to finish, and it was, she thought, close to right. Not perfect — nothing this big ever was. But close enough that she could hand it to Hammond and he could actually make a decision off it.

She set it down.

O'Neill looked up from his own reading — Carter's updated threat assessment, marked up in his own hand.

"Ready," he said. Not a question.

"For the mission," she said. "Yes."

"And the other thing."

She looked at the document. "Close enough," she said.

He nodded. Went back to Carter's threat assessment a second, set that down too. Looked at the hockey stick in the corner. She'd asked him about it once, six months back, and he'd said it was a reminder, and she'd never asked what of, since the way he'd said it told her the answer lived in the same territory some of her own answers lived in — hers, and not for sharing.

"When we get back from P5C-353," he said.

"Yes sir."

"I'm putting in the commission paperwork," he said. "Formal promotion to brevet second lieutenant. You're ahead of the timeline, but the coursework's done, the clearances are done, and you've been doing the job six months now." He looked at her. "It's time."

She held onto that.

"Yes sir," she said.

"You're not going to argue about the timing."

"No sir," she said.

He looked at her a moment. The almost-smile — and this time it didn't fade right away, held a full two seconds, longest run yet. "Good," he said. "Get some sleep. 0500 comes fast."

She picked up the disclosure document. "I'll get this to Hammond in the morning before we go."

"He'll be in at 0430," O'Neill said.

"I know," she said. "I'll be there at 0435."

She walked out. Down the corridor, past the gate room where the gate stood in its bay with its patient symbols, down to her quarters — she'd stopped thinking of them as temporary sometime in the last month — put the document on her desk for the morning, and sat down on the edge of her bunk.

She thought about P5C-353. About a settlement of two hundred people in a mountain valley who'd been the Edjet longer than anybody had records for, with no idea somebody was coming to tell them what they were. She thought about Ba'al in his domain, efficient and dangerous and currently unaware of what was sitting inside the territory he thought he controlled.

She thought about going in quiet and coming out quiet and everything in between.

She thought about what O'Neill had said. The commission paperwork. The formal designation. The thing that had been building since a senator's office in Sunnydale in 1999, and a demon on her porch telling her the world needed her somewhere specific.

She thought about Joyce, who she was calling in the morning before the 0500 launch, the same thing she did before every mission, and which Joyce always met with that same specific combination of grace and terror that was pretty much Joyce Summers's entire relationship with her daughter's work.

She thought about Giles. About Willow. About Xander and his terrible math and his borrowed soldier memories and his flat refusal to ever let her go into something alone. She thought about all of them the way she'd learned to — grief and love held together, since they never actually came apart, you just learned to carry both at once.

She thought about the settlement on P5C-353, all those people doing what she did without ever knowing what they were doing, and what it was going to mean to be the one who told them.

She lay down.

She slept.

0435, Hammond's office.

She set the document on his desk. He looked at it, then at her.

"Final draft," she said.

He picked it up. Read the first page with the same focused attention he gave every document that mattered, which was really all of them, but especially this one. Looked back up.

"I'll have a response by the time you're back," he said.

"Yes sir."

"Cadet Summers."

She waited.

"Whatever you find on P5C-353," he said, in the tone he used for things meant to stay said, "you bring them home safely. All of them."

"Yes sir," she said. "That's the plan."

She walked out of his office, down to the gate room where SG-1 was assembling — Shyla there already, gear checked, carrying that readiness she always carried; Teal'c at point, already oriented toward departure; Daniel reading something off a tablet, which he put away the second she walked in; Carter running a final check on her scanner with the same quiet efficiency she brought to everything.

O'Neill stood at the ramp.

He looked at her when she came in. The assessment, the confirmation, the small nod that meant good.

She nodded back.

"Alright," he said. "Let's go."

The alkesh was waiting. The mission was waiting. The settlement on P5C-353, and its people, and whatever the line had turned into there across however many generations it had been running — all of it waiting.

Buffy squared her shoulders, walked up the ramp, and went to find out.
