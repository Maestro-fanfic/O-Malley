Chapter 4 of 6

Fourteen Generations

The alkesh came down through P5C-353's atmosphere at 0340 local time, a window Teal'c had picked because Ba'al's patrol cycles in this sector had a consistent gap between 0300 and 0500 — one that had held up long enough to be structural instead of coincidental, the kind of gap that forms when nothing's ever happened in a sector worth closing it for.

Nothing had ever happened here, because there'd never been anything worth happening for.

That was about to change.

Buffy sat in the forward compartment and felt the planet rise up beneath them the same way she felt every planet — that particular Slayer trick of reading dimensional pressure straight through the hull, like a hand pressed through water. P5C-353 was thick. Thicker than Earth, thicker than P3X-774, thicker than anything she'd felt through a gate or an alkesh hull since she'd started paying attention to the variation at all.

This planet had been Edjet a very, very long time.

"Thirty seconds to LZ," Teal'c said.

She checked her gear. Checked it again. Looked across at Shyla, doing exactly the same thing with that baseline economy of hers — everything necessary, nothing extra.

Shyla caught her eye. Ready.

O'Neill stood at the cockpit doorway. Caught her look, gave her the nod.

The alkesh set down in a mountain meadow the UAV data had confirmed sat outside the settlement's sightlines, and SG-1 plus Shyla was on the ground within ninety seconds, and the alkesh lifted immediately on Teal'c's remote and moved off to the holding position forty kilometers northeast, ready to wait.

The air hit her first.

She'd expected the pressure — the thickness she'd already felt through the hull. She hadn't expected it to feel like standing inside something instead of next to it, the way the Hellmouth felt on its worst nights, minus the malevolence, minus the darkness underneath. Just pressure. Dense, old, layered, the way soil gets layered, every stratum holding its own accumulated record of everything that ever happened above it.

She stood in the meadow a moment.

"You feel it," Shyla said beside her. Not a question.

"Yeah," Buffy said.

"I felt it from the ship," Shyla said. "It is different here."

"Different how," O'Neill said.

"More," Shyla said simply.

O'Neill looked at Buffy.

"She's right," Buffy said. "Whatever's been going on here's been going on a long time, and it's in the land." She looked at the treeline at the meadow's edge, at the mountains rising past it, at the valley the UAV data said held a settlement of two hundred people who'd been the Edjet so long they didn't remember not being it. "We go carefully."

"Always the plan," O'Neill said. "Move out."

The settlement announced itself before they ever saw it.

Not sound, or not mainly. What Buffy felt first was the spread-out weight of multiple presences, each one carrying the signature she'd learned to call Edjet — a thickness that was personal, not just geographical. On Earth she'd never been able to feel another Slayer at a distance. She'd met Kennedy once in a hospital room in Sunnydale, and even then it barely registered, and only at close range, only once Kennedy actually spoke.

Here she felt them from four hundred meters out.

Not two. Not four. More than four.

She held up a hand and stopped. The team stopped with her.

"Report," O'Neill said quietly.

"Multiple presences," she said. "Ahead. The settlement." She counted as best she could, which wasn't precise at this range, not with whatever the ambient pressure here was doing to her range. "More than I expected."

"How many more," he said.

"Don't know yet," she said. "At least a dozen. Maybe more."

O'Neill glanced at Carter, whose scanner was already running. She shook her head slightly. "Not picking up the anomaly signature at this range," she said quietly. "But the ambient readings are off baseline, same as what we've seen on Cali when she's—" Carter stopped. "When the Slayer aspect's active."

"It is the land," Shyla said. "It is in the land here. The scanner is reading the land."

O'Neill took that in. "We continue," he said. "Same approach. Summers, Shyla, point. I want thirty seconds before anybody does anything. Read the room."

"Yes sir," Buffy said.

They moved.

~~~LELB~~~

The settlement's edge came out of the pre-dawn dark as a line of structures that had grown out of the mountain valley the way things grow when they've been there long enough to belong — stone and timber, the built and the natural blended together the way they get once a community's stayed in one spot for generations and stopped thinking of the landscape as anything separate from itself.

A few lights. Not many. A handful of windows glowing with something that was either fire or close to it.

And a person standing right at the settlement's edge.

Waiting.

Older than Buffy had expected, somewhere in her sixties, carrying that coiled readiness Buffy recognized before she recognized anything else about her — the Edjet signature, carried in a body that had held it for decades and had organized itself around the weight of it, the way old trees organize themselves around whichever way the wind usually blows.

She looked at Buffy.

Buffy stopped.

Twenty meters apart, and the woman's eyes in the pre-dawn light were doing exactly what Erran's had done across a table on P3X-774, what Shyla's had done in a doorway on P2X-416.

Recognition. The Edjet knowing the Edjet.

But there was something else in it too. Something Buffy hadn't felt with Erran or Shyla — a depth that came from this woman having carried it a very, very long time, someplace where the ground itself remembered.

"You came from the ring," the woman said. Her Standard was accented differently than Shyla's, phonemes shaped by a language all its own that had borrowed Standard the way communities borrow the languages of travelers — selectively, on their own terms.

"Yes," Buffy said.

"We have been expecting someone," the woman said. "Not you specifically. Someone. The land has been unsettled for some time." Her eyes moved past Buffy to the team. To O'Neill, Carter, Daniel, Teal'c, Shyla. They came back to Shyla and held there. "You are also one of us."

"Yes," Shyla said.

"From a different world."

"Yes."

The woman took that in with the equanimity of someone who'd decided a long time ago that the world was bigger than most people gave it credit for, and had simply been waiting for the evidence to catch up. "Come inside," she said. "My name is Dessa. There are others you should meet."

~~~LELB~~~

Others turned out to be a serious understatement.

The building Dessa led them into was a communal space, big enough for several dozen people, fire burning in the center, the walls holding that particular smell that comes from generations of use. Around the fire sat eleven people, ranging from what looked like the late teens up to somebody clearly older than Dessa.

Twelve total. Twelve.

Buffy stopped just inside the door and felt all of them at once, and it was the single most extraordinary thing she'd experienced since the device on P4X-639 — that one extraordinary on a cosmological scale. This was extraordinary on a human one. Twelve people around a fire, each one carrying the exact same thing she carried, each one its own expression of the same pattern in a different key, and the combined weight of all of them in one room together was something she had no framework for, because she'd never once needed a framework for it.

She'd been the only Slayer in a room her whole life.

She was not the only Slayer in this room.

O'Neill touched her elbow briefly. Just contact. Grounding. She took a breath.

"Thank you," Dessa said to the room, and all eleven looked at Buffy and Shyla with that same quality Dessa had — recognition, no surprise, like they'd already known this moment was coming and had just been waiting for it to arrive.

The youngest, a girl maybe sixteen, carrying the particular energy of somebody newly activated, somebody who'd just come into the weight of it and hadn't yet learned to spread it out evenly, looked at Buffy with an expression Buffy recognized instantly, because she'd worn it herself once.

What am I. Why am I like this. Am I the only one.

She was not the only one.

Buffy sat down at the fire.

~~~LELB~~~

What followed was three hours of conversation Daniel's recorder was just going to have to be enough to document, since there was no way to take notes and actually be present for it at the same time, and Buffy had already decided being present was the priority.

Dessa talked. The others talked too, the way communities talk when they've been together long enough that talking's become a collective act — each person adding their piece without needing to be asked, because they knew exactly which piece was theirs. The settlement's history, older than anybody in it could date precisely. The line running in the founding families, propagating the way Daniel had theorized, spreading through the community over generations until reaching a current state where roughly one in fifteen people carried some expression of it — twelve of the current generation carrying it fully enough to count as active.

The Goa'uld had come through twice in the settlement's history. Both times, they'd left.

The first time was before anybody living could remember, recorded in the settlement's oldest documents as a visitation that ended when the Jaffa reported back to their lord that the valley people weren't worth the effort — a phrase the documents had preserved word-for-word, since the community found it funny in the dark way people find things funny when they know the real reason and have decided not to explain it to whoever's already leaving.

The second time was forty years back. Dessa had been young, the current eldest of the twelve not even born yet. Ba'al's Jaffa had come through the gate, marched to the settlement, and stopped right at its edge. The commander had spoken with Dessa's predecessor, a woman named Aleth, whose name Dessa said with the weight of someone speaking a name that also functioned as a definition. The conversation had been brief. The Jaffa were gone by afternoon.

"What did Aleth say to him," Daniel asked.

Dessa looked at Daniel with the same consideration she'd been giving all of them, reading who each person was and what they could be trusted with. "She said nothing," Dessa said. "She stood at the settlement's edge and looked at him. And he looked at her. And then he looked at the settlement. And then he took his Jaffa and left."

"He felt it," Buffy said.

"Yes," Dessa said. "They always feel it. The ones aware enough to feel things. They look at the valley and feel what is here, and they make a calculation." She looked down at her own hands. "Aleth said afterward his eyes were afraid. Not of her specifically. Of the feeling. The way the valley felt to him. He had no words for what the feeling was, but his body knew it was something he could not overcome with Jaffa."

Buffy looked at the fire. At the twelve people sitting around it. At the ambient pressure she could feel in the very air of this room, layered and dense, the accumulated weight of generations of exactly this.

The Edjet as deterrent. Not fighting. Just being.

The youngest, the sixteen-year-old, had been watching Buffy with an intensity that hadn't let up since she sat down. Her name was Mira, activated, by Dessa's account, three months ago, and she hadn't stopped being scared of what she was since the moment it happened.

Buffy knew that feeling with a precision that felt almost physical.

Once the formal conversation settled into something more like the natural back-and-forth of people who'd already built enough trust to speak freely, Buffy moved around the fire and sat down next to Mira.

"Three months," Buffy said.

"Yes," Mira said.

"What's the hardest part."

Mira looked at her. The question had caught her off guard, which was the whole point. "Not knowing if it ends," she said. "My grandmother says it doesn't end. But she's carried it sixty years and says it like it's a good thing, and I don't—" She stopped.

"You don't know if you believe her yet," Buffy said.

"I want to believe her."

"That's enough for now," Buffy said. "Wanting to believe it is where you start. You don't have to believe it yet. You just keep going until you do. How long did it take me? Longer than three months. But I got there."

Mira looked at her with the hunger of somebody who needed to hear that the person in front of them had been exactly where they were and had actually made it out. Not as inspiration. Just as proof it was possible.

"You're afraid sometimes," Mira said.

"Yes," Buffy said.

"But you keep going."

"Yes."

"Why."

Buffy thought about that seriously, because Mira deserved a real answer, not a slogan. She thought about Giles and Willow and Xander, and about Joyce, probably sitting right now in the house on Spruce Street in Colorado Springs, having agreed to O'Neill's security protocol, making something with her hands just to have something to do with them. She thought about O'Neill across the fire, doing what he always did in situations like this — being present, letting her work, staying available if she needed him. She thought about Shyla and Erran and Dessa and Mira and the eleven others and the eight gate addresses left.

"Because there are people," she said. "Specific people, not people-in-general. Specific ones who matter to you specifically. And once you know who they are, going gets easy. Going's the only thing that makes any sense. Because the alternative is not going, and then something happens to them that you could've stopped."

Mira took that in. "I don't have people like that yet," she said quietly. "Most of the people I had before I became this—" she stopped. "They don't understand. They look at me differently now."

"They'll come," Buffy said. "The right ones. They find you." She thought about a commissary and bad coffee and a man who'd stayed the whole night in a hospital chair with a copy of Field and Stream. "Sometimes they're already there and you just don't know it yet. Sometimes they show up right when you least expect it. But they come."

Mira looked at her a long moment. Then over at O'Neill, like she'd followed Buffy's line of sight and drawn her own conclusion. Then back at Buffy.

"Okay," she said. Not convinced. Not unconvinced. Just holding the possibility, which was exactly where you started.

"Okay," Buffy agreed.

They stayed seven hours instead of four, something O'Neill had already planned contingencies for, and every extra minute of that compressed extraction window turned out worth it, because what the settlement gave them in those seven hours was irreplaceable.

Records. Dessa had records going back fourteen generations, kept in a script native to this community that Daniel spent two hours with in a state of pure focused rapture, while Carter ran physiological assessments and Teal'c had a long conversation with the settlement's eldest man about Jaffa combat doctrine and exactly what Jaffa commanders were trained to flag as threats worth engaging versus threats worth just avoiding.

What Teal'c learned in that conversation, reported back in the alkesh on the way out, was this: Jaffa doctrine on worlds with the Edjet was never written down anywhere in the Goa'uld military corpus. Passed orally, commander to commander, treated as operational wisdom rather than formal policy. The wisdom boiled down to: if the people feel wrong, leave. Don't try to explain to your lord why they felt wrong. Just report the world's got nothing worth taking, and move on.

"They've been managing the information for generations," O'Neill said. "Keeping it out of any record that could actually be analyzed."

"Yes," Teal'c said. "A System Lord who fully understood the Edjet would respond very differently than one who only felt an undefined wrongness. The undefined wrongness is manageable. Clear understanding is an invitation to attempt systematic elimination."

"So the Goa'uld have been perpetuating their own ignorance," Carter said.

"Yes," Teal'c said. "Deliberately. At the commander level. They know there is something they do not fully understand, and they have decided they prefer not to understand it fully, because full understanding would demand a response they may not be capable of giving."

Buffy looked out at the stars through the viewport. "That changes once we start coordinating."

"Yes," Teal'c said.

"Ba'al's different from Ra," O'Neill said. "More analytical. A lot more willing to sit with a problem until he understands it instead of avoiding it."

"He will notice the change," Teal'c said. "Once the Edjet begins coordinating, the undefined wrongness becomes a defined pattern. And Ba'al will pursue a defined pattern."

"How long," O'Neill said.

Teal'c considered. "Perhaps one year before the pattern becomes visible enough to draw his specific attention. Less if he is already looking. More if we are careful."

"A year," O'Neill said. He looked at Buffy. "Eight more addresses."

"Seven," she said. "P5C-353's done."

"Seven more," he said. "A year."

"We can do seven in a year," she said. "With what we know now and what the Tok'ra records give us, the planning'll move faster."

"It'll also need to be more careful," he said. "The further we go, the more visible the pattern gets."

"I know," she said. "But we don't get to slow down just to stay invisible. The people on those worlds are already out there. Without what we know. Without coordination. Vulnerable to Ba'al figuring it out before we do."

O'Neill was quiet a second. "I know," he said. He looked out at the stars. "We go faster and we go careful at the same time. Those two things are going to be in tension constantly."

"Yes sir," she said.

"And you're going to want to push toward faster the second that tension gets uncomfortable."

"Yes sir."

"And I'm going to push back."

"Yes sir."

"And we're going to argue about it."

"Probably, yes sir."

He looked at her with the expression she'd learned to read as genuine amusement he was choosing not to show fully, since showing it fully just wasn't his mode. "Good," he said. "At least it'll be interesting."

She looked back at the stars. At the gate addresses she'd memorized, all nine of them now, P5C-353 checked off and seven left. At the work that wasn't finished, and that she wasn't finished with either.

"Sir," she said.

"Yeah."

"When this is done," she said. "When all seven are done and the coordination exists and whatever comes next comes next. What do you think it looks like."

O'Neill was quiet a long moment. Not the quiet of a man without an answer — the quiet of a man deciding whether to give the one he had.

"Honestly," he said.

"Yes sir."

"I think it looks like a different kind of problem," he said. "The coordination exists, the Goa'uld find out about it, Ba'al makes a move, we deal with the move. Then there's another move. We deal with that. What it doesn't look like is finished. In my experience, this kind of thing doesn't finish. It just changes shape."

"I know," she said. "I've been doing this since I was fifteen. It doesn't finish."

"No," he said. "But the shape it changes into—" he stopped, started again. "It gets better. If you do the work right. Not easier. Better."

She looked at him. At the face she'd been learning for three years now, every specific line of it — what it looked like when something mattered, what it looked like when he was holding onto something, what it looked like at two in the morning in a Georgia hospital room when he'd asked is there something I should know with the face of a man choosing to trust her instead of demanding an answer.

"Yeah," she said. "It does."

~~~LELB~~~

They came back through the gate at 0630 Mountain time to find Hammond already waiting in the gate room, which wasn't standard, and which meant something.

He watched them come down the ramp, running the automatic headcount and condition check every commanding officer runs when their people come home, and his face finished the count and settled into something that wasn't quite relief, since Hammond didn't let relief show — but close enough to be in the same family.

"Debrief at 1000," he said. "Get food. Sleep if you can." He looked at Buffy. "Cadet Summers."

"Sir."

He held out a folder. She took it.

Inside was the disclosure document she'd handed him before the mission, notated in the margins in his hand, and one single line written at the top of the first page in his careful script.

Approved as written. Implementation begins at your discretion.

She looked up.

Hammond was watching her with that same steady assessment that had been there since the gate room, on her very first day, the moment she'd looked at the Stargate and said well. "The document's sound," he said. "The reasoning's sound. It's your framework and you built it well." A pause. "The implementation's going to be harder than the document."

"I know, sir," she said.

"Going to take longer than either of us wants it to."

"I know."

"And there will be people who react badly," he said. "Even among allies. Even among people we trust. This information's huge, and some people don't handle huge information well."

"I know that too," she said. "I've been handing people huge information since I was fifteen. Some of them do fine. Some don't. You learn to tell in advance as best you can and deal with whatever you get."

Hammond looked at her a moment. Then, with the precision of a man who'd spent a career choosing exactly when to say things that mattered: "You have done exceptional work, Cadet Summers. I want that on the record."

She held onto that. Let it actually land instead of deflecting, which was something she was still practicing. "Thank you, sir," she said.

He nodded. "1000," he said, and walked out.

O'Neill appeared at her left, reading the notation on the document over her shoulder. She let him.

"Approved as written," he said.

"Yes sir."

"Implementation at your discretion."

"Yes sir."

He was quiet a second. "Any idea where you want to start."

She looked at the gate. At the empty bay, gate dormant, chevrons dark, no event horizon because there was nowhere it was currently going. She thought about the Tok'ra, the Asgard, the free Jaffa movement Teal'c cared about the way he cared about the things that sat at the very center of who he was. She thought about Dessa and Mira and Erran, the seven remaining addresses, a year before the pattern went visible.

She thought about what Shyla had told her — on her world, the relationship between the Edjet and whoever chose to stand closest was its own category. The anchor. The person who kept you tied to the world when the work tried to pull you loose of it.

She thought about Jacob Carter and Selmak saying the word Edjet with two thousand years of Tok'ra history sitting underneath it.

She thought about Teal'c, who'd looked at the device on P4X-639 over her shoulder and understood before she'd even explained it that something significant was happening, and who'd carried that understanding the way he carried everything — solidly, no drama.

She thought about Hammond reading fifty-two page reports before breakfast, about Davis building cases, about Abel losing a memo that needed losing, about a sergeant on a Georgia hillside kneeling beside her and checking her pulse, unable to believe what his own fingers were telling him.

She thought about Joyce, in the house on Spruce Street, four batches of cookies deep, that particular frequency her voice hit that meant I already know something's wrong, tell me right now.

She thought about Willow and Xander and Giles, the way she always thought about them — grief and love held together, since they never really came apart, you just learned to carry both.

She thought about a fifteen-year-old girl in a library in Sunnydale who'd been told she was the Chosen One and felt it as an enormous inconvenience she'd never asked for, and the woman now standing in the SGC gate room who finally understood what all that had actually meant, and had stopped flinching from it.

She looked at O'Neill.

"The Tok'ra first," she said. "Full disclosure. Then the Asgard. Then we bring Teal'c into the conversation about the free Jaffa. And we keep hitting the addresses. All seven. Before the year's out."

O'Neill looked at her. The almost-smile, the full version, which had been showing up steadily since Georgia and had stopped being anything she bothered filing as notable, since it had just become part of the landscape of him now.

"That's a lot of work," he said.

"Yes sir," she said.

"You're not complaining."

"No sir," she said.

He looked at the gate. At the room. At her. "Get some food," he said. "1000 comes fast."

"Yes sir," she said.

She walked out of the gate room and into the corridor, the Mountain humming around her at its usual frequency, seventeen floors below the Colorado earth, under fourteen levels of granite and two hundred miles of sky.

She'd been here six months. She was going to be here a lot longer. She was going to leave and come back and leave and come back, through that gate and out into a galaxy older and bigger and stranger than she'd ever understood at fifteen, fighting vampires in a graveyard in Sunnydale — which was really just to say she was going to keep doing what she'd always done since the very beginning.

She was going to do the work.

She headed to the commissary and got food, because Slayer metabolism didn't pause for anything, and the debrief was at 1000, and there was a call to make to Joyce before that. Shyla was already at their usual table. Daniel was eating while still reading Dessa's records, which somebody was going to have to physically pry out of his hands before the debrief or he'd wander in still holding them. Carter was working through her scanner data. Teal'c was eating something nobody had ever quite identified, with the same equanimity he brought to everything.

O'Neill came in four minutes later, got coffee, sat down, and picked up a copy of the debrief template he'd produced from somewhere, already marking it up.

Buffy ate and looked around the table and thought — this was what it looked like. The thing she'd been building toward since Sunnydale. This table, right now, these people.

Not simple. Not finished. Not safe in any absolute way, since nothing that actually mattered was ever safe in any absolute way. But right. The shape of it was right, the way things are right when they've fully grown into themselves, when every piece has finally found where it goes.

She ate breakfast, called her mother, went to the debrief, came back to the table after, went to find Daniel to pry Dessa's records away from him before the next meeting, and the Mountain hummed on around all of them at its constant frequency, and outside, the stars sat exactly where they'd always been, and the gate was there, and the addresses were there, and the work was there.

And she was there.

That was enough. That was, on balance, exactly enough.
