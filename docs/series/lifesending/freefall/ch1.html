Freefall

The ground was coming up fast.

Buffy had maybe four seconds before impact. Most people would have spent those four seconds screaming. She had the Slayer riding shotgun inside her, so she got to actually use them.

Main chute's gone. Reserve's gone too. Maybe eight hundred feet up. Came out of a C-17, so not at terminal velocity yet — call it sixty miles an hour, give or take.

Still enough to kill me.

Oddly, she wasn't scared. That was the weird part. Whatever part of her brain was supposed to be panicking right now had apparently punched out early, and in its place was that strange crystal-clear focus the Slayer gave her when things went really, truly wrong. Everything got sharp. Every option lit up at once. Time practically slowed down, like she had actual hands she could reach in and grab the gears with.

Options.

There. The pine tree line at the edge of Friar Drop Zone. She'd clocked it three days ago during the pre-jump brief, back when Sergeant Abel had walked them through the approach vectors in that flat, no-nonsense voice of his, like he was giving directions to a gas station instead of describing the patch of Georgia they were about to fall into. Trees meant branches. Branches meant the impact would spread out instead of landing all at once. Not exactly good, but a whole lot better than open clay at sixty miles an hour.

She angled toward the trees.

Two seconds.

Arms crossed over her chest. Chin tucked. Knees bent. Everything the PLF training had drilled into her — spread the energy out, don't take it head-on, let as much of her body share the hit as possible. The Slayer would handle whatever was left over. The Slayer had handled worse.

She hit the far edge of the drop zone right at the treeline, and the world went white.

"Jumper down! Jumper down, southwest corner!"

Abel had the bullhorn to his mouth before Summers had even finished hitting the ground. Thirty years in the Army, twenty of them with the 507th, and he had never once seen a double chute failure outside a training scenario — not once, not in three decades. He'd run those numbers plenty of times, the way you did when your job involved gravity and nineteen-year-olds, not because he was morbid but because you had to think about worst cases or the worst cases caught you flat-footed. And every single time he'd run them, the answer had come back the same. A double failure did not leave survivors.

He was already running before the echo of his own shout died out.

Behind him, the drop zone dissolved into organized chaos — Black Hats scrambling to reorganize, medics grabbing gear, and behind all of that the rest of the chalk standing frozen, all of them staring southwest. He caught Wilson, the Marine lieutenant, already breaking toward the treeline against every rule in the book. Under normal circumstances Abel would have chewed him out for it. Right now he just filed it away. It told him something about the kid he hadn't known before.

Two hundred yards at a dead sprint, and Abel got there ahead of the medics.

She was alive.

He stood over her for a second — a whole second, which was a long time to just stand there doing nothing, but he'd never admit that to a soul — trying to make his brain catch up with what his eyes were showing him. She was flat on her back where the Georgia clay met the pine needles, her lines tangled around one leg, both ruined canopies hanging in the branches above her like somebody'd strung up laundry. A cut across her cheekbone had already mostly stopped bleeding. Her eyes were closed.

Her chest was moving. Up. Down. Up.

Abel dropped to a knee and pressed two fingers to her pulse, because thirty years of habit didn't care what his eyes were telling him. The pulse was there. Strong. Steady. So steady that he actually pulled his fingers back and stared at them like they'd lied to him.

"Summers," he said.

A beat.

Her eyes opened. Green, blinking hard against the Georgia sky, finding his face.

"Did I pass?"

Abel sat back on his heels and clapped a hand over his mouth.

The medics arrived and Abel stepped back to give them room. He used the thirty seconds before they got fully set up to look at the two ruined chutes still tangled in the branches above her. He didn't touch them. He didn't need to.

The smell got to him first.

Sharp. Chemical. The kind of smell a man recognized instantly after a career spent around ordnance and synthetics — he'd smelled accelerants, propellants, cleaning solvents, every nasty compound the Army had a name for.

This wasn't any of those.

This was acid.

Abel's face went very still.

Both chutes. Not one. Both.

He turned around. Summers had already caught his eye over the shoulder of the corpsman checking her pupils. He held her gaze for a second. She gave him one small nod — the kind that said I already know.

Of course she did.

~~~LELB~~~

They loaded her into the medical vehicle, and Abel climbed in before anybody could think to stop him. She watched Georgia roll by through the little rear windows for a while without saying anything.

"You know it wasn't an accident," she said finally.

"Yes," Abel said.

"Both chutes."

"Both chutes," he confirmed.

She nodded once, filing it away. Then: "Sergeant Abel."

"Summers."

"I'd appreciate it if you could hold off notifying my emergency contact for a couple hours. There are some calls I need to make first."

Abel studied her. Nineteen years old, flat on a gurney after surviving something that had no business being survivable, and already three moves ahead, her voice as level as a range officer calling the line. He'd commanded decorated soldiers who wouldn't have held it together half this well. He didn't know what she was. He didn't know what she was connected to, though that anomalous-personnel memo had come screaming back to him the second he'd caught that acid smell, and it hadn't left since.

What he did know was that she wasn't panicking.

"Two hours," he said. "After that I can't stop them."

"That's plenty. Thank you."

~~~LELB~~~

The hospital ran her through every test they had, and Buffy cooperated with all of it, because she'd learned a long time ago that the fastest way out of any institution was to give it exactly what it wanted and not argue. The results were going to raise some eyebrows. That part was unavoidable, and she'd deal with it when it came.

While the techs worked, she thought.

Somebody had sabotaged her parachutes. Somebody with access to the equipment locker, knowledge of exactly which gear was hers, and enough sophistication to rig a timed chemical agent into both the main and the reserve so the failure cascaded at altitude — right at the moment surviving was supposed to be flat-out impossible.

Supposed to be.

Buffy stared at the ceiling tiles and started sorting what she knew into columns, the way they'd taught her at the Academy, the way she'd watched O'Neill do a hundred times over Saturday dinners without even seeming to try. Organize first. React second.

Column one: who knew she'd be here. Colonel O'Neill. Her mother. The Academy. The Fort Benning admin staff. Captain Powers in billeting. The Black Hat cadre doing in-processing. Art, Billy, Chuck — the guys she'd told over dinner, none of whom had any reason on earth to want her dead. Joe Wilson, same deal.

Column two: who would want her dead. Two categories here, and she made herself look hard at both of them.

The first was the category she'd been swimming in since she was fifteen. Vampires. Demons. All the things that went bump in the night and got cranky when you bumped back. That category had tried to kill her a hundred different ways over the years, but never like this. Never with acid and coveralls and careful knowledge of a training schedule. Vampires didn't case equipment lockers. Demons didn't have offshore accounts.

The second category was newer, and colder. The human, institutional kind. The kind that wiped hard drives and poured concrete over evidence and wrote memos titled unusual personnel. The kind that had left her in a cell at the Initiative and then buried the whole operation and called it tidy. That category had resources. Patience. The ability to walk right onto a military base without setting off a single alarm.

She thought about the man in the maintenance coveralls. She'd clocked him twice during her time at BAC — once near the equipment building, once crossing the parade ground at a time and pace that didn't match any work schedule he was supposed to be on. She'd noted him. Filed him. Never stared, because staring told a surveillance asset he'd been made, and she'd wanted to see what he'd do next.

Turned out what he'd done next was try to kill her.

She needed to make some calls.

The call to Colonel O'Neill took twelve minutes. Nine of those were Buffy giving her report. Three were silence on his end — and that silence said more than most people managed with actual words.

"You're sure it was acid," he said.

"Sergeant Abel smelled it. I trust his nose."

Silence.

"Are you hurt."

She chose her words carefully. "Bruising. No fractures. I'll be stiff tomorrow."

The silence that followed had a whole separate conversation buried in it, all about the gap between bruising and fell eight hundred feet, and everything that gap implied.

"Cali." His voice had gone very level, the kind of level that took real effort to hold onto. "That landing should have killed you."

"I know."

"So we're going to talk about that."

"I know that too."

She heard him exhale, long and slow — the sound of a man deciding to table a very big conversation because there just wasn't the bandwidth for it right now. "I'm on the next flight out. Don't sign anything. Don't talk to anybody official without me in the room. Understood?"

"Yes sir."

"Call your mother."

Buffy closed her eyes. "Yes sir."

"I mean it, Cali. You call her before somebody else does."

"I will."

A pause. Then, in a completely different register: "Don't do anything. Don't go anywhere. Just sit in that hospital bed and exist for a few hours. Can you manage that?"

Buffy looked around the room. At the window and its view of Georgia pines. At the closed door, where Abel had posted a corpsman before he'd left.

"I can manage that," she said.

Something came through in his voice then that she didn't have a clean word for. The same territory as the moment he'd handed back her knife in Sunnydale and called her little lion. Something that said you're one of mine now, and I'm not stopping for anything to keep you that way.

"Good," he said. "Stay put."

The call to Joyce Summers was the hard one.

Not because Joyce fell apart — she never did that anymore, hadn't in years, not since she'd spent three years on the Hellmouth quietly rebuilding herself into someone who could take bad news standing up. It was hard because the second Buffy's voice hit the line, Joyce's voice dropped into that particular frequency — I already know something's wrong, tell me right now — and there was no armor thick enough to stop that frequency. It just went straight through.

"How bad," Joyce said. Not a question.

"I'm okay. I'm really okay. I'm at Martin Army Community Hospital, I've got some bruising, they want to keep me overnight, but I promise, I'm okay."

Silence.

"What happened."

Buffy told her. The real version — not the one for official inquiries — because Joyce had earned the real version a long time ago, three years running as the mother of the Slayer. Partway through, her mother's breathing changed, the way it always did when Joyce was holding herself together through pure willpower.

When she finished, there was a pause.

"I'm coming down there," Joyce said.

"Mom, it's a bruise. Well — okay, it's more like a whole bruise-themed situation. Bruise-adjacent. But I'm fine—"

"I am coming down there, and I would very much like to see you try and stop me." A beat. "The colonel's already on his way."

Not a question. Joyce had built up a pretty complete working model of Colonel Jack O'Neill by now.

"Of course he is," Buffy said. "Did he beat you to the phone tree, or did you beat him?"

"That's classified."

"You've been spending way too much time around Air Force people, Mom."

"And whose fault is that." Another pause, weighted this time. "Buffy."

"Yeah."

"I need you to not be cavalier with me right now. I need you to tell me honestly. Are you all right."

Buffy thought about those four seconds of freefall. About the fear that never showed up. About everything she'd turned into since she'd walked out of Sunnydale with a duffel bag and forged letters from three dead men. The joke sat right there, easy reach, ready to go.

She let it go past.

"Yeah," she said. "I really am."

A shaky breath from the other end. "Okay. I'm booking a flight."

The man going by Carlisle for this particular job was sitting in a diner off Fort Benning Road, eating eggs and waiting on his flight confirmation out of Columbus Metropolitan, when the news alert hit his phone.

Training accident at Fort Benning. Air Force Academy cadet survives parachute malfunction.

He read it twice.

He set his coffee down very, very carefully.

He'd placed the acid himself. Both chutes. Checked his own work three separate times, because checking your work three times was exactly what separated the professionals from the amateurs, and in nineteen years he had never once made a mistake. There was no variable he hadn't accounted for. There was no physics on earth by which a hundred-and-ten-pound girl walked away from a double failure at eight hundred feet. The math didn't lie.

And yet here it was, lying to him.

He left a twenty on the table, walked out to his car, and sat there a long while with the engine off. Then he pulled the secondary phone out and dialed.

It picked up on the second ring.

"We've got a complication," he said.

Abel tracked down Colonel Whitmore in the admin offices and laid it all out — fact by fact, no editorializing, the way thirty years of putting information in front of officers had taught him to do it, leaving no room for argument.

Whitmore listened. When Abel finished, he sat quiet for a moment.

"You're sure about the acid," he said.

"Yes sir."

"You understand what that means. The investigation, the security breach—"

"Yes sir. I need to walk the equipment locker."

Whitmore nodded. "Take Sergeant Rodriguez. Document everything." He paused. "And Abel. The cadet. How in the hell is she alive?"

Abel had been asking himself the exact same question since the moment he'd knelt down beside her and found that pulse still going strong.

"I don't know, sir," he said. "But she is."

~~~LELB~~~

Abel and Rodriguez spent forty minutes going over the equipment locker.

What they found was clean. Methodical. Professional enough that it made Abel's jaw tighten just looking at it. The lock had been picked, not forced — no damage to the mechanism at all, just the faintest scoring on the tumblers that you'd never catch unless you already knew to look. Whoever'd gone in had re-secured it afterward. Done the job, walked out, left almost nothing behind.

Almost nothing.

Rodriguez found it — a faint chemical shadow staining the interior wall right where the parachute pack would've sat. Barely more than a discoloration in the paint, but with that same acrid edge Abel had caught out at the treeline. The acid had leaked, just a little, from whatever it had been carried in. Whoever'd placed it had been careful. Just not quite careful enough.

"Photograph everything," Abel said. "Then we seal this room, and nobody goes in or out without my say-so."

Rodriguez nodded and got to work.

Abel stood there a moment longer, just thinking. About Summers in the ambulance, already three moves ahead, asking him to hold off two hours so she could make her calls first. About the memo. About that anomalous-personnel flag on her file that had pinged the day she arrived, the one he'd set aside because the girl had been nothing but exemplary from the very first day.

He thought about what it meant that somebody wanted her dead badly enough to compromise a United States military installation to make it happen.

Then he locked the door and went to go file his report.

Jack was on a transport out of Peterson inside forty minutes. He'd called Hammond from the car and given him the short version. Cadet under his sponsorship. Sabotaged equipment. Double chute failure. Survived.

He listened to Hammond go quiet on the other end, the kind of quiet that had a whole lot of unspoken math about physics and probability packed into it.

"I'll make some calls," Hammond said.

"Appreciate it, sir."

"Jack." A pause. "She's all right?"

"So she says."

"You believe her?"

Jack thought about Cali's voice on the phone. Flat. Clear. Already three moves ahead of everyone else. "Yeah," he said. "I do."

On the transport he sat with a legal pad on his knee and made himself think in lists, the way he'd trained himself to do whenever things went bad and emotion wanted to grab the wheel. Lists meant you were still driving. Emotion in the driver's seat meant you ended up in a ditch somewhere.

Someone had tried to kill Cali. Someone with access to a military installation, knowledge of her schedule, and chemical resources that pointed way past a lone actor and straight at institutional backing. This wasn't a supernatural problem. Supernatural problems didn't case equipment lockers. They didn't coordinate around a maintenance schedule.

This was the other thing. The human, classified, buried-in-paperwork thing he'd been catching the edges of for two years now. The thing with its fingerprints all over the Initiative, the concrete trucks, the wiped hard drives, and McNamara — who was inconveniently dead and unavailable for the conversation Jack had been planning to have with him someday.

He thought about Maybourne. About the sub-committee. About certain people in certain chairs who'd decided a long time ago that the rules simply didn't apply once the stakes got high enough. He'd been bumping up against that particular attitude for years without getting a clean shot at it, because the people who held it were very, very good at staying just inside the lines.

They'd gone after Cali twice now.

That was going to stop.

Jack didn't waste any time thinking about what stopping it might cost him — political capital, career capital, whatever else. He'd started doing that math at twenty-two years old, and he'd long since learned how it always came out. Some things you just paid for. You paid for them because the alternative was being the kind of man who let them slide, and Jack O'Neill had looked hard at that alternative more than once over the years, and he had never once been able to live with it.

He put the legal pad away and watched Georgia come up underneath him through the transport window.

They kept her overnight. Buffy lay in the hospital bed in the dark and didn't sleep — Slayer healing didn't need sleep the way regular human healing did, and her mind was running way too hot for sleep to touch it anyway.

She thought about what she was going to tell him.

Not all of it. She wasn't ready for all of it, and honestly, he wasn't ready for all of it either. There was a version of this conversation that went badly enough to cost her the thing she'd spent two years building with him — the closest thing to an uncomplicated, trusting adult relationship she'd had since Giles. She wasn't about to blow that up by overcorrecting.

But she'd promised him honesty and she'd meant it, and the gap between what she'd told him and what was actually true had been growing wider since Sunnydale, and she could feel the stress fractures in it the same way you felt a bad landing in your knees for days afterward.

Someone had tried to kill her with professionally sabotaged parachutes. She'd survived because she was the Slayer. Those two facts were going to need some kind of bridge between them in any real conversation with O'Neill, and the only material available to build that bridge out of was the truth.

Some of it. Enough of it. The minimum that would hold weight.

She was still working out the blueprint when the door opened, and she knew from the footsteps it was him.

O'Neill looked like he hadn't slept since Tuesday. Jeans, a grey sweatshirt. He did his automatic sweep of the room before his eyes landed on her, then pulled the chair up to the bed and sat down and just looked at her for a second.

"Hi," she said.

"Hi," he said.

He leaned forward, elbows on his knees. "Your mother lands at 0830."

"I know. I talked to her."

"Teal'c sends his regards. Wanted to come himself."

That warmed something in her chest. "Tell him thanks."

O'Neill nodded, looked at the floor for a second, then back up. She knew that look. It was the one he used right before he said something real instead of something professional.

"Cali," he said. "You fell eight hundred feet."

"I know."

"You're lying here with bruising."

"I know."

He held her gaze. There was nothing accusing in it. This wasn't a man who'd already made up his mind about something. This was a man choosing, deliberately, to trust her with a question instead of demanding an answer out of her.

"Is there something I should know," he said.

Buffy looked at him. She thought about the knife, clean and sharp. She thought about little lion. She thought about Webb's body in that elevator shaft and O'Neill still doing his job anyway. She thought about what she'd told Kennedy once, about honesty, about oaths, about the men who stood guard at a door.

She took a breath.

"Yes," she said. "There are some things you should know."

He nodded once and waited.

"Some of it's classified above both our clearances," she said. "Same category as the Initiative. I can give you the shape of it without the specifics."

"Understood."

"And some of it's just personal. No classification level at all. It's just mine."

He was quiet for a moment. "You don't have to tell me everything tonight. You don't have to tell me everything ever. But I need enough to keep you safe. That part's not negotiable."

It was, she realized, the most honest thing any commanding officer had ever said to her. Not you owe me this. Not I outrank you. Just — here's what I need, and here's why.

That was very him.

She nodded. And she started talking.

She didn't tell him about the Slayer. Not directly. Not yet. There weren't words for it that a rational military officer could take in at two in the morning in a hospital room, and she needed him to process what she could give him before she handed him anything more.

So this is what she told him.

That she wasn't a standard-issue cadet. That there were physiological differences behind the anomalies in her records, and behind a lot of what had happened since Colorado Springs. That these differences were real, not the result of anything she'd sought out or any substance she'd taken. That they were basically congenital — the same way some people are born with perfect pitch, or born color-blind, except a good deal more significant, and a good deal more complicated.

That she believed whoever had left her in the Initiative was connected to whoever had tampered with her chutes. That based on what she'd seen at BAC, she thought there were two separate organizations involved, not one — which changed the threat picture considerably.

That she'd known about the surveillance since her second day at BAC and had chosen not to say anything right away, because she'd wanted more information before the guy knew he'd been made.

O'Neill's face went through several things while she talked. None of them small. When she finished, he was quiet long enough that she could hear the nurses' station clear down the hall.

"The surveillance," he said. "Describe him."

She did. Medium height, medium build, maintenance coveralls, a camera hidden in his watch, twice in her peripheral at times that didn't line up with any work schedule he was supposed to be on.

O'Neill nodded slowly. "The chutes. You said two organizations."

"The first group wanted information. That's who the surveillance belonged to. The second group escalated to termination. I think they added the acid to the reserve chute after the first group had already gotten to the main. Maybe without the first group even knowing."

He looked at her steadily. "That's a very precise read for someone who didn't find out about the tampered chutes until they were already falling."

"I had eight hundred feet to think about it," she said.

Something moved behind his eyes. Not quite a smile. Something in that neighborhood. "Right," he said. Then: "The physiological differences. That's why you survived."

Not a question.

"Yes," she said.

He sat with that. She watched him take the piece and turn it over and fit it up against the others he'd been quietly collecting since the first time he'd looked at her APFT scores and said nothing — since Sunnydale, since that combat knife she'd taken into the hallway and whatever had been on the blade when Teal'c picked it up. She watched him build the whole structure in his head and choose, on purpose, not to ask the questions that would force her to say things she wasn't ready to say.

That was also very him.

"Okay," he said finally.

Just okay. Not what are you. Not explain this to me. Not I need a full debrief. Just okay.

"Okay?" she said.

He met her eyes. "You survived because you're you. And we're going to go deal with the people who made surviving necessary in the first place. That's what okay means right now. The rest of it can keep. Unless something in the rest of it is an immediate operational concern."

She thought it over honestly. "Not tonight," she said.

"Then it keeps." He sat back. "Tomorrow your mother gets here and Hammond's people are going to want a statement and Abel's already filed a report that's about to kick this thing right up the chain. We're going to be busy. You should try to sleep."

"I don't actually—" she started.

He raised an eyebrow.

She closed her eyes.

"Don't stay all night," she said.

"No promises," he said, and she heard the chair creak as he settled back, and the soft rustle of a magazine getting picked off the side table, and then nothing but the quiet, steady rhythm of a man planning to still be there in the morning.

Buffy didn't sleep. But she got close, and for a Slayer running on adrenaline and unresolved classified intel at two in the morning in a Georgia hospital, close was close enough to count.

Joyce Summers walked in at 0847, seventeen minutes after her stated landing time of 0830, which meant she'd cleared the gate at Martin Army Community Hospital about four minutes after touchdown and hadn't stopped for a single thing — not even baggage claim, which Buffy knew for a fact because her mother walked into the hospital room carrying nothing but her purse, wearing the exact expression of a woman who'd made some decisions somewhere over Kansas.

She stopped in the doorway. Looked at Buffy. Looked at O'Neill, who had in fact stayed the whole night and was currently asleep in the chair with a copy of Field and Stream spread across his chest — which Buffy found so unexpectedly human that she'd spent part of the night carefully not looking at him, just in case he woke up and caught her.

Joyce looked back at Buffy.

"I'm okay," Buffy said.

Joyce crossed the room, sat down on the edge of the bed, and wrapped both arms around her daughter. Buffy let her. The smell of her mother's perfume and the exact shape of her mother's hug lived in a place that had nothing to do with the Slayer, nothing to do with the Air Force, nothing to do with any of the columns and threat assessments she'd been sorting through all night. For about forty-five seconds Buffy just sat there inside it and didn't think about anything at all.

"Okay," Joyce said quietly, into her hair. "Okay."

Behind them O'Neill made the small sound of a man waking up and taking in the whole room in one glance. Buffy heard the magazine slide off his chest, then the quiet, deliberate sound of him getting up and heading for the door to give them some space — all of it done with barely a whisper of noise, which she suspected had a lot more to do with Special Ops training than with tact, though with the colonel it was frequently hard to tell where one ended and the other began.

She heard the door open.

"Colonel," Joyce said, without letting go of her daughter.

"Mrs. Summers," he said. "Glad you made it."

"Thank you for being here with her."

A brief pause. "Of course," he said, in the way he said things he actually meant. Then the door shut and it was just the two of them.

Joyce leaned back, held Buffy by the shoulders, and looked her over the way she'd been looking her over since the Slayer, since the Hellmouth, since she'd learned exactly what kind of things her daughter walked into and, mostly, walked back out of. Taking stock. Finding the damage. Already ranking what needed doing first.

"Tell me," she said.

So Buffy told her. More than she'd told O'Neill, less than the whole truth — the version built for a woman who already knew about the supernatural and already knew about the institutional and had been walking the tightrope between the two for years now, since long before Buffy had ever set foot through the Academy gates.

Joyce listened without a single interruption, a skill she'd built up over years, and one Buffy had come to appreciate more than almost anything else about her.

When Buffy finished, Joyce sat quiet for a moment.

"The people who did this," she said.

"We're going to find them," Buffy said.

"The colonel."

"Yes."

Joyce glanced at the door he'd gone through. Then back at her daughter. "He stayed all night."

"He did."

"With a Field and Stream."

"Apparently."

Joyce's face did something complicated. She had opinions about Colonel O'Neill that she mostly kept to herself, which was another skill developed over years. Whatever those opinions were, Buffy mostly got them secondhand — the way Joyce said his name, or the way she watched him at their Saturday dinners those times she'd made the trip up to Colorado Springs.

"He's a good man," Joyce said finally, and the way she said it had the weight of a verdict, not a compliment.

"I know," Buffy said.

Joyce nodded. Then she dug into her purse and pulled out a container of homemade chocolate chip cookies sealed under three layers of plastic wrap — so completely, entirely her mother that something loosened in Buffy's chest she hadn't even realized had been clenched.

"I baked last night," Joyce said. "I needed something to do with my hands."

Buffy took the container. "How many batches?"

"Four."

"Mom."

"I said I needed something to do with my hands." Joyce reached out and smoothed the hair back from Buffy's face, careful around the cut on her cheekbone. Up close her expression carried things it only ever carried when it was just the two of them — the things she'd been carrying since Sunnydale High, since the police station, since the two urns that used to sit on the mantel at the house on Revello Drive and now sat on the mantel of the house on Spruce Street. "You scared me," she said quietly.

"I know. I'm sorry."

"Don't be sorry. Just—" Joyce stopped. Started again. "Just keep coming back."

"Always the plan, Mom." Buffy squeezed her hand. "Best plan I've got. Also the only one with cookies at the end of it."

Joyce laughed, wet and short — which had been the whole point.

Joyce squeezed her hand again, then straightened up, and Buffy watched her shift, right in front of her, from mother back into the woman who'd managed her own gallery and her own grief and her own move across the country and her own entire reinvention, and was more than capable of handling whatever came next.

"Now," she said. "Tell me about this Sergeant Abel."

Abel found them an hour later — mother and daughter sitting on the hospital bed passing cookies back and forth, talking in the easy shorthand of people who'd been through something together, while a colonel who'd apparently appointed himself permanent guard duty leaned against the wall outside with a cup of terrible hospital coffee and an expression that dared anyone to make his morning any more complicated than it already was.

Abel had dealt with plenty of officers over his career and had a pretty solid mental filing system for all of them. This one didn't fit any category he already had. He made a new one.

"Master Sergeant Abel," O'Neill said, before Abel had even opened his mouth to introduce himself.

"Colonel," Abel said. "Didn't realize you'd arrived."

"About six hours ago." He didn't elaborate, and he didn't ask how Abel knew his name either, which meant he already knew that Abel knew — the kind of shorthand Abel associated with people who'd spent time in places where the loudest talkers were usually the least worth listening to. "I appreciate what you did for Cadet Summers yesterday."

"She's one of mine till she pins those wings," Abel said. "What happened to her equipment happened on my watch."

O'Neill studied him a second. "You've secured the locker."

"Photos, sealed, Rodriguez on the door."

"Good man. I'll need copies of your report and Rodriguez's photos."

"Already pulled." Abel handed over the folder.

O'Neill took it, glanced at the top page. Something in his face sharpened and went still — the way a face did when it got confirmation instead of surprise. He flipped to the next page. Then the next.

"The scoring on the tumblers," he said.

"Professional job. Somebody who knew exactly what they were doing."

"And the chemical residue?"

"Same source as what I smelled at the tree line. Fast-acting. Would've hit both canopies within two seconds of deployment." He paused. "Which means both chutes got treated before she even boarded. Whoever did it knew her jump order and her locker assignment."

O'Neill closed the folder. "Inside job, or outside?"

"Access says inside, or somebody real good at faking inside. The maintenance cover works both ways."

O'Neill nodded slowly. "The memo," he said.

Abel looked at him.

"The anomalous personnel memo," O'Neill said. "You flagged her file the day she arrived."

"Standard procedure for that kind of notation."

"What notation, exactly?"

Abel took a second. Then: "The one that came down from NORAD. Unusual physical parameters. Report any anomalous activity."

"And did you?"

"Was about to," Abel said. "Then she survived a double chute failure from eight hundred feet, and I figured I'd have a conversation first."

O'Neill held his gaze for a long moment. "Smart call."

"Been doing this thirty years, Colonel. I know the difference between a memo that exists to protect somebody and a memo that exists to flag somebody for somebody else's purposes."

Something shifted in O'Neill's expression. Not much. Just enough. "Which one's this?"

"The second one," Abel said. "In my assessment."

O'Neill nodded, looked down at the folder, then at the door to Buffy's room, then back at Abel.

"I'm going to need you to lose that memo," he said.

"What memo," Abel said.

O'Neill almost smiled. "You'll be hearing from General Hammond's office."

"Looking forward to it," Abel said, and he meant it.

Carlisle — going by Mercer on this particular phone — sat in an airport hotel room in Columbus and listened to the voice on the other end finish laying out its assessment of the situation. He'd known this call was coming since the news alert hit. He hadn't been looking forward to it.

"The primary objective was not achieved," the voice said.

"Correct."

"You confirmed both placements personally."

"I did."

A pause. "Then there's a variable you failed to account for."

Carlisle didn't say anything, because there was nothing to say. It was true. There was a variable, and he had no idea what it was, and that bothered him a whole lot more than plain professional embarrassment. In nineteen years he had never once missed a variable. He had never had a target survive something that shouldn't have been survivable. The girl had fallen eight hundred feet with both chutes destroyed and walked away. The only framework he had said that couldn't happen. And it had happened anyway, which meant the framework needed some serious updating.

"She's in the hospital," he said. "There may still be an opening—"

"No." The voice was flat. "The installation's under investigation now. Military CID inside twenty-four hours. That window is closed."

"Then my recommendation is extraction and reassessment."

"Agreed. However. The second client will need to be notified."

Carlisle thought about the second client. The second client was not the kind of organization that took unexpected variables well, and they took contractors who produced those variables even worse.

"I'll handle the notification," he said.

"Yes," the voice said. "You will." And then, in a tone that made it very clear his continued usefulness to the first organization now hinged entirely on how well he handled the second: "Be thorough."

The call ended. Carlisle sat there a moment, looking at the phone. Then he opened his laptop and started making arrangements to be somewhere else entirely by morning.

The second client was going to be a problem. They had long memories, and a real particular dislike of loose ends, and Carlisle had just become one of those.

He started typing faster.

~~~LELB~~~

Abel worked it the way he worked everything — methodically, all the way through, no shortcuts, because shortcuts were how you missed things, and missing things in his line of work ranged anywhere from embarrassing to fatal depending on the day.

He started with the roster.

Fort Benning's maintenance staff ran two hundred and fourteen civilian employees across the whole installation, thirty-seven of them assigned to the training areas that covered the 507th's equipment buildings. Of those thirty-seven, Abel pulled the access logs for the seventy-two hours before Summers' third jump and cross-checked them against the physical sign-in sheets at every checkpoint — because the Army, being the Army, ran both systems separately and had never quite gotten around to reconciling them, which meant discrepancies fell right into that gap and just sat there unless somebody went looking.

Abel went looking.

He found three discrepancies. Two of them had easy explanations. One didn't.

The one that didn't was a badge swipe at 0214 hours two nights before the jump, equipment building four, logged under the ID of a maintenance worker named Gerald Purcell — who, according to the sign-in sheet, had clocked out at 1730 the day before, and whose supervisor confirmed hadn't set foot on the installation since.

Abel pulled Purcell's file. Eleven months on the job. Clean background. Solid reviews. The photo on file showed a medium-height, medium-build white guy with brown hair, no distinguishing features — close enough to Summers' description of the surveillance asset to matter, and vague enough to be useless in a lineup.

He pulled the camera footage for equipment building four at 0214.

The east entrance camera had gone down at 0209 and come back at 0227. Eighteen minutes of nothing. The west entrance camera showed nothing unusual, which made sense, because whoever'd gone in the east side hadn't come out the west — meaning they'd left through the east entrance during that eighteen-minute gap, meaning the malfunction hadn't been a malfunction at all.

Professional, Abel thought. Real professional.

He called CID and gave them Purcell's name, his badge number, the camera timestamps, and then sat back and thought about the memo that no longer existed and the colonel who'd taken his folder with the look of a man confirming something he'd already suspected, and he thought about what it meant that somebody had managed to forge installation credentials, spoof a badge system, and loop a security camera, all clean enough that it had taken him and Rodriguez forty minutes crawling around on their hands and knees just to find the one thing they'd left behind.

That wasn't a lone actor. That was an organization with resources, patience, and advance knowledge of the whole security setup.

Abel didn't much like what that suggested about how long they'd been planning this.

He picked up the phone and dialed the number O'Neill had left him.

~~~LELB~~~

O'Neill took the call standing in the hospital parking lot, in Georgia heat that was already working itself up to the kind of performance that explained why Sherman had considered it a weapon.

He listened to Abel lay it all out. The badge spoof. The camera loop. The eighteen-minute window. Purcell's ID used by somebody who wasn't Purcell.

"How long would it take to set up a camera loop like that," he said.

"Somebody who knew the system? Twenty minutes on site, maybe less. But they'd need the architecture in advance. That's not public information."

"So either somebody on the inside sold it, or somebody with enough pull got it officially."

"That's my read," Abel said.

"The badge. Purcell."

"Real employee, real badge, somebody cloned it. He's clean. Already talked to him."

O'Neill looked over at the hospital entrance — at the doors Joyce Summers had walked through that morning like a woman who'd decided nothing standing between her and her daughter was going to slow her down for one second longer than it took to find it and move it, an attitude he respected without reservation.

"You'll get a call from a Major Davis at the Pentagon," he said. "He'll be coordinating with CID. Give him everything."

"Understood." A pause. "Colonel."

"Abel."

"Thirty years, I've run a lot of courses. Had a lot of students come through." Another pause, with something under it. "Whatever Cadet Summers is, she's worth the trouble of finding out who did this."

"Yeah," Jack said. "She is."

He hung up, stood in the parking lot another minute turning the phone over in his hand, and then called Davis.

~~~LELB~~~

The wings ceremony was four days later.

Thursday. Hot and bright and smelling like pine and red Georgia clay. The families and sponsors who'd shown up to watch sat in bleachers set up on the parade ground outside the 507th's main building, arranged with that competent, no-frills efficiency the Army brought to every ceremony that wasn't full dress. The jumpers stood in formation in their BDUs, and Abel stood out front with the kind of posture that only looks effortless once you've practiced it for thirty years straight.

Buffy stood at parade rest in the second row and felt good.

Not complicated good. Not good-with-an-asterisk. Just good, plain and simple, the kind that shows up on the other side of something hard, once you've done the thing and it tried to kill you and you declined to die and here you were anyway, standing in the Georgia sun with your hands behind your back waiting for somebody to pin something to your chest.

Her left shoulder was still a little stiff. That would be gone in another day. The cut on her cheekbone had healed up so clean that the doctor clearing her for the ceremony had looked at it twice, then at her chart, then back at her with an expression that was the opening move in a set of questions she had absolutely no intention of answering.

She'd redirected. Every single one.

Somewhere back in the third row her mother was sitting next to Colonel O'Neill, a combination Buffy had feelings about that she was tabling for later. She could've listened in from here — Slayer hearing being what it was — but she'd made a deliberate call not to eavesdrop on her own mother and her own commanding officer, because some things were genuinely better off unheard.

What she did catch, not through eavesdropping so much as through simple physical proximity, was her mother saying something that got past O'Neill's defenses enough to pull out that quiet laugh of his — the one that was gone almost before you could be sure you'd heard it at all.

She filed that one too.

Abel called the formation to attention.

~~~LELB~~~

Joe Wilson stood three people to her left in the formation — she knew that because she'd checked her nine when they fell in, not because she was keeping tabs on him, but that's just where he happened to be. He stood at attention with the posture of a guy who'd decided today was going to be a good day no matter what the last three weeks had thrown at him, and the last three weeks had thrown plenty: a double dose of SEAL commentary, one unfortunate incident with a directional control on his fourth jump that he'd recovered from well enough that Abel hadn't said a word about it, and the ongoing reality of getting outscored on every single physical metric by a girl who came up to his shoulder.

He'd handled all of it a lot better than she'd initially given him credit for, Buffy thought. That apology in the mess hall hadn't been a fluke. There was something under the Marine Corps posturing worth something, once you got past the layer that had decided its whole job was to be irritated at her.

She made a mental note and put it away, because Abel had started calling names and the ceremony needed her attention now.

~~~LELB~~~

Abel called Summers last. She figured that was on purpose, and she got her confirmation when she caught the faint shift in his expression as she stepped forward — not quite a smile, but something in the neighborhood of approval, which from Abel counted as a standing ovation.

She came to attention in front of him.

He looked at her a second, the same way he'd been looking at her since the tree line — still measuring something he hadn't finished measuring yet. Then he leaned in and pinned the jump wings to her chest, careful and precise, the way a man moves when he's done this a hundred times and still treats every single one like it matters.

"Cadet Summers," he said.

"Sergeant Airborne," she said.

"Well done." Just that. Two words, same flat range-officer voice he used for everything. But she'd spent three weeks learning that voice's every frequency, and she knew exactly what those two words had cost him. She took them the way they were meant.

"Thank you, Sergeant Airborne," she said. "For everything."

He held her gaze a second, then gave one sharp final nod and called the next name, and she stepped back into formation with jump wings on her chest, her shoulder mostly healed, and two separate organizations out there who'd tried to kill her and failed. All things considered, she felt pretty good.

~~~LELB~~~

O'Neill found her afterward in the crowd, Joyce at his shoulder. He looked at the wings. Then at her face. Then back at the wings — and something crossed his expression that he shut down fast, fast enough that most people would've missed it entirely. Buffy had spent two years learning that face. She caught it clean.

Pride. The unguarded kind. There and gone in about a second flat.

"Nice hardware," he said.

"Thank you, sir." She glanced at her mother. "Mom. Don't."

"I'm not doing anything," Joyce said, though her eyes were noticeably brighter than the occasion strictly called for. She reached out and straightened the wings on Buffy's chest with the same careful hands that had straightened collars and fixed hair and put on butterfly bandages for twenty straight years, and Buffy let her. "They're crooked," Joyce said. They weren't.

"Sure," Buffy said.

O'Neill watched the whole thing with the look he saved for situations that were none of his business, which he was going to watch anyway because the alternative was staring at the parade ground, and the parade ground had nothing on this. He caught Buffy's eye over Joyce's shoulder, and she gave him a look that said everything about mothers, and he gave her one right back that said he wasn't touching that with a ten-foot pole.

"Dinner," Joyce said, stepping back, apparently satisfied with her adjustments. "I found a place in Columbus with actual food. Not whatever that was in the dining facility."

"The stir fry is genuinely unidentifiable," Buffy agreed.

"The stir fry is fine," O'Neill said, and then, when both Summers women turned to look at him: "I've eaten worse."

"That is the saddest thing I've heard all week," Joyce said, "and I spent part of this week watching my daughter recover from falling out of an airplane, so that's saying something." She turned toward the parking lot with the air of a woman who'd made a reservation and fully intended to keep it. "Colonel, I assume you're coming."

Not really a question.

"Yes ma'am," O'Neill said — a sentence Buffy was fairly sure she'd never once heard him say to another living soul.

She fell in beside him as they followed Joyce across the parade ground and said, quiet enough that it was just for him, "Abel found the badge spoof. CID has it."

"Davis called this morning," he said, just as quiet. "Got a partial on Purcell's real identity. Give it forty-eight hours."

"And the second organization?"

He glanced at her sideways. "Working on it. Nothing solid yet. Maybourne's been asked some questions."

Buffy took that in. Maybourne asking questions was one thing. Maybourne being asked questions was a whole different animal, and that distinction told her plenty — about how seriously O'Neill was taking this, about how much political capital he was willing to burn, and about exactly what he thought of people who went after cadets under his sponsorship.

"How much trouble is that going to cause you," she said.

"Not your problem."

"Sir—"

"Cali." He said it in the voice that shut that particular conversation right down. Then, a beat later, different register entirely: "How's the shoulder."

She thought about being diplomatic. "Fine," she said.

He gave her the look.

"Better than it should be," she said.

"Yeah." He kept his eyes forward, on Joyce, who was already waiting by the rental car with the patience of a woman who'd been waiting for Buffy Summers to catch up to her for twenty years running. "We're going to need to talk about that. Not tonight."

"I know."

"Soon."

"I know," she said again.

He nodded. Then — because he was O'Neill, and O'Neill occasionally did things that weren't in any field manual, things she'd come to understand as his version of what other people said with a lot more words than he was ever comfortable using — he reached out and gripped her shoulder, the good one, once, and let go, and kept walking.

Buffy walked beside him across the Georgia parade ground, jump wings on her chest, and kept walking too.

The second client's name, when it mattered, was Simmons. Colonel Simmons, though the rank did a lot less work for him these days than it used to, on account of a certain chain of events that had left several corners of his operation more exposed than he'd have liked.

He sat in an office in a DC building that didn't show up on any public map and read the report that had landed on his desk at 0700 that morning. Read it twice. Then set it down and stared at the wall for a while.

The asset had failed. The girl was alive. Walked away from a double chute failure with nothing but bruising and an overnight hospital stay, which was a sentence Simmons read three separate times, because that's how many times it took before it stopped looking like a typo and started looking like data.

The data said this: Buffy Anne Summers could not be killed by conventional means. Or at least not by the means he'd used, calibrated to be unsurvivable for any normal human being, and apparently not up to the job.

That told him something. He wasn't sure yet exactly what, only that it meant updating his whole operational model, and that was going to cost him time and resources he'd rather have spent elsewhere.

The second problem was the investigation. CID getting involved was routine enough on its own — installations had incidents, CID managed them. But Colonel Jack O'Neill getting involved was an entirely different order of complication. O'Neill had a real gift for finding loose threads and pulling on them until things collapsed in places you never expected to be load-bearing. He'd done it before. He'd do it again.

O'Neill was the actual problem here.

Simmons picked up the phone and made a call. "I need options for a different approach," he said the second it connected. "Not kinetic. Something more elegant."

He listened to the response.

"No," he said. "Not the mother. The thing about O'Neill is, he expects direct. So we don't go direct." He picked the report back up. Looked again at the part about the wings ceremony. At the part about her accelerated clearance and her planned move into the SGC. "We go through the program," he said. "She wants into the Mountain. We let her in. And we watch."

He set the report back down.

"In the meantime," he said, "find out what she is."
