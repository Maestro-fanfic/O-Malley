The Pattern

Simmons got the report on the P4X-639 mission through a channel that wasn't supposed to exist, and which he maintained with the same careful attention he gave every channel that wasn't supposed to exist, since those were usually the ones worth the most.

He read it twice.

The artifact had responded to Summers. Tripled its energy output. Thrown out a coherent field centered directly on her. The SGC's instruments logged all of it, and the log made its way to him within six hours, courtesy of the asset he ran on level eighteen — a civilian contractor named Hooper, fourteen months in place, who understood perfectly well that his continued comfortable government-adjacent employment depended entirely on delivering exactly this kind of information, on schedule.

Simmons set the report down and stared at the wall for a while.

The artifact responding to her was interesting. The artifact responding to her and nobody else — including a Jaffa warrior whose own physiological profile was already anomalous by any measure — was a lot more than interesting. It meant whatever Summers was, ancient alien technology apparently recognized it as its own distinct category.

He'd spent the three weeks since Fort Benning trying to build some kind of framework for her and hadn't managed it. His research division had pulled everything they could find on the Initiative's work, which wasn't nearly as much as he'd have liked given the whole concrete-truck situation, and the best working theory they'd produced was that she was some kind of genetic variant, an enhanced human, maybe even a prototype of the exact soldier the Initiative had been trying to manufacture artificially.

The artifact data blew that theory out of the water. Enhanced humans were still humans. Whatever the Ancients had built their technology to recognize, it wasn't enhanced humans — enhanced humans were a twentieth-century development, and the Ancients had been gone for millions of years.

Which meant Summers was something that had existed, as a category, for at least that long — something the Ancients had considered worth programming their technology to notice.

Simmons found that extremely interesting, and also more than a little alarming, in the specific way it's alarming to discover you've been casually trying to eliminate something that predates human civilization by several orders of magnitude.

He picked up the phone. "I need the full research file on pre-Goa'uld biological anomalies," he said. "And everything we've got on Ancient technology's known response parameters." A pause. "And I need Hooper to find out what O'Neill said to Summers after that debrief."

He set the phone back down.

Direct approaches had failed twice already. Monitoring was producing data, but not leverage. What he actually needed was something in between — close enough to figure out what Summers was without setting off the protective response O'Neill would mount the second he felt his people were threatened.

The problem was that O'Neill's definition of his people ran unusually broad, and his response to threats against them ran unusually disproportionate, and Simmons had always found both of those qualities professionally inconvenient.

He looked back at the report. At the line about Summers standing in the field of the artifact and saying it felt like recognition.

He needed to know exactly what it recognized.

Three weeks into her time at the SGC, Buffy had a routine going, which was something she built into any new environment right away — routine was infrastructure, and infrastructure was what you stood on when things went sideways and you needed solid ground under your feet.

0500: up, PT, which in the Mountain meant the gym on level nineteen and a run that was technically a string of corridors, though she'd mapped a route through them long enough to be worth doing twice through. The gym usually sat empty at 0500 except sometimes for Teal'c, meditating in the corner, and she'd learned to just nod at him and leave him be, a protocol he'd approved of, since he hadn't shot her the first time she walked in.

0630: commissary, food, enough of it to make new people do a double-take, though by her second week the commissary staff had just started pre-plating for her without comment.

0700 onward: work. Briefings, mission preps, the academic coursework she was still finishing remotely, the reading O'Neill had assigned her on the SGC's mission history — a lot of it, and genuinely astonishing in several places — plus the practical orientation, which mostly amounted to following SG-1 around and paying attention.

She was very good at paying attention.

She was learning the Mountain the same way she'd learned the Hellmouth — walking it, listening to it, getting a feel for its rhythms and pressure points and its own particular way of being dangerous. The Hellmouth had been dangerous for what tried to come through it. The Mountain was dangerous for what it sent out and what occasionally tried to follow that trail back home. Different kind of danger. Same skill for reading it.

She walked the levels. Learned the faces. Learned who was worth talking to and who was performing competence instead of actually having it, and who was the person you went to when you needed something faster than the official channels could deliver — which at the SGC turned out to be a sergeant named Siler, who could fix nearly anything and treated the universe's endless supply of unfixable problems as a personal challenge.

She also started, with O'Neill's blessing and Daniel's help, going through the SGC's database of encountered species and phenomena, cross-referencing it against everything she knew from the Slayer's side of the ledger.

The overlap was bigger than she'd expected.

"Okay," Daniel said, leaning over the display where she'd flagged seventeen separate database entries. "Walk me through your read on these."

They were in Daniel's office, the most book-crammed space Buffy had run into since Giles's apartment, which stirred up a feeling she'd learned to recognize — the particular overlap of grief and comfort you get when both are attached to the same thing. She'd told Daniel that once, briefly, and he'd looked at her with the expression of a man who understood exactly what she meant without needing her to spell it out any further, and that had cemented something between them that was already proving useful.

"These three," she said, pointing. "The entities the SGC catalogued on P3X-118 and then again on Tollana during first contact. The way they moved between hosts, the energy signature Carter recorded." She looked at him. "Those aren't a Goa'uld subspecies."

Daniel looked at the entries. "What are they?"

"We call them Bringers on our side. Different name, same thing. Interdimensional parasites, organic hosts, but not symbiotic like the Goa'uld — consumptive. The host doesn't last more than a few weeks. They were supposed to have been wiped out of this dimension about four hundred years ago. There's a record of a Slayer doing it in 1604. But if they're showing up in Goa'uld space, either that elimination wasn't complete, or they've found a new way in through the gate network."

Daniel was already writing. He wrote the same way he did everything that interested him — total focus, completely checked out as a social being for the moment, running purely as a recording device. "The 1604 record. Can you access it?"

"I can get a copy. The Council kept records going back to the Bronze Age. Most of it's in England, but there are copies floating around. I used to have access to the digital archive. There's a woman named Giles running things now, since—" she stopped. "Since the people I knew."

Daniel looked up. "I'm sorry," he said, the exact same way O'Neill had said it — plain, no qualifier.

"Thank you," she said, the same way she'd answered O'Neill, taking it instead of deflecting it, which was something she was practicing.

"The archive," he said gently, steering them back, which she appreciated.

"I can request access through channels that still exist. Take a couple of weeks." She glanced at the other flagged entries. "These four are a different category. The energy discharge events on P4X-883, the ones originally logged as weapons tests." She pointed. "That's hellmouth activity. Small, minor convergences. Whatever dimensional infrastructure that planet has, it's been destabilized."

"By the Goa'uld?"

"Maybe. Or by something the Goa'uld disturbed without knowing what it was." She looked at him. "Do the Goa'uld know about the supernatural?"

Daniel thought about it. "The Goa'uld pose as gods. Their entire power structure runs on populations believing in supernatural forces and accepting the Goa'uld as the ones controlling those forces. Whether they know there's an actual supernatural ecosystem underneath the belief systems they exploit — that's something we've never nailed down. Ra's original population acquisition was on Earth. Ten thousand years ago. That's well within the window when the supernatural ecosystem would've been a lot more active and visible than it is now."

Buffy took that in. "Ra knew about the Slayer line."

"No hard evidence of that specifically."

"But it's possible."

"It's possible," he said.

She looked at the remaining flagged entries. "These ten," she said. "This is really what I wanted to show you."

Daniel looked them over. Spread across eight different mission reports, five different planets, four years of SGC operation. The thread connecting them was subtle enough that the database's own search function hadn't caught it — hadn't even come close. She'd found it herself, by reading, the particular kind of reading six years of Slayer work had trained into her, where you hold a dozen pieces in your head at once and wait for the pattern to show its face.

"What's the thread," Daniel said.

"Survivors," she said. "Every one of these events has a survivor who shouldn't have survived. Different species, different circumstances, different kinds of disasters. But every single time, there's one person who walks away from something that kills everyone else around them, and every single time, the SGC's after-action notes flag a physiological anomaly in that survivor's profile that never gets followed up, because there are always bigger fires to put out."

She watched Daniel run through the entries with that rapid, systematic focus he brought to pattern-hunting.

"You think these survivors are—" he started.

"I think these survivors are what happens when the Slayer line isn't limited to Earth," she said. "I think whatever mechanism produces somebody like me isn't exclusive to humans. I think it shows up wherever you get the right kind of dimensional pressure meeting the right kind of biological material. I think the Ancients knew about it. I think that's why the artifact on P4X-639 recognized me. And I think if the Goa'uld ever figure out what I am, and what these survivors are, they're going to want to either control it or wipe it out."

Daniel sat back.

She watched him work through it, watched him do the thing Daniel always did — take something huge and tangled and find the framework that made it make sense.

"That's—" he said. "That's a big hypothesis."

"I know."

"If you're right, the Slayer line isn't a terrestrial thing. It's galactic."

"Or older," she said.

Daniel looked at her.

"The Ancients seeded the gate network across the whole galaxy," she said. "The convergence points I can feel on Earth, the hellmouths — they're places where the wall between dimensions runs thin. That thinness has to come from somewhere. If the Ancients built the gate network, and the gate network connects into some larger dimensional structure—"

"Then the hellmouths could be a byproduct of Ancient construction," Daniel said, already writing again. "Or older. Pre-Ancient. Which would mean the Slayer line predates the Ancients."

"Which would explain why their artifact recognized me," she said. "They didn't create it. They just documented it."

The office sat quiet for a second.

"Jack needs to hear this," Daniel said.

"I know," she said. "Wanted to work it out with you first. Get the logic solid."

He looked up at her with the expression she was starting to associate with how much he enjoyed people who actually thought things through carefully. "You'd have made a good archaeologist," he said.

"I'd have made a terrible archaeologist," she said. "I'd keep wanting to hit the things I dug up."

Daniel laughed — a good, uncomplicated sound — and she filed it away the way she filed all the good ones, carefully, meaning to come back to it later.

She told O'Neill that evening. Not in his office this time — in the commissary after the dinner rush, when it was mostly empty and the staff was wiping down tables and the coffee was fresh because somebody had made a new pot twenty minutes back. They sat across from each other with their coffee, and she walked him through it the same way she'd walked Daniel through it — start to finish, logic to conclusion.

He listened without a single interruption, which was one of the things she'd come to value about him — the way his listening stayed active without ever getting intrusive.

When she finished he turned his coffee cup slowly on the table.

"Galactic Slayer line," he said.

"Possibly," she said. "Daniel's calling it a working hypothesis, not a conclusion."

"But your gut says."

"My gut says yes."

He nodded. "The artifact on 639."

"I want to go back," she said. "With Daniel and Sam, better equipment. I think it's got more to show us, and I think it's going to show it specifically to me."

"Hammond's already scheduling a follow-up." He looked at her. "The hypothesis about the Goa'uld knowing. Ra's population acquisition."

"It's a gap in what we know," she said. "One worth closing."

"Agreed." He looked down at his coffee, then back at her. "Simmons is going to figure out some of this."

"Some of it," she said. "Not all of it. He doesn't have the framework."

"He's building one," O'Neill said flatly. "He's got an asset somewhere in this Mountain. We haven't found them yet, but they're here."

Buffy took that in. "I know," she said.

His head snapped toward her.

"I've had Slayer senses for six years," she said. "Somebody on level eighteen gets nervous every time I'm nearby. Not scared exactly. Nervous the way people get nervous when they're doing something they already know they shouldn't be doing."

O'Neill's face went through several things in a row. "And you didn't say anything."

"I told you I'd tell you anything operational immediately," she said. "I've been watching to see if it turned into anything operational. So far it's just watching." A pause. "But I can point him out for you whenever you want."

He looked at her a long moment. "You've been running a counter-surveillance operation inside my own Mountain."

"I've been paying attention," she said. "Which is what you asked me to do."

Another beat. Then, despite everything: the almost-smile. "Hooper," he said.

She looked at him.

"Civilian contractor, level eighteen," he said. "We've had a flag on him for six weeks. Haven't moved yet because we wanted to see who he was talking to."

"You've been running a counter-surveillance operation inside your own Mountain," she said.

"I've been paying attention," he said. "It's kind of my whole thing."

They looked at each other across the commissary table.

"Simmons," she said.

"Has to be," he said. "Confirms the link between Fort Benning and the NID faction. Which is exactly what Davis needs to move." He picked his coffee back up. "I'll need your formal statement about Hooper. What you observed, when, exactly what triggered the nervous reaction."

"I can do that now."

"Tomorrow morning. Davis is coming in."

She nodded, wrapped both hands around her cup, and thought about Simmons in whatever office he worked out of, building his framework, and about how much time they had before that framework got complete enough to be dangerous.

"He's going to move again," she said. "Once he's got enough of the picture."

"I know," O'Neill said. "We're going to move first."

He said it with that flat certainty of a man who'd already made the decision somewhere she hadn't been in the room, and had been executing on it ever since — which she'd learned to recognize as his default operational mode, and which, on balance, she found deeply reassuring.

"What do you need from me," she said.

"Same thing I've always needed," he said. "Show up. Pay attention. Don't die."

She picked up her coffee. "I can do that."

"I know you can." And then, because he was O'Neill: "Finish your coffee. It's actually good tonight."

She finished her coffee.

Outside the Mountain, the Colorado night was doing what Colorado nights did in July — clear and huge and full of stars she could see fine from the surface but not from down here, and above the two of them, somewhere in a direction she was only just beginning to understand, the galaxy went on stretching toward places she was going to have to go.

She finished her coffee and got ready for whatever came next.
