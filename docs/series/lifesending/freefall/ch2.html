Recognition

The Mountain was exactly what it sounded like.

Buffy had driven past Cheyenne Mountain enough times over her two years at the Academy that the sight of it from the highway had gone unremarkable, the way anything goes unremarkable once you've seen it enough — which was a strange thing to realize, since the thing in question was a mountain with a classified military installation buried underneath it. You'd think that would hold onto its novelty a lot longer. Apparently not.

What hadn't gone unremarkable was driving toward it with a pass that got her through the outer checkpoint, then the next, then the one after that, each one staffed by MPs who checked her ID with the kind of careful attention that came from a job description built entirely around not being fooled. At the third checkpoint a sergeant who'd clearly been told to expect her made a quick call and waved her through, pointing her toward an underground parking area, and that was the moment it started feeling real — realer than the outside of a mountain could ever quite manage.

She parked. Sat there a second. Looked up at the concrete ceiling of the parking structure.

She was inside Cheyenne Mountain. She was here because Whistler had shown up on her porch in Sunnydale three years ago, because the Powers That Be had decided the world needed her somewhere — an origin story she was fairly sure was never going to make it into her official personnel file.

She got out of the car and shouldered her bag.

The elevator went down a long, long way.

Buffy counted floors out of habit, the same reflex that made her count exits and check sightlines and clock everybody's position the second she walked into a room — three years of habits from being both the Slayer and a cadet at the same time, and together they added up to a pretty solid situational awareness package. Seventeen floors down, the elevator opened onto a corridor that smelled like recycled air and industrial cleaner and something electrical she couldn't name, something that hummed at a frequency she could feel in her back teeth.

A sergeant was waiting. Young, professional, a little wide-eyed the way people sometimes got around her — petite and blonde, an expression that made her look about sixteen right up until you actually looked at her eyes, at which point most people recalculated fast.

"Cadet Summers," he said. "Lieutenant Ferretti will escort you to General Hammond."

"Lead the way," she said.

Ferretti turned out to be a compact, dark-haired guy with laugh lines and the easy movement of someone who'd been shot at enough times that it had permanently rearranged his relationship with ordinary space. He shook her hand with the kind of directness that meant he didn't waste handshakes on people he hadn't already decided were worth the effort.

"Colonel O'Neill's told us about you," he said, falling in beside her.

"Should I be worried about what specifically," she said.

Ferretti grinned. "Said you were good. Coming from O'Neill, that means you're probably terrifying." He glanced at her sideways. "Also said you eat a lot, which I only bring up because the commissary here's actually decent, and I figured you'd want to know your options."

Buffy considered him. "I think we're going to get along fine, Lieutenant."

"Lou," he said. "Everyone calls me Lou."

~~~LELB~~~

General George Hammond was a broad, calm man with the kind of face that had settled so completely into authority it didn't need to announce itself anymore. He stood behind his desk when she came in and looked at her with the eyes of a man who'd been thoroughly briefed and was now running a side-by-side comparison between the briefing and the actual person standing in front of him.

Buffy came to attention. "Cadet Buffy Summers reporting as ordered, sir."

"At ease, Cadet." He studied her a moment. "Sit down."

She sat. He sat. He folded his hands on the desk with the ease of a man who was completely comfortable with silence and had no interest in filling it just for the sake of filling it, which she respected.

"Colonel O'Neill speaks highly of you," he said.

"He's been generous, sir."

"He's also been accurate, which in my experience is rarer." He looked at her steadily. "I've read your file, Cadet. Both of them."

She kept her face neutral. "Sir."

"The official one's impressive. The other one raises questions I've been told to expect you to answer over time, not all at once."

"That's an accurate characterization, sir."

Hammond nodded slowly. "I want to be direct with you, Cadet Summers. This command operates in an environment that is, by definition, extraordinary. The people who work here were partly selected for their ability to take in information that falls outside any framework they had for understanding the world." He paused. "I say this because what I'm about to show you is going to fall outside every framework you've got, and I want you to know your reaction will be noted, but not judged."

Buffy thought about the Master's bones crumbling to dust. About the Judge and the rocket launcher. About forty-some demons in a Sunnydale corridor with nothing but a combat knife. About Whistler on her porch, talking about parasitic aliens and stargates and a future that needed her, specifically, in a specific place.

"Sir," she said carefully, "I've got a somewhat more flexible framework than most people."

Hammond looked at her. Then: "O'Neill said something along those lines." He stood. "Come with me."

~~~LELB~~~

The gate room was two levels down, through a security door that took Hammond's palm and retina both, and when it opened and she walked through, Buffy stopped dead.

She'd known it was there. Whistler had told her — called it the Chappa'ai, said it was a device for traveling between worlds, built by some ancient race and used by parasitic aliens, and the whole reason the Powers That Be needed her here in the first place. She'd known all of that the way you know things somebody's told you about.

She hadn't known what it looked like.

It was enormous. That hit her first — the sheer physical scale of the thing, a ring of pale alien stone thirty feet across, standing upright in a bay built specifically to hold it, covered in symbols she somehow recognized as significant without being able to say why, the way certain sounds register as meaningful before you've actually parsed the words. Chevrons glowing orange. A ramp leading up to it. A control room visible through the windows above.

It wasn't active. Just standing there. And it was, hands down, the most alien thing she had ever laid eyes on — which was saying something, given her track record with alien things.

She stood and stared at it a long moment.

"Well," she said.

"Yeah," said a voice behind her. "That's about what everyone says."

She turned. O'Neill was leaning against the wall to her left, arms crossed, wearing the expression he used when he was enjoying something and had decided not to let it show — which she'd learned was, in fact, exactly how he showed it.

"You could've warned me," she said.

"Could've," he agreed, in the tone of a man who'd thought about it and rejected it. "Wouldn't have helped much though. You've gotta just see it."

Hammond had stopped a few paces back, watching the two of them with the look of a man filing information for later. "The Stargate connects to a network of similar devices scattered across the galaxy," he said. "We've been using it four years now. Explored over a hundred worlds. And made enemies considerably better armed than we are, who've been to this planet before, and would love to come back."

Buffy looked at the gate. At the symbols carved into it. At the faint residual energy she could feel coming off it, something the Slayer noticed and her brain immediately filed away for later.

"The Goa'uld," she said.

Hammond's head snapped toward her.

"I've been briefed," she said carefully. "Generally. Not specifically. I know the shape of it."

Hammond and O'Neill traded a look she didn't bother trying to read, because she already had a decent guess what it said. She'd spent enough time now watching those two men communicate to have a working grasp of the grammar.

"Then you understand what we do here," Hammond said.

"Yes sir."

"And you understand what you're being invited into isn't a training assignment. You'll be a full working member of this command, responsibilities included — field operations too, once you've been assessed and cleared."

"Understood, sir."

"And you understand the classification level on everything you encounter here is absolute."

"Sir, I've been keeping secrets since I was fifteen years old." She said it plain, no heat behind it. "I'm good at it."

Hammond looked at her a moment. Then, with the precision of a man choosing his words carefully: "O'Neill said that too." He glanced at the gate. "You'll be assigned to SG-1 for your initial orientation. Colonel O'Neill's team. We'll assess your placement after that." He looked back at her. "Questions?"

Buffy looked at the Stargate. Thought about Whistler on the porch. About the Powers and their reasons. About three years of early mornings and APFT scores and jump wings and a combat knife and forty demons, and finally, finally, here.

"When do I start?" she said.

She started the next morning.

SG-1's briefing room sat on level twenty-seven and smelled like coffee that had been sitting way too long, mixed with that chalk-and-dry-erase smell rooms get when a lot of thinking happens in them. Buffy showed up at 0745 for an 0800 briefing and found she wasn't even the first one there.

The guy already at the table was reading something with way too many footnotes for comfortable reading, scribbling notes in the margins in what looked like three different languages, switching between them without seeming to notice he was doing it. He had that slightly unfocused look of a man who mostly lived somewhere inside his own head and tolerated the outside world as a necessary inconvenience.

He looked up when she walked in.

"Oh," he said. "You must be Buffy."

"Cadet Summers," she said.

"Right, sorry, Colonel O'Neill told me—" he caught himself and started over, in the manner of someone who did this a lot. "Daniel Jackson. Team archaeologist and linguist." He stuck his hand out and she shook it. "Jack talks about you. A lot."

Buffy sat down across from him. "He talks about me."

"Well." Daniel considered that. "He mentions you. Frequently. With the specific inflection he gets when something actually matters to him, which, if you know Jack, is pretty different from the inflection he uses for things that don't." He seemed to realize he'd said more than he'd meant to. "Anyway. Welcome."

"Thanks."

He looked at her with the open, direct assessment of someone who'd decided a long time ago that looking straight at things was more efficient than pretending not to. "He also said you were extraordinary. Which, coming from Jack—"

"Means something," Buffy said. "Lieutenant Ferretti made the same point."

"Lou's perceptive." Daniel went back to his notes, then looked back up almost immediately. "Can I ask you something?"

"You can ask."

"Your file. The public one. Your performance metrics are—" he paused, hunting for the word. "Anomalous."

"I've been told."

"And the less public file's got some notations that are—"

"Also anomalous," she said.

"Yeah." He studied her. "I ask because I've spent my whole career looking at things that don't fit the existing framework, trying to figure out what framework would fit them. And you're giving off the same—" he waved a hand vaguely. "The same quality."

Buffy looked at him. He was, she thought, probably the most straightforwardly honest person she'd met since Willow — a thought that came with its usual freight of grief, one she'd gotten pretty good at not letting show on her face. He wasn't trying to pump her for information. He was just genuinely curious, the kind of curious that was practically his whole way of engaging with the world.

"I'll tell you what I've told everybody else," she said. "There's some stuff that's mine that I'm not ready to lay out yet. But I'm not going to lie to you."

Daniel nodded like that answer was entirely satisfactory. "That's more than most people offer." He went back to his notes. "Coffee's on the counter. Been there since six, so manage your expectations."

Buffy got up and poured herself a cup. It was terrible. She drank it anyway, because Slayer metabolism plus six hours of sleep meant she needed the fuel regardless of quality, and she was standing at the counter with her bad coffee when the door opened and Teal'c walked in.

~~~LELB~~~

She'd met Teal'c before — in a hallway in Sunnydale, in a hospital room. But meeting Teal'c socially and meeting Teal'c professionally turned out to be two completely different experiences. Professionally, Teal'c walked into a room the way weather moved into a valley — total inevitability, and everything around him rearranged itself accordingly. He was in BDUs, carrying his staff weapon with the easy familiarity of a man carrying something that had been part of his body for decades. Because it had been.

He saw her and stopped.

She looked back at him.

"Buffy Summers," he said.

"Teal'c," she said. "Thank you again. For Sunnydale."

He inclined his head. "You have recovered."

"I have."

He gave her that particular look — the one she'd already come to think of as Teal'c genuinely assessing something, not just glancing at it. "You are different than before," he said.

"I had a rough summer."

"Indeed." He crossed to his chair with the unhurried certainty of a man who always knows exactly where he's going, sat down with a kind of deliberate economy that made Daniel's slightly scattered presence look even more scattered by comparison, and set his staff weapon against the wall with almost geometric precision. "I am pleased you survived the incident at Fort Benning."

"Me too," she said.

"Those responsible have not been identified."

"Not yet."

Teal'c looked at her steadily. "They will be," he said, and it wasn't a prediction so much as a statement of intent — a description of a future he planned on personally arranging. Buffy believed him completely.

~~~LELB~~~

O'Neill and Carter arrived together at 0758, which Buffy logged automatically because she logged everything, talking in the shorthand of two people who'd spent so much time together that full sentences had become an inefficiency they'd mostly abandoned. Carter was tall and blonde and moved with the compressed energy of someone almost always thinking faster than the room around her required, and she looked Buffy over with the eyes of a scientist who'd just spotted an interesting problem, mixed with the professionalism of someone who'd already decided the interesting problem was also a colleague and deserved to be treated as such.

"Captain Samantha Carter," she said. "Sam."

"Cadet Summers. Buffy. Cali, if you prefer. I answer to most things."

Sam smiled — a real one, the kind that reaches. "I've read your file."

"The public one, or—"

"Both," Sam said. "The second one's interesting." She said it with pure scientific appreciation, no agenda behind it, just interest, and Buffy filed her instantly under people who are going to be fine.

O'Neill had gone to the head of the table and was watching all of this with the look of a man who'd set something in motion and was now tracking its trajectory. He caught Buffy's eye, and she gave him the small nod that meant I'm okay, this is fine, stop hovering — a whole message she could send now in a single look, two years of Saturday dinners having built that particular shorthand.

He stopped hovering. Marginally.

"Alright," he said, once everyone had settled. "Here's how this works. Summers, you're with us for the orientation period, minimum six weeks, Hammond's call whether it extends. You'll be at every briefing, every mission prep, and any missions cleared for your participation, which for now means anything below whatever threat level Hammond decides to set for you." He looked at her. "Questions so far?"

"No sir."

"You'll also be finishing your academic coursework, which I hear you've already been doing remotely, and which your professors have been told is a special assignment. Which it is." He glanced around the table. "Everybody plays nice. Everybody answers questions within their clearance level. Everybody makes sure the newest member of this team knows where the infirmary is, where the commissary is, and how to work the coffee machine on level eighteen, which is the only one worth using."

"Level eighteen," Buffy said. "Got it."

"This one's terrible," Daniel said, without looking up.

"It is terrible," Sam agreed.

"I made it," O'Neill said.

A brief silence.

"It's terrible, sir," Sam said.

O'Neill looked like he was considering arguing the point and then decided against it. "Briefing in twenty on P4X-639. Dismissed to go find better coffee."

~~~LELB~~~

P4X-639 turned out to be a planet with ruins interesting enough to make Daniel practically vibrate when he talked about them, a UAV survey full of terrain Sam wanted to map properly, a mineral deposit on the SGC's acquisition list, and a threat profile Hammond had cleared Buffy to participate in — which she understood meant it was supposed to be relatively straightforward, and therefore was probably not going to be.

She'd spent three years on the Hellmouth. She knew exactly how "straightforward" tended to work out in practice.

Gearing up took an hour. She noticed, without much surprise, that the armorers had already been briefed on her weight and build — the load-out they'd put together fit her specs without a single adjustment, which told her O'Neill had planned way ahead, which in retrospect wasn't surprising at all. She checked everything with the same care she'd given her parachutes at BAC, only more so, because Fort Benning had taught her something extra about the value of double-checking things nobody had ever tampered with, and she wasn't in a hurry to learn that lesson twice.

Teal'c watched her check her gear.

"You are thorough," he said.

"I try to be."

"It is a quality that has served you." He glanced at her P90. "You have trained with this weapon."

"At the Academy. Some."

"On the range, or in application."

"Range. And once somewhere that wasn't a range, though the weapon I had on me at the time was a different model."

Teal'c took that in. "You performed adequately."

"I survived," she said. "Whether that's adequate depends on your standards."

He gave her that assessing look again. "In my experience," he said, "survival is the foundation all other standards rest on."

"Yeah," she said. "That's been my experience too."

She ran the P90's action one last time, checked her sidearm, checked her vest, checked the knife she'd started carrying in a boot sheath ever since somebody handed her back a combat knife clean and sharp in a Sunnydale hospital room, and stood up.

"Ready," she said.

~~~LELB~~~

The gate activating was not something you got used to, she decided — even if you'd been through it a hundred times. She'd been through it exactly zero. The kawoosh of energy blasting outward and then folding itself back into that shimmering event horizon sent every Slayer sense she owned crackling straight to high alert before she managed to rein them back in, and the event horizon itself had a quality she didn't have a word for — a kind of potential that felt ancient and enormous and patient, the way very powerful things sometimes felt when they weren't currently being used.

She kept her face neutral.

"First time," Sam said beside her. Not a question.

"That obvious?"

"A little." Sam said it kindly. "Doesn't stop being impressive. You just get better at not showing it."

"Right now I'm grateful for that as a goal," Buffy said.

O'Neill was already at the top of the ramp. "SG-1, move out." A glance back. "Summers."

She looked at him.

He didn't say anything. Just the look — the one that said I've got you, and I know you've got you, but I've got you anyway — a look she'd learned, over two years, how to just take without needing to make into something he'd feel obligated to walk back.

She nodded. Turned to the gate. Squared her shoulders.

She walked through.

~~~LELB~~~

The other side was blue sky and pine-smelling air, close enough to Earth's that she could breathe it without gear, different enough that her Slayer senses flagged it in a way she couldn't quite put into words — like food tastes slightly different out of somebody else's kitchen, even with all the same ingredients. The gate stood in a clearing on a hillside overlooking a valley complex Daniel had already pegged as pre-Goa'uld, maybe Ancient, maybe older, which was the reason his voice had that vibrating quality she was starting to recognize as his version of O'Neill's almost-smile.

She came through the gate, cleared the event horizon, checked her nine and three. Treeline fifty meters left, open hillside right, a path leading down into the valley, clear sky, no immediate threats.

Teal'c was already at her left. He'd come through the gate the same way he came through doorways, like the whole landscape already belonged to him, staff weapon up, scanning without a trace of urgency.

"Clear," he said.

"Clear," she confirmed.

He looked at her. "Your response time is adequate."

From Teal'c, she was starting to understand, adequate was something you worked your way up to.

"High praise," she said.

"It was not intended as such."

"I know," she said. "I'm choosing to take it that way anyway."

Something shifted in Teal'c's expression that she thought — if she was being generous — might be the very earliest architectural stages of amusement. He turned back to the treeline without another word.

O'Neill came through last, checked that the gate was closing behind them, and moved up to join her. He looked out over the valley. "Okay," he said. "Standard formation. Carter, Teal'c, point. Daniel, with me. Summers, with Daniel." A glance her way. "Watch his six. He gets interested in things."

"I do not—" Daniel started.

"You absolutely do," Sam said.

"That's not—"

"Daniel," O'Neill said.

"Fine," Daniel said. "Yes. I get interested in things. It's never caused a serious—"

"P3X-888," O'Neill said.

"That was different."

"Nem," Sam said.

"Also different."

"The Touched," Teal'c said.

"Alright," Daniel said. "I take the point."

Buffy fell in beside him as they started down the hillside path toward the valley. "I'm going to need the reference files on all of those," she said quietly.

"I'll pull them for you," he said, with the cheerfulness of a man who loved explaining things and had just found a fresh person to explain them to. "There's context. There's always context."

"There usually is," she said, and checked her nine and three, and watched his six, and headed down the hillside toward whatever came next.

~~~LELB~~~

What came next was ruins.

Significant ones — she could tell that even without Daniel's commentary picking up speed as they moved through the outer perimeter of the complex. Stone structures built to last, not just to impress, though they'd clearly managed both. Carvings on the sections of wall still intact, which Daniel photographed and murmured over, mostly at his recorder and only sort of at his actual surroundings, exactly the behavior O'Neill had warned her about.

She watched his six. She also watched the ruins.

There was something here. She couldn't have named it, only that the Slayer noticed it the same way she noticed certain cemeteries — places where whatever made a spot charged had either been used hard or was still active. Not evil exactly. Just old. The way the Hellmouth was old, the way certain convergence points she'd learned to spot on patrol were old, places where the wall between categories ran thin.

She didn't say anything about it. She just filed it away.

O'Neill worked the outer perimeter with the systematic attention of a man who'd been shot at in enough different places to take every single one seriously until proven otherwise. He checked in on the radio every three minutes — she found herself timing it without meaning to, then noticed she was doing that and decided it was useful information to hang onto anyway.

Carter was over at the valley's eastern edge, running some kind of scan with a device Buffy couldn't have named, and whatever readings it was throwing back had her picking up the pace and narrowing her focus.

"Interesting," Sam said.

"Good interesting or bad interesting," O'Neill said over the radio.

"Currently unclear," Sam said. "There's a power signature under the structure in grid four. Faint, but there. Pre-Goa'uld, by the look of it."

"Ancient?" Daniel said, spinning toward her so fast Buffy had to shift to keep his six covered.

"Possibly," Sam said. "Or something earlier."

"There's nothing earlier than the Ancients," Daniel said. "Well — technically there might be, we've never actually established a firm—"

"Daniel," O'Neill said.

"Right. Power signature. Grid four. I should go look at the carvings." He was already moving. Buffy moved with him.

Her hand was on her P90. Not raised, just ready. The Slayer was doing something low and steady at the back of her mind — not the sharp spike of immediate danger, more like the constant hum of a situation with a lot more underneath it than the surface let on.

She trusted that hum. Trusted it for six years now, and it had gotten her through things that shouldn't have been survivable. No reason to stop trusting it now.

"Daniel," she said quietly.

"Mm."

"Slow down."

He slowed, visibly making himself do it. "Do you see something?"

"Not see," she said. "Feel." She checked her nine, her three, the shadows in the ruined doorways ahead. "Something in this area's active. Whatever the power signature is, it's not dormant."

Daniel looked at the ruins. Then at her. "You can feel it?"

She kept her eyes moving. "Problem?"

"No," he said, already rapidly reorganizing something in his head. "No, that's actually extremely interesting, would you be willing to describe—"

"Later," she said. "O'Neill."

"Copy," O'Neill said over the radio. "Report."

"Unknown active element in the grid four structure. Recommend we hold and assess before proceeding."

A brief pause. "On my way." Another pause. "Carter, with me."

Buffy held her position outside the grid four threshold, Daniel beside her doing a pretty solid impression of a man who was not vibrating with curiosity, her hand still on her P90, Slayer senses opened up as wide as she could manage, and she listened to whatever was inside make that low, patient hum of something that had been waiting a long, long time and had just noticed someone standing at its door.

She didn't go through the door.

Six years on the Hellmouth had taught her a lot of things, and one of the big ones was this: when ancient things notice you, the smart move is to wait for backup before you give them anything else to notice.

O'Neill got there in ninety seconds flat, which was fast for this terrain, and took up position on her right.

"Talk to me," he said.

"Active power source inside. Unknown type. Registered when we got close." A pause. "I think it registered specifically. Like it's aware of proximity."

O'Neill looked at the doorway, then back at her. "How sure are you."

She thought about the Hellmouth. About three years spent learning to read what the Slayer knew before she herself did. "Ninety percent," she said.

Sam arrived and had her scanner up before she'd fully stopped moving. Pointed it at the doorway. Checked the readings. Pointed it at Buffy. Checked those too. Looked back at the doorway.

"Huh," Sam said.

"Carter," O'Neill said.

"It's definitely aware," Sam said. "Readings spiked the second Cadet Summers moved within fifteen meters." She looked at Buffy with the expression Buffy was starting to recognize as Sam's own version of Daniel's vibrating curiosity, only more contained, but structurally identical. "Whatever's in there is responding to something specific about you."

Buffy and O'Neill looked at each other.

"Well," O'Neill said, in the tone of a man who'd long since learned to just accept that his life kept producing situations like this, since resentment wasn't going to be worth the trouble. "That's new."

"Not entirely," Buffy said carefully.

He looked at her.

"Things respond to me sometimes," she said. "It's part of the—" she paused, calibrating — "the anomalous part."

O'Neill held her gaze a moment. Then he looked at the doorway. Then back. "Can you tell if it's hostile."

She reached out with the Slayer sense, carefully, the way you'd reach a hand into a space before you actually put it in. Whatever was inside was old and patient and awake, and it was focused on her in a way that wasn't aggressive and wasn't welcoming either — just interested, on that enormous impersonal scale of something that processed the world at a size where individual intentions barely register.

"Not hostile," she said. "Just aware."

O'Neill sat with that for about four seconds, which she'd come to understand was roughly how long he needed to make decisions most people would take a lot longer over. "Okay," he said. "Carter, full scan from out here before anyone steps foot inside. Daniel, I want a read on every symbol we can see from this position. Teal'c, perimeter." He looked at Buffy. "You're with me. If it's responding to you, I want you where I can see you."

"Yes sir," she said.

She followed him to the threshold, and they stood there looking into the dimness where something very old had been waiting and had just decided she was worth its attention, and Buffy figured this was probably what Whistler had meant when he'd told her she was needed here — and that the Powers That Be had, as usual, been drastically understating the case.

~~~LELB~~~

The inside of the structure turned out to be bigger than the outside had suggested, which shouldn't have been architecturally possible, and was probably the Slayer's first real clue that whoever built this place had been working off a totally different rulebook than the builders she was used to. Stone floors. Walls covered in script that Daniel would end up spending six hours studying later. And in the center of the main chamber, half-buried in debris that looked more like centuries of slow accumulation than sudden destruction — a device.

Roughly the size of a card table, made of something that probably wasn't metal but had the feel of metal that had been reimagined from scratch. It glowed faintly at its center with that same low, patient light she'd been feeling since the treeline, and when she stepped through the doorway right behind O'Neill, it brightened several degrees and let out a tone she felt in her sternum more than she heard with her ears.

"Okay." O'Neill had stopped two steps in. P90 up. "That's reacting to you."

"Yes sir," she said.

"Carter."

Sam was in the doorway with her scanner. "Energy output just tripled." Her voice had that particular scientific composure that only barely covered a whole lot of excitement underneath it. "It's producing a coherent field, and that field's centered right on Cadet Summers."

"What kind of field," O'Neill said.

"Unknown. Nothing in our database matches this signature. It's not harmful, far as I can tell. The field seems to be—" another pause — "evaluative."

Buffy stood there in the middle of an unknown, evaluative field and looked at the device. The device looked right back at her with whatever it had instead of eyes. It wasn't unlike being sized up by Teal'c, honestly — a comparison she planned to keep entirely to herself, because Teal'c was a person and this was a machine and she was well aware that comparing the two said something weird about her relationship with both of them.

She took a step toward it.

The tone in her sternum climbed higher.

"Summers," O'Neill said.

"It's fine," she said. "It's not threatening."

"The fact that it's doing something to you doesn't exactly fit my definition of fine."

"I know your definition of fine, sir. This is different." Another step. "It's—" she reached for the words — "it's the way the Hellmouth feels when you're standing on it and you know exactly what you are. It's recognition."

Silence behind her.

She hadn't meant to say that. That was more than she'd planned to hand him here, in a ruined structure on some alien world, in front of his whole team. She'd had a careful conversation planned out for when they were back at the SGC, just the two of them, when she had the whole thing properly built.

She turned to look at him.

O'Neill's face had gone very still. Not closed off — just still, the way water goes still right before something moves through it underneath. He was looking at her with the exact same eyes he'd used across that hospital bed in Georgia, the ones that had asked is there something I should know — except with a whole lot more already lining up behind them now.

"Hellmouth," he said.

"Sir—"

"The thing in Sunnydale."

She held his gaze. "Yes."

"Which is not a geological feature."

"No. It's a dimensional convergence point. It's—" She stopped. Daniel was standing very still in the doorway to her left, not writing a single thing down, just soaking in every word. Sam had lowered her scanner and was watching too. Somewhere outside, Teal'c held the perimeter with the kind of serenity that probably meant he could hear every syllable and had simply decided to let the situation play itself out. "Sir," she said. "This is the conversation. The one I told you we needed to have. I was planning on it happening somewhere that wasn't an alien ruin in the middle of a classified mission."

"Yeah," O'Neill said. "Me too." He looked at the device, still humming out its patient, ancient tone. Then back at her. "Can we table it till we're not literally standing inside an unknown artifact?"

"Yes," she said, mostly relief. "Yes sir, we absolutely can."

"Good." He glanced at the device. "Carter, is this thing dangerous in the next thirty minutes?"

Sam checked her scanner. "Almost certainly not."

"Daniel, can the carvings wait till we come back with better equipment?"

"They've waited this long," Daniel said, which was his way of saying yes.

"Then we pull back, document from outside, go home, and I write a report that's going to give Hammond a headache and a reason to schedule a follow-up." He looked at Buffy one last time. "And we have the conversation. Tonight. My office. Door closed."

"Yes sir," she said.

The device let out one last tone as she stepped away from it — lower than the others, something she felt less in her sternum and more somewhere close to her conscience, the sound of something very old registering that the interesting person was leaving, and that it would just wait for her to come back.

She believed it would.

She followed O'Neill out of the structure into the alien afternoon light and didn't look back. She thought about tonight, and the closed door, and the conversation. She thought it was probably past time. And she thought that whatever came after it, she was ready.

She checked her nine and three, fell in behind him on the path back to the gate, and got on with it.

The debrief ran two hours, and Hammond sat through the whole thing with the focused patience of a man who'd been surprised so many times by this program that surprise itself had turned into a category he managed rather than one he actually felt anymore. He asked precise questions. He got precise answers. Twice during the debrief he looked at Buffy with the particular attention of a man quietly reassessing something, and didn't say a word about it, which she took as a good sign.

After the debrief O'Neill said "Get some food" to the whole team in the tone of a CO cutting people loose, then said "Summers, my office, thirty minutes" in a completely different tone, and that was that.

She ate in the commissary with Daniel and Sam, who spent most of it deep in a technical conversation about the power signature, occasionally requiring Daniel to sketch something on a napkin, and she worked through two plates of whatever the commissary was serving that night — some kind of chicken thing, better than it looked — and thought about thirty minutes.

Sam looked over at her during a lull. "You okay?" she said.

"Yeah," Buffy said. "Just—" she considered — "preparing."

Sam nodded the way somebody nods when they get it without needing it spelled out. "For what it's worth," she said, "he's harder to shock than he looks. And he already looks pretty hard to shock."

"I know," Buffy said. "That's not actually what I'm preparing for."

Sam looked at her. "Then what."

Buffy looked down at her plate. "Saying it out loud," she said. "To somebody who doesn't already know." She stopped. Started over. "There's a version of it that gets simpler once it's finally said. And there's a version where you say it and watch the person recategorize you, and you can't go back to the category you were in before." She looked up. "I like the category I'm in with him."

Sam was quiet a second. "I don't think the category changes," she said finally. "In my experience of him, anyway. I think it just gets bigger."

Buffy held onto that. Then she nodded and finished eating.

O'Neill's office had the organized bare-bones look of a guy who spent most of his time somewhere other than an office and had furnished the space accordingly. A desk. A chair on either side. A window looking out at the mountain, since the office happened to be inside the mountain. A framed photo she made a point of not looking at directly. A hockey stick in the corner she had questions about and had never once asked.

He was already behind the desk when she came in. He looked at her, then at the chair across from him, and she sat, and he closed the file he'd been reading and set it aside and gave her his full attention — the way O'Neill listened when he'd decided something mattered enough to earn every bit of it.

She took a breath.

"The Hellmouth," she said. "The thing I mentioned."

"The dimensional convergence point in Sunnydale," he said. "Which is real."

"Which is real. And there are others. All over the world. Places where the wall between this dimension and the others runs thin." She watched him take that in. "That's the infrastructure. The context is, those thin places attract things from the other dimensions. Things that come through and feed on this world, or use it, or try to end it, depending on what they're after."

O'Neill didn't say anything. He was doing the very-still thing.

"Someone has to deal with those things," she said. "Has to, in the sense that regular military and law enforcement don't have the toolkit for it and don't even know it exists, and can't know it exists without triggering the kind of civilizational panic that would be its own disaster. That's not a new problem. It's always been true. It's just that for the last several thousand years, the toolkit's been held by one specific line of people."

"A line," he said.

"One at a time," she said. "Always a girl. Always young — the how and why of that's complicated. Given the power early, trained or not depending on the century, sent out to deal with it. Alone. Until she died, and it passed to the next one."

Something moved through O'Neill's expression. Not disbelief. More like one very large piece finally clicking into place.

"A Slayer," he said.

She looked at him.

"The word," he said. "I've heard it. Twice. Sunnydale, and—" he stopped. "The NID material we recovered from the Initiative had a reference. One line. Heavily redacted. I figured it was a code name for a weapons program."

"It's not a weapons program," she said. "It's me. I'm the Slayer." She let that land. "Have been since I was fifteen. Heightened strength, speed, healing, senses, the specific ability to see and engage supernatural threats. Prophetic dreams sometimes. The healing's why Fort Benning didn't kill me. The sensing's why that artifact in grid four responded to me. The rest of it's why my performance metrics don't match my physical profile, why the Initiative wanted me, why two different organizations have tried to kill me, and why Whistler showed up on my porch in Sunnydale in 1999 and told me the world was going to need me here — specifically here, at this installation, in this work."

She stopped talking.

O'Neill sat with it a moment. Then: "The girl before you."

She'd expected logistics, or skepticism, or a request for proof. She had not expected that question first. It caught her somewhere she hadn't defended, and she took a breath against it. "Kendra," she said. "She died a few years back. And the one before her was me, technically. I died first. For about a minute. When I was sixteen. Xander brought me back. Then I died again and came back again, but — that's for a different time."

O'Neill's jaw tightened. "You died," he said. "At sixteen."

"For a minute."

"And then they sent you right back out."

"That's how it works."

He was quiet for a moment, and she watched him build something out of everything she'd just handed him, watched him fit it up against everything he already had — the scores, the knife, the hallway at the Initiative, the hospital bed, two years of Saturday dinners, Fort Benning.

"The people who tried to kill you," he said. "They know what you are."

"They know something's anomalous. I don't think they've got the full picture. Not yet."

"Simmons," he said, which told her Davis's forty-eight hours had been productive.

"Probably."

"He's NID-adjacent. Has been for years." O'Neill's voice went flat in the way it did when he was talking about people who'd already used up every benefit of the doubt he might've offered them. "We've been building a case."

"I know. I've been watching you build it."

He looked at her.

"From a distance," she said. "I couldn't tell you what I knew without telling you what I am, and I wasn't ready."

"Are you ready now," he said.

"I was ready about twenty minutes into that conversation on the planet," she said. "Just a little slow getting the rest of the way here."

Something shifted in his face — the almost-smile. There and gone. "Yeah," he said. "Well." He looked down at the desk a second, then back up. "The Council. The organization that runs the Slayer program."

"Ran," she said. "Past tense. Dissolved about a year before I left Sunnydale. Institutional collapse. Also for a different time."

"But there's no oversight structure now."

"Just me," she said. "And the people I trust. Short list."

"How short."

She looked at him. "My mother," she said. "And you."

The stillness in his face was different this time. Not the processing kind. This was the stillness of something that had landed and been accepted and was going to stay put.

"Okay," he said.

Just okay. Same as Georgia.

"Okay?" she said.

"The Simmons situation, we handle through channels. Everything you've given me tonight stays in this room until we decide together what to do with it." He held her gaze. "I'm not taking this up the chain without talking to you first. You know what I'm risking by saying that."

"I do," she said.

"And you know I need something back from you."

"What."

"No more tabling," he said. "Not with me. Something's operational, something's a threat to this team, this command, or you — I need to know. Supernatural, Slayer, whatever it is. I need to know."

She thought it over seriously, because he deserved a serious answer. "Some of it's not mine to hand out," she said. "Stuff about the other people in this work. Identities, locations."

"Understood. Within that."

"Within that," she said, "yes."

He nodded, then leaned back in his chair, doing the thing she'd watched him do before at the end of hard conversations once he considered them resolved — let the weight of it settle, set it down, and look ahead to what came next.

"So," he said. "Supernatural threats." He looked up at the ceiling. "Vampires."

"Yes."

"Real."

"Very."

"For crying out loud." He said it to the ceiling, no heat behind it, the way you'd note the weather turning worse. "Snakes in people's heads weren't enough. Fine. Sure. Vampires."

"Huh." He seemed to actually sit with it. "The Initiative was studying them."

"Capturing them. Experimenting. Goal was a controllable supernatural soldier, which is a goal I have a whole lot of opinions about."

"I bet you do." He looked at her. "Demons."

"Lots of varieties. Mostly hostile. Some not. Depends on the context."

"Of course it does." He rubbed his face with one hand. "And you've been dealing with this since you were fifteen."

"Yes sir."

"Alone."

"Mostly. I had people. Friends. A Watcher." She said that last word carefully. "Until the graduation."

He was quiet a moment, fitting that against what he already knew about Sunnydale, about why she'd left, about the things she never quite talked about.

"I'm sorry," he said. Just that, no qualifier, said the way he said things he actually meant, all the way down.

"Thank you," she said.

They sat there a moment in that office inside the mountain, and Buffy thought about being fifteen, about dead vampires, about a library full of people she'd loved, about a plunger detonator and too much fertilizer, and about how grief settled in you differently after three years than it had at first — heavier in some places, quieter in others — and she thought about what it meant that she'd finally said all of this to somebody who was actually going to do something useful with it.

"Okay," O'Neill said eventually. "New protocol. Anything we run into in the field that reads anomalous to you, you tell me. Immediately. Not after. Not once you've assessed it. Immediately."

"Yes sir."

"And the sensing thing. You can feel stuff before the instruments pick it up."

"Usually."

"That's an asset," he said. "Not a liability. Not something to manage or hide. An asset. We treat it that way."

She nodded.

"Carter's going to want to study you," he said. "I'm not giving her permission. But she's going to want to."

"I know," Buffy said. "She can ask. I'll tell her what I can."

"Fair enough." He stood, which meant the conversation was over, and she stood with him. He came around the desk and stopped right in front of her. Held out his hand.

She looked at it a second, then shook it.

"Welcome to SG-1, Cadet Summers." Formal phrasing, but with a whole lot less formal weight behind it. "For real this time."

"Thank you, Colonel," she said. And then, because she'd been the Slayer for six years now and had learned some things were better said flat-out instead of buried in looks and handshakes: "I'm glad I'm here."

He nodded. The almost-smile again. "Yeah," he said. "So am I."

She walked out of the office and down the corridor, squared her shoulders, and got on with the next thing, fourteen levels of granite and two hundred miles of Colorado sky sitting above her.
