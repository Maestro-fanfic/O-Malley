The Costume

The first thing Xander noticed about Ethan's Costume Shop was that everything cost more than it looked like it should.

The second was that the place smelled like somebody had locked a theater wardrobe in a basement for twenty years and opened the door that morning.

The third was Buffy.

She stood in front of a long dress the color of old champagne, one hand resting on the sleeve as if it might bolt if she moved too quickly. Lace climbed the bodice. The skirt had enough fabric to hide a marching band.

Xander looked from the dress to the price tag, then back to Buffy.

“You're going to need a wider front door.”

“Isn't it beautiful?”

“That's one word. ‘Load-bearing’ is another.”

Buffy ignored him. She had the look she got whenever Angel entered a room: half dreamy, half determined not to be caught dreaming. Xander had learned that commenting on that look was like checking whether a stove was hot with his face.

Willow hovered at Buffy's shoulder with a sealed costume bag hugged against her chest. The smiling model on the insert wore a short black skirt, a fitted top, and enough confidence to qualify as protective equipment. A folded white sheet had been clipped to the outside of the bag.

“Still going with the ghost?” Xander asked.

Willow pulled the sheet higher. “Ghosts are traditional.”

“So are eye holes.”

“I intend to have those.”

Buffy looked away from the dress long enough to give Willow an encouraging smile. “The sheet is optional.”

“The sheet is foundational.”

Xander grinned. “Willow Rosenberg: bringing structural engineering to the afterlife.”

She bumped him with one shoulder. It was gentle, but it moved him directly into a rack of plastic swords.

None of the swords survived close inspection. Neither did the pirate coat with vinyl cuffs, the rubber monster feet, or a military uniform whose manufacturer had decided that every soldier in history wore the same shade of green. Xander had twelve dollars in his pocket, eight of which were supposed to last until Friday. His mother had said there was money on the kitchen counter. There had been an empty bottle, a grocery receipt, and thirty-seven cents.

He could have worn his own clothes and called himself a civilian casualty. It had thematic weight.

Buffy disappeared toward the changing rooms with the dress. Willow followed, still defending the tactical necessity of the sheet. Xander wandered toward the rear of the store, where the lighting weakened and the costumes stopped pretending anyone had organized them.

A cardboard box sat beneath a rack of mascot heads. Someone had written CLEARANCE—INCOMPLETE across one side in black marker.

That was more his neighborhood.

He found one roller skate, a foam crown, three left gloves, and something that might once have been the tail of a dinosaur. Beneath them lay a grey hooded sweatshirt. It was soft at the cuffs and a little too large, but unlike most of the stock it felt like clothing rather than an argument with petroleum.

Folded inside it were a pair of wire-framed glasses without lenses and a black object shaped vaguely like a remote control. The remote was smooth and oval, with several shallow keys and a dark circular lens at one end. It looked too well made for a toy and too useless to be anything else.

There was also a badge.

Xander wiped dust from the plastic with his thumb.

ELI WALLACE

DESTINY EXPEDITION

The photograph showed a round-faced young man in the same hoodie, wearing an expression Xander recognized. It was the expression of someone who had just made a joke in a room where nobody else had noticed they were supposed to laugh.

No character name. No show logo. No helpful picture of a spaceship.

“Eli Wallace,” Xander read aloud. “Boldly going to fix the printer.”

The black remote warmed in his hand.

One of its keys flashed green.

Xander nearly dropped it. He pressed the key again. Nothing happened.

“Battery,” he decided.

The word sounded thin in the quiet corner.

He put on the glasses. The empty frames changed nothing, but the badge had a lanyard and the remote fit comfortably in his palm. With the hoodie, it made a costume if a person was willing to grade generously.

More importantly, it looked like the costume of a guy who had reached the spaceship without needing a uniform, muscles, mystical birthright, or a family credit card.

The man had a name badge. That meant somebody had expected him to be there.

Xander tucked the remote into the sweatshirt pocket and carried the pieces to the register.

The shopkeeper watched him approach.

He was lean, dark-haired, and English in a way that made Giles seem almost insufficiently committed. His sleeves were rolled with care. The name printed on the brass plate beside the register was ETHAN RAYNE.

Ethan took the badge between two fingers.

For the first time since Xander had entered the shop, the man's pleasant expression became entirely still.

“Where did you find this?”

Xander pointed toward the clearance box. “Archaeological dig beneath the giant chicken.”

Ethan glanced toward the back corner.

“Interesting.”

“That's usually what adults say right before something becomes more expensive.”

Ethan turned the badge over. There was no manufacturer's mark on the back, only a pattern of fine lines almost hidden beneath the cloudy laminate. His thumb passed over them once.

“Who is he?” Xander asked.

“I haven't the faintest idea.”

“Strong sales pitch.”

“Some costumes tell you what a person wished to be.” Ethan placed the badge on the counter. “Others leave room for discovery.”

He picked up the black remote. Its green light did not answer him.

“How much room are we talking?”

Ethan looked at the register. No price appeared when he entered the tag number. He tried again, more slowly.

“Three dollars.”

Xander waited for the correction. It did not come.

“For all of it?”

“Consider it a contribution to the spirit of the evening.”

That sounded suspicious. Three dollars sounded stronger.

Xander paid before either could change his mind.

Ethan put the costume in a plain paper bag. As he handed it across the counter, the bell above the front door rang once.

The door had not moved.

Ethan's gaze shifted toward it.

Xander turned, but Buffy emerged from the changing room before he could decide whether a stationary doorbell ranked above or below spontaneous combustion on the Sunnydale Weirdness Index.

The dress fit her perfectly.

That should have been impossible. The sleeves had been too long on the hanger and the waist too narrow. Now the fabric sat as though someone had measured Buffy for it, and her face had gone soft with delight.

“Well?” she asked.

Xander made himself smile. “You're going to need your own weather system, but otherwise? Excellent.”

Willow stepped out behind her, saw Xander looking, and immediately pulled the folded sheet in front of her costume.

“Found something?” she asked.

He held up the bag. “Apparently I am Eli Wallace, brave warrior of technical support.”

Buffy examined the badge. “Destiny Expedition sounds important.”

“See? Finally, a costume with upward mobility.”

“You don't know what it's from?” Willow asked.

“Probably a show that aired opposite something people watched.”

Behind the register, Ethan was still looking at the bell.

Then he smiled again.

“Whoever he was,” Buffy said, handing the badge back, “at least he looks comfortable.”

That decided it.

By sunset, Sunnydale had filled with tiny superheroes, plastic witches, cowboys, princesses, and creatures whose taxonomy had been abandoned halfway through manufacturing.

Principal Snyder divided the high-school volunteers into groups with the grim satisfaction of a man assigning penal labor.

Xander pulled the grey hood over his head and clipped the badge to its cord. The black remote—Kino remote, according to a strip of faded tape on its underside—went into his pocket.

Buffy descended the Summers staircase in the champagne dress and briefly forgot how stairs worked. Willow followed beneath the white sheet. Only her red hair showed at the bottom.

“You know,” Xander said, “if the ghost plan is total anonymity, the hair may be a clue.”

“I can still change my mind about haunting you.”

Joyce took pictures. Buffy protested, Willow tried to hide behind a child dressed as a bee, and Xander smiled on reflex when the flash went off.

In the picture, discovered much later, Buffy looked toward a life she imagined had once been simpler. Willow's sheet concealed almost everything. Xander stood between them in a secondhand hoodie, holding a machine that would not be invented for years.

Its lens was glowing.

No one noticed.

Ethan locked the shop at eleven minutes past six.

The street beyond the glass remained busy with families and costumed children. He turned the sign to CLOSED, lowered the shade, and carried the Janus bust from his office to the center of the back room.

Two faces looked in opposite directions. One young, one old. Beginning and ending sharing a single stone skull.

Ethan lit the candles.

He had performed more difficult workings. More dangerous ones, certainly. This spell required breadth rather than force. Hundreds of little transformations, each shaped by expectation, vanity, fear, and the ancient human habit of becoming someone else behind a mask.

He cut his palm and let seven drops fall.

“The world that denies thee,” he began, “thou inhabit.”

The candles bent toward the statue.

“The peace that binds thee, thou corrupt.”

That was not the next line.

Ethan stopped.

The words in the open book had changed.

Ink moved slowly across the page, forming letters in a hand older than the paper.

THE THRESHOLD THAT CALLS THEE, THOU SHALT CROSS.

The young face of Janus watched the closed door.

The old face watched Ethan.

For perhaps the first time in his adult life, Ethan Rayne considered leaving well enough alone.

Then, because he was Ethan Rayne, he smiled and read the line aloud.

The bell above the front door rang.

Every door in the shop opened at once.
