The Beat Goes On

Willow felt the cowl stop being fabric at the exact moment Buffy called her name from across the shop, and felt her own body go with it in that same silent half-second: skin giving way to something faceted and cool, hair stilling into something that no longer needed a breeze to know how to move, eyes resolving into a patterned, inner light that had nothing at all to do with color the way eyes were meant to have color — which left her approximately four seconds to decide how to sound normal before anyone looked at her closely enough to notice there was no longer anything normal left to look at.

"Yeah," she called back, and was startled by how easily the ordinary word came out of a voice that no longer had a mouth to shape it with, exactly, and yet somehow still sounded entirely like her own: how little it cost her, in that first instant, to sound like herself while something ancient and patient and entirely too calm unfolded itself into the space directly behind her own thoughts, quiet as a second shadow falling across a room lit. "Find something?"

This one's — it's asking for me.

She'd meant to say something else. Something normal, something Willow-shaped: ooh, spooky, I like it or this'll totally work — and instead that sentence had arrived from somewhere she didn't yet have a name for, arrived fully formed and slightly too solemn for a costume shop on a Tuesday, and she'd said it out loud before she could stop herself. Buffy had laughed. Willow had laughed too, half a beat behind, buying herself just enough time to work out whether the laugh belonged to her.

It did, she decided. Mostly.

Small one, something said, gently, from a distance that felt both vast and, somehow, no distance at all: the way a voice can seem to come from across a canyon and from directly behind your own eyes in the very same breath. You are frightened. That is reasonable. I will not ask you to stop being so.

Who are you, Willow thought, as clearly as she could manage while her hands kept moving on their own, picking up the small warm egg-shaped ornament like this was the most natural transaction in the world, like she did this every day, geometric fingers closing around it with a precision that had nothing hesitant in it at all.

A traveler. A listener, more often than either. The small ones who find me generally call me Nada, though it was never truly a name in the way your kind means the word. Names imply endings. I have not yet had one of those. A pause, and something in it that might, in a voice with more warmth to spare, have been amusement. You are very loud, small one, for someone trying so hard to be quiet.

I'm not trying to be loud.

No. You are trying not to scream. From where I am standing, the two produce a remarkably similar effect.

Willow held herself in something normal-shaped for the rest of the walk through the shop — through the transaction, three dollars counted out same as Xander's, through Ethan's expression, which had gone very carefully, deliberately unreadable the moment he actually looked at what he'd just sold her, through the door with its apologetic bell — and out onto a street where the sky had, silently, spectacularly torn open above the whole unsuspecting town, a shape hanging where the moon should have been sitting alone, and the rest of her life had finished reorganizing itself around a word that wasn't a name, whether she'd caught up to that fact yet or not. She held the shape of fine through Buffy's last joke about Xander's philosophy of laziness, both of them apparently too caught up in their own separate unraveling to properly register what the other had become. She held it through the goodbyes, the see-you-tomorrows, the first few steps toward home along a sidewalk she'd walked a thousand times and would now, for the rest of however long she had left, walk in a body that no longer remembered how to be tired the ordinary way. It was only once she was well and truly alone — streetlights strung out ahead of her like beads on a string, the hollow quiet Sunnydale wore after dark, no cars, no voices, nothing but the quiet, precise sound of her own new footsteps and whatever was riding along inside her thoughts now — that she finally let the shape of fine drop.

Her phone buzzed. She'd texted something to the group chat, she realized, without any memory of composing it: something short, something that had sounded enough like her that nobody had questioned it. She genuinely could not remember what she'd written, or how fingers that no longer looked like fingers had managed to type it.

You are unraveling, Nada observed, without judgment, the way one might remark on weather moving in from the coast.

"I'm not—" Willow stopped walking. Stood there, right on the empty sidewalk, some geometric approximation of a hand pressed to where her face used to be, and let herself, for the first time all evening, actually stand still with it. "I don't know what's happening to me. I don't know if this is a spell, or a — a possession, or if I'm just going crazy, and I don't have anyone I can call right now because everyone else is dealing with their own stuff, I can feel it, something's wrong with Buffy too, and Xander's probably somewhere doing something Xander-shaped and impossible, and I'm just — standing on a sidewalk that doesn't recognize me anymore, talking to a voice in my head that used to be a costume—"

You are not alone, Nada said, and something in the register shifted: not warmer, warmth wasn't quite the correct word for what Nada was capable of, but steadier. More present, in the specific way of someone leaning slightly closer rather than simply speaking louder. I have sat with a great many frightened small ones, across a very long time. I do not know how to make this feel smaller than it is. I only know how to stay.

"That's not really comforting."

It was not meant to comfort. It was meant to be true.

Willow made a sound that wanted badly to be a laugh: wet, startled, half a sob dressed up in something steadier — and felt whatever passed, now, for tears prickle uselessly behind eyes that no longer had any water left in them to shed. "Okay. Okay, that's — actually, weirdly, that helps. A little. More than I expected it to."

She started walking again, slower now, and made herself finally, properly look up at the shape that had been hanging there the entire time she'd been too afraid to check: vast and glittering and entirely, spectacularly wrong for a Tuesday night in California, its shadow sweeping slow and deliberate across the ground like something that had brought its own private weather system along for the ride.

That is the Anomaly, Nada said, and for the first time there was something in the words that might have been fondness. It has followed me a very long way, small one. I believe it is, in its own fashion, rather glad to have arrived somewhere with a proper sky to sit in.

"It's enormous."

It is home, Nada said. Which is a different thing from enormous, though it happens to also be that.

Buffy walked the opposite direction from the Bronze with her arms wrapped tight around herself against a cold that had nothing whatsoever to do with the actual temperature, and let herself, once she was certain nobody was watching at all, stop performing okay.

It came in pieces, the way grief that isn't yours tends to arrive: not a single wave but a slow accumulation, each piece slotting into place with a weight that felt, somehow, like it had always lived there and had simply been waiting for room to unfold. A corridor she'd never walked. People she'd never met, dying of something she didn't have a word for. A decision that hadn't been hers, made in a moment that hadn't been hers, by a version of a woman she was only just beginning to understand she now, permanently, partly was.

I'm sorry, she thought, uselessly, to nobody, to people who didn't exist in her world and never would. I'm sorry I couldn't save you. I don't even know your names.

You will not learn them, something said back, not unkindly, just factually, an old ache with its own long, careful history of being honest about what could and couldn't be fixed, no matter how badly either of them wanted otherwise. That is, I think, one of the harder parts of it. Grief without a name to hold onto tends to simply live in the body instead, with nowhere else to go.

"Great," Buffy said, out loud, to the empty street, to the ache, to the whole absurd shape of her evening. "That's — great. Awesome. Very fun Tuesday."

She looked up. The sky didn't look right, and it took her a long moment, standing very still on the sidewalk, to work out why: not what was different about it, exactly, but that the wrongness wasn't visual at all. It was something underneath the looking. Because it isn't right, the answer arrived, from somewhere that wasn't quite her own thinking, calm and old and a little bit sad. Because the sky lies, sometimes, about what's actually sitting behind it, and you may be the only one left who can tell the difference.

That was new. That was, she suspected, going to be a genuine problem on some perfectly ordinary future day when she badly needed the sky to just be the sky, and it simply refused to cooperate.

Her phone buzzed. Willow's name on the screen, a text that read, simply: i'm ok. give me a little time. — w

Buffy looked at it for a long moment, feeling her own ache settle by half a degree, just from the small, specific relief of knowing someone else out there was carrying something too heavy tonight too, and that neither of them was carrying it alone, even from two different directions of the same dark town.

ok, she typed back. take whatever you need. we'll be here.

She slid the phone back into her pocket, and stood there a moment longer in the quiet, and became aware, gradually, of a second presence in the stillness — different from the first, entirely different in texture, like two instruments playing in the same room without ever once trying to play the same note.

Okay, so, not to make this weirder than it already objectively is, said a voice that was unmistakably not Artemis's, not old, not grieving, faintly, helplessly awkward in a way that read as almost embarrassingly human by comparison, but I want to be upfront that I have no idea how any of this works, and I've been sitting here for like ten minutes trying to figure out the polite way to say hi.

Buffy went very still. "Okay, that's a new one."

Yeah. Sorry. I'm — okay, this is gonna sound insane, but about twenty minutes ago I was playing a video game. Which, on its own, isn't even the weird part, which is honestly a sentence I never expected to say about my own life. I'm a writer. Or I was, before tonight, is maybe the more accurate tense. Self-insert stuff, mostly, just for fun, nothing anyone was ever gonna publish. And it turns out, apparently, three separate times now, if you write yourself into a story with enough sincerity, sometimes the story just decides to reach back and grab you for real. Happened with one story, then a second, then a third, three different cities, three different wars, three different versions of me having to figure out how to survive somewhere I only ever meant to visit on paper. I got good at it, eventually. Had to. But this time I wasn't even writing anything. I was just playing. And it grabbed me anyway. Four for four now, and that's before whatever just happened tonight, which I'm gonna go ahead and call five. It's a lot. I'm processing.

"You were playing Artemis."

I was playing a Traveller, yeah, in the game the whole... everything's apparently based on. Stop number four, technically, not that I was exactly keeping a scorecard before tonight. A pause, something rueful threaded through it. And now I'm doing it again, evidently, because apparently once you're the kind of person stories reach out and grab, that just becomes your whole thing now, writing or not. Five for five. I don't love the pattern, but here we are.

Buffy almost laughed, surprised by how much lighter this voice felt, even carrying its own clear undertow of bewilderment, compared to the vast patient grief she'd been sitting with all evening. "I'm gonna need a name. I already met Artemis tonight, and honestly I need a way to keep track of which voice in my head is which."

Mike. Michael Allen, technically, but nobody's called me that since my mom got mad at me. Something that felt, unmistakably, like a wince. And I want to say up front, I am so sorry about this. Whatever this is. You didn't sign up for a second passenger, I'm guessing, on top of the first one.

"No," Buffy admitted. "Not really part of the plan, no." She was quiet a beat, curiosity winning out over the exhaustion. "What were the other three? The stories, before this one. You said cities, wars — what were they actually called?"

Oh. Uh. Something that read, unmistakably, like faint embarrassment at having to say the titles out loud to an actual stranger. "Security!" was the first one. It was supposed to be a fix-it, originally — the whole point of writing it was getting there in time to get one specific girl out of one specific locker before the worst day of her life finished happening to her. I didn't have any powers of my own in that one, just a badge and a very steep learning curve, and I spent a genuinely embarrassing amount of both real time and story time just trying to be in the right hallway at the right hour. A pause, something wry threading through the embarrassment now. Then "I, Panacea," which turned out to be the same me, same city, still no powers, except this time I woke up literally inside somebody else's actual head instead of just standing near her. A healer. Sixteen years old, powerful enough to rebuild a person from the inside out with a thought, and carrying more quiet self-loathing than any one person should ever have to hold alone, long before I ever got there. That one wasn't really about a villain. That one was just about trying to help somebody stop being the worst thing that had ever happened to herself. Something in the tone shifted, heavier now, before he pushed through it. And then "I, Scion." Which the internet insists on calling the crack sequel, and mostly, yeah, that tracks — except for the one small detail that "Scion," in that particular story, isn't a nickname or a metaphor. It's him. The actual him. The single biggest, worst, most world-ending thing that whole setting ever produced, and somehow I ended up stuck inside that too, staring out at an entire planet through eyes built for something considerably less human than mourning it properly. A small, helpless laugh, more relief than humor in it. They're not exactly the kind of thing I ever pictured explaining to anyone outside a very specific corner of the internet, let alone out loud, inside somebody else's actual head.

"I have literally no context for any of those words," Buffy said, "and somehow that's still not close to the strangest thing I've heard tonight."

Fair. Completely fair, honestly.

For what it's worth — and I know it's probably not worth much, from a guy who showed up uninvited in your own head on what already sounds like a genuinely terrible night — I'm not looking to run anything. I'm not gonna try and take the wheel or whatever. I just. Apparently know some stuff now. Useful stuff, some of it, from wherever that life actually happened. I figured the least I could do is let that be useful to you, instead of just sitting in here being extra weight you didn't ask for.

Something in that landed, unexpectedly, gently, against the raw place Artemis's grief had spent the whole evening pressing on. "That's actually really decent of you."

I'm trying, Mike said, and there was real warmth underneath the awkwardness now, something steadying rather than adding to the strangeness of the night. I don't have the first idea how to do this well. But I'd like to try and be useful instead of just being one more thing you have to carry tonight, if that's alright with you.

"Yeah," Buffy said, quiet, meaning it. "That's more than alright."

She's hurting an awful lot, by the way, Mike said, quieter now, something careful in it, the tone of a man picking his words with real deliberation. The other one. Artemis. I can feel it too, from over here, and I don't know her yet, not really, but I gotta say — it sits heavy. Heavier than I think even she's letting herself notice, or letting herself matter, if that makes sense. Like she's already decided her own grief isn't worth taking seriously next to everyone else's.

I am aware of my own grief, thank you, Artemis said, cool, an old, tired edge surfacing underneath the words that hadn't been there a moment before. I do not require a stranger's assessment of it, however well-intentioned.

It's not an assessment. It's just — you matter too. Whatever you lost, whoever you were before all this, that matters, the same as anybody else's does. I'm not trying to fix it or talk you out of it. I just don't think you should have to carry it like it doesn't count. Mike didn't back down, exactly, but something in him gentled immediately, some old habit of not pushing further than a room could currently hold. That's kind of just how I try to treat people, is all. Like they actually matter. Guess that doesn't stop just because one of them happens to be sharing my current address.

Buffy felt both of them settle, uneasy, into the same small space, two very different kinds of company sharing one set of ribs, and understood, distantly, that this particular tension probably wasn't going anywhere soon. "You're both allowed to be in here," she said, mostly to herself, testing the words out loud for the first time. "I guess we're all just gonna have to figure out how that actually works."

She didn't send anything else. Some kinds of care, she was beginning to understand, worked better delivered as space than as words.

Xander's text came in twenty minutes later, from a signal that had no business reaching a phone at all, let alone one currently standing in an alley behind the Bronze.

hey. u seeing this. dont panic. its fine. i think its fine. — X

fine is a big word, Buffy typed back. where are you

long story. short version: i have a spaceship. also i think i used to be a different guy. we should talk

yeah, she sent, and meant it more than three words could really carry. we should. also — will's not answering much. i think she needs space. gonna let her have it.

yeah, Xander wrote back, after a pause long enough that she could picture him staring at the screen, working through the same instinct she was. yeah, ok. she'll find us when she's ready.

It wasn't nothing, deciding that. It cost both of them something, not pushing: the old reflex to fix, to check, to hover, sitting right there and being deliberately, carefully set aside instead. Buffy put her phone away and looked up once more at the two impossible shapes crossing the sky, and let herself, for just a minute longer, be exactly as tired and as unmoored as she actually felt, before she made herself start walking home.

It took two full days before Willow felt steady enough to leave her room for longer than meals, and most of that time she spent sitting cross-legged on her own bed, curtains half-drawn against a sky she wasn't ready to look at directly yet, the small warm ornament resting in her lap while she listened — actually listened, the deliberate, effortful way you listen to a language you've only just begun to learn — to a voice that never once demanded anything of her, never filled silences that didn't need filling, and never, across those two days, told her she needed to be anything other than as overwhelmed as she currently was.

Her mother knocked once, on the second afternoon, asked if she was coming down for dinner, and Willow said she wasn't feeling well, which wasn't even a lie.

"You are doing better than you believe," Nada told her, sometime in the golden hour of that second afternoon, watching sunlight crawl slow across the bedroom ceiling with what might have been the closest thing to contentment either of them could currently manage between them. "Most small ones take considerably longer than this."

"I don't feel like I'm doing better."

That is generally how it feels, from the inside, while it is actually happening. A pause, gentler than the words themselves. I do not say this to rush you. There is no clock on this, small one. I have simply learned, across a very long time, to notice the shape of healing even when the one doing it cannot yet see it themselves.

"What are you, exactly? I mean — I know your name. Sort of. I know you're old, and patient, and you keep calling me small one like I'm a puppy you found on the side of the road."

Something that might have been laughter moved through the space behind her thoughts — not sound, more like warmth arriving a half-second before words did. I am a Priest. Or I was called one, by those who first found language for what I do. I have sat at the center of a place many travelers pass through, and I have listened to a very great number of them tell me what they are looking for, and I have, more often than you would perhaps expect, actually been able to help.

"And now you're — what, retired? On sabbatical?"

Now I am here. Simple, unadorned, and somehow, in its simplicity, reassuring. Which is, I find, its own kind of answer.

By the second evening, Willow had stopped flinching every time Nada's voice surfaced unbidden in the middle of an ordinary thought. By the start of the third day, she'd begun, almost without noticing, to think of the presence less as an intrusion and more as company: strange, ancient, occasionally baffling company, but company all the same, in a house that suddenly felt very quiet with everyone else pretending things were normal downstairs.

On the morning of the third day, she went to see the Anomaly properly.

She told her mother she was meeting friends, which was, depending on how generously you counted, either a lie or the truest thing she'd said all week. She walked out past the edge of town, past the last stretch of ordinary houses with their ordinary lawns, out to where the land opened up into scrubby empty field, to the spot where the station's vast shadow crossed lowest and longest across the ground. She stood there a long while, just looking up at something the size of a small moon, feeling — not quite calm, not yet, but steady enough to stand on.

You do not need to simply look at it, Nada said, gentle. It has been waiting for you to notice that the shadow is not merely a shadow.

Willow looked down at the dark shape pooled across the grass at her feet, and understood, the way she'd understood the mannequin and the ornament and her own name spoken back to her in a voice that wasn't hers, exactly what she was being offered. She stepped into it.

The field simply wasn't there anymore. The Anomaly was, all around her, vast and humming and entirely unlike anything a shadow had any business containing: landing pads stretching out in every direction, light falling from no fixture she could identify, the whole structure breathing slow and patient the way a very large, very old thing breathes.

A figure was waiting for her a short distance off, where the station's strange light did something unresolved to the air around it: not quite solid, not quite not, dressed in robes not unlike the ones she'd lifted off a mannequin three nights ago, carrying the particular, patient stillness of someone who had been waiting exactly as long as necessary and not one moment longer than that.

"You're Polo," Willow said, though she couldn't have explained how she knew, any more than she could have explained the ornament's warmth or the voice behind her thoughts.

"Nada speaks of you!" the figure said, and their voice was warm, layered, delighted, unmistakably its own separate person rather than any echo of the presence already living in her head: a second, distinct kind of company, standing right there in front of her in the flesh, practically vibrating with the pleasure of the introduction. "She does not speak of many small ones so quickly, or so often, and I confess I have been rather impatient to see for myself what had earned it, ever since she first mentioned you! A traveler within a traveler — my goodness, what a marvelous, complicated thing you are turning out to be, and we have not even properly begun!"

Willow found, standing there in the station's strange, shifting light, that she could actually smile, for what felt like the first time in days. "And? What's the verdict?"

Polo considered her for a long moment, head tilted at an angle that read as delighted curiosity rather than judgment, though not remotely as unhurried as the question deserved — already halfway to bursting with an answer. "The verdict, my dear new friend, is that we have a great deal of wonderful time ahead of us in which to find out properly! I have charted a truly astonishing number of things in my long career, and do you know, not one of them has ever been quite so promising as a small one who arrived wearing her own fear like a very fine coat and decided to keep walking anyway. That takes something! I do love a traveler who takes something."

"That's very — enthusiastic of you."

"Enthusiasm," Polo said, with the delighted, slightly wounded dignity of a man defending his very favorite quality, "is simply what patience looks like when it has finally found something worth being patient for. There is a difference, small as it may seem from where you are standing today."

They talked for a while after that: not about anything urgent, not about the sky or the spell or the strange doubled shape of Willow's own thoughts, just easy, meandering conversation, the kind two people have when neither one is trying to get anywhere in, Polo peppering nearly every third sentence with some fresh, delighted aside about a world he'd once catalogued or a species he'd once nearly, gloriously offended through simple overenthusiasm. Willow asked about the station, about how many people — how many kinds of people — actually lived aboard something that size, and Polo answered some of it plainly, eyes going bright with the telling, and deflected the rest with a warmth that made the deflection feel less like evasion and more like an invitation to keep asking, later, when there was more time to properly do it justice.

"You'll meet them! All of them, in time," Polo said, eventually, when Willow finally asked when she'd get to see the rest of it, both hands spreading wide as if to encompass the whole impossible scale of the station at once. "The Anomaly does not rush its introductions, wonderful as they invariably are. Neither, I think, should you, my new friend. Some things are worth savoring properly rather than rushing through like a man late for his own departure!"

For the first time in three days, standing aboard something the size of a small moon with an alien cartographer who seemed constitutionally incapable of being anything less than thrilled about existing, and a voice in her head that had somehow, impossibly, become the steadiest thing in her entire week, Willow believed that might actually be true — and that she, too, could afford not to hurry.

She found her own way back to the shadow-doorway eventually, Polo walking her to it with the same unhurried delight he'd greeted her with, and stepped back through into the ordinary morning light of the empty field, the station's vast shadow settling back into being merely a shadow behind her. She texted the group chat on her walk into town, late morning sun warm on her shoulders.

i'm okay. really okay, not just saying it. can we get pizza tonight?

YES, Buffy wrote back, almost instantly, three exclamation points trailing after it that Willow suspected had taken real effort not to send five minutes earlier, the moment the first text came through. thank god. missed you.

missed you too, Willow sent, and meant it all the way down, in a way that felt, for the first time since Halloween, entirely and uncomplicatedly like herself.
