Chapter 24 of 59

The God at the Door

The Watchers Council had classified Janus incorrectly for three hundred years.

Daniel found the first error in a seventeenth-century translation. Giles found the second in a commentary copied from it. By noon, they had assembled a chain of scholarship in which each Watcher cited the last until repetition became authority.

The earliest texts said nothing about chaos.

They spoke of gates. Thresholds. Departures and returns. The first step of a journey and the last step home. The instant in which one condition ended and another began.

Past and future looked in opposite directions through the same opening.

“Janus presides over change,” Daniel said. “Not random change. Chosen passage. The Romans invoked him at beginnings, at endings, at war, at peace—whenever something crossed from one state into another.”

Giles read the Council translation again.

“They reduced transition without permission to chaos.”

“People in power often do.”

Basil stood at the end of the library table. He had arrived from London with two locked document cases, no visible luggage, and news that made Giles sit down.

“Mr. Rayne has been found.”

Ethan had tried to reach a Council safe property outside Bath.

He had known the old warding phrases and three of the obsolete alarms. He had not known that Basil's teams had secured every property listed in the Council's finance records, including the ones concealed as maintenance trusts.

He was now in a guarded interview room in London.

“He has requested counsel,” Basil said. “He has also requested Mr. Giles, two bottles of acceptable Scotch, diplomatic immunity, and protection from whatever he believes he summoned.”

“In that order?” O'Neill asked.

“He revised the order when a door closed unexpectedly.”

The secure screen activated.

Ethan sat behind a plain table. A solicitor occupied the chair to his right. Two guards stood beyond the camera's view; their reflections appeared faintly in the dark observation glass behind him.

“Ripper,” Ethan said.

Giles did not answer the greeting.

“Tell us the exact invocation.”

Ethan's eyes traveled across the people visible on his screen.

“And good afternoon to you as well.”

Basil placed one hand on the document case.

“Mr. Rayne, you are not presently charged in connection with the events in California. That is a question for the relevant American authorities. You are being asked about an ongoing danger to several hundred lives. Cooperation will be recorded. So will refusal.”

Ethan looked at his solicitor.

She gave the smallest possible nod.

“The invocation was standard,” he said. “An appeal to Janus to strip away the tedious mask of ordinary life and permit the hidden self to walk.”

“That isn't what you said,” Giles replied.

For the first time, Ethan's amusement failed to arrive on command.

“It was what I intended to say.”

Daniel leaned toward the screen. “The words changed.”

“After the candles bent toward the image. I had prepared one text. I heard another in my own voice.”

“Recite it.”

Ethan looked toward the interview-room door.

“No.”

“Why?”

“Because I am many things, Doctor Jackson. Suicidally repetitive is not among them.”

“Translate it, then.”

Ethan's fingers pressed flat against the table.

“To the keeper of the hinge. To the face before and the face behind. Let those who wear another life cross to meet it. Let what waits beyond the door answer.”

Xander felt Destiny shift inside his awareness.

Not movement. Attention.

TJ's medical sensor began to accelerate.

“I'm okay,” he said before she could speak through the radio.

Ethan heard him.

“Is that the boy?”

Xander moved into the camera's view.

Ethan studied him. Something almost like remorse crossed his face, but it had spent too many years being avoided to know how to remain.

“You sold me the badge,” Xander said.

“It was not mine.”

“You still sold it.”

“Yes.”

Giles opened the reconstructed inventory. “The badge appeared in the shop without a supplier, a price, or an entry in your purchasing records.”

“I assumed one of my assistants had failed to record it.”

“You have never employed an assistant,” Giles said.

“Then I was unusually optimistic.”

“You saw the lines on the back.”

Ethan went still.

Xander remembered the moment through his own eyes now: Ethan's thumb passing over the fine pattern beneath the cloudy laminate, the brief glance toward the rear room, the price that had not appeared in the register.

“You knew it was different,” Xander said.

“I knew it was interesting.”

O'Neill leaned into view. “A spaceship is parked over California and hundreds of people are dying by inches because you found a cursed name tag interesting.”

“Colonel, if I had known it was attached to a spaceship, I would have charged more.”

No one laughed.

Ethan's gaze dropped.

Daniel placed an image of the broken Janus bust beside the oldest recovered text.

“The spell didn't create Destiny. It opened a path to it.”

“Yes.”

“You knew that before Giles broke the statue.”

“I suspected it.”

“You warned him.”

“I did.”

“Why?” Giles asked.

Ethan looked up.

“Because the statue answered.”

The interview room seemed smaller.

Giles's voice lost all heat. “Explain.”

Ethan described the shop after the spell began. The costumes had taken hold. The bell rang with the door motionless. Every mirror showed the room from an angle at which he was not standing.

Then the two faces of the statue turned.

Not far. Only enough that the young face looked toward the front of the shop and the old face looked toward Ethan.

There was a man between them.

Ethan could not describe his age. Whenever he looked at one side of the face, the other seemed older. He wore a key at his throat and stood with one foot in candlelight and the other in a darkness that did not belong to the room.

“I told him I had called on Janus,” Ethan said. “That the night's chaos was offered in his name.”

His mouth twisted at the memory.

“You wanted chaos,” Ethan said.

“You opened a door,” Janus answered.

Daniel did not write the words down. They were already being recorded.

“What door?” Ethan had asked.

Janus raised the key.

The walls became transparent.

On one side, Ethan saw a young man alone aboard a dying ship, surrounded by sleeping people and a darkness between galaxies.

On the other, he saw Xander Harris step into the street wearing that young man's name.

Beneath both lay the Hellmouth, vast and hungry, a wound that generations of Watchers had catalogued without closing.

“The ship and the Hellmouth,” Daniel said. “Opposite doorposts.”

“Yes.” Ethan swallowed. “The boy crossed far enough to touch the man. The man crossed far enough to answer. And the vessel followed the name by which it knew him.”

Xander looked upward.

“Why Sunnydale?”

“Because you were here. Because the Hellmouth is a threshold. Because that night every person wearing one of my costumes had agreed, however ignorantly, to become something else.”

“That still doesn't explain why Janus answered you,” Giles said.

“He did not answer me.”

Ethan looked toward Buffy.

“He answered her.”

Buffy's arms folded.

“We haven't met.”

“He knew you.”

Ethan's voice had gone quiet.

“A girl approaches a doorway her keepers have made into a grave,” Janus said.

Ethan had not understood. Not then.

“The Slayer,” he had answered.

“She has died once already. They shall not call repetition tradition.”

Nobody in the library moved.

Buffy's face emptied of expression in the way it did when feeling too much was more dangerous than feeling nothing.

Giles sat with the Cruciamentum records open before him.

Daniel saw the structure first.

“The doorway wasn't only between Xander and Eli.”

Basil followed him. “Destiny's arrival caused Colonel O'Neill to investigate Sunnydale.”

“Which caused the report to General Hammond,” Willow said.

“Which reached the President,” O'Neill continued.

“And the Crown,” Giles finished.

Buffy looked at the files describing the death planned for her eighteenth birthday.

“He brought a spaceship across the universe to get somebody to read the paperwork?”

“God of doorways,” Xander said. “Not god of reasonable proportions.”

It earned a brief, startled sound from her that was not quite laughter.

Ethan watched them from London.

“I asked what would happen if the image were broken,” he said.

Janus had turned both faces toward him then.

“The invitation ends.”

“And what crossed?”

“Closing a door does not return everyone who already walked through it.”

The figure vanished. The walls returned. The statue became stone again.

Minutes later, Giles entered the shop.

Daniel closed the oldest book.

“Breaking the statue stopped the active spell. It ended the transformations and prevented anything else from crossing. It could not undo a completed passage.”

“Destiny crossed,” Carter said over the channel from the ship.

“And part of Eli crossed into me,” Xander said.

Giles looked at Ethan. “What holds the ship here?”

“I don't know.”

O'Neill's voice hardened. “Try.”

Ethan stared past them, remembering two faces and one key.

“A door is defined by where it begins and where it ends,” he said. “You have found the beginning.”

His eyes settled on Xander.

“Perhaps you should ask what has not yet finished crossing.”
