Chapter 9 of 59

Necessary Awakenings

TJ certified the pods before she certified the people standing beside them.

She spent six hours moving through the stasis bay, comparing current readings with Destiny's records from before the transit. Carter followed with a separate instrument and the disciplined restraint of someone permitted to observe systems she desperately wanted to disassemble.

The pods were stable.

Whatever had carried Destiny to Earth had acted on the ship as a whole. No occupant showed radiation injury, cellular disruption, or interrupted suspension.

That was the good news.

The bad news occupied every other system.

Destiny had arrived with the damage it already carried. Power remained sufficient for stasis and life support, but large sections of the ship were inaccessible. Several repair processes had stopped during the transit. The navigation system continued insisting that the vessel occupied a position forbidden by ordinary mechanics.

TJ presented her findings in the observation deck. Young stood at the head of the central console. SG-1 occupied one side. Xander sat where Earth was visible without filling his entire field of view.

Hammond attended through the communications link.

“The crew can remain in stasis safely for now,” TJ concluded. “I can't certify long-term stability until engineering evaluates the power system and the interrupted repairs.”

Hammond looked to Young. “Recommendation?”

“Wake Doctor Rush.”

Xander felt the room change before anyone spoke.

Young continued. “He has the most complete understanding of Destiny's systems. We also wake Camile Wray. The expedition includes civilians from multiple nations. Decisions about their status should not be made by military personnel alone.”

“Anyone else?”

“Not yet. If Rush identifies a technical requirement, I will submit additional names.”

Hammond considered the answer. “Lieutenant Johansen?”

“Medically, both can be revived,” TJ said. “Psychologically, nobody is prepared for this.”

“Understood.” Hammond returned his attention to Young. “Approved. Rush and Wray only. Brief them before granting systems or communications access.”

“Yes, sir.”

The link ended.

O'Neill leaned back. “Your scientist worries you.”

“My scientist worries everyone,” Young said.

Xander remembered Rush looking at a problem as though any person standing near it had volunteered to become a tool.

He also remembered Rush refusing to stop searching when everyone else had accepted failure.

“He's going to want to see the engines,” Xander said.

“He won't.”

“He's still going to want to.”

“That isn't a command problem.”

✳

Rush woke quietly.

His eyes opened before the pod finished cycling. He waited until the glass retracted, then sat up without assistance and looked directly at Young.

“How long?”

“Unknown.”

“Why am I awake?”

“Destiny underwent an uncontrolled transit. I need you to evaluate the ship.”

Rush stepped out of the pod. TJ blocked him before he reached the nearest console.

“Medical first.”

“I'm quite capable of determining whether I'm conscious.”

“Your personality appears intact.”

Xander made a noise that escaped before he could stop it.

Rush turned.

His face changed.

“Eli?”

The hope lasted less than a second. Rush saw the body, the empty glasses, and the badge hanging against a chest too narrow to belong to the person named on it.

“Who is that?”

“Alexander Harris,” Young said. “Seventeen. Civilian. He temporarily became Eli during the transit.”

Rush looked at Young as if he had answered in the wrong language.

“Became.”

“We'll brief you after TJ clears you.”

“Where is Eli?”

“Gone.”

Rush's eyes returned to Xander.

This time he was not looking at his face. He was calculating.

“What does he remember?”

Young stepped between them.

“You will speak to him, not about him.”

“If he retains Eli's understanding of the ship—”

“Medical first,” TJ repeated.

Rush's gaze flicked toward her. Something in her expression persuaded him that the argument could be postponed without being won.

He sat for the examination.

Xander left the bay.

✳

Wray woke asking for Young.

She listened to the initial briefing without interrupting. Young described the transit, Earth, the date, contact with Hammond, Eli's disappearance, Xander's arrival, and the decision to keep the crew asleep.

Only then did he bring her to the observation deck.

Wray walked to the glass.

Earth's night side lay beneath them. City lights traced nations that did not yet know an international expedition would one day leave them behind.

“1997,” she said.

“Yes.”

“Confirmed?”

“By Destiny and the SGC.”

“Our counterparts?”

“We have not searched for them.”

Wray turned. “Good.”

Young had prepared for an argument.

“You agree?”

“I agree that nobody should learn about their own future because one of us wanted reassurance at three in the morning.” She looked back at Earth. “That does not mean indefinite isolation is acceptable.”

“It isn't intended to be.”

“Who knows?”

“General Hammond, SG-1, necessary SGC personnel, and three civilians connected to Harris.”

“Governments?”

“The United States only.”

“This expedition is international.”

“This is also 1997. Most of the agreements that created the expedition don't exist.”

“The people do.”

Young nodded. “That's why I woke you.”

Wray studied him, then looked at Xander.

He had changed into the clothes brought by SG-1. Jeans, sneakers, a dark sweatshirt without a future name attached. Eli's badge remained in his pocket.

“May I ask you a question?” she said.

The request surprised him more than the question could have.

“Sure.”

“Do you remember me?”

“Yes.”

“As Eli did?”

“I don't know any other way.”

Wray accepted that carefully.

“Do you consider yourself a member of this expedition?”

Xander looked at Young, then Earth, then the console that would open if he touched it.

“I consider myself somebody who wants to go home.”

Wray's expression softened.

“So do we all.”

Rush entered the observation deck wearing borrowed clothes and irritation like rank insignia. TJ followed.

He ignored Earth for four full seconds while reading the primary display.

Then he saw the planet.

His hand stopped above the console.

“The date?” he asked.

Young told him.

Rush did not say Gloria's name. He did not need to.

“We need a complete systems review,” he said.

“You will have supervised access,” Young replied.

“Supervised by whom?”

Carter stepped forward. “Me.”

Rush looked at her captain's bars, then at the instruments she had arranged beside the console.

“And you are?”

“Captain Samantha Carter.”

Recognition flashed across his face and was suppressed.

Carter noticed.

“We've met?”

“Not yet.”

Silence settled over the room.

Earth turned below them, carrying younger versions of almost everyone aboard through lives they had not finished living.

Wray was the first to say it aloud.

“Before we decide whether we've come home, we need to determine how many of us are already here.”
