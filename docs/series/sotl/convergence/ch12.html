Chapter 12 of 59

The Accord

The interview established three things.

First, Xander could activate Destiny's systems through Eli's cognitive authorization.

Second, authorization did not equal understanding.

Third, Rush considered that distinction a challenge.

Carter placed five controlled tasks on the console: open a noncritical internal sensor display, locate a stored maintenance report, call a Kino, dismiss an alert, and identify a damaged relay without opening its controls.

Xander completed four.

The fifth brought Eli's memory forward so violently that he answered in Ancient and did not realize it until Carter touched his shoulder.

He recoiled.

“Xander.”

The observation deck returned around him.

TJ stood on his other side. Young had moved between him and Rush without anyone asking.

“What happened?” TJ asked.

“I remembered fixing it.”

Carter checked the untouched relay report. “Did Eli fix this specific system?”

“No. Similar one. Different deck.” Xander rubbed his eyes. “For a second I couldn't tell the difference.”

“Testing ends,” TJ said.

Rush objected. “We have barely established a baseline.”

“And the baseline produced dissociation. Testing ends.”

Young looked at Xander. “Agreed?”

The fact that he asked made the answer easier.

“Agreed.”

Carter closed her instruments. “We have enough to report that the access is real and the response carries risk.”

Rush regarded the dormant console. “We also have enough to know his memory may contain operational knowledge unavailable anywhere else.”

“My memory,” Xander said.

Rush looked at him.

“If I'm stuck carrying it,” Xander continued, “you don't get to pretend it's a filing cabinet Eli left unlocked.”

For once, Rush did not answer immediately.

“Fair,” he said at last.

It was not an apology. From Rush, it might have been more expensive.

✳

Hammond returned from a secure call with the President shortly after noon.

He did not describe the conversation. He took his place in the briefing room and placed a single folder before him.

“I have provisional authority to establish terms governing contact with Destiny until a broader review can be conducted.”

Young stood at the head of the observation-deck table with Wray beside him. Xander and SG-1 occupied the opposite side. Hammond watched all of them through the main display; they would depart for Earth when the conference ended.

“These terms do not resolve ownership, international representation, citizenship questions, or permanent command,” Hammond continued. “They are intended to keep everyone alive and prevent irreversible mistakes while those matters are considered.”

He opened the folder.

“One: Colonel Young retains operational command aboard Destiny under my present authority. Shipboard orders pass through him.”

Young nodded. “Understood.”

“Two: Stargate Command controls official contact between Destiny and Earth. Emergency exceptions are limited to immediate threats to life or planet.”

Rush disliked that visibly. Wray raised one finger.

“Civilian review.”

“You will receive all non-tactical communications restrictions and may challenge them directly to me.”

“Accepted provisionally.”

“Three: the crew remains in stasis except where Colonel Young recommends an awakening for medical, technical, security, or representative necessity. I approve additional awakenings until a joint process is established.”

“Agreed,” Young said.

“Four: no expedition member will contact a present-day version of themselves, a family member, or another person known through future experience without authorization.”

Rush spoke. “And the standard for authorization?”

“An immediate operational need or a preventable threat to life, reviewed individually.”

“Personal suffering does not qualify.”

Hammond's expression softened without yielding. “Personal suffering qualifies for consideration, Doctor. It does not automatically grant permission to alter someone else's life.”

Rush looked away from the camera.

“Five: future knowledge is compartmentalized. Information required to address an immediate threat may be used. Strategic, political, technological, and personal history will be reviewed before disclosure.”

Carter leaned forward. “Sir, some of their technical knowledge may allow us to prevent casualties now.”

“And some may create them. You will help establish the review criteria.”

“Yes, sir.”

“Six: all personnel aboard Destiny are persons under protection, not captured intelligence assets. Medical procedures and research require consent except in a life-threatening emergency.”

Wray nodded once.

“Seven: Alexander Harris remains a civilian minor. He is not enlisted, detained, assigned to Destiny, or subject to military command. His technical participation is voluntary.”

Xander waited for someone to add a qualification.

No one did.

“He will return to Sunnydale with SG-1,” Hammond continued. “He will retain a monitored emergency channel to Destiny and this command. Lieutenant Johansen recommends follow-up assessment after any significant memory event.”

“She does?” Xander asked.

TJ's voice came through the display. “She does.”

“Right.”

“Eight: SG-1 will serve as the initial liaison team. Captain Carter will coordinate technical assessment with Doctor Rush. Doctor Jackson will coordinate Ancient records and the historical aspects of the displacement. Colonel O'Neill will lead the ground investigation in Sunnydale. Teal'c will provide threat assessment.”

O'Neill looked at the map projected beside Hammond.

“About Sunnydale.”

Carter brought up the latest positional analysis.

“Destiny's geographic center hasn't shifted by more than twelve meters since arrival. It is not drifting with orbital mechanics or responding normally to Earth's rotation.”

“The ship is holding itself there?” Hammond asked.

Rush answered from Destiny. “No. Propulsion output remains at station-keeping minimum. The force maintaining position does not originate in any system we can identify.”

Daniel displayed a second map. “The line beneath the ship intersects Sunnydale. We have two relevant locations: Ethan Rayne's costume shop, where the spell was performed, and Sunnydale High School.”

“Why the school?” Young asked.

Giles's voice joined by telephone. “Because the school stands above the Hellmouth.”

Silence followed.

O'Neill rubbed his forehead. “And a Hellmouth is?”

“A point at which the barriers between dimensions are unusually weak.”

Daniel looked at the image of Destiny.

“A doorway.”

“Yes,” Giles said.

On the main display, Destiny hung above the town with impossible precision.

Hammond closed the folder.

“Then that is where we begin. SG-1 will accompany Mister Harris home and conduct an initial investigation with Mister Giles. Colonel Young, maintain passive observation and report any change in the field.”

“Yes, sir.”

Wray looked down at Earth. “General, there is one issue not covered by your terms.”

“Which is?”

“What we call this situation. Quarantine, asylum, temporal displacement—each assumes an answer we don't have.”

Hammond considered the ship, the sleeping crew, and the town below it.

“For now, Miss Wray, we call it contact.”

✳

The shuttle separated from Destiny in the early afternoon.

Xander sat beside O'Neill. Carter and Daniel occupied the forward seats while Teal'c watched Earth expand across the display.

Young's voice came through the cabin.

“Shuttle guidance is locked to the landing coordinates. Destiny will retain control through descent.”

“Understood,” O'Neill said.

Xander looked back at the ship.

Distance reduced Destiny to a long dark shape against the stars. Somewhere inside, Young stood on the observation deck. TJ monitored the pods. Rush and Carter's borrowed instruments had begun arguing with each other. Wray was drafting rights for people who already existed twice.

Eli's badge rested in Xander's pocket.

“You planning to come back?” O'Neill asked.

“To the ship?”

“That's the one.”

Xander considered it.

Yesterday, space had been the distance between stars in movies. Now he knew how Destiny smelled after the air recyclers had gone too long without maintenance. He knew which corridor lights flickered. He knew where Eli had stood when Earth appeared.

“Yeah,” he said. “I think I have to.”

“Because of the memories?”

“Because they're real people.”

O'Neill nodded as if that were the answer he had hoped for.

Below them, Sunnydale waited beneath the descending shuttle.

The town looked ordinary from space.

That was its oldest trick.
