After the Fire

The alarms continued after the battle ended.

Destiny had no setting for victory.

It had pressure loss, fire, radiation, failed power distribution, wounded crew, and sections of hull still hot from the strike meant for Earth. Greer's teams moved through all of them. Scott held the ship in a stable orbit with half the maneuvering system unavailable. Park and Brody kept life support ahead of cascading shutdowns. TJ turned the mess into a second infirmary when the first ran out of beds.

Young remained on the observation deck until Earth was no longer in immediate danger.

Then he went looking for the missing.

Technical Sergeant Mina Patel and Airman First Class Aaron Rusk had been on the wrong side of the starboard breach. Their pressure door closed after the atmosphere was gone.

They had remained at their station long enough to isolate the shield feed and prevent the failure from spreading into the stasis compartments.

Greer recovered them when the outer section was sealed.

Specialist Lena Ortiz died in the infirmary twenty-three minutes later from injuries sustained when the weapons bus exploded.

Eleven others remained under treatment. Four would require surgery Earth could provide more safely than Destiny. TJ supervised their transfer personally.

Young spoke each name over the command circuit.

He described where they had served and what their actions had protected. He did not call their deaths acceptable. He did not say they had known the risk as though knowledge converted loss into consent.

When he finished, he reported them to Hammond.

“Their families?” Hammond asked.

Young looked down at the date on the display.

“Some haven't started those families yet. Rusk's parents are still in high school.”

The dead had living younger selves on Earth. Their parents did not yet know the children they would one day lose. Notification could not mean what it normally meant.

“We record them now,” Hammond said. “We determine what can be told without erasing the lives they died protecting.”

Young accepted the answer because no better one existed.

“Yes, sir.”

Sunnydale counted its own cost.

Two Air Force personnel died holding the western medical route. One of Teal'c's volunteer Jaffa was killed carrying a wounded soldier out of staff fire. Thirty-seven defenders required treatment. Buffy had three cracked ribs, a burn across one shoulder, and instructions from Doctor Fraiser that she interpreted as a negotiation.

No civilians died in the assault zone.

The evacuation plan had mattered as much as the battle.

The high school did not reopen. Its western wing was unstable, the football field contained a crater, and three federal agencies disagreed over which fictional accident best explained the damage.

Snyder objected until Basil asked him to repeat his objections for a classified intergovernmental inquiry.

He discovered flexibility.

Captured Jaffa filled a secured temporary facility away from the Hellmouth. They received medical care, food, and individual interviews. Some still worshipped Janus. Some had lowered their weapons only because Cardea fell. Some asked Teal'c questions they had never before permitted themselves to form.

No one required an immediate declaration of loyalty in exchange for humane treatment.

The choice would be real or it would be another uniform placed over obedience.

Neris arrived at the SGC inside Cardea's restrained body.

The Tok'ra medical team prepared extraction. Fraiser demanded to understand every stage before allowing it to begin. Selmak explained the neural connections, toxins, and points at which Cardea might deliberately injure the host.

For twelve hours, the Goa'uld refused every offer to surrender peacefully.

Neris remained awake beneath her.

The operation began anyway—not as punishment, but because leaving the host imprisoned was also a choice with consequences.

Buffy waited outside the medical suite.

So did O'Neill.

Neither pretended waiting helped. Neither left.

✳

Cardea's Ha'tak remained in high orbit under a joint guard.

It did not make Earth a space power overnight.

Half its systems required Goa'uld command codes. Its engines needed materials Earth could not manufacture. Its weapons were damaged, its surviving crew had surrendered under different conditions, and every attempt to study it raised questions of ownership, safety, and whether expertise obtained through slavery could be separated from the system that produced it.

The captured scout was smaller and only slightly less difficult.

Carter assembled teams to document before dismantling. Young refused to strip Destiny's repair capacity to rebuild captured weapons. The Tok'ra received the intelligence promised by the agreement and no unrestricted claim to either vessel.

Technology had been captured.

Mastery had not.

On Earth, secrecy fractured.

Civilian observatories recorded the first hyperspace emergence. Radar stations in six countries tracked objects too large and too fast to explain. Amateur astronomers photographed the destruction of the flagship through backyard telescopes. Pieces of debris entered the atmosphere over the Pacific and produced daylight trails visible from three continents.

Foreign governments asked questions their intelligence services already knew were not about meteors.

The President received the first diplomatic demand before Destiny's fires were extinguished.

He did not answer with another lie large enough to require forgetting the sky.

He ordered preparations for controlled allied disclosure and accepted that broader disclosure would follow.

That problem belonged to the world after survival.

For one night, survival was enough.
