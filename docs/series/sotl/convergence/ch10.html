Chapter 10 of 59

The Duplicate Problem

They began with Young.

It was the least intrusive choice only because the intrusion had already happened. His service number had identified a living officer. The Air Force had confirmed his duty status before Hammond ever answered Destiny.

Carter placed the result on the observation-deck display.

EVERETT YOUNG — ACTIVE DUTY — RECORD RESTRICTED

No current photograph. No assignment. No location.

Young read the single line and nodded.

“Keep it that way.”

Wray stood across the console from him. “You don't want confirmation that he is actually you?”

“Biometrics already confirmed it.”

“That wasn't what I asked.”

Young looked through the glass at the planet below. “Knowing where he is won't help either of us.”

Rush made an impatient sound. “That principle may become less satisfying when applied to everyone else.”

“It's not a principle. It's an order.”

“From whom?”

“General Hammond.”

“Who commands a facility that, from our perspective, is barely operational.”

Young turned. “He is a major general in the United States Air Force and the commanding officer of Stargate Command.”

“In 1997.”

“That's where we are.”

Rush looked ready to argue with the calendar.

Carter interrupted before he could. “General Hammond authorized a limited comparison for the purpose of assessing temporal duplication. Names, birth dates, and identity markers only. Current locations and personal files remain sealed.”

“And if a member of this expedition is in immediate danger?” Wray asked.

“We refer it to him.”

“A convenient concentration of authority.”

“A temporary one,” Carter said. “We have an Ancient vessel over California, future personnel inside it, and no governing agreement covering any of this. General Hammond is trying to keep a bad first night from becoming a permanent disaster.”

Wray accepted the rebuke without yielding the issue. “Then let's give him enough information to make better decisions.”

The roster contained military personnel, civilians, international partners, and several people whose legal categories had become complicated even before time travel entered the discussion. Destiny had carried survivors of the Icarus evacuation, members of the Lucian Alliance, and people added or lost across years of travel.

They started with the original expedition.

Carter transmitted one encrypted group at a time. The SGC compared the names against military, tax, education, immigration, and public records. Only the result returned to Destiny.

LIVING MATCH.

LIVING MATCH.

NO CONFIRMED MATCH.

LIVING MATCH.

Each result made the stasis bay feel more crowded.

Scott existed below them, too young to know he would wear an Air Force uniform. Greer existed. TJ existed. Brody, Volker, and Park existed. Chloe Armstrong existed somewhere in the United States, years before her father would help send her through the gate.

Camile Wray watched her own confirmation appear.

She did not ask for the sealed details.

“Most of us,” Carter said when the first pass finished. “Not all. Some records are incomplete or unavailable, and some members of the expedition were not born on Earth. But most of the people aboard have living counterparts in this time.”

“They're not counterparts,” Rush said. “They are us.”

“They are who we were,” Wray replied.

“A semantic distinction.”

“Not to them.”

Young looked at Xander. “Eli Wallace.”

Xander's mouth went dry.

Carter hesitated. “We don't have to run that search now.”

Everyone else in the room possessed the right to decline on someone else's behalf. Xander did not know what right belonged to him.

Eli's memories supplied his mother's full name, her birthday, the layout of a kitchen, the sound of her breathing after chemotherapy. They supplied an address that might or might not be current in 1997.

They also supplied nothing at all about what Eli would have wanted if a stranger wearing his memories stood above his childhood.

“Run it,” Xander said.

Young watched him. “You sure?”

“No.”

It was the most honest answer available.

Carter sent the identifiers.

The result took longer than the others.

ELI WALLACE — LIVING MATCH. MINOR.

Below that:

MARION WALLACE — LIVING MATCH.

Xander gripped the edge of the console.

For one instant, Eli's need moved through him with enough force to erase the room. His mother was alive. Not a memory preserved in grief. Not a voice inside a recording. Alive beneath the same sky.

The display offered a sealed reference number. One request to Hammond might open it.

Xander stepped back.

“Hide it.”

Carter closed the result.

Rush stared at the empty screen. “You could know where she is.”

“Yeah.”

“And you choose not to.”

“I choose not to decide for him.”

Rush's expression hardened. “How noble.”

Young moved before Xander could answer. “Enough.”

“We have been delivered into the past with knowledge of deaths, diseases, wars, and personal tragedies. Pretending ignorance does not preserve some pristine timeline. Our arrival has already destroyed it.”

“That does not give you permission to contact anyone.”

“Gloria is alive.”

The name silenced the room.

Rush said it again, more quietly. “She is alive, and she is not ill. If the date is correct, there may be years before—”

He stopped.

Young's anger changed shape.

“I know.”

“No, you don't.”

“I know what it is to have someone down there.”

Rush looked toward the planet. “Then perhaps you're a coward as well.”

Young absorbed the blow without answering it.

Wray did.

“Or perhaps he understands that the woman below has not consented to meet a husband from a future she has not lived.”

Rush's face went still.

“You don't get to use consent as a decorative word when it protects decisions you favor,” Wray continued. “Gloria has rights in this situation. So does your younger self.”

“And what of my rights?”

“You have them. They do not include ownership of another person's future.”

Rush turned away.

No one tried to stop him from leaving the observation deck. Young waited until the doors closed.

“Captain Carter.”

“Sir?”

“Transmit our recommendation. No present-day identities are accessed without General Hammond's authorization and a demonstrated operational need.”

Wray added, “Include a process for individual review and representation. Quarantine cannot become indefinite detention by another name.”

Young looked at her.

“Include it,” he said.

✳

Hammond read the results in his office.

The folder had been printed because the facility's secure digital systems were not designed for records dated years after their own replacement schedules. Paper felt less absurd. It was not.

Young's sealed present-day assignment sat beneath the roster. Hammond had not opened it.

O'Neill stood before the desk. “That's restraint.”

“It's necessity.”

“Those overlap occasionally.”

Hammond closed the folder. “Every person aboard that vessel represents a potential intelligence source, temporal casualty, and constitutional problem.”

“Also a person.”

“That comes first, Colonel.”

O'Neill nodded. He had wanted to hear it aloud.

“The President has been notified that we have established contact with an Ancient vessel containing personnel displaced from the future,” Hammond continued. “For the moment, he has authorized me to contain the situation and negotiate provisional terms.”

“How did he take it?”

“He asked whether this was likely to become stranger.”

“What did you tell him?”

“The truth.”

O'Neill waited.

“I told him I didn't know.”

The telephone rang.

Hammond answered. He listened, then held out the receiver.

“It's Mister Giles. He wants to discuss the legal status of Alexander Harris.”

O'Neill took the phone.

“Of course he does.”
