Chapter 23 of 59

The Engineers

Dale Volker woke to Nicholas Rush telling him not to panic.

This produced exactly the result Rush ought to have expected.

TJ caught Volker's shoulder before he fell against the open pod. Young stepped into view. Brody was reviving two places down, his eyes open but unfocused. Park's pod had begun its final cycle.

“You're safe,” TJ said. “The ship is stable. You have been in stasis for less time than expected.”

Volker tried to speak. His throat produced a rasp.

“Water first.”

Rush stood at the edge of the medical area with his arms folded.

“We require an immediate engineering assessment.”

TJ did not look around. “You require patience for another four minutes.”

“We may not have—”

Young turned his head.

Rush stopped.

Brody managed to sit upright. “Did Eli fix the pod?”

No one answered quickly enough.

His eyes found the empty place where Eli should have been.

“Where is he?”

“Alive,” Young said. “The answer gets complicated after that.”

Park's pod opened.

✳

Young briefed them in a compartment without windows.

He began with the date. Then the transit. Then Earth.

Brody interrupted at Earth.

“Our Earth?”

“Yes.”

“Not an alternate planet that looks like Earth.”

“No.”

“And it's 1997.”

“Yes.”

Volker looked at the wall as though it might become more reasonable if he refused to look at anyone else.

Park asked the first useful question.

“What happened to Eli?”

Young told them.

He did not call Xander a host. He did not call him a copy. He explained that a seventeen-year-old boy in California had worn a costume representing Eli, that something had opened between them, and that Eli's memories and the ship's recognition had passed to Xander while Eli himself had vanished from Destiny.

“Is he Eli?” Brody asked.

“No,” Rush said.

Young looked at him.

Rush's expression remained severe. “He has Eli's memories. He does not claim Eli's identity. Pretending otherwise will not improve matters.”

It was the nearest thing to protection Rush had yet offered Xander when Xander was not present to hear it.

Park absorbed that.

“Can we talk to him?”

“After the immediate danger is addressed,” Young said. “He is in communication with the ship, and Lieutenant Johansen has medical authority over contact that may affect him.”

Volker finally looked back from the wall.

“You said Earth is outside.”

Young led them to the observation deck.

Brody stopped in the doorway.

Park covered her mouth.

Volker walked to the glass until his reflection touched the Pacific.

For several minutes, nobody discussed power.

Then Brody asked, “Do we exist down there?”

“Yes,” Young said.

Park lowered her hand. “Have you contacted—”

“No personal contact. That's the standing order.”

“Whose?”

“Mine, confirmed by General Hammond.”

Brody stared at South America emerging around the planet's curve.

“Is my family—”

“We are not using future knowledge to enter their lives.”

Young let the refusal stand long enough to be felt.

“If circumstances change, we'll deal with them. Right now, several hundred people in stasis require you to keep this ship alive.”

Park turned from the glass first.

“Show us the readings.”

✳

For nine hours, Destiny became an engineering problem again.

That was a mercy.

Brody traced power flow from the damaged collectors through distribution nodes the ship had rerouted before any human boarded it. Volker constructed models from telemetry that contradicted ordinary orbital mechanics. Park entered maintenance trunks with a lamp clipped to her shoulder and emerged blackened with dust from machinery built before humans had language.

Carter worked from the ship after taking the shuttle up at dawn. She and Rush disagreed about nearly everything except the data.

The engines were functional.

The primary power pathways could support a low-output maneuver.

Structural integrity was degraded but adequate.

Nothing mechanical prevented Destiny from moving.

At thirteen minutes past ten, Young received Hammond's approval for a controlled test.

The test would last three seconds.

Propulsion output would remain below one percent. The intended movement was six meters away from Earth, along a line chosen to avoid altering the ship's geostationary relationship with Sunnydale in any meaningful way. Carter placed independent instruments throughout the observation deck. Brody and Park monitored distribution. Volker watched navigation. Rush controlled the test console.

TJ stood beside the open channel to Sunnydale.

Xander sat in the library with a medical sensor on his finger, Buffy on one side and Willow on the other. O'Neill had cleared the building of everyone not required. Giles and Daniel waited beside a table covered in books.

“All stations report,” Young said.

The answers came in sequence.

Power ready.

Propulsion ready.

Navigation ready.

Structural integrity ready.

Carter looked up from her instruments. “External measurement ready.”

TJ checked Xander's display. “Medical ready. Terminate immediately if his heart rate rises above threshold.”

Rush placed his hand over the control.

“Executing in three. Two. One.”

Destiny's engines engaged.

For less than a second, the ship moved.

The stars outside stretched into bright arcs.

The observation deck seemed to narrow, not in width but in possibility. Every route leading from it folded toward a single invisible point. Carter saw three mutually exclusive positions appear on her display at once. Volker's navigation model inverted. The floor rose beneath Young's boots and then dropped away.

In Sunnydale, Xander screamed.

“Abort!” TJ shouted.

Rush had already cut power.

Destiny snapped back.

The sound that passed through the hull was too deep to hear. Young felt it in his teeth. Lights failed along the port side, returned, and failed again. A crack appeared across one observation panel before the transparent material sealed it in a ripple of pale light.

“Report!”

Brody's voice came first. “Power stable. We lost two secondary relays.”

“No hull breach,” Park said.

Volker stared at the navigation display. “We moved six meters.”

Rush looked through the glass.

Earth occupied exactly the same place it had before the test.

“No,” Carter said. “The engines moved us six meters. Space moved us back.”

On the ground, Buffy held Xander upright while TJ questioned him through the radio.

“Can you hear me?”

“Yes.” His voice shook. “Don't do that again.”

“We won't.”

Rush said nothing.

Young looked at him until he gave a short nod.

“What did you feel?” Daniel asked.

Xander took a breath.

“Destiny tried to go somewhere that wasn't part of the sentence.”

Volker frowned at the channel. “What sentence?”

“The spell.”

Carter brought her readings onto the central display. “Engine output was normal. The response preceded measurable displacement by twenty-three milliseconds.”

“Preceded?” Brody asked.

“The field reacted before Destiny moved.”

Rush stared at the timing.

“Then it isn't restraining the engines.”

“It knows the intended transition,” Daniel said.

Giles looked down at the Council volume open before him.

“Because transitions are precisely what was invoked.”

He turned the book so Daniel could see its title page.

De Ianuis et Limitibus.

Of Doors and Boundaries.

In the lower margin, beside a Council accession mark dated 1843, someone had written a single name.

Janus.
