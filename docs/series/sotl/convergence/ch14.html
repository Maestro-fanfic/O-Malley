What You'll Never Know

It happened three days after the Apophis engagement, in a debrief nobody in that room expected to matter nearly as much as it ended up mattering.

"There's a planet," Xander said, before he'd fully thought through why he was saying it out loud at all, watching Sam pull up a star chart on the main display. "P3X — I don't remember the exact designation, it's not gonna match your catalog yet because you haven't found it, not for a while yet. Like, a while while. I'm talking spoilers from a season you guys haven't aired yet. But there's a Lucian Alliance operation building there, right now. Naquadria mining, unstable core, the kind of setup that looks fine for a while and then very suddenly isn't. If somebody goes in heavy-handed instead of quiet, in about eight months, the whole operation comes apart and takes a survey team down with it. People who don't have to die, if somebody knows to be careful."

The room went very still, the kind of stillness that has weight to it.

"How do you know that," O'Neill said, in a voice that had gone flat in a way Xander had already learned, over the past several weeks, to take very seriously indeed.

"I don't know how I know it. I just — know it. It's in there, same place all the Apophis stuff was, same place Colonel Carter's future rank was. I wasn't gonna say anything, actually. I sat on it for two days already. Then I figured — if it might save people, isn't saying nothing worse than the risk of saying something?"

"Maybe," Young said, quiet, careful, clearly turning over some private calculation he didn't much like the answer to. "Or maybe you just told four military officers you're sitting on detailed information about missions that haven't happened yet — information you don't fully control, don't fully understand the source of, and can apparently produce on demand whenever it's convenient for you to do so. Xander. Do you understand what that actually sounds like, from where the rest of us are sitting?"

"It sounds bad." Xander held his gaze, didn't look away. "I know it sounds bad. I've known it sounds bad since the Colonel Carter thing happened, months ago, I just — I don't know what I'm supposed to do instead. Sit on everything I know forever? Let people I could've warned die because I was too worried about how it looks to say something out loud?"

"I don't have an easy answer for you," O'Neill said, leaning forward, elbows on the table. "That's the honest truth, and I'm not gonna pretend otherwise just to make this easier on either of us. But I'll tell you what actually worries me here, and it isn't that you're dangerous, kid, because I don't think you are. It's that you don't seem worried enough yet about what happens on the day you're wrong. You've been right so far, every single time. What happens the day the thing you remember isn't quite the thing that actually happens anymore, because we moved differently than whatever version of events you're carrying around in your head — and somebody dies anyway, because you told us all to trust a memory that turned out not to be as solid as you thought it was?"

Xander didn't have an answer for that. He sat with the silence for a long, uncomfortable moment, feeling something cold settle in where the earlier, quiet satisfaction of doing the right thing had been sitting a minute ago.

"I hadn't thought about that part," he admitted, finally.

"No." O'Neill's voice gentled, just slightly, at the edges. "I don't imagine you had. Most people don't, the first time. It usually takes losing something to really understand it."

✳

They acted on the warning anyway — a survey team rerouted under a manufactured cover story about equipment failure, the naquadria operation flagged for a different, more cautious kind of intervention than Xander's memory had originally described happening. It went smoothly, start to finish. Nobody died. The mission that hadn't happened yet, in the version of events Eli remembered, safely, never would now.

Xander should have felt good about that. Mostly, in the days that followed, he felt sick in a low, persistent, hard-to-name way that didn't have an obvious source until he sat with it long enough to properly identify it: he'd changed something. Actually changed it, not just accurately predicted it and watched it unfold. Whatever version of the future his borrowed memories had originally come from no longer matched the one now actually unfolding around him, and he had absolutely no way of knowing, going forward, what else might have shifted in the gap between the two.

He found Jack on the front steps again, days later, back on Earth briefly for a supply run, sitting in the same spot they'd first really talked, months back now, like some things about this particular friendship had simply already found their shape and settled into it.

"I keep thinking about what you said," Xander told him, not bothering with any kind of opener. "About the day I'm wrong."

"Yeah?"

"I changed something. On purpose. To save people, which felt right at the time, still feels right, honestly. And now I don't actually know what else might be different because of it. There could be a hundred small things I used to know for certain that just — aren't true anymore, and I won't find out until it's already too late for it to matter, if I ever find out at all."

O'Neill was quiet for a moment, looking out at the ordinary street, the ordinary evening light going gold and then gray, in the particular way of a man deciding how much of himself he was willing to hand over tonight.

"You know what I did," he said, "before any of this. Before the gate, before any of it at all." He didn't wait for a response. "I volunteered for a mission I fully expected, going in, to be one-way. Didn't much care, at the time, whether it was or not. I had a reason not to care, that I don't especially feel like getting into with you tonight, maybe not ever, but the short version of it is: I know what it's like to carry something so heavy that you stop trusting your own judgment about almost everything else in your life. I know what it's like to make a hard call and then spend years afterward wondering if it was the right one, because you'll never actually know the other version well enough to properly compare the two."

Xander looked at him properly, hearing something underneath the words that hadn't quite been there in any of their previous conversations, something older and more worn.

"You're not gonna get to know," O'Neill went on, quiet now. "That's the actual cost, kid. Not getting caught. Not getting in trouble with anybody. Just — never knowing, for the rest of your life, whether the version of things you changed turned out better than the version you erased without ever getting to see it play out. You did it for a good reason tonight. That matters, and I want you to hold onto that part. It doesn't make the not-knowing any lighter to carry around, though. Nothing makes that part lighter. You just get stronger at carrying it, eventually, if you're lucky and you've got the right people around you."

"How do you live with that?"

"Badly, some days." A small, humorless smile. "Better, other days, if I'm honest. You find people who'll sit with you on the bad days and not ask you to explain the whole thing, and you let that be enough, because it has to be enough, most of the time." He glanced sideways at Xander, something warmer moving under the dry delivery now. "For what it's worth — you're not carrying it alone. I don't know everything you know, not even close. But I know what it's like to make an impossible call and live with never being sure afterward whether it was the right one. That's something, even if it's not much, even if some nights it doesn't feel like nearly enough."

Xander didn't say anything for a while after that. He didn't need to. They sat together on the steps as the evening deepened around them, two people who understood, each in their own separate, hard-won way, what it cost to hold something too big and keep going anyway, and let the quiet between them do the rest of the talking neither of them had words left for.

✳

Young requested the file on his own service record three days after Kinsey's investigation notes finally, quietly made their way to people who actually needed to see them, and read it standing up, alone, in a corridor nobody else happened to be using that particular hour.

Major Everett Young. Nine years younger than the man reading the page. Not yet promoted, not yet posted anywhere that mattered, not yet recruited to a classified deep-space program that, in this version of the timeline, hadn't so much as been proposed yet. A whole separate, unstarted life, still entirely his own to lose.

He found Xander in the mess hall later that evening and sat down across from him without asking, which had, by that point, become its own kind of statement between the two of them.

"You read the file," Xander said. It wasn't a question.

"I did." Young turned his coffee cup slowly on the table, not drinking from it. "There's a kid out there — man, I suppose, not a kid, but he reads like one to me now — who's gonna get recruited to Icarus Base in about nine years, if nothing changes. Gonna go through exactly what I went through. Gonna end up exactly where I ended up, assuming any of it happens the same way twice."

"You want it to happen differently."

"I want it to not happen to him at all, if I've got any say in it, which I apparently might, now, for the first time in my life." Young was quiet a moment, turning that over. "It's a strange thing, sitting with. I'm not sad about my own life, Xander. I made my peace with the choices that got me here a long time ago, and I'd make most of them again given the choice. But there's a version of me out there who hasn't made any of those choices yet, who's got an entirely blank future still in front of him, and I find I want that future to actually stay blank. I want him to get to choose something else. Anything else. I want the Icarus program to simply not need him, nine years from now, because we already fixed the thing that made it necessary in the first place."

"That's a big ask."

"It's the exact same kind of ask you made about that survey team," Young said, steady, meeting his eyes. "Just bigger, and further out, and considerably more personal than I expected it to feel until I actually sat with it properly. You want to know what I've decided?"

"Yeah."

"I'm gonna spend every scrap of leverage I've got making sure the Icarus disaster never happens at all. Not just rerouted, not just survived better. Prevented. Every single person on that roster gets to keep the whole blank future they're currently walking around with, completely unaware they ever had anything to lose." He finally took a drink of the coffee, grimaced at how cold it had gotten while he wasn't paying attention to it. "It won't change anything for us. We already lived it. But it might mean nobody else ever has to."

Xander thought of a twenty-two-year-old somewhere outside Chicago, currently between jobs, entirely unaware that a version of his own future had just been quietly, deliberately rewritten by people he'd never meet.

"Good," he said, and meant it more than the single word let on.
