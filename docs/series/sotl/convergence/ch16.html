Chapter 16 of 59

Direct Means Direct

The Prime Minister had been in office for less than six months when the President of the United States informed him that an ancient British organization was deploying children into an undeclared war.

The call had begun with Destiny.

That alone would have justified waking every relevant minister in Whitehall. A vessel built by a civilization millions of years old had appeared above California carrying an international expedition from the future. Its American commander had placed himself under the authority of a secret military program operating inside Cheyenne Mountain. The ship was fixed over a town containing what the President described, with audible distaste, as an interdimensional rupture.

Then he reached the girl.

“Her name is Buffy Summers. She is seventeen years old, an American citizen, and she has been fighting since she was fifteen.”

The Prime Minister looked across the secure cabinet room at the Foreign Secretary and Cabinet Secretary. Neither man appeared to have heard the name.

“Fighting whom?”

“Vampires, primarily. Other hostile nonhuman entities as they appear.”

The Prime Minister waited for the qualification.

None came.

“Your people have confirmed this?”

“Direct observation by a United States Air Force team. Instrumental evidence. Historical corroboration. The officer in command is not given to imaginative reports.”

“And the British connection?”

“A body calling itself the Watchers Council trains and directs the girl through a British national assigned to her. It pays him. It does not pay her.”

The Cabinet Secretary stopped writing.

“Does Miss Summers serve voluntarily?” the Prime Minister asked.

“She believes that if she stops, people die. That is not the same question.”

No, it was not.

“What does the Council provide?”

“One underfunded Watcher. Books. Training. Orders.”

“Medical care?”

“No.”

“Insurance?”

“No.”

“Protection for her family?”

“Her mother does not know.”

The Prime Minister placed one hand flat on the table.

“How long has this organization operated?”

“Possibly longer than either of our governments. Its own doctrine treats the succession of adolescent girls as continuous. General Hammond reports that most die in service in relatively short order.”

“And another is chosen.”

“Called, according to the terminology. The Council considers the line permanent and each individual girl replaceable.”

The President allowed that to settle before continuing.

“Prime Minister, an organization based in your country has deployed an American child into an undeclared war on American soil. If it has done so under authority derived from the Crown, then Her Majesty needs to know exactly what has been done in her name.”

The Prime Minister looked at the Cabinet Secretary.

The older man had gone pale.

“One moment, Mr. President.”

He muted the call.

“You know the name.”

The Cabinet Secretary removed his glasses. “I know that a sealed brief concerning the Council passes from one Cabinet Secretary to the next. I was instructed that it was not to be opened except upon request from the Sovereign or in response to an event designated Black Crown.”

“A child fighting vampires does not qualify?”

“Until this moment, Prime Minister, I had no reason to believe vampires were real.”

“Get the brief.”

It arrived nine minutes later in a locked red case carried by an official who surrendered it without asking for a receipt.

The case contained a charter copy, a list of properties, the name of one royal adviser, and a handwritten instruction renewed during the first year of the present reign.

If Council action threatens the security of the Realm, the welfare of the Slayer, or the honor of the Crown, inform the Sovereign directly.

The Prime Minister read it twice.

He returned to the call.

“Mr. President, this matter is older than my government. I shall have to involve the Palace.”

“I was afraid you might say that.”

“I rather think the Palace was afraid we might someday have to.”

Sir Basil Covington arrived through the Privy Entrance carrying a battered leather case and an umbrella despite the clear sky.

He was seventy-one, silver-haired, and built with the narrow economy of a man who had spent a lifetime declining second helpings without appearing to notice food had been served. His public biography described advisory work for three ministries, two embassies, and the Royal Household. It omitted the werewolf negotiations in Northumberland, the sealed rooms beneath the British Museum, and the reason certain bishops returned his calls before those of the Archbishop.

The equerry took his umbrella.

“Her Majesty is waiting.”

“Then someone should have telephoned me sooner.”

He entered the private sitting room and bowed.

The Queen sat beside a small writing table. The Council charter lay open before her. The Prime Minister stood near the window with the Cabinet Secretary.

“Sir Basil.”

“Ma'am.”

“You know why you are here?”

“I have read the American preliminary report.”

“Have you read the Council's reports concerning Miss Summers?”

“No, Ma'am. The Council has not offered them.”

“Did you ask?”

Basil met her eyes. “Not before today.”

The answer did not excuse him. It was not intended to.

The Queen touched the charter with one finger.

“Does this document permit the Council to command the Slayer?”

“It recognizes the Council's duty to identify, instruct, support, and advise her. The language of command is notably absent.”

“And does it permit them to send children abroad without compensation or protection?”

“No.”

“To conceal their work from the government of the country in which the child lives?”

“No.”

“To treat her death as a method of succession?”

Basil paused. “No, Ma'am.”

“Yet we do not know what they have done.”

“Not yet.”

The Queen closed the charter.

“You will report to me directly, Sir Basil.”

“Of course, Ma'am. I shall keep your Private Secretary fully—”

“Direct means direct, Sir Basil.”

He became very still.

“Yes, Ma'am.”

“If children have been sent to die under privileges granted by the Crown, I will not receive a summary after someone has decided which details I ought to know.”

“No, Ma'am.”

“You will investigate the Council as an institution. You will also examine every case individually.”

“The archive may extend back centuries.”

“Then you had best begin.”

“You want the record of every Slayer?”

“Every Slayer. Every Potential taken into Council custody. Every Watcher responsible for her welfare. Every official who authorized a test, concealed a death, denied assistance, or ordered a girl removed.”

Basil inclined his head.

“I will not have murdered girls reduced to a historical average,” the Queen said. “Nor will I have innocent Watchers condemned merely because their superiors failed them. The institution and the individuals will answer for what they actually did.”

“Yes, Ma'am.”

“Every single one.”

She moved the American report onto the charter.

“I want a raid on the Council's records this afternoon.”

The Prime Minister spoke for the first time. “The Home Secretary and Metropolitan Police are prepared to act once we establish the necessary authority.”

Basil considered the hour. “The Council maintains protected archives at more than one location. If they receive warning, material will disappear.”

“Then they are not to receive warning,” the Queen said.

“They have friends throughout government.”

“Which is why you are not reporting through them.”

“We will need specialists for the wards.”

“Take them.”

“Child-protection teams for the training residences.”

The Prime Minister nodded. “Already being assembled.”

“And authority to suspend privileges attached to the charter.”

The Queen picked up her pen.

“You have it.”

Basil bowed again.

“I will make the arrangements.”

“Sir Basil.”

He stopped.

“The first report comes to me.”

“Directly, Ma'am.”

The smallest acknowledgment passed across her face.

“This afternoon,” she repeated.

In Sunnydale, O'Neill answered his secure telephone.

Hammond did not waste words.

“The British government is acting against the Council. Effective immediately, no one in your group contacts Council headquarters or any affiliated office.”

O'Neill looked through the library doors. Giles stood at the table with an open book. Buffy and Willow were arguing about whether a secure military radio counted as an accessory. Xander was asleep in a chair beneath a jacket someone had placed over him.

“Giles isn't going to like being treated as a leak.”

“This is a records-preservation measure, not a judgment of guilt. Explain that distinction.”

“And if he calls anyway?”

“Prevent the call.”

“Understood.”

Hammond's voice lowered. “The order comes with presidential authority and the cooperation of the Crown.”

O'Neill looked at Giles again.

“That ought to get his attention.”
