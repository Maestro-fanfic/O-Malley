The Deeper It's Dug

Senator Robert Kinsey had built an entire career on knowing which threads to pull, and the moment he read the line item, buried three layers deep in a routine oversight briefing nobody else on his staff had thought worth flagging, he recognized it immediately for exactly what it was: an unscheduled, unregistered gate address, origin unknown, an active wormhole into the single most secure facility in the federal government that Earth's own side had not opened, could not trace, and had not been the one to close. A single strange sentence sat folded in the same debrief, almost an afterthought by comparison: a rescued civilian on the other end of that connection had addressed a serving officer using a rank she wouldn't hold for years. Kinsey read that part twice, filed it as useful, and kept reading, because the sentence that actually mattered to him was the one about the open door nobody at Cheyenne Mountain had been the one holding.

He didn't move on it right away. That had never been his style, not once in twenty years of doing exactly this kind of work. He let it season for a week, requesting supplementary material through channels that wouldn't obviously trace back to his own office, building the shape of a question, piece by careful piece, before he ever asked it out loud where anyone could attribute the asking to him directly.

"General Hammond." He said it warmly, the first time he finally raised it, in a subcommittee room with three staffers present and a court reporter dutifully taking down every word for a record that would, Kinsey privately hoped, prove considerably more useful to him than to Hammond. "I wonder if you could clarify something for the record. Your program's own logs show two separate unscheduled, unregistered gate activations, inside the same six-hour window, address matching nothing in your database. I'm told the system carries a built-in failsafe, thirty-eight minutes, and the connection drops on its own whether anyone wants it to or not. Neither of these two came anywhere close to that mark. Both closed early, General. Not by your people. By whoever was on the other end of them, on their own schedule, both times, well inside a window your own engineers built specifically so you'd never have to find out what happens when you don't control the handle of that door."

"I can't fully explain that, Senator," Hammond said, flatly, because it was true, and because he'd learned decades ago, the hard way, that lying to Kinsey only ever gave the man more rope to work with later.

"You can't explain it," Kinsey repeated, letting the words hang in the room a beat longer than strictly necessary, watching them land. "There's also a detail in this same debrief I find almost quaint by comparison, General, given everything else in it: a teenage civilian on the other end of that open connection addressed Major Carter using a rank she would not hold for several more years, by any account your own program has on file. I have to ask, is there a security leak somewhere in your program? Or is there something about Colonel O'Neill's own conduct that hasn't yet made it into any official file this committee has seen?"

The insinuation did the work it was designed to do. By the following week, informal inquiries had hardened into a formal request for documentation. By the week after that, the request had become a subpoena for O'Neill's personal communications going back a full three years, filed under language broad enough that anyone reading it plainly could see this was no longer, in any real sense, about a single rescue operation at all.

✳

O'Neill read the subpoena twice in Hammond's office, sitting on the edge of a chair he hadn't been invited to sit in, and set it down on the desk with the calm of a man deciding, very deliberately, not to say the first six things that occurred to him.

"Three years of personal communications," he said, finally. "You know what's in three years of my personal communications, sir? Fishing trip logistics. An ongoing argument with my sister about Thanksgiving. Nothing that explains a kid I met five weeks ago knowing Carter's rank before she does."

"I'm aware," Hammond said. "Doesn't mean he won't get the file."

"Doesn't mean I have to enjoy watching him go through my Thanksgiving argument."

"No," Hammond agreed, dry despite himself. "It does not."

✳

The trouble, for Kinsey, was that the harder his people dug, the less anything they found looked anything like misconduct.

Xander Harris's own records checked out perfectly, which was, if anything, more unsettling to Kinsey's investigators than a gap would have been — birth certificate, school records, a paper trail as thoroughly ordinary as any other Sunnydale teenager's, right up until the exact night in question, after which the boy had simply, inexplicably started knowing things he had absolutely no earthly way of knowing. No leaked files. No breach anywhere in security protocol. No pattern of prior contact between O'Neill and anyone even remotely matching the boy's description, before that night. The ninety sleeping crew members proved considerably, disturbingly stranger still.

The fingerprints matched real service records without exception. That part, on its own, was manageable. What was not manageable, what took the investigation from merely strange to something none of Kinsey's people had a proper word for, was that every single one of those records belonged to someone currently, verifiably alive and going about their own ordinary life — a life that was, in each case, running roughly nine years behind the version of them currently asleep aboard the ship. A Major Everett Young, presently and unremarkably stationed at a domestic posting, not yet a colonel, not yet anywhere close to it by his own service timeline, answered his own phone on the second ring when a junior investigator finally worked up the nerve to place the call, sounded entirely bewildered by the questions being asked of him, and had, by his own account, never set foot aboard any spacecraft in his life. A Nicholas Rush, currently a junior faculty member several years out from tenure, still building the body of published research that would, in another nine years, make him the man Destiny's crew argued with daily, proved similarly baffled, similarly ordinary, similarly certain he had never once left the country that year. And an Eli Wallace, considerably younger than the version of him currently folded into someone else's memories, took the call with the flat, faintly amused confusion of a young man who assumed, for the first two full minutes, that he was the target of an elaborate prank — a discovery that landed harder on Xander than any other single line in the entire file, once he eventually read it, because it meant somewhere out there was a younger, unfinished version of the person whose memories he now carried, nine years and an entire unlived life away from ever becoming him, who had no idea any of it had happened to anyone at all. The same held for the others, one after another, down the full ninety-person roster: ordinary people living ordinary, less experienced, less far along lives, utterly unaware that an older version of themselves, unaged past the point of their own eventual disaster, was currently resting in a stasis pod above a town most of them had never once visited.

Nobody on the investigative team had a working theory that survived contact with a second phone call. What there was, instead, piling up week after week in a file that kept growing stranger rather than more damning, was an eight-hundred-meter vessel of entirely unknown origin, a station the apparent size of a small moon, ninety sleeping people who were each, somehow, also demonstrably still walking around living their own separate lives elsewhere, and a teenager whose own life read perfectly ordinary right up until it very suddenly, very publicly, didn't.

Each new report made the shape of the mystery larger, stranger, and Kinsey's original insinuation correspondingly smaller. A proper scandal needs a villain who actually did something wrong. What Kinsey's own investigators kept bringing back to him instead, week after increasingly frustrating week, was something closer to a ghost story, and ghost stories, however genuinely unsettling, don't make for indictments, no matter how many subpoenas you attach to them.

He kept pushing anyway, because stopping now would mean admitting he'd spent six weeks of real political capital chasing nothing at all, and that particular admission was, in his own calculation, a worse outcome than simply being wrong in public eventually.

✳

Leo McGarry brought it to Bartlet the way Leo brought most things that actually mattered: flat, unadorned, three full steps ahead of whatever question the President was about to ask him.

"Kinsey's been building a file on O'Neill and Hammond for six weeks," Leo said, closing the door behind him without being asked. "Wants it to look like misconduct. It isn't misconduct, Jed. Every single thread they pull just gets stranger, not more damning. I had CJ's people flag it because there's real risk of this going public in a way we can't control the frame of, once it does."

Bartlet looked up from the folder at that, something briefly flickering across his face that had nothing to do with Kinsey at all. Leo didn't use that name in this office, not once, not in eight years, not even on the worst nights either of them could name if pressed. Whatever this actually was, it had rattled Leo enough to reach for it without seeming to notice he'd done so.

"Strange how," Bartlet said, filing the observation away for later rather than raising it now, already reaching for his glasses, reading the summary Leo had dropped in front of him without being asked to.

By the time he finished the second page, his expression had stopped being merely curious and started being something colder.

"Leo." His voice had gone very quiet, which everyone who'd worked in this building long enough already knew to be considerably more dangerous than loud. "There's a girl in California who's apparently been fighting something called a Hellmouth. Alone. For years. And nobody, not this administration, not the one before it, not the one before that, as far as I can currently tell from this summary, ever knew, or did a single thing about it."

"That's the read, yeah."

"How is that possible." It wasn't really a question, and Leo didn't treat it as one. "How does a child carry something like that, generation after generation, and every single person in a position to help just — never knew? Never asked?"

"There's an organization," Leo said, watching Bartlet's face. "British, historically. Private. Very, very good at staying invisible, apparently for centuries."

"Get me everything," Bartlet said. "Everything on the girl. Everything on this Council. Everything on Kinsey's fishing expedition, start to finish. And get Sam in here, and Toby, and CJ. I want to understand how deep this goes before I decide how loud I'm going to be about any of it."

He read through the night. Nobody who saw him the next morning needed to be told, even in passing, how much sleep he hadn't gotten.

✳

The West Wing ran on a particular kind of motion that outsiders rarely appreciated until they'd actually stood inside it — nobody walked anywhere without also talking, nobody talked without also carrying at least two other problems in their free hand, and the whole building operated on the shared, unspoken assumption that the walk from one office to another was never wasted time, only time that hadn't yet been properly used. Senior staff gathered most mornings in a room too small for the actual size of what got decided in it, coffee going cold in mismatched mugs, everyone talking over everyone else in a rhythm that looked, to the untrained eye, like chaos, and was, to everyone actually in the room, simply how the work got done. Sam Seaborn kept a legal pad he never once seemed to finish filling. Toby chewed on pens he never once seemed to actually write with. CJ tracked three separate crises in her head at any given moment and somehow still remembered whose birthday it was. Josh counted votes the way other people counted breaths, automatically, constantly, whether or not there was currently anything to count. It was, on any ordinary day, a good place to work, full of people who mostly liked each other and mostly believed in what they were doing. This was not going to be an ordinary day.

Sam Seaborn read the file twice before he trusted himself to speak about it out loud in front of anyone. He'd always been the one on staff most likely to take a thing personally when he thought the government wasn't living up to what it was supposed to be for, and this file, more than most, seemed built for exactly that nerve.

"This isn't a security question," he said, in the senior staff meeting the next morning, his voice tighter than his usual careful cadence. "This is a moral one, plain and simple. There's a decades-long pattern of institutional failure sitting underneath all of this, and Kinsey's trying to turn the one thread that surfaced into a weapon against the two men who happened to be standing closest to it when it did. I keep thinking about every appropriations hearing I've ever sat through where somebody used the word oversight like it was a virtue in itself, and somehow, through six administrations, oversight never once reached this."

"He's not wrong that it's strange," Toby said, arms crossed, chewing on the end of a pen he'd apparently forgotten he was holding, working the problem the way he worked every problem: from the argument outward rather than the feeling inward. "He's wrong about what that strangeness means. There's a real difference between something being unexplained and something being illegitimate, and Kinsey's betting the country won't stop to notice the difference before his headline runs. That's not oversight, Sam, whatever he's calling it. That's a man mistaking a locked door for a guilty room."

"He's also betting we won't fight back," CJ said, flipping fast through her own notes, mentally drafting three different versions of a statement she sincerely hoped she'd never actually have to deliver, her mind three steps down the road toward every reporter who'd call the moment any of this leaked. "Which tells me he doesn't actually understand what he's stepped in here. I can bury a real scandal. I have buried real scandals. I cannot spin the government quietly ignored a child fighting monsters for generations into anything except exactly what it is, and neither can he, which means the only way this stays contained is if it never has to be spun in the first place."

"He knows what he's stepped in," Josh said, leaning back in his chair, counting votes in his head the way he counted everything, a man who measured most crises in terms of who'd need calling afterward. "He just thinks it's worth the exposure regardless. That's the part that should actually worry us... he's not being careless about this, he's being confident. Somebody's telling him this plays well for him, somewhere, and I'd like to know who, because whoever it is has apparently decided a seventeen-year-old girl is worth spending on."

"Then we make sure it doesn't play well for him at all," Bartlet said, from the head of the table, in a tone that ended the debate before it could properly get started. "I want him in my office. Not a hearing. Not a leak to preempt him. Him, personally, in this building, today."

The room emptied out fast after that, everyone already half a step into their own next task before they'd fully cleared the door, and Bartlet let them go before turning to Leo, who'd lingered, sensing without needing to be told that this particular conversation wasn't quite finished yet.

"You called me Jed this morning," Bartlet said, mild, watching Leo's face for the exact moment the memory of it landed. "In here. First time in eight years, unless my memory's considerably worse than I'd like to think it is."

Leo went very slightly still. "Did I."

"You did."

"Huh." Leo considered that, genuinely, like a man checking a fact against his own memory and finding it, uncomfortably, accurate. "I didn't clock it happening."

"I know you didn't. That's rather the point of my bringing it up." Bartlet studied him a moment longer, something quieter than curiosity in it now. "You've known me a long time, Leo. I don't think you've called me by my first name in this building since the campaign. Whatever this Kinsey business actually is to you, underneath the professional outrage, I'd like you to know I noticed it cost you something personal to walk in here with it."

"It's a seventeen-year-old girl, Mr. President," Leo said, quiet, the formal address deliberately, visibly reasserted now that he'd been caught. "I've got two daughters. I don't imagine I need to draw you a further picture than that."

"No," Bartlet said, gently. "I don't imagine you do." He let that sit a moment, then reached for his glasses again, the warmth in his voice not quite fading, just making room for the work still ahead of them both. "Go get Kinsey in this building, Leo. And thank you. For noticing before I did."
