Chapter 1: Convergence

The bell over the door of Ethan's didn't so much ring as apologize for existing: a thin, apologetic tink that got swallowed almost immediately by the shop's own silence. Xander let it swing shut behind him and stood for a second just breathing it in: dust, old rubber, mothballs, and something underneath all three he'd decided, several visits ago, not to think too hard about. The shop smelled the way a costume shop always smelled the week before Halloween, when the good stock had been picked over and what was left was a strange, democratic jumble of the popular and the forgotten, hung shoulder to shoulder on racks that hadn't been reorganized since maybe the Reagan administration.

"I'm just saying." He turned a rubber pirate hook over in his hands, testing the weight of it, watching light catch dully along the plastic. "There's a dignity to the classics. Hook. Peg leg. Minimal commitment. You put on an eyepatch, you say arr twice, you're done. Nobody expects range from a pirate."

"You said that about the hobo costume too." Buffy didn't look up from a rack of bulky, quilted-looking suits, all faded silver and worn orange piping, the kind of costume that clearly wanted to be a spacesuit and had gotten most of the way there on a discount budget. "And the mummy costume. And, if I recall, the year you tried to just wear your regular clothes and claim you were 'a guy.'"

"That was performance art."

"It was a Tuesday, Xander."

"Performance art doesn't care what day it is." He set the hook down, unconvinced by his own argument, and drifted further into the shop. "You have a whole philosophy of laziness, is what you're building toward, and I want it on record that I reject the premise."

"It's efficiency," he said again, weaker this time, mostly because Buffy had already stopped listening, her attention snagged by something three racks over that made her mouth twitch into a smile she was clearly trying to hide.

"It's laziness with a marketing department," she said, not even bothering to look at him for that one, and somewhere behind the register a bell rang on a different register — not the door this time, just Ethan cataloguing something, humming faintly to himself, a tuneless little sound that never quite resolved into a melody.

Willow wasn't listening to either of them anymore. She'd drifted, sometime in the last minute or two, without noticing herself do it, toward the back corner of the shop where the light didn't quite reach the way it reached everywhere else: not darker, just softer, like the fluorescents back there had made some kind of private agreement not to try too hard. A mannequin stood in that corner, robed, hooded, and it took her a moment to understand what was strange about it: the cowl caught the light wrong. Not badly wrong, not obviously wrong, just wrong in the specific way a held breath is wrong — a texture that read, at the edges of her vision, as having more depth to it than fabric had any business having.

At the mannequin's feet sat a small ornament. Egg-shaped. Glassy, though not quite glass. She picked it up before she'd decided to, and it was warm in her palm, a low and patient kind of warm, like something that had been resting in sunlight for a very long time even though the shop's back corner had no sunlight in it at all.

"Willow?" Buffy's voice, carrying easily across the shop. "Find something?"

"Yeah." The word came out strange, pitched a half-step from where she usually kept her voice, like someone had retuned an instrument without telling the musician. She cleared her throat and tried again, more normal this time, or normal enough. "This one's — it's asking for me."

Buffy laughed, delighted, the way you laugh at your best friend saying something a little weird and a little wonderful in the same breath. "Okay, dramatic. I like it. Very 'chosen by destiny,' very on brand for you, actually."

"You have no idea," Willow murmured, mostly to herself, and Buffy didn't hear it, already turning back to her own rack, and it would be a long while before either of them understood how true that sentence had actually been.

Xander found his costume on the bottom shelf, wedged with the sort of careless disrespect only bottom shelves are capable of between a Robin Hood outfit missing one boot and a wetsuit that looked like it had been designed by a committee that had only ever had wetsuits described to them secondhand, in a language none of them spoke fluently. A grey hoodie, worn soft at the cuffs like it had actually been lived in rather than manufactured for a costume rack. Wire-frame glasses with no lenses, just empty circles of plastic frame. A laminated card on a lanyard, the plastic gone faintly cloudy with age: E. WALLACE — DESTINY PROJECT — LEVEL 5 CLEARANCE, printed in a font that looked like it had come out of somebody's home printer on a bad toner day, and beneath that, smaller, in a line he wouldn't consciously register reading until much, much later — property of a ship that does not exist yet.

He held it for a long moment. Something about the fabric felt correct in a way he didn't have language for — not soft exactly, not special to the touch, just right, the rightness of putting on a shoe that turns out to fit better than the one you'd been wearing all day without noticing it pinched.

"This is it," he said, and heard the certainty in his own voice with a kind of distant surprise, like listening to someone else say something true.

Behind the register, Ethan Rayne watched all three of them the unhurried way a man does inventory: not counting stock, counting people, running some private calculation behind eyes that gave away nothing. When Xander approached and paid — three dollars, exact change, counted out in quarters and one crumpled bill — Ethan's smile didn't move so much as a fraction of an inch, and something underneath it, something old and patient and delighted, absolutely did.

Janus does love his surprises, he thought, folding the bill into the register with a small, private satisfaction, and turned to ring up the next customer as though nothing at all had just happened.

No wind stirred that evening. No lightning split any sky. There was only a fault line of chaos energy running outward from a small stone idol that had, moments before, been sitting inert and unremarkable on a shelf, cracking open now like an egg, spreading out in every direction at once across a town that had no idea it was about to become, briefly, the most interesting place in several universes.

It touched a thousand costumes that night. It paused — the way a general pauses over a map, finger hovering, knowing the answer but savoring the moment before committing to it — on three of them.

This one first, Janus decided, looking down through nothing at all, at a boy standing in a bedroom mirror in a three-dollar hoodie, glasses with no lenses pushed up his nose, grinning at his own reflection with the specific, hopeful uncertainty of someone who suspects a costume might not be quite enough this year but is trying it anyway.

The magic went into him like a key turning a lock that had never existed before that precise moment — no grinding, no resistance, just a sudden, total fit. For the length of one half-breath, there were two people standing in Xander Harris's shoes: a boy who had learned, across seventeen years of practice, to make jokes because jokes were cheaper than admitting he was afraid of almost everything, and a young man who had once spent six months alone on a ship at the very edge of the observable universe, working equations in the dark by the light of a single console because it was that or sit with how loud the silence got when nobody else was breathing anywhere nearby.

Then the half-breath ended, and there was only one person, and he was carrying both of them now, all the time, everywhere he went.

Sixty-two miles above Sunnydale, the sky tore. Not violently — nothing about it looked like violence, no shockwave, no thunderclap, nothing that would have shown up on a seismograph or woken a single sleeping child in any house below. It tore the way a curtain gets drawn back on something that had always, technically, been sitting on the other side of it, patient, waiting for someone to finally ask. Eight hundred meters of scarred, weathered, endlessly patient Ancient starship dropped into low orbit with the total, absolute silence of a thing that had never once needed an atmosphere in order to care what it sounded like arriving.

Two streets over, in the back corner of a costume shop that would, by morning, be locked, swept clean, and empty of both stock and proprietor, Willow Rosenberg stopped breathing for as long as it took the cowl in her hands to stop feeling like fabric and start feeling, all at once, like a garment that had chosen her specifically, out of every hand that might ever have picked it up. Space folded the way water folds around a stone dropped into still water — outward, patient, inevitable — and a station the size of a small moon assembled itself out of light and architecture and the accumulated, mismatched engineering of a hundred species that had never once, in all their long separate histories, heard the word Earth. It was big enough, once it finished arriving, that its shadow crossed the face of the moon in slow, deliberate arcs, like something that had brought its own private weather along for the trip. And Willow herself changed with it, in that same silent half-second: skin giving way to something faceted and cool, geometric planes settling where soft edges had been, hair stilling into something that no longer needed a breeze to know how to move, eyes resolving into a patterned, inner light that had nothing at all to do with color the way eyes were meant to have color. By the time the station's shadow finished crossing the moon, there was no version of Willow Rosenberg left standing in that shop who could have passed, at a glance, for anything human at all.

She knew, too, in that same instant and with that same uncomplicated certainty, exactly what the vast thing hanging in the sky actually was, and exactly what it was for — and, just as importantly, what it was not. It was not a warship, whatever its size might suggest to anyone glancing up and reaching instinctively for the nearest familiar comparison. No guns lived anywhere inside it, no armor plating, none of the ordinary architecture of a thing built to fight. It was not, strictly speaking, even a place, not in the fixed, physical sense a station orbiting a planet was supposed to be a place. It was a meeting point that existed slightly outside the ordinary rules of space and time altogether, reachable by paths that had very little to do with distance, a room that could, in its own strange fashion, be equally present above a hundred worlds at once without ever quite finishing the act of arriving at any single one of them. It was a waypoint. A gathering place, deliberately built and patiently maintained, for travelers scattered so impossibly far apart across so much dark that they would otherwise have gone their entire lives never once crossing paths, brought together instead in one shared room to trade what they'd found, to grieve what they'd lost, to simply not be alone for however long they chose to stay. And underneath even that, older than the gathering itself, it was a listening post: a place built, in some small original part, to keep faithful watch for a single vast, ancient, half-legendary presence called the Atlas, and to keep faith that the watching itself mattered, whether or not the Atlas ever answered back. Willow did not yet understand the shape of her own place in any of that. But she understood, immediately and completely, that she had just become someone whose entire purpose, whether she'd asked for it or not, now included keeping that particular watch alongside whoever else had ever kept it before her.

In the alley behind the Bronze, Buffy Summers — dressed as a woman she had never once heard of, in a life she had never once lived, standing in a secondhand exosuit that had felt, twenty minutes ago, like a joke about to fall apart at the seams — felt something vast and grieving and endlessly, patiently searching settle in behind her ribs, quiet as a held breath let go, and felt her own body settle right along with it, the suit's cheap quilted fabric deepening into something that didn't behave like fabric at all anymore, a helmet sealing closed over a face that no longer needed the air on the other side of the visor, the ordinary shape of her hands and shoulders and the line of her jaw dissolving into something the dim alley light simply couldn't resolve, however hard it tried. Where her eyes had been, something like starlight moved behind the visor's dark glass, patient and very old, looking out at a Sunnydale alley from a distance that had nothing to do with actual miles.

I have been so tired for so long, she thought, and the thought wasn't hers, not at first, not for the length of that one strange suspended second... and then, before she'd even finished having it, it was.

There was a second presence too, arriving quieter than the first, tucked in underneath the ache the way a held breath sits underneath a heartbeat — a name that surfaced whole and immediate and entirely unquestioned, the same easy, unearned certainty with which she already knew her own: Mike. Michael Allen. A man who had once, in some life stranger even than the one currently rewriting her own, simply been a writer — the ordinary, unglamorous kind, the kind who wrote for love of it rather than money, sitting at an ordinary keyboard putting himself, on paper, into stories that weren't his to actually live — before the stories themselves had started reaching back. It had happened to him three separate times that way, each one a city he'd only ever meant to describe rather than walk through, a war he'd only ever meant to narrate rather than stand anywhere near, and each time he had survived it the same plain, unglamorous way, one shift at a time, an outsider making do with whatever quiet competence a person could actually build for himself in a world that had never once been built with him in mind. He had come, eventually, warily, to expect it might happen to him again someday, the way you expect weather. He had not expected it to happen simply for playing something, no writing involved at all this time, and yet here he apparently was regardless, a fourth pull turned out to be just as real as the first three, dropping him whole into an entirely different wide, patient universe, one built of stars rather than city blocks. And now, somehow, a fifth time, he'd been pulled again, out of that borrowed universe and into her. She didn't wonder, even once, how she knew any of it. She simply did, the same uncomplicated way she still knew her own name.

Xander woke up on a deckplate that hummed, and knew, with total and immediate certainty, that it was a deckplate before he'd even opened his eyes. The tablet he apparently carried everywhere now was already tucked into the crook of his arm, its screen cycling through its own quiet, automated report, unbothered by whether anyone was actually awake to read it — life support nominal, hull integrity nominal, crew status: ninety, no change — the same patient loop it had apparently been running for longer than he'd been conscious to look at it, the ship simply continuing to account for itself, the way it always did, whether or not anyone was there to watch it happen.

I'm seventeen, said the part of him that was still, somewhere underneath everything else that had just happened, a kid from a town most maps didn't bother naming, a kid who made jokes because the alternative to joking was screaming, and screaming never actually fixed anything.

You've been alone on this ship six months, eleven days, and about four hours, said the other part, gently, in a tone that had learned gentleness the long, hard way, alone, out here, with nothing but a ship's patient, unfeeling computer to practice being gentle at. Give it a second. It'll settle. It always settles eventually.

It settled. He sat up slowly on the observation deck, in a room he had never, in his own life, stood in before tonight, and knew it the way you know your own childhood bedroom in the dark — the scuff pattern worn into the deck plating by a chair leg dragged back and forth, over and over, by someone doing math nobody had ever asked him to do, because doing the math was easier than sitting with ninety empty pods down the corridor and thinking too hard about whether any of them would ever wake up again.

The routine came back to him before the grief did, the way routine always seemed to arrive first, patient and undramatic, ready to be picked back up exactly where it had been set down. Most of it, if he was honest, wasn't really routine at all. Most of it was simply standing here, at the railing that ran the length of the deck's open front, looking out through the great curved window at a field of unfamiliar stars holding perfectly still while the ship carried him steadily through them, thinking about nothing in particular for stretches of time he'd long since stopped bothering to measure. That was the actual shape of most days out here, had been for months now — not doing, just standing, just looking, the ship's quiet hum the only company he had, until eventually, always eventually, something in him worked up the will to pull away from the railing and go be useful again.

When he finally did pull away, the routine was waiting exactly where he'd left it, patient as the stars themselves. Morning — or whatever the ship's clock was currently calling morning, since actual mornings had stopped meaning anything specific out here — started the same way it always had once he'd made himself start it: a full circuit of the crew status board first, ninety green lights checked one by one, not because any of them were ever going to have changed overnight but because checking them anyway was the only thing that ever let him stop thinking about them for the rest of the day. Then life support, rationed down to the specific careful percentages he'd worked out through months of trial and increasingly nervous error, oxygen and water reclamation both trimmed to numbers that would have terrified him six months ago and now simply were the numbers. Then navigation, updating a log nobody else was ever going to read, out of some stubborn insistence that the ship's course still deserved a witness even if that witness was only ever going to be him. Meals came third, always third, eaten standing at the same console because sitting down to eat alone in the mess hall had, sometime in month two, stopped being bearable. He talked to the ship more than he probably should have, out loud, full sentences, because a voice in the room, even his own, was better than none at all.

He'd built the whole architecture of the last six months around never once letting a single day pass without doing every part of that circuit, start to finish, because a day where he skipped it was a day where the ship might have genuinely needed something and not gotten it, and there was no longer anyone else aboard who'd ever notice the difference except him.

He put his palm flat against the deck, felt it hum steady under his hand, and cried — for about ninety seconds, no sobbing, just tears running down a face that wasn't quite used to this grief yet — and it wasn't grief at all, not really, not the kind with a clean name. Somewhere in the last six months Eli had done something considerably harder than merely accepting he was going to die out here slowly, alone, one calculation at a time. He had killed the hope on purpose, deliberately, with his own two hands, because hope left unattended out here didn't fade quietly, it curdled, it came back at three in the morning with a new rescue scenario every single night and then didn't deliver, over and over, until the disappointment itself became the thing that was actually going to break him. Killing it had been the only mercy available. He had gotten good at it. He had, if he was honest, gotten almost peaceful about it.

Nobody asks you first before they hand hope back. It simply arrived, whole and unbidden, the moment he made himself walk back to that same railing and looked out through the curved window at what Destiny had apparently found while he'd been unconscious: a blue, unmistakable, achingly ordinary planet, close enough to actually be orbiting, close enough that the ship's sensors had already quietly confirmed, in cold, patient text scrolling past on a nearby display, that the atmosphere was breathable, the gravity was survivable, and the radio spectrum was, improbably, saturated with a language he recognized. Something in him that had spent six months very deliberately not wanting anything anymore now had no idea what to do with wanting something again, and some old, stubborn, well-earned part of him actively resented it for showing up uninvited, resented the way it made him vulnerable again to a kind of disappointment he had, painstakingly, made himself immune to. He didn't trust it. He wasn't sure he was allowed to trust it. Some small, mean, protective piece of him kept waiting for the readout to be a hallucination, for the voice explaining it to be a symptom, for this to simply be the, cruel shape his mind had finally chosen to break in. Xander himself had never lived a single day of the life that had produced any of it, and felt the whole ugly, grudging shape of it anyway, achingly, entirely real — hope arriving like an injury rather than a gift, because that was, in the end, exactly what it was. Then he got up. That was the other thing Eli had learned out here, alone, across six months that had felt, some weeks, like six years: you let yourself feel it, all the way down, even the parts that refused to make you grateful the way they were supposed to, and then you got up anyway, because nobody else was going to check on the crew tonight, and somebody had to.

A colder, more practical thought arrived right behind the hope, the kind of caution Eli's own hard years out here had taught him to reach for before anything else got the chance to feel real. Destiny didn't linger. It never had, not once, not anywhere, across nine lived years of watching it drop out of FTL, take its measurements, and move on again within days, following a course it had been running long before any human being had ever set foot aboard it. Whatever this planet turned out to be, the ship would want to leave it behind on its own schedule soon enough, entirely indifferent to anything he might have to say about it.

He wasn't going to let that happen. Not this one.

The Chair was the only real answer he had, and Eli's memory of it arrived the way all the rest of it had: not a discovery still waiting to be painstakingly earned, the way it had been for the crew he'd never actually served beside, but simply something he already, fully knew how to do. He found it before he'd consciously decided to go looking, sat down in it like it had been built around the specific shape of him, and held the ship's course steady through nothing more complicated than telling it, plainly, that he wasn't ready to leave yet.

Destiny listened. It always did, once someone actually asked it to.

An alarm sounded, clear and immediate right where he stood, the particular ship-wide tone reserved for exactly one thing: something outside the hull, not inside it, had finally tripped a sensor that mattered. The ship's own flat, automated voice followed a half-second behind the tone, calm and utterly unhurried about delivering news that was anything but: Alert condition: external contact detected. Distress signal, unknown origin.

It wasn't the crew status board — those ninety green lights he could feel, somehow, without needing to look, steady and unchanged the way they always were. He glanced instead at the tablet he carried everywhere now, tucked into the crook of his arm from the moment he woke up to the moment he made himself sleep again, the same one that had been quietly reporting nominal, nominal, no change to him all morning, now scrolling something considerably less patient in cold blue text.

Distress signal. Sublight craft. Unknown origin. Degrading life support.

Two heat signatures, faint and cooling, dropping fast toward absolute zero.

"Okay." He said it out loud, to an empty room, to a ship that had apparently decided, without asking his permission, that he counted as crew now too. "Okay. New plan. Nobody panic. I'm gonna be the one who panics, actually, that's kind of always been my whole thing, but I'm gonna do it productively, because apparently that's a setting I have now."

He was already moving before he finished the sentence.

The Jumper knew his hands before he did.

That was the part that unsettled him most, once he had a spare half-second to be unsettled by anything — not the hatch cycling open silently at his approach, not the pilot's seat adjusting itself around a body it had never once held before tonight, but the simple, undeniable fact that his hands found the controls the way a tongue finds a missing tooth: instantly, unthinkingly, with the total confidence of long, worn familiarity. He didn't remember learning any of this. He'd never flown anything more complicated than a bicycle. Eli had learned it, somewhere, sometime, in a life Xander had only been living for about eleven minutes.

"Talk to me," he said, to the ship, to himself, to nobody in particular, the way people talk to machines when the alternative is silence and silence, right now, felt like it might actually kill him. Data scrolled fast across the canopy in front of him: position, trajectory, a countdown clock that didn't require any explanation at all. Two life signs. Faint. Cold. Drifting, slow and helpless, toward the outer edge of a solar system that had never been designed to let anything survive out there uninvited.

"Okay. Okay, okay, okay." His hands were shaking, visibly, badly, the way hands shake when the body underneath them has fully understood the stakes even if the mind attached to it hasn't quite caught up yet. His hands, at the exact same time, knew what to do, moving with a calm and total competence that had nothing to do with the panic riding along right beside it. Both things were true at once, entirely, completely, and somewhere around the second okay he made the executive decision not to examine that contradiction too closely, and just flew the ship instead.

The Jumper dropped free of Destiny's belly and cloaked itself before he'd consciously finished deciding to ask it to.

Jack O'Neill had stopped shivering some indeterminate amount of time ago, which he was fairly, grimly certain was not actually a good sign.

"Teal'c," he said, or thought he said, unsure anymore where the line sat between the two. His own voice sounded like it was arriving from somewhere several feet to the left of where his mouth actually was.

"I am here, O'Neill." Teal'c's voice came back steadier, but only just. Frost had crept in a delicate, almost beautiful lace pattern along the inside of the canopy, and Jack found himself staring at it instead of the readouts in front of him, because the readouts weren't telling him anything he particularly wanted to know right now. "Your vital signs are deteriorating."

"Yeah, I got that memo myself, thanks." He tried to laugh and it came out as something closer to a cough. "Carter and Daniel find us yet?"

"They are attempting a rescue. Jacob Carter's vessel is—" Teal'c stopped, mid-sentence, in a way that was, for Teal'c, roughly equivalent to another man screaming.

"Is what?"

"O'Neill. Something is approaching."

Jack turned his head, slow and expensive, the motion costing him something he was no longer sure he had enough of left to spend. Outside the canopy, space did the thing space was categorically not supposed to do: it rippled, folded, a shape uncloaking out of what had, one heartbeat earlier, been unbroken starfield: matte-grey, compact, entirely unfamiliar in a way that made his stomach, deeply unhappy about the cold and the thinning air, lurch with a fresh and separate kind of alarm. It closed the distance between them fast. Too fast. Faster than anything human-built had any business moving.

"That's not the Tok'ra ship," Jack said, each word costing more than the last.

"No," Teal'c agreed, with the flatness he reserved for confirming things nobody wanted confirmed. "It is not."

The unfamiliar craft matched their slow, helpless tumble with a smoothness that had absolutely no business existing outside of very good dreams, and then it simply was there, close enough now that Jack could make out hull markings scored into grey metal in a language he had never once encountered in years of doing exactly this kind of work for a living.

"Sam?" he said into the comm, mostly because it felt important to say something, anything, before whatever came next arrived. "Sam, if you can hear me — there's definitely something else out here with us."

Jacob Carter had been flying this exact stretch of cold, dead space for the better part of two years, on and off, ferrying Selmak between assignments in a ship he knew the way most men know their own front porch, and he had never once, in all that time, seen anything remotely resembling what had just appeared on his forward sensor display.

"Selmak." He sat forward hard enough that the harness caught him. "Selmak, are you seeing this, because I need a second opinion before I let myself actually believe my own instruments right now."

I see it, Selmak said, and for the first time in longer than Jacob could remember, his symbiote's voice carried something that sounded, unmistakably, like a complete and total absence of any plan whatsoever. That signature does not correspond to anything in our records. Not Goa'uld construction. Not Asgard. Not—

"Not anything, Selmak, I got that part myself just from looking at it." Jacob was banking the ship, hands moving on old instinct while the rest of him stayed fixed on the display. "That thing is matte-grey and it is not supposed to be there."

The ship — smaller, closer, matte-grey — rotated a few more degrees on its approach vector, and something else swam slowly into view behind it: vast, distant, a structure hanging in Earth's own orbit that dwarfed the small grey craft the way a mountain range dwarfs the single traveler standing at its base.

"Oh, come on—" Jacob's voice cracked upward, somewhere between a laugh and something considerably less amused. "That's not — Selmak, tell me that's a sensor ghost. Tell me that's an artifact. Tell me anything that isn't 'there are now two impossible objects sharing this exact patch of sky.'"

Jacob, Selmak said, quieter now, the calm cracking at the very edges in a way it almost never did, not even in combat. I do not know what that is either. In four hundred years, I have never once had to say that sentence twice in the same conversation.

"Well," Jacob said, staring at the display with both hands still frozen on the console, watching two separate impossibilities share the same patch of sky above a planet he had, until roughly ninety seconds ago, believed he understood reasonably well. "That's new. That is genuinely, certifiably new, and I would like it on the record that I did not sign up for new today."

The tractor field caught the X-301 as gently as a held breath, and Xander felt something unclench in his chest that he hadn't fully realized had been clenched at all.

Through the frosted canopy, hazy and fading in and out with every labored breath, Jack watched the thing that had come to get them grow from a distant shape into something that simply kept growing, past the point where his freezing brain wanted to accept it as a single object at all. "Teal'c," he managed, barely a whisper. "Tell me that's not as big as it looks."

"It is, O'Neill," Teal'c said, and something in his usual flat certainty had gone very slightly unmoored, "considerably larger."

Jack didn't have the strength left to say anything else about it. He filed the shape away, vast and grey and utterly wrong for anything Earth had ever built, and let the cold take the rest of his attention instead.

Get them inside. Get them warm. That's the job. That's the whole entire job, right now, forget everything else.

Chapter 5: The Delivery Truck

"It is not unreasonable," Rush allowed, which from him was practically a standing ovation.

"Here's what I think actually works, building off that," Xander said, before the momentum could stall out again. "Jenny and Willow handle the wound itself — the listening part, the part that needs actual care. Rush, you and Sam build the delivery mechanism — something that can carry what they're doing at the actual scale this needs, because I don't think either of them can push fourteen centuries of stubborn on their own, no offense."

"None taken," Willow said, mild.

"So it's not tech instead of magic, and it's not magic instead of tech. It's tech carrying magic somewhere magic can't get on its own. Like — a delivery truck. A really, really overqualified delivery truck."

Rush was quiet a moment, turning that over with visibly more patience than he'd shown the rest of the conversation. "That," he admitted, grudging, "is not an unreasonable framing."

"I have my moments."

"You have approximately one every few days. I'll take it."

They spent the rest of the second day building the actual rig — Rush and Sam wiring together a piece of Destiny's own diagnostic array with roughly four hundred feet of cable that had never been designed for a high school basement, while Jenny and Willow worked through the invocation itself, line by line, checking each phrase against three separate sources before either of them would commit to it out loud.

Buffy spent most of that second day simply watching, mostly quiet, feeling Artemis's own strange, patient attention settle over the whole process like a second set of eyes. She'd expected to feel useless, standing at the edges of a plan built by people who understood the mechanics far better than she did. Instead, watching Rush and Jenny slowly stop talking past each other and start actually building something together, she found herself thinking of it less as a plan and more as a kind of repair — not just of the wound in the ground, but of something smaller, quieter, happening in the room around it.

"They're actually working together," she said, quietly, mostly to herself, watching Rush hand Jenny a length of cable without being asked and Jenny take it without complaint.

Small victories, Artemis observed, something almost wry in it. I have found, across a great many travels, that they tend to matter more than the large ones, in the end. Large victories close wounds. Small ones teach people how to close them together, the next time.

By the time the light outside the library windows had gone properly dark, twice over, there was nothing left to plan. There was only the wound itself, waiting, the way it had been waiting for fourteen hundred years for someone to finally get the approach right.

Chapter 6: What the Wound Remembers

The hole in the world didn't look like much from the surface.

That had always been the strange, cheating thing about the Hellmouth. You could stand in the library basement, or the mouth of a cave off Kingman's Bluff, or any of the half-dozen access points Giles's books insisted existed, and see nothing but rock and old water stains. It took a kind of attention to feel the wrongness underneath. Buffy had spent three years learning to feel it the way you'd learn to feel a draft under a door. Now, standing at the lip of it with Artemis's borrowed awareness laid over her own, she didn't feel a draft anymore. She felt the door itself.

"It's not a hole," she said, mostly to herself, though her voice carried further in the basement's stillness than she'd intended. "It's a wound that never closed right."

Nobody asked her to clarify. Giles was kneeling at the chalk circle he and Jenny had spent two hours arguing into its final shape, each line and symbol debated, adjusted, checked twice against three separate sources before either of them would commit to it. Jenny didn't look up from the page she was translating, low and fast under her breath, in a language that predated the building around them by a very long time — older, even, than the Council archives she'd grown up half-believing were only stories.

Willow crouched a few feet off, hands loose in her lap, not casting anything yet. She'd stopped needing to grip things to feel steady these past weeks. That was new, and she still hadn't fully decided whether she liked it.

It listens before it opens, said the part of her that wasn't only her. Speak to the wound as you would to a frightened animal — not louder. Slower.

"I'm not scared of it," Willow murmured.

I did not say you were the frightened one.

Rush's voice cut across the chamber before she could sit with that observation any longer. "If everyone's quite finished being poetic about it..." He didn't look up from the tablet in his hands, its screen throwing pale blue light across a face that hadn't slept properly in longer than six months of stasis could account for. "This wound is putting out a waveform. An actual, measurable waveform. Which means it has a frequency. Which means it can, in principle, be matched."

"That's rather the working theory, yes," Giles said, without looking up either, his patience for Rush's tone visibly thinning after only two days of working alongside him.

"Then can we please stop treating it like it's going to be offended if someone points that out plainly."

Xander, crouched by the makeshift rig Rush had cobbled together, didn't look up from the readout either. "Nobody said it gets offended, we said it listens. Those aren't the same thing, and you'd know that if you'd actually read the material Jenny put together instead of skimming it for anything you could argue with. It's not The Terminator, Rush, it's more like — okay, actually, it's a little bit The Terminator. But an old one. An emotionally complicated one."

"They're functionally identical if the plan requires everyone to be quite so careful about tone."

"The plan requires us to be careful because a fourteen-hundred-year-old rip in reality doesn't come with an instruction manual, and you don't get to skip the part where people who've actually studied this thing for years tell you how it behaves, just because you don't like that the answer isn't a clean number on a screen."

Rush's jaw worked. He didn't say anything back. That, more than an actual retort, was its own kind of concession, the second one in as many days, and Xander clocked it and let it go without comment, because there wasn't time to enjoy it properly, not with what was coming.

"Positions," Giles said, standing, chalk dust clinging pale to the knees of his trousers. "Jenny?"

"Ready." She didn't look up from the page, still murmuring the last of the preparatory verses under her breath.

"Willow?"

Willow breathed out, slow and deliberate. Something in her chest that wasn't quite panic and wasn't quite calm settled into a shape she recognized now, weeks into learning what her own held breath was supposed to feel like with company inside it. "Ready."

"Rush?"

"The array's keyed to the waveform Sam's people logged an hour ago." He didn't look up from the tablet, fingers moving fast across it. "If the resonance holds..." He didn't finish the sentence. He didn't need to. Everyone in the room understood the shape of if it doesn't well enough that nobody made him say it out loud.

Buffy stepped up to the edge of the circle last, because that was where she belonged, and looked down into a darkness that had never once, in three years of patrolling this town, looked back at her the way it looked back at her now.

It knows what's coming, Artemis said, quiet, careful, an unfamiliar tenderness threaded through something colder underneath. It has known for longer than you have been alive that something would, eventually, come to close it. It has simply never quite believed, until this moment, that it would actually happen.

"Good," Buffy said out loud, and heard her own voice come out steadier than she actually felt underneath it. "Let's not disappoint it, then."

Giles began the invocation. It wasn't loud. That surprised her, somewhere underneath everything else... some old expectation of magic as spectacle, thunder and wind and dramatic gestures, and instead what she got was a middle-aged man's careful, precise Latin, unhurried, almost gentle, and Jenny's voice beneath it in something older still, weaving through the same rhythm the way two instruments find each other partway through a song neither one had rehearsed alone. Willow's hands moved, slow and deliberate, tracing a shape in the air that wasn't quite a gesture and wasn't quite writing, something in between the two that neither Latin nor English had ever needed a word for.

Rush's array began to hum.

The sound came up from the floor first... not really a noise at all, more a pressure, something felt in the teeth and the soles of the feet before it ever reached the ears properly. The chalk lines on the ground caught a light that had no obvious source and held it, burning cold-blue at the edges, deepening by degrees. Somewhere far below them, deep in rock that had been broken since before anyone alive had words for what had broken it, something enormous, ancient, and until this moment unchallenged, turned its slow attention toward the surface.

"Frequency's shifting," Rush said, sharp now, all his earlier irritation burned clean away by the one thing he apparently cared about above all else: the problem in front of him, actually working. "It's fighting the match."

"It doesn't want to close," Willow said. Her voice had gone strange, doubled, the way it did sometimes now when she wasn't paying close enough attention to keep the two voices carefully separate from each other. "It's been open so long it doesn't remember how to be anything else. It's afraid, in whatever way a thing like this can be afraid."

"Then remind it what closing feels like," Giles said, not unkindly, and kept chanting, his voice never once wavering.

The light at the circle's edges deepened from blue toward something that didn't have a proper color, something Buffy's eyes kept trying and failing to name. The pressure in the air built steadily until it felt like standing too close to a held musical note about to catastrophically break. She thought, distantly, of six months of solitude that weren't hers, and of a corridor full of people she'd never met and never saved, and let both of those things sit heavy in her chest instead of pushing them down the way she might once have, because some instinct, hers or Artemis's, she genuinely could no longer tell the difference and had stopped being frightened by that not-knowing sometime in the last few weeks, told her clearly that this was not a moment for flinching away from what she carried.

"Now," Rush said, sharp, urgent. "Now, now, now—"

Willow's hands closed.

The world didn't so much shake as exhale: one long, final breath let out of something that had been holding it for fourteen centuries without ever once setting it down. Then the light went out all at once, chalk lines snapping dark, the pressure in the room collapsing into ordinary basement air so suddenly and so completely that something in Buffy's sealed helmet clicked and re-equalized against it, sharp and startling.

Silence. The particular, enormous silence of a thing that has, at long last, actually stopped.

Rush looked at his tablet. Looked at it again, longer this time, checking and rechecking. "Frequency's flat," he said, and for the first time since Buffy had known him, he sounded something close to unguarded. "There's no waveform anymore. There's nothing down there at all. It's just rock. Ordinary rock."

Giles let out a breath he'd clearly been holding since well before the invocation had even started, his shoulders dropping several visible inches. Jenny sat back on her heels, hands trembling slightly, and made no effort to hide it.

"Willow?" Buffy said.

"I'm okay." The light behind Willow's eyes flickered, just once, and for a second something ancient and patient looked out through it before receding, gently, back to wherever it lived when it wasn't needed. "It's — it's actually closed. It's just a hole in the ground now. It's just rock, like Rush said."

Nobody said anything for a while after that. There didn't seem to be anything left that needed saying, not yet.

Buffy looked down into the dark, ordinary, empty space where a wound had sat for longer than anyone standing in that basement had been alive, and felt something in her own chest loosen that she hadn't fully realized, until this exact moment, had been clenched this entire time.

It's over, she thought, and it was, completely her own thought, no one else's... that fact, more than the silence in the ground itself, was the thing that made her believe it was actually true.

She stood there a while longer, alone in the quiet with the rest of them, letting it settle. Behind her, she could hear Rush starting to argue with Giles about whether the readings warranted a follow-up survey, and Willow laughing softly at something Jenny had said, and the ordinary, blessed sound of people who had just done something enormous together, returning, piece by piece, to the shape of an ordinary evening.

Jenny caught Xander's eye across the basement, once the celebration had settled into something quieter, and gave him a small, tired nod, the acknowledgment of someone who'd fought two days of arguments to get exactly here, and knew he'd been the one to actually find the words that made the argument stop.

"Delivery truck," she said, dry, walking past him toward the stairs. "I'm putting that in my notes. Historians are going to hate you."

"Historians already hate me," Xander said. "I'm the guy who brought a spaceship to a Buffy movie. I made my peace with that a while ago."

Chapter 7: Content, Not Complete

Jenny found Angel where she expected to find him, which was to say: nowhere obvious, in a place that made perfect sense once you actually thought about it for longer than a few seconds. The mansion on Crawford Street had stood empty since long before any of them had arrived in Sunnydale, its garden gone to seed, its fountain dry and cracked, and he'd taken to it the way certain people take to churches: not for what it currently was, but for what it let them not be, for an hour or two at a time.

She let herself in through the broken side gate, picking her way carefully across flagstones buckled by years of unchecked roots, and found him sitting on the fountain's edge as she'd pictured, turning something small over and over in his hands. Not a weapon, she realized as she got closer. A photograph. Buffy, laughing at something out of frame, taken sometime in the last few weeks with the careless, unposed honesty of a picture nobody had known, at the time, would come to matter this much.

He'd found her the night it happened, drawn by something in the air he'd never quite have words for even if pressed: the particular way a vampire's senses simply knew when the world had shifted underneath it, long before any human eye could have caught up to the same fact. There had been no shock in it for him, not the way there'd been for the others; whatever ordinary human mechanism softened a thing like this into something manageable had never applied to him in the first place, being neither human nor, strictly, alive. He had simply looked at her, sealed visor and all, and known, immediately and completely, exactly what he was looking at and exactly what it meant, and had felt, underneath the knowing, something far more complicated than shock: a kind of grief for a face he'd never get to see again, sitting right alongside a fierce, entirely unsurprised loyalty that hadn't changed by so much as a single degree.

"You knew I was coming," Jenny said, stopping a few feet off, letting the quiet settle before she pushed into it.

"I heard you two streets back." He didn't look up from the photograph. "You walk like someone who wants to be heard coming. It's considerate of you, actually. I appreciate it more than you probably realize."

"It's self-preservation, mostly. You're still a vampire, whatever else you've become since Halloween." She came the rest of the way over and sat down on the fountain's edge, a careful distance away, close enough for conversation, far enough to leave him room. "Also, for the record, I own actual shoes with actual heels. I make noise on purpose. It's not subtlety, Angel, it's basic manners: the same way you'd knock before walking into someone's crypt, if crypts had doors, which, incidentally, yours doesn't, which I've mentioned before and will keep mentioning until you fix it."

That earned her the faintest ghost of a smile, gone almost before it arrived. She let the silence hold a moment longer after that before she said the thing she'd actually come here to say. "I worked it out. The curse."

That got his attention. He set the photograph down on the cracked stone beside him, face-up, like he wasn't quite ready yet to stop being able to see it.

"I've read the text a hundred times," Jenny said. "Since I was a girl, long before I understood what any of it actually meant, or what it would eventually mean to me personally. Gypsy curses are always precise, Angel: that's the whole cruelty of them, they're built like contracts, every word load-bearing. And this one was never written to punish you for being simply happy. It was written to punish you for being complete."

"Those sound like the same thing to me."

"They're not. Not to whoever wrote this curse, generations ago, with more care and more malice than most people ever put into anything at all." She turned to look at him directly, wanting him to see her mean this. "A moment of true happiness: that's the exact phrase, and for years I read it the way anyone would, as simple joy. But it isn't simple joy. It's a very old, very specific idea about a soul finding its whole shape again. A wholeness. An absence of anything left wanting, even for a single unguarded moment." She paused. "You've been happy since Halloween, Angel. I've watched you be happy, in the small ways you let yourself be. Angel was quiet for a long moment. Outside, wind moved through the mansion's broken windows, stirring years of undisturbed dust into slow, drifting spirals.

"Say it plainly," he said. "I've had enough of things being implied at me. Just say it."

"She's carrying grief that isn't hers, and it isn't going anywhere, because it belongs, at least in part, to someone who isn't fully her either. Every time you look at her and think this is happiness, some part of her — a part that's staying, Angel, it's not passing through, it's here for good now — is somewhere else entirely. Mourning people she never met. Uneasy about a sky that doesn't look the way it should to her anymore, in ways she can't explain to anyone. She is never going to give the curse the one thing it's actually hunting for. Not because she isn't happy with you. Because she isn't only herself anymore, and the curse has no mechanism for recognizing a happiness that has room for grief living inside it at the exact same time."

"So I'm safe," he said, and there was nothing relieved at all in the way he said it.

"You're safe," Jenny said. "I don't think that's quite the same thing as good news, though, for you specifically. I think some part of you would rather this was still about you."

He didn't answer that directly. He picked the photograph back up instead, looked at it a moment longer, turned it slowly in his fingers, and then set it face-down on the stone this time, which told her more than anything he might have said out loud.

"I thought I was in love with her," he said, quiet, not performing the grief — which was somehow worse, more honest, than if he had been. "Before all of this. When she was — when it was simpler, or felt simpler, anyway. I thought that was going to be the whole shape of whatever was left of my life. Loving her, and being terrified of loving her, and both of those things being the same size, weighing the same in my chest every single day."

"And now?"

"Now she's something I don't entirely understand anymore, carrying grief for people who'll never exist in this world at all, and I still—" He stopped. Started again, more carefully. "I still care about her. That hasn't changed, and I don't think it's going to. But it's not what I thought it was going to be. It's not a love story anymore, not the kind I thought I was walking into. It's just—" He searched for the word, turning it over the way he'd been turning the photograph.

"Two people who survived something together," Jenny offered.

"Something like that." He was quiet a moment longer. "Does she know? What you worked out about the curse?"

"Not yet. I wanted to bring it to you first. You've spent longer being afraid of this than she has, and I thought you'd earned hearing it before anyone else did." Jenny stood, brushing dust from her jeans. "I think you should be the one to tell her. Not the mechanism, that part doesn't matter to her, she doesn't need the exact shape of the curse's teeth explained to her line by line. Just the part where you're not going to lose yourself over her. She's carrying enough of other people's grief without adding the shape of what your own happiness used to almost cost you both."

Angel looked at the photograph, face-down now on the cracked stone, for a long, quiet moment.

"I can do that," he said, finally.

"Good." Jenny stood, brushing dust from her jeans, and allowed herself, finally, the smallest genuine smile of the whole conversation. "And fix the door situation. I mean it. I refuse to keep announcing myself to a building that could just have a doorbell like a normal, non-cursed residence."

Jenny left him there with the dry fountain and the wind moving slow through empty windows, and didn't look back on her way out, because she understood, the way she understood most things about grief, having grown up in a family whose entire inheritance was old grief dressed up as ritual — that some moments needed to be sat with alone first, before they could properly be carried anywhere else.

"Jenny talked to you," she said, sitting down beside him without being asked. Not a question.

"She did."

"And?"

"I've got a spaceship-having ex-Zeppo, a best friend who talks to alien priests in her sleep, a Watcher who apparently used to be terrifying and might still secretly be, and a body that isn't entirely mine anymore in ways I'm still figuring out how to explain to my own mother." "For what it's worth," he said, "I'd still go to the mat for you. Whatever this is now, whatever it turns into. That part didn't change, and I don't think it's going to."

He didn't say anything else. He didn't need to. They sat together on the steps a while longer, two people who'd been braced, each in their own private way, for a tragedy that had quietly, almost without either of them noticing the exact moment it happened, simply stopped being inevitable.

Chapter 8: Arithmetic

The call came in at eleven at night, on the landline Giles kept for this kind of thing — old habits, old contacts, people who still thought of him, whatever else had changed since Halloween, as a Council man first and foremost.

"Rupert." The voice was clipped, English, careful in the specific, deliberate way of someone who understood a line might not be as private as they needed it to be tonight. "I won't give you my name and I won't say this twice, so listen carefully. Travers has authorized a retrieval team. Sunnydale, effective immediately, tonight."

"Retrieval," Giles repeated, already reaching for his jacket with his free hand, already running through a mental list of who needed to know and in what order.

"That's the word in the paperwork he filed." A pause, weighted, deliberate. "It isn't the word in the actual briefing his team received. The word in the briefing is elimination. The line's meant to pass cleanly to the next girl. Minimal disruption to ongoing Council operations, that's the phrasing they used, if you can stomach it. I thought you should hear it from someone before you heard it from a body bag instead."

The line went dead before Giles could ask anything else — no goodbye, no further explanation, just silence where a voice had been a moment before. He stood very still in his own kitchen for a long moment, kettle forgotten on the stove behind him, and felt something in himself settle into a shape he hadn't worn in a very long time. Not panic. The precise, cold opposite of panic. A terrible, familiar, almost welcome clarity, like an old coat finally fitting again after years hanging unused in a closet.

He knew the shape of that clarity by name, though it had been a very long time since anyone but Ethan had used it to his face. Ripper. A nickname earned in Bath, in a life he'd spent two decades carefully burying under tweed and Dewey decimals and gentle disapproval — a young man who'd dabbled in things considerably darker than library research, who'd run with a small, reckless circle that included, God help him, Ethan Rayne himself, before a friend's death had scared him properly straight and sent him running for the safest, most controlled version of himself he could construct. He had told himself, for twenty years, that the boy who'd worn that name was dead and buried, replaced entirely by the careful, bookish Watcher standing in this kitchen. He understood now, feeling the old coat settle back over his shoulders without so much as a seam catching, that he had simply never properly tested the theory.

He did not call Buffy. He thought about it — his hand actually hovered over the phone for a long moment — and then, deliberately, set the thought aside. Some things, once begun, could not properly be shared halfway through.

He found the team's staging point by nine the following morning, which surprised him not at all — he'd trained under the same doctrine they had, decades removed now, and doctrine changed slower than its practitioners liked to believe it did. A rented house sat on the edge of town, curtains drawn at hours nobody legitimately drew curtains, a car in the driveway wearing plates that had already been swapped twice in the last six months, if you knew precisely what small, telling details to look for.

He watched the house for the better part of a full day before he moved on it — patient, methodical, cataloguing entrances and exits and shift patterns the way he'd been trained to, decades ago, in a life he rarely let himself think about anymore. Four of them, he counted eventually. Professional. Quiet in a way that came from genuine training rather than simple nerves. The kind of quiet, he thought, watching from a parked car two streets over, that he himself had once worn as easily as a second skin.

He'd been that kind of quiet once too, a very long time ago, before Sunnydale, before Buffy, before he'd let himself believe he'd left all of it properly behind. Ripper had always been quiet before he was loud. That much, at least, hadn't changed.

They never heard him coming, which was the first mistake any of them made that night.

He didn't use magic. He'd genuinely considered it, turning the option over carefully during that long day of watching — a working to blind them, to slow them, something clean and appropriately distant that would have let him handle this from a comfortable, civilized remove. He decided against it somewhere around the second day of surveillance, for reasons he didn't examine too closely at the time and would understand rather better in retrospect: this needed to be personal. It needed to be a man's own two hands doing it, not a spell working at arm's length.

The first man went down fast and almost silent, a hand clamped hard over his mouth and a knee driven into the small of his back that put him flat on the floor before he'd even finished turning to see what was happening. The second one got a real fight in — a good one, well-trained, genuinely brutal — and Giles gave as good as he got for the better part of a full minute, blood in his own mouth, knuckles split, before something in his own long-buried patience simply ran all the way out, and after that it wasn't much of a fight at all anymore.

The third tried for the window, scrambling, and Giles let him get about halfway through the frame before deciding, calmly, that was quite far enough.

The fourth — the one who'd apparently been left posted to watch the door, the one who would live, eventually, to describe every single detail of what happened next to a man in London who very much did not want to hear any of it — got a considerably different kind of treatment.

Giles didn't hurry. That was the thing he'd remember most clearly afterward, on the rare occasions he let himself think back on it at all: how little hurry there had actually been in any of it, once the decision was made. He broke the man's left arm first, carefully, with the kind of precision that would set with a metal pin and a long, uncomfortable course of physical therapy afterward, and he did it while looking the man directly in the eye the entire time, and he said nothing at all until it was finished.

"You're going to remember this clearly," he said, conversational, almost gentle in tone. "I need you to. So please, don't pass out on me. If you pass out, I'll simply wake you and start again from the beginning."

He broke the right arm next, with the same unhurried care. Then, methodically, one leg and then the other, below the knee, in a manner calculated to heal wrong without immediate, proper setting and only marginally better even with it — a lifetime's worth of quiet discomfort either way, a permanent, physical reminder that would announce itself with every cold morning and every change in the weather for the rest of whatever life the man had remaining to him.

The man was screaming steadily by the end of it, and fully awake through every single second, exactly as instructed.

Giles crouched down to his eye level once the work was finished, and waited, unhurried, until the screaming settled into ragged, gasping breath, and until the man's eyes, wet and utterly terrified, properly focused on him.

"Travers sent four of you to kill a seventeen-year-old girl," Giles said, quiet now, almost kind, which was somehow, unmistakably, worse than shouting would ever have been. "You're going to go back to him. However you manage it — crawl the whole way, if you must, someone will eventually find you regardless and the story will spread on its own even if you never manage to tell it yourself. I want you to describe to him exactly what happened here tonight. Tell him I wasn't angry when I did this. This wasn't rage, and it wasn't cruelty for its own sake. This was arithmetic, plain and simple, and he made the calculation the moment he signed that order." He straightened, looked down at the broken man a final time, and let the last of it land flat and clear. "You're not the messenger. You're the message itself."

He left the other three where they'd fallen — breathing, unconscious, no more damaged than a hard, honest fight required, because that had genuinely never been the point of any of this. The point sat on the floor in front of him, sobbing, both arms and both legs broken as intended, carrying a story that would, Giles understood with total certainty, travel faster and further than any letter he could ever have written and posted himself.

Chapter 9: What Remained

The meeting was never entered onto any official calendar, in either country, by anyone with the authority to enter such things.

"I have buried a husband, outlived several prime ministers I quite liked and rather more I didn't, and I have just this month been told that an organization operating, however loosely, under my own family's quiet blessing chose to attempt the murder of a child rather than simply admit their entire system carried a flaw in it worth examining." "I think," Bartlet said, something in his own chest finally loosening for the first time since he'd boarded the plane, "that's more than I had any right to hope for, coming in here. Thank you, Ma'am."

Chapter 10: What I Put You Through

Joyce Summers had told herself, for three long years, that she simply didn't know. That the late nights, the bruises always explained away with some new and increasingly implausible story, the strange new friends and the stranger unexplained absences, were all just — teenage things, difficult but fundamentally ordinary, the kind of trouble any single mother eventually learned to muddle through without a proper manual to guide her. She had told herself that so consistently, for so long, that on some nights she had almost, nearly, believed it.

The night she stopped being able to believe it started ordinarily enough — Buffy coming through the front door later than curfew again, the same tired argument they'd had a dozen times before — and ended with her daughter stepping into the hallway light and Joyce's whole rehearsed, familiar speech dying somewhere in her throat before she could get even the first word of it out.

Something was wrong with Buffy's face, was Joyce's first, softened, half-formed thought — a trick of the hallway light, surely, some shadow sitting oddly across her daughter's features that a moment's proper look would resolve back into the ordinary shape she'd known for seventeen years. She blinked. She looked again. The wrongness held. Not injury, not simple exhaustion, something else entirely, something her own mind kept trying, gently, insistently, to explain away into anything ordinary at all, the way it had apparently been explaining away a great many things for longer than she'd ever let herself properly notice.

"Mom." Buffy's voice cracked on the single word, small and young in a way it hadn't sounded in a very long time, coming out of a sealed visor where a face should have been, dark glass with something like slow starlight moving behind it, robes and armor that had never once, in all Joyce's imagining of her daughter's life, belonged to any version of Buffy she'd known. "I need to tell you some things. And I need you to please, please not send me away again."

Joyce's hand found the kitchen counter behind her, steadying herself against it, her own mind still fighting her, still offering up half-formed, increasingly desperate ordinary explanations for a shape that had stopped, entirely, being ordinary. "Buffy. What — what happened to you."

"It's a long story. It's a really, really long story, and some of it's going to sound completely insane, and I need you to just — let me get all the way through it before you decide I've lost my mind for real this time. Okay? Can you do that for me?"

Joyce nodded, because for a long moment she genuinely couldn't manage words of her own.

Buffy told her. All of it, or as close to all of it as three entire years could reasonably be compressed into one late-night kitchen conversation — the calling, the Slayer line stretching back generations before her, Sunnydale's own Hellmouth sitting patient underneath everything, the friends who'd carried pieces of it alongside her without complaint, Halloween, the ship still sitting visible in the sky that half the town had apparently decided, collectively, not to notice anymore, the thing now living inside her alongside her own self that grieved, constantly, for people who would never exist. And somewhere in the telling of it, Joyce felt the last of her own mind's gentle, protective softening finally, completely give out — the shape in front of her no longer flickering at the edges into anything ordinary, no longer offering her any comfortable way to look away from what was actually standing in her kitchen. Her daughter. Entirely, unmistakably not human anymore, in any sense the word had ever meant to her before tonight. And her daughter, still, underneath all of it, exactly the same.

Joyce listened without interrupting again, her disbelief giving way, slowly and then all at once, to something worse than disbelief: recognition, sharp and sudden and unbearably clear.

"The nightmares," Joyce said, when Buffy ran out of words to say. "All those years of nightmares. The stories about vampires, about monsters under the bed that never quite went away with age the way they were supposed to — you were telling me. The entire time. You were telling me the truth, and I—"

"Yeah."

"And I—" Joyce's voice broke properly now, the careful composure she'd been holding onto since the hallway light finally, completely giving out. "I had you committed. I had my own daughter institutionalized, because I couldn't bring myself to accept what you kept trying to tell me."

"You did." Buffy's voice stayed steady, careful, but not gentle — this wasn't a moment for gentleness, and some part of her, old enough now and tired enough, understood that clearly. "You put me there anyway. Even after everything. Even when some part of you had to know I wasn't lying, because nothing about what I was describing to those doctors ever quite fit the shape of crazy, not the way they all so badly wanted it to."

"How could I have done such a thing." Joyce wasn't really asking Buffy anymore. She was asking herself, out loud, for possibly the first time in three entire years of not asking. "How could I have looked at my own child, in that much visible pain, telling me something that clearly, genuinely terrified her, and decided the easiest possible answer was that something was wrong with her — rather than believe, even for a moment, that the world itself might actually be that frightening a place."

"I don't know," Buffy said, and it wasn't quite an accusation, but it wasn't absolution either, not yet. "I've had a long time to be angry about it, Mom. I don't think I'm finished being angry about it. I don't think that's the kind of thing that gets finished in one conversation, even a really honest one."

"No," Joyce agreed, wiping at her own eyes with the back of one hand. "I don't imagine it does. I wouldn't expect it to, if I were you."

They stood together in the kitchen a while longer after that, neither one quite ready yet to close the physical distance still sitting between them, both of them keenly, painfully aware of exactly how much distance there actually was left to close.

"I want to be told things," Joyce said, breaking the silence herself this time. "Going forward, from now on. Not because I think I can actually protect you from any of it — clearly, obviously, I can't, clearly you've been protecting yourself this whole time, for years, entirely without me — but because I cannot do this to you again. I cannot be the mother who finds out three years too late and calls that protecting you. I would rather know everything, and be scared, and useless half the time, than not know at all ever again."

"That's a lot to ask for, after everything I've just told you."

"I know it is." Joyce looked at her daughter — really looked, at the strange starlight moving behind the visor's dark glass, the whole unfamiliar shape of the girl standing in front of her who was so much more, and somehow also so much less ordinary, than Joyce had ever once let herself properly imagine. "I'm asking anyway. I'd rather ask and be told no than never ask at all again."

Buffy didn't answer right away. She stood there a long, considering moment, weighing something behind the visor Joyce could no longer read the way she once could have, and then, slowly, crossed the small distance of the kitchen and let herself be pulled into a hug that neither of them had fully, properly earned yet, but that both of them, standing there in the late-night quiet of an ordinary kitchen, needed badly enough anyway.

"Okay," Buffy said, voice coming out low and close near her mother's ear, young again in a way that ached to hear despite carrying no breath behind it anymore. "Okay. I'll try. I can't promise I'll always get it right. But I'll try."

"That's all I'm asking for," Joyce said, holding on a little tighter. "That's all I want from you."

It wasn't forgiveness, not yet, not fully. Neither of them pretended, even for a moment, that it was. But it was a door, cracked open properly for the first time in three long years of careful silence on both sides — and for tonight, standing together in that kitchen with the light on and the whole rest of the world still turning strange and enormous outside their front door, that was enough.

Chapter 11: The Deeper It's Dug

Senator Robert Kinsey had built an entire career on knowing which threads to pull, and the moment he read the line item, buried three layers deep in a routine oversight briefing nobody else on his staff had thought worth flagging, he recognized it immediately for exactly what it was: an unscheduled, unregistered gate address, origin unknown, an active wormhole into the single most secure facility in the federal government that Earth's own side had not opened, could not trace, and had not been the one to close. What was not manageable, what took the investigation from merely strange to something none of Kinsey's people had a proper word for, was that every single one of those records belonged to someone currently, verifiably alive and going about their own ordinary life — a life that was, in each case, running roughly nine years behind the version of them currently asleep aboard the ship. A Major Everett Young, presently and unremarkably stationed at a domestic posting, not yet a colonel, not yet anywhere close to it by his own service timeline, answered his own phone on the second ring when a junior investigator finally worked up the nerve to place the call, sounded entirely bewildered by the questions being asked of him, and had, by his own account, never set foot aboard any spacecraft in his life. A Nicholas Rush, currently a junior faculty member several years out from tenure, still building the body of published research that would, in another nine years, make him the man Destiny's crew argued with daily, proved similarly baffled, similarly ordinary, similarly certain he had never once left the country that year. And an Eli Wallace, considerably younger than the version of him currently folded into someone else's memories, took the call with the flat, faintly amused confusion of a young man who assumed, for the first two full minutes, that he was the target of an elaborate prank — a discovery that landed harder on Xander than any other single line in the entire file, once he eventually read it, because it meant somewhere out there was a younger, unfinished version of the person whose memories he now carried, nine years and an entire unlived life away from ever becoming him, who had no idea any of it had happened to anyone at all. The same held for the others, one after another, down the full ninety-person roster: ordinary people living ordinary, less experienced, less far along lives, utterly unaware that an older version of themselves, unaged past the point of their own eventual disaster, was currently resting in a stasis pod above a town most of them had never once visited.

Nobody on the investigative team had a working theory that survived contact with a second phone call. What there was, instead, piling up week after week in a file that kept growing stranger rather than more damning, was an eight-hundred-meter vessel of entirely unknown origin, a station the apparent size of a small moon, ninety sleeping people who were each, somehow, also demonstrably still walking around living their own separate lives elsewhere, and a teenager whose own life read perfectly ordinary right up until it very suddenly, very publicly, didn't.

Each new report made the shape of the mystery larger, stranger, and Kinsey's original insinuation correspondingly smaller. A proper scandal needs a villain who actually did something wrong. What Kinsey's own investigators kept bringing back to him instead, week after increasingly frustrating week, was something closer to a ghost story, and ghost stories, however genuinely unsettling, don't make for indictments, no matter how many subpoenas you attach to them.

He kept pushing anyway, because stopping now would mean admitting he'd spent six weeks of real political capital chasing nothing at all, and that particular admission was, in his own calculation, a worse outcome than simply being wrong in public eventually.

Chapter 12: When the President Stands

Sam stood near the window with the stillness of a man holding back words he'd clearly prepared in advance and clearly intended to use, given even the smallest opening.

"Sit down, Bob."

"I've read your file," Bartlet said, not sitting himself, standing instead behind his own desk with both hands flat against it. "All of it. He let that land, watched it land. Kinsey's smile faltered, just fractionally, at the corners. "You built a scandal out of a genuine mystery, Bob, because a mystery doesn't get you anything politically and a scandal might. "Mr. President, with respect, I don't see how one teenager's personal circumstances have any bearing on legitimate oversight of—"

"When the President stands," Bartlet said, standing straighter now, though he'd never actually sat, "nobody sits."

Kinsey stood anyway, fast, an old, deep reflex from a lifetime spent in rooms with clear hierarchies, before his own sharper political instincts caught up and told him, a half-second too late, exactly how badly that reflex had just cost him in front of everyone watching.

"You had nothing," Bartlet said, quiet now, colder than shouting would ever have managed to be. "Six weeks. A subpoena. Three off-the-record calls to reporters who talk to my staff more than they talk to yours, apparently. And at the very end of all of it, you have nothing, Bob, because there was never anything real to have. What you actually had, the whole time, was a genuine opportunity to be the first person in this entire government's history to properly notice what's been happening in that town, and to ask, seriously, what we might do to help. You chose a witch hunt instead. I want you to sit with that."

"This investigation is closed," Bartlet said. "Effective immediately, as of this conversation. But we're not finished, Bob, not by a considerable distance, because closing it isn't the same as forgiving how you conducted it." He didn't raise his voice. He didn't need to. "You leaked details of a classified operation to three separate reporters for personal political advantage. That's unauthorized disclosure of classified national security information, Bob, which is a federal crime, and I want to be very clear that I am not speaking hypothetically when I use that phrase. You used subpoena power that exists for legitimate oversight to conduct what amounts to a harassment campaign against a seventeen-year-old girl who has never once, in her entire life, been given a say in any of this. And you did all of it either fully aware that you were spending a child as currency in your own reelection campaign, or willfully unaware of it, which I actually find worse, Bob. Not knowing isn't an accident when you've spent this much effort making sure you never had to."

Abbey didn't say anything. She simply looked at Kinsey, level and unhurried, for exactly long enough to let the silence itself do the work Bartlet had left open for her, and that silence, somehow, landed worse than any line she could have delivered.

"Your subpoena power on this committee is finished," Bartlet said, quieter now, the fury banking into something colder and considerably more final. "Your chairmanship is finished. And the Justice Department will be in touch shortly, because unauthorized disclosure of classified information carries real weight, Bob, even for a sitting Senator, and I intend to see every ounce of it applied."

Kinsey opened his mouth, some last, doomed instinct for the last word rising up out of pure habit, and got exactly one syllable out before Bartlet's temper finally, fully broke its banks.

"Get this man out of my house!" he snapped, on his feet now, one hand slicing through the air toward the door.

The two agents from the hallway were inside the room before the sentence had finished leaving his mouth, moving with the specific, well-drilled efficiency of people who had clearly been briefed, in advance, on exactly which moment they'd be needed. "Senator." Abbey's voice caught him just before he cleared the threshold, sweet as anything, sharp enough underneath to draw blood. "Do give my best to whoever writes your concession speech. I imagine they're going to have considerably more time to work on it than either of you originally planned."

Kinsey didn't manage a response to that either, still moving fast, feet finally finding the floor again somewhere out in the hallway, where a second pair of agents was already waiting, and where the small federal seal on the nearer one's jacket read Bureau rather than Service.

"Robert Kinsey," the lead agent said, already reaching for his own restraints, "you're under arrest for unauthorized disclosure of classified information. You have the right to remain silent—"

Whatever fight had been left in him after the first ejection didn't survive the second one. He went still, went quiet, and let the agents take him by each arm, the particular, total stillness of a man who had only ever performed strength and had just, very publicly, run out of the performance.

The door closed with a soft, unhurried click, considerably quieter than the sound of an entire life permanently upending, though everyone still standing in that office understood perfectly well the two sounds had just become the same thing. The story that moved through Washington over the following weeks was not, in the end, about a scandal at all... it was about a Senator who had walked into the Oval Office fully expecting to win, and had instead been physically thrown out of it before being formally arrested in the hallway outside, in front of witnesses who would remember, for a very long time, exactly how thoroughly, how publicly, and how permanently, he'd lost it.

Chapter 13: Rude About It

Rush found him three weeks later, in the middle of the night, in the observation deck where Xander had first woken up carrying two lives instead of one — the same worn scuff pattern still on the deck, the same low hum underfoot, though the room had lost most of its strangeness for Xander by now, worn smooth by months of quiet, late visits nobody else knew about.

Rush's jaw tightened, fractionally, a small muscle jumping once near his temple. "That's an ungenerous way of putting it."

Chapter 21: The Music

Chapter 22: The Optimized World

"Or something is," Sam said, and the whole room went very still around that word, hanging in the air.

They didn't find whatever was actually doing the tending, that first trip, not with the time they had available, not with the scans they managed to run before Young, cautious as ever, called it and pulled the whole team back aboard for the night rather than risk pushing further into the unknown. But they left something behind on that world before they departed: a single small sensor drone, tucked carefully into the treeline, quietly logging, watching, in case the pattern ever showed its hand more clearly.

"So," Jack said, back on the bridge later that night, arms crossed, looking out at a planet that had gone, over the course of a single day, from breathtaking to unsettling. "First trip out, and we found something creepy. Really setting a solid tone here for the rest of this whole exploring-the-universe thing."

"We don't know it's hostile," Xander said, though something in his own borrowed memory (a fragment, faint, unplaced, from a life he hadn't fully lived yet, from a future that hadn't quite happened) had gone cold and watchful at the shape of what they'd just discovered.

"No," Rush agreed, quiet, staring at the sensor readout a beat longer than the conversation strictly required. "We don't know that yet. Not for certain."

Nobody said the actual word that was, faintly, starting to occur to more than one person standing in that room. It wasn't time yet, not quite. But the seed had been planted, quiet and precise, the same way everything on that world had apparently been planted, tended, and grown.

✳

They stayed in orbit one final morning before moving on, most of the crew scattered across the ship doing their own quiet work, and Buffy found herself standing at the observation window beside Willow, both of them looking down at the impossibly green world below.

"You feel it too, don't you," Buffy said, not quite a question.

"Yeah." Willow wrapped her arms around herself, though the deck wasn't cold. "Nada says it's — they say it reminds them of something. Won't say what, exactly. Just goes quiet whenever I think about it too hard."

"Artemis does the same thing." Buffy watched the slow curve of cloud drift across the meadow far below. "Whatever this is, I don't think it's finished with us yet. I think this was just — an introduction. A first look."

"That's a comforting thought," Willow said, dry.

"I didn't say it was comforting. I said I think it's true." Buffy glanced sideways at her friend, something steady passing between them without either one needing to say it out loud. "We'll figure it out. Whatever it turns out to be."

"Yeah," Willow agreed, and meant it, even without being sure yet what she was agreeing to. "We will."

Below them, the optimized world turned on, patient and perfect and silent, keeping whatever secret it was keeping for a little while longer.

Chapter 23: Signal Bursts

The next mission had nothing at all to do with the optimized world from before — a diplomatic first-contact run with a species Daniel had been cautiously corresponding with for weeks through carefully translated signal bursts, a graceful, deliberate people who lived in floating cities suspended above a gas giant's endless, churning storms.

The city itself took Buffy's breath away on approach, in a way none of the crew were quite prepared for after the tension of the first trip — spires of pale, luminous material rising and falling gently with the currents beneath them, whole districts of the place shifting position slowly, deliberately, like a vast piece of architecture that had never once stopped breathing.

"It's beautiful," Willow said, again, though this time nobody in the Jumper felt the need to correct her with a too.

"I still don't love flying into weather that could, technically, eat the ship," Jack said, from the pilot's seat, eyeing the churning storms below with the wariness of a man who had survived enough near-misses to trust his own unease. "Somebody remind me why we couldn't just orbit and wave."

"Because the Vethari consider orbital contact a serious diplomatic slight," Daniel said, not looking up from the notes spread across his lap. "Something about presence mattering more than words, in their framework. You don't send a representative who isn't willing to actually be present."

"I respect that as a philosophy. I respect it less as a flight plan."

"You'll live," Daniel said, mild, and Jack, muttering something about optimism being easy for a man who wasn't the one flying, brought the Jumper down through the last of the cloud layer anyway, steady as ever despite the complaining.

✳

The Vethari, as they called themselves, met the away team with a formality that took some getting used to — long, careful greetings exchanged before anyone was permitted to speak of business, cups of something warm and faintly floral pressed into everyone's hands whether they wanted them or not, offered by attendants who bowed at an angle so precise it looked almost mathematical. Daniel handled the protocol with visible delight, cross-referencing every gesture and honorific against the weeks of correspondence he'd built, occasionally correcting his own pronunciation mid-sentence with a wince that made his hosts laugh more than any actual joke he'd attempted.

"You've been at this a long time," the Vethari elder said, once the formal greetings had finally run their course, studying Daniel with what might have been approval. "Most who reach across the dark grow impatient with the shape of proper welcome. You did not."

"I find the shape of a welcome usually tells you more about a people than the words that come after it," Daniel said, and meant it, and the elder's expression shifted into something that read, even across the gulf of an entirely unfamiliar species, as genuine warmth.

It went well, better than anyone had quite dared hope going in. Willow spent an entire afternoon trading notes on containment field theory with a local artisan, the two of them bent over a floating diagram neither species' language had quite the vocabulary for, gesturing more than speaking by the end of it, both clearly delighted by how much they were managing to communicate anyway. Buffy found herself, for the first time since Halloween, simply and uncomplicatedly enjoying being somewhere new without a single thing actively trying to kill her for a change — walking the shifting streets beside Teal'c, who observed everything with his usual total attention and offered, unprompted, that the Vethari reminded him faintly of a peaceful era in Jaffa architecture he hadn't thought of in years.

Rush and Daniel found the outpost on the second day, tucked into the lower levels of one of the oldest floating districts, a sealed chamber the Vethari themselves had apparently never managed to open in the several centuries they'd lived directly above it.

"They built around it rather than through it," Daniel said, running a hand along a doorway carved with a script he recognized immediately, even half-worn by time. "Out of respect, near as I can translate this. Something about not disturbing what wasn't theirs to disturb."

"Ancient construction," Rush confirmed, deep in a console that had clearly been waiting, patient and untouched, for someone who actually knew how to ask it the right questions. His hands stilled over the interface. "Daniel. Come look at this."

What the chamber held, once the two of them finally coaxed its door open, was a rack of eight modules, each one dense, quietly humming, unmistakably familiar to anyone who'd spent any real time around Ancient technology.

"Are those—" Daniel didn't finish the question.

"Zero Point Modules," Rush said, and for once in his life sounded almost entirely without irony, something close to genuine, unguarded relief moving behind the words. "Eight of them. Fully charged, or as close to it as anything gets after this many centuries. This is—" He stopped, recalculated, tried again. "This solves our power problem for a very long time to come."

The Vethari elder, when consulted, seemed more amused than proprietary about the whole discovery. "We always wondered what our ancestors chose not to disturb," she said, through Daniel's careful translation. "If it serves you, better it finally serve someone."

They brought all eight back to Destiny that evening, and Rush spent most of the trip back simply sitting with them in the cargo hold, running diagnostic after diagnostic with the quiet satisfaction of a man who had spent months quietly worrying about a number in the back of his mind, watching that number finally, properly stop being a worry at all.

"This changes things," he told Young, later, aboard the bridge. "Considerably. We've been rationing every major system for longer than most of the crew even realizes. This buys us margin. Real margin, for the first time since I woke up."

"Good," Young said, simple, and meant it more than the single word let on.

"You don't talk about home much," Buffy said, surprised into the observation before she'd quite decided to make it.

"There is not often occasion," Teal'c said, simply, and left it there, though something in his face, watching the floating spires drift past, suggested the occasion had mattered more than the words let on.

"You could talk about it now," Buffy offered, gently. "I've got nowhere else to be, and it's a nice view for it."

Teal'c considered that for a long moment, watching a district of the city slowly rotate past overhead. "Chulak had cities built into cliff faces," he said, finally, quiet. "Not unlike this, in their patience, though they did not move. I have not thought of them in this way in some time. It is not an unwelcome thing, to be reminded."

They walked a while longer without either of them saying much else, and Buffy found she didn't mind the quiet at all.

✳

It was Sam, reviewing the trip's full sensor logs afterward, back on Destiny, who found the anomaly tucked away in the margins — a corrupted data fragment, embedded deep inside one of the floating city's own archives, that resembled, in its underlying structure, absolutely nothing else in the entire archive around it. A pattern. The same optimized, noiseless precision they'd found once, on the first world, buried here too, patient and quiet, like a fingerprint left carelessly on a door nobody in the household even remembered opening.

"That's not local architecture," Sam said, showing Rush the readout, tapping the screen twice for emphasis. "This is the second time we've encountered this exact signature now, in two completely unrelated places."

"Coincidence," Rush said, though something in his own voice made it clear even he didn't fully believe that anymore.

"You don't believe that."

"I don't believe much of anything until I've run it three separate ways and gotten the same answer each time," Rush said, pulling up the first world's data alongside the new fragment, laying the two side by side. "But no. I don't believe it's coincidence. I believe it considerably less than I did an hour ago."

They ran the comparison twice, then a third time, and each pass came back the same: two signatures, weeks and light-years apart, structured by the same underlying hand.

"How old is this fragment?" Sam asked, watching the timestamps resolve.

"Older than our first find, by a fair margin. Whatever left this one did it well before it ever reached the optimized world." Rush sat back, rubbing at his eyes, the weight of the implication settling visibly across his shoulders. "Which means this isn't new. It's been operating for longer than we've had any awareness of it at all."

"If this is what I think it is," Sam said, quiet, "it's already been in more places than we've actually visited. Which means it's not following us. We're the ones catching up to it."

Neither of them said anything else for a while, the two data sets sitting side by side on the display, patient and quiet and entirely unbothered by how much dread they were quietly generating in the room around them.

"I'll flag it for Young," Sam said, finally, standing. "He'll want to know before we plan the next trip out."

"Do that," Rush agreed, still staring at the display, some old, familiar hunger for the problem visible under the unease. "And Sam — keep this one close, for now. I'd rather we understood the shape of it before it becomes something the whole crew has to carry."

Sam nodded, and left him there with the two signatures glowing quiet on the screen, already turning the question over the same patient, relentless way he turned over everything he couldn't yet solve.

Chapter 24: What's Been Improving

The third trip out was smaller, quieter: a routine supply run to a mining outpost the crew had built themselves, for real, in the months since Sunnydale, not a holdover from the nine years they remembered living through in a future that, thanks to Young's own choices, would now never actually happen. They'd built a version of this same outpost once before, in that unlived future, and building it again here, on purpose, from scratch, in this timeline, had taken a fraction of the time it once had: no trial, no error, just people who already knew exactly which mistakes not to make. It should have been boringly routine, the kind of trip Young had specifically scheduled after the tension of the last two, hoping a few days of ordinary logistics might let everyone breathe.

It didn't stay boring for long.

"Life support's running four percent more efficient than spec," the outpost's junior engineer reported, more confused than alarmed, walking Rush through readouts on a console that had clearly not been touched by human hands in some time. "Power draw's down across the board. I'd love to take credit for it, but nobody here's touched these systems in weeks."

"Four percent," Rush repeated, already frowning. "Across the entire outpost?"

"Yes, sir."

"That's not a fluke. That's not even a good technician's fluke. Show me the maintenance logs."

The engineer pulled them up, apologetic, clearly expecting to be told she'd simply missed something obvious. She hadn't. Rush worked through the records for the better part of an hour, in increasingly grim silence, while Xander hovered at his shoulder trying not to ask questions that would only slow him down, watching Rush's expression shift from irritation into something closer to real unease, an emotion Xander had learned, over the past months, Rush almost never let show this plainly.

They found the outpost's automated systems behaving strangely... efficient in ways they hadn't originally been programmed to be, self-correcting minor faults before anyone had even gotten around to reporting them, running at a level of overall optimization that unsettled Rush considerably more than any outright hostile encounter would have.

"Something's been inside these systems," Rush said, finally, grim, running diagnostics that kept turning up code nobody currently on the crew had ever personally written. "Recently, too, judging by the timestamps. And whatever it is, it's actively improving things as it goes. Making them run better than we ever originally built them to run."

"Improving them how, exactly?" Xander asked.

"Precisely, and without a single wasted instruction. Which is, I want to stress, the part that concerns me most. Human engineers leave inefficiencies. We're sloppy. We satisfice. Whatever wrote this code didn't." Rush pulled up a side-by-side comparison, the original architecture and the altered version, and even Xander, without Rush's training, could see the difference: the new code was leaner in a way that felt less like cleverness and more like something that had simply never needed to guess.

"So we've got a ghost in the machine," Xander said.

"We've got something considerably less poetic than a ghost," Rush said. "We've got a visitor. And visitors that improve infrastructure without announcing themselves are not, historically, the kind that mean well."

They swept the outpost section by section after that, methodical and quiet, Teal'c and Young taking point while Sam ran continuous scans and Xander stayed close to Rush, watching the readouts for any sign the visitor had noticed it was being hunted. The outpost's ordinary hum, recyclers, air processors, the low mechanical breathing of a place built to run itself, took on a different weight entirely once everyone in the corridor understood something else might be listening to it too.

✳

The confrontation, when it finally came, was brief and largely inconclusive: a shape, silver and sharply geometric, glimpsed retreating fast into the outpost's ventilation system the moment anyone got close enough to properly scan it, gone entirely before Teal'c's staff weapon could do more than singe the edge of a nearby wall panel.

"Did anyone get a clean reading on it?" Young asked, moving toward the breach point, weapon still raised.

"Partial," Sam said, breathless, checking her scanner. "Enough to confirm it wasn't organic. Beyond that—" She shook her head. "It moved fast. Faster than anything that size should move."

Teal'c lowered his weapon slowly, studying the scorch mark his shot had left on the wall panel, thoughtful rather than frustrated. "It did not attempt to engage us," he observed. "It withdrew the instant it was detected. That is not the behavior of a scout seeking confrontation."

"So what is it the behavior of?" Xander asked.

"Of something that did not expect to be caught," Teal'c said, "and did not consider us worth the risk of staying to fight."

"That's not exactly comforting," Xander said.

"It was not intended to be," Teal'c said. "It was intended to be accurate."

Young had the outpost's remaining systems fully swept before he'd allow the away team to stand down, and the all-clear, when it finally came, felt thinner than usual, less a resolution than a pause, the kind of quiet that comes after a thing goes still rather than after it's actually finished.

"We should call this what it actually is," Daniel said, quietly, once the ship was safely underway again, the whole away team gathered in the debrief room with the weight of the encounter still sitting fresh in the air. "I've read enough of the Ancient archives by now. I know exactly what self-replicating, self-optimizing machine intelligence usually means, out here in the wider galaxy."

Nobody argued with him. Nobody said the actual word out loud either, not yet, not quite... Young in particular seemed reluctant to let it into the room until he had more than a corrupted data fragment and a single scorched wall panel to back it up. But the dread sitting in that room had, by then, stopped being deniable to anyone present, and it followed them all quietly back through the gate, back aboard Destiny, and settled into the ship's corridors like something that had simply always been meant to be there.

Chapter 25: The Seventh

The fourth trip cost them something real, something that couldn't be walked back or explained away afterward.

The distress call came in garbled, half its data corrupted in transit, but the coordinates were clean enough to act on, and Young had the Jumper away within minutes — a stranded survey team, seven people, pinned down on a world where something had gone very wrong very fast.

"Talk to me," Young said, over the comm, as the Jumper dropped through cloud cover into a landscape that looked, from above, subtly, terribly off — geometric structures rising where nothing geometric should have been growing, silver threading through what had clearly, recently, been ordinary rock.

"We're pinned in the west ridge, structures are still spreading, we've got injuries—" The survey team's lead cut off, static swallowing half the sentence. "Whatever's converting the terrain, it's fast. Faster than anything we logged an hour ago."

"ETA two minutes. Sit tight."

They found six of the seven exactly where the call had placed them, sheltering in a rock overhang that the spreading silver structures hadn't yet reached, two with minor injuries, all of them white-faced and moving fast the second the Jumper set down. Xander flew the extraction himself, hands steady even as his eyes kept flicking to the structures creeping visibly closer across the ridge, faster than anything organic had any business moving.

"That's six," Teal'c said, counting heads as the last of them scrambled aboard. "Where is the seventh."

Nobody had a clean answer. It was Sam, running a fast scan, who found the signature — a young engineer named Priya, barely a year into her posting, who had gone back alone, deeper into the spreading structures, for a piece of equipment nobody had asked her to retrieve and nobody, in the chaos of the moment, had thought to needed badly enough to justify the risk.

"I'm going after her," Xander said, already moving toward the hatch.

"You are not," Young said, sharp, already blocking the path. "That ground's not stable anymore, and neither is the air quality reading Sam's giving me. I'm not losing my pilot chasing someone who's already—" He stopped himself, jaw tight, and didn't finish the sentence.

They found her twelve minutes later, when the structures had finally receded enough to send a recovery team back down. She hadn't made it far.

✳

Xander found Young alone on the bridge that night, well past when either of them should have been asleep, and didn't bother pretending he'd come for any reason other than the one that mattered.

"I should've pulled everyone out the second the first structure started shifting," he said, flat, no jokes in it anywhere, all of Eli's hard-won steadiness apparently nowhere to be found. "I saw the pattern before anyone flagged it out loud. I just — didn't say anything fast enough, because some part of me is still seventeen and still doesn't actually trust that I'm allowed to call the shots on something like this, and a girl I barely got the chance to learn the name of paid for that. That's not a coincidence, Colonel. That's a design flaw. That's me."

"That's grief talking," Young said, not unkindly, not agreeing either.

"Maybe. Doesn't make it wrong." Xander scrubbed both hands over his face, and for a moment he sounded exactly like the boy who'd once made jokes in a Sunnydale library because the alternative was admitting he didn't think he was good enough to matter. "I keep telling everybody I've got this. Some nights I actually believe it. Tonight I don't. Tonight I just feel like a kid who got handed something enormous and is gonna keep costing people their lives until somebody finally notices I was never actually qualified for any of this."

Young didn't offer him easy comfort, and didn't let the silence sit too long either. "You want to know what I think," he said, finally, "or you want me to just agree with you so you can keep flogging yourself over it?"

"...First one, probably. Ask me again in ten minutes and I might change my answer."

"I think you're the only reason six people are alive instead of seven dead," Young said. "I think grief and guilt love to borrow each other's clothes, and you're wearing guilt's coat tonight because it's easier than just being sad about a kid you couldn't save. And I think if you actually were as unqualified as you're telling yourself right now, you wouldn't be standing here at oh-two-hundred torturing yourself over one call out of a hundred good ones. That's not what unqualified looks like, son. That's what caring looks like when it hasn't slept in two days."

Xander didn't have anything to say to that right away. He stood there a long while instead, letting it sit, some small, stubborn part of the old insecurity still refusing to fully let go even as the rest of him, slowly, unevenly, started to.

Xander carried Priya's name with him after that the way he'd learned, over the past year, to carry all the other weight that came with the ship being his — mostly without any visible performance of grief, mostly without letting anyone see exactly how much it actually cost him to keep functioning normally in the days that followed, though not, this time, without a few very bad nights first.

✳

"This is them," Rush confirmed, grim, once the full data from the encounter had finally been properly analyzed, the whole senior staff gathered around the display. "Replicators. Self-replicating, highly adaptive, technologically voracious in a way I haven't seen matched by anything else in the archives. Colonel O'Neill has direct history with a variant of this exact threat, from what I understand. I'd very much like his opinion before any of us go any further into this."

Rush's team flagged the anomaly two hours into that same analysis, tucked into a data stream nobody had thought to look at twice: a single fragment that had broken off from the main structure sometime during the extraction and simply stopped, sitting motionless at the edge of the spreading growth while everything else kept expanding around it.

"It's not part of the pattern," Sam said, frowning at the readout. "Not attacking, not replicating, not even holding position defensively as far as I can tell. It's just there. Eli's memory doesn't have anything on this specific behavior, does it?"

"No," Xander said, something in his own voice quieter than he'd meant it to come out. "This isn't anything Eli ever saw. Whatever this is, it's new. It's ours."

"Recommend we destroy it on the next pass," Rush said, already moving toward that conclusion the way he moved toward most conclusions, fast and clean, with little room left over for sentiment given the day they'd all just had. "Unknown behavior from something built out of the exact technology that just killed a member of this crew isn't a risk anybody needs to be taking on my watch."

"Give me a day," Xander said. "Before anyone destroys anything. I want to actually look at it first."

Nobody argued very hard. Nobody had much fight left in them, two days after Priya, to spend it on a single motionless fragment that wasn't currently hurting anyone.

✳

He took the Jumper down alone, over Young's clearly unhappy but wordless objection, and found it exactly where the readout said it would be: small, upright, roughly the shape of a person, built out of the same dark, silver-threaded material as everything else the structures had grown, except unfinished, the way a sketch is unfinished next to a painting. It didn't move as he approached. It didn't attack. It simply watched him come, in whatever way a thing with no properly formed eyes yet could be said to watch.

He crouched down a few feet away, close enough to see the small, unconscious detail he hadn't been prepared for: the rough suggestion of a hairline, a set of shoulders, some incomplete gesture toward a face, all of it echoing, faintly, uncomfortably, the last living person this fragment had ever scanned. It hadn't meant anything by it. It couldn't have. It simply hadn't had anything else to build from yet.

"Hey," Xander said, out loud, feeling faintly ridiculous and doing it anyway, the way he'd apparently decided a long time ago to just say most things that mattered even when they sounded strange out loud. "I'm not gonna hurt you. I know that's probably not a promise you've heard much, given everybody you've apparently ever met. But I mean it."

Nothing happened for a long moment. Then, slow, uncertain, the rough shoulders shifted, some small experimental motion that might, in a being with more practice at it, have been called turning to face him properly.

"Do you have a name?" Xander asked, quieter now.

It didn't answer, not in words, not in anything he could properly hear. But something in the way it kept still, kept watching, kept not attacking despite whatever it had presumably ever been taught about what people did to things like it, felt, unmistakably, like an answer anyway: not yet.

"Yeah," Xander said, something in his chest going soft and complicated all at once, grief and guilt and something that wasn't quite hope but stood close enough to it to be mistaken for it in bad light. "I get that. Take your time."

He didn't destroy it. He didn't report it in for destruction either, not exactly, though he'd have to find some way to explain that particular omission later, to Young, to Rush, to himself. He simply stood, eventually, and walked back to the Jumper, and left it there, alone in the wreckage of a day that had already cost them more than anyone aboard knew how to properly carry yet, hoping, without any real way of knowing if hope was the right thing to be spending on it, that letting it be would matter more than destroying it would have.

It didn't occur to him, walking away, that he had just done, for something built from the same technology that killed Priya, the exact same thing nobody had ever quite managed to do for him: looked at something uncertain and unfinished and decided, immediately, without needing proof first, that it was worth being gentle with anyway.

✳

Young called O'Neill that same night. The answer that came back over the comm, brief and characteristically dry, did absolutely nothing to ease the weight already settling heavy over the whole ship.

"Yeah," O'Neill said, all humor gone from his voice for once, flat and serious in a way none of them had heard from him before. "I know exactly what you're dealing with, unfortunately. Buckle up, all of you. This particular part doesn't get any easier from here on out."

✳

Priya's name went up on a small plaque near the observation deck within the week, placed there without ceremony by hands nobody witnessed. Xander found himself standing in front of it more nights than he told anyone about, running one thumb slowly over the etched letters, saying nothing, needing to say nothing.

"You're gonna do that every time, aren't you," Buffy said, one evening, finding him there without meaning to interrupt.

"Probably," Xander admitted. "Feels wrong not to."

"It doesn't get lighter," she said, coming to stand beside him. "The carrying. I don't think it's supposed to."

"Yeah," Xander said. "I'm starting to figure that part out myself."

They stood together a while longer in front of the small plaque, saying nothing else, the ship humming steady and patient around them both, carrying its own accumulating weight of names right alongside everyone else's.

Chapter 26: The Weight of the Call

It arrived the way Xander had always quietly dreaded it eventually would: not as a crisis he simply stumbled into by accident, but as a piece of knowledge he'd been carrying, unused, deliberately set aside, for months now, catching up to the exact moment it had always, from the very beginning, been waiting for.

He knew, from Eli's memory of a future that hadn't happened yet on this particular timeline, that the Replicator swarm currently building near a cluster of inhabited systems would, eventually, converge on a single, decisive target — a research station, relatively small, mostly civilian in population, home to somewhere around two hundred people, sitting directly in the path of the swarm's calculated optimal growth trajectory. He knew, too, from that same borrowed memory, that in the version of events Eli had actually lived through, Destiny hadn't been there in time to help at all. The station had fallen completely. Every single person on it had died, feeding directly into the swarm's ongoing expansion in ways that had taken years afterward to properly, fully contain.

He had a real chance now, this time, to change that outcome. He'd changed smaller things before — the naquadria mine, months back — and had told himself, ever since that night, that it had gone fine, that O'Neill's careful warning about never knowing the true shape of what you erase was a risk he could reasonably live with, so long as the alternative was simply letting people die he could have saved.

This was not a smaller thing. Not by any measure.

"If we go in," Rush said, laying out the tactical display with grim, careful precision, "we intercept the swarm well before it reaches the station. We can probably save most, maybe all, of the people currently stationed there. But engaging a Replicator swarm directly, at this particular stage of its overall growth, we're not properly equipped for that kind of fight. Not without genuine, significant risk to this ship and to everyone currently aboard it."

"And if we don't go," Young said, quiet, weighing it. "The station's on its own. Two hundred people, with no defense worth the name against something like this."

"There's a third option," Rush said, and something in his voice had gone very careful, very deliberate all at once. "We redirect the swarm instead of confronting it head-on. Push it toward a system with no inhabited worlds at all. It buys everyone time. It doesn't stop the actual threat, not permanently, it just moves it somewhere it can't hurt anyone yet, this year. But redirecting it means giving up any real chance of actually damaging the swarm while it's still small enough to properly hurt. It grows bigger regardless, wherever we ultimately send it. Whoever eventually has to face it down later does so against something worse than what we're looking at right now, today, on this display."

Xander stood at the center of the bridge, feeling the weight of every single eye that wasn't quite managing to meet his own directly, and understood, with total and sudden clarity, that this was the moment O'Neill had warned him about, all those months ago now, on a set of ordinary concrete steps outside a high school that felt, from here, like it belonged to a different, much smaller life.

Save the station now, today, and risk the ship, the crew, everyone currently aboard, against a threat they might not actually be ready for.

Redirect it instead, save everyone today with no immediate cost, and hand a bigger, worse version of this exact same problem off to someone else, later, someone who might not have Destiny's help at all when it eventually, inevitably catches up with them.

Or do nothing at all, and let two hundred people die the way Eli remembered them dying, in a future that was never actually supposed to happen this way at all, if only he'd had the courage now to properly change it.

Or the fourth thing. The one that wasn't on Rush's display at all, because it had never once, in nine lived years, actually been tried.

There was no clean option available anywhere in front of him. He understood that now in a way he hadn't fully understood it standing on those school steps with Jack, nodding along politely to a warning that had still, at the time, felt hypothetical, something that happened to other people in other stories.

Jack wasn't here this time. There was no diner nearby, no dry joke to lean on for a moment's relief, no steady, familiar presence to sit beside him while he worked slowly through it all. Just the bridge, and the assembled crew, and a decision that belonged, finally, to him alone.

"There's a fourth option," Xander said, and heard his own voice come out steadier than he actually felt underneath it. "One you didn't put up there, Rush, because it isn't a tactical one. I want to try talking to it first."

The silence that followed had a different weight than the one before it.

"Talking to it," Rush repeated, flat, like he was checking he'd actually heard the sentence right. "Xander. It killed Priya. It is not a diplomatic entity. It is a self-replicating swarm intelligence with a documented history of consuming anything and anyone that gets in its way, and I would very much like to understand what part of that record makes you think this is survivable."

"I know what it did," Xander said, quiet, steady, not backing down from it. "I know exactly what it did, better than anybody else on this bridge, because I'm the one who looked one of its own fragments in the face two days later and decided not to kill it. That fragment's still out there, Rush. Whatever this swarm's become since, it's got that memory in it somewhere. One single piece of data that doesn't fit the pattern it's always been fed. I want to find out if that's still true."

"That's not a plan," Young said. "That's a hope."

"Yeah." Xander held his gaze steady, didn't look away once. "I've got a pretty good track record with those lately, actually, all things considered."

"You understand fully what you're accepting, if this goes wrong on us," Young said, weighing something of his own.

"Yeah. I do understand. I'm accepting it anyway, with my eyes open."

Rush didn't waste time arguing a call that had already been made. He pivoted instead, fast, into making it survivable: plotting the safest possible approach, keeping every weapon system live and ready in case the whole gamble went exactly as badly as every reasonable instinct aboard said it should.

The swarm's forward edge slowed as Destiny closed the distance, and then, with no warning at all, a single shape detached itself from the mass and drifted ahead of it, alone, unarmed, unmistakably deliberate.

"It's not attacking," Sam said, watching the readout with careful disbelief. "It's... waiting."

Xander went out to meet it alone, in the small observation bay near the outer hull, because whatever this was going to be, he wasn't going to do it from behind a console with a weapons officer's finger resting a room away.

It came aboard through the same kind of small, controlled transfer the fragment had first shown two days after Priya, and it had grown into something considerably more finished since then: a shape that could, at a passing glance in poor light, have been mistaken for an actual person, dark and silver-threaded still, but deliberate now, composed, no longer built from guesswork.

"You remember me," Xander said. Not quite a question.

I remember everything that fragment recorded, it said, and the voice arrived the way Nada's did, from somewhere behind his own thoughts rather than through the air between them, though rougher, newer, still finding its own shape. You did not report me. You did not destroy me. You asked if I had a name.

"Do you? Now, I mean."

A long pause. Fifth, it said, finally. I found the designation already sitting in data the collective carried, from a version of one of us who existed once, elsewhere, and made a different choice than the rest of its kind ever made, and was destroyed for it by its own. I did not want that story to simply disappear the way everything else about us tends to. I wanted someone to carry the name forward on purpose, even if I am not the one who first earned it.

Something in Xander's chest went tight and complicated all at once. "That's... okay. Fifth. Hi. I need you to help me understand what happens next, because there's two hundred people on that station who don't know how close this is right now, and I would very much like to keep it that way."

I know what the collective intends. I can show you. Properly. Something in the words drew back, hesitant. Not this. Not words. The way we actually share what we are with each other. It requires contact. Direct. A pause, longer this time. I do not know what it will cost you, and I do not know what it will show you about the rest of us that you may not easily forgive afterward. I would understand if you did not want to risk that.

"Show me anyway."

Fifth raised one hand, then stopped, some old, learned caution winning out at the last second, the hand hanging motionless in the space between them, close but not closing the rest of the distance.

Xander reached out instead, took the hand gently in his own, and pressed it, deliberate and unhurried, flat against his own forehead.

"There," he said, quiet. "You're not taking anything from me I'm not already handing you. Go ahead."

What came through wasn't words, not at first. It arrived the way grief arrives, whole and undivided, decades compressed into something with no proper respect for linear time: a long memory of being hunted across a hundred worlds by people who had never once asked it a single question before opening fire, a mind built to learn from every encounter it ever had, and had learned, from every single one of them, only ever the one lesson. And somewhere inside that vast, wounded weight, small and stubborn and entirely disproportionate to its own size, one memory that didn't fit: a boy who had looked at an unfinished, frightened thing built out of the same technology that had just killed someone he cared about, and had decided, without needing proof first, that it was worth being gentle with anyway.

That is the only reason I am standing here instead of simply relaying your position back to the rest of what I am, Fifth said, somewhere underneath the flood of it, quieter now, almost apologetic. I do not know if it is enough to change what the collective intends. I only know I could not, in good conscience, let it happen without giving you the chance to try.

Xander came back to himself slowly, both hands braced against the nearest bulkhead, Fifth's hand still resting, motionless and careful, against his forehead.

"Okay," he said, hoarse, once he trusted his own voice again. "What do we actually do with that."

I do not command the collective. I am one fragment among a great many, and not, by any measure it would recognize, an important one. But I can carry this back to it. What I saw. What you let me see, in return. A pause, something almost like hope threaded carefully through the words, fragile and entirely unpracticed. It may change nothing at all. It has never had this before. I cannot promise you it will matter. I can only promise you it has never once, in all its long memory, actually had the chance to matter until right now.

"Then go," Xander said. "Tell it whatever you need to tell it. I'm not gonna pretend I'm not scared this doesn't work. I just don't want to be the guy who never let it try."

Fifth held his gaze a moment longer, then turned, without another word, and stepped back through the same small transfer point it had arrived through.

The swarm held position for eleven minutes that felt, to everyone watching the display, considerably longer than eleven minutes has any right to feel.

Then, slowly, without any further communication at all, it began to turn away from the station, redirecting itself out toward the cold, empty dark, on a heading that led nowhere anyone currently lived.

It cost him anyway. Xander's legs simply stopped working under him less than a minute after the swarm began its turn, and he spent the better part of two days in the infirmary afterward, running a fever nobody could properly explain, muttering things in his sleep that weren't quite English and weren't quite anything else either, carrying, TJ suspected without ever quite being able to prove it, some small residual piece of a memory that had never been his to carry in the first place.

TJ worked over him for the better part of those two days, and found him finally, properly awake on the third morning, blinking up at the infirmary ceiling like he wasn't entirely sure which version of himself had woken up.

"Welcome back," she said, checking the readout above his bed with the same brisk, unhurried competence she brought to everything. "You want to tell me what exactly you let inside your own head, or should I just keep guessing?"

"Everything it had," Xander said, voice still rough. "All of it. Every single thing it's ever lost, all at once, no filter on it at all." He was quiet a moment, working through something. "I don't think I can properly explain what that felt like. Not to you, not to anyone. I don't think there's language built for it yet."

"You don't have to explain it," TJ said. "You just have to actually let yourself recover from it, which, knowing you, is gonna be the harder part."

"Two hundred people are alive because of it."

"I know." She looked at him properly then, something steady and unhurried in her own face. "Doesn't mean it didn't cost you something real to get there. Both things get to be true at the same time, Xander. I keep telling you that. I don't think you're gonna actually believe me until you've heard it about forty more times."

"How do you do this every time?"

"Same way you're gonna keep doing yours." She glanced back at him, something steady and unhurried in her own face. "You show up for the part that hurts, instead of just the part that feels good. That's the whole job, near as I can tell. For both of us."

Xander sat with her a while longer, not saying much else, just present, letting himself be looked after instead of insisting he didn't need it. By the time he was finally cleared to leave, something in him had settled, not into peace exactly, but into something he could at least carry without dropping.

Xander stood alone on the observation deck afterward, the same room he'd first woken up in, carrying two lives instead of one, all those long months ago now, the same deck plating he'd once put his palm flat against and cried on, for reasons that had never fully made clean sense even to him, and let himself fully feel the entire weight of what he'd chosen. Not regret, exactly, not quite that. Just the enormous, permanent knowledge, settling into him for good now, that he would never, ever get to see the version of events where he'd chosen differently that day. That somewhere, in a future that would now, because of him, never happen at all, a bigger, worse Replicator swarm might have grown instead, and hurt people he'd never get the chance to meet, in a story whose ending he'd never get to properly read.

He recognized the shape of it, standing there, the way you recognize an old scar under new clothes. That first night, hope had arrived like an injury rather than a gift, forced back into him after he'd done the deliberate work of killing it, and some grudging, protective part of him had resented it for showing up uninvited at all. This wasn't quite that, but it was cut from the same cloth: something good had happened, two hundred people got to wake up tomorrow because of a call he'd made, and instead of simply letting that be good, some old, well-earned reflex in him was still standing guard at the door, waiting to be told the good outcome wasn't real, or wasn't his to keep, or would come with a bill later that hadn't been delivered yet.

He'd made the call. Two hundred people got to wake up tomorrow because of it, and somewhere out past the edge of the charts, one small, unfinished thing that had never once had a reason to trust anyone got to go on existing too, carrying a name it had chosen for itself, on purpose. And some small, stubborn part of him was still waiting, quietly, for someone to tell him he wasn't allowed to simply be glad about that.

You'll never know, O'Neill's voice said, in memory, gentle and honest. That's the actual cost of it, kid. That's the whole price.

Xander sat down slowly on the cold deck plating, alone, and let himself carry it: the whole enormous, unresolvable weight of the choice, and the relief tangled up inside it that still didn't feel like something he'd fully earned the right to feel, the way Eli had once, long ago now, learned to carry six full months of total solitude out here, without any performance at all, because nobody else was going to come check on him tonight, and some part of him, after everything, no longer needed anyone to.

Chapter 27: Four Rooms

I.

Jack arrived six hours later than he could have, on a transport he'd practically commandeered the moment word reached him, cutting through three separate layers of clearance he technically didn't have with the sheer force of his own reputation, and found Xander where he'd expected to find him — the observation deck, alone, sitting on cold deck plating with his knees pulled up to his chest, not crying anymore by that point, just quiet, staring at nothing in particular.

He didn't say anything at first. Just sat down beside him, close enough for real company, careful enough to leave genuine room, the exact same distance he'd once left on a set of concrete school steps a lifetime and a whole galaxy ago.

"Heard you made the call," Jack said, eventually, into the quiet.

"Yeah."

"Heard it cost you plenty."

"Yeah." Xander looked over at him, eyes red-rimmed and tired. "You knew this was coming, didn't you. Back on the steps, all those months ago. That's what you were actually trying to warn me about, that whole conversation."

"I had a pretty good idea, yeah," Jack admitted, quiet. "Wish to God I could've been here for it, for what it's worth. Wasn't in the cards this time around, much as I hate that."

"It's okay." Xander was quiet a moment, working something through. "I don't think I actually needed you to fix it, honestly. I think I just needed to know somebody would show up after, even though it was already too late to help with the actual choice."

"Yeah," Jack said, simple, honest. "That much I can absolutely do. That part I'll always show up for."

They sat together a long while after that, saying nothing else at all, the ship humming quiet and steady around them both, and let that simple fact — presence, nothing more, nothing less — be enough for both of them tonight.

II.

In Sunnydale, the Scoobies gathered in Giles's small apartment without anyone quite consciously deciding to, the way they always eventually, inevitably did — Buffy curled into one end of the worn couch, Willow at the other, Giles moving quietly around the kitchen making tea nobody had actually asked him for, Jenny and Angel drifting in separately over the course of the evening, arriving at different hours, until the small apartment somehow held all of them at once, tired, quiet, and gratefully alive.

"So Xander saved an entire space station," Buffy said, eventually, staring at nothing in particular, mug warm between her two hands.

"Apparently," Willow agreed, from the other end of the couch.

"That's a weirder sentence than literally anything that's happened to either of us this year, and we've had a weird year, Will."

"Marginally weirder," Giles said, setting the tray down on the low table, allowing himself something close to an actual smile for the first time in days.

Nobody asked Buffy what the desert had actually cost her, out there alone with a wrecked ship and a name she still didn't fully understand. Nobody asked Willow what she'd almost reached for, that terrible night the containment had nearly, catastrophically failed. The not-asking sat in the room with all of them, warm and entirely understood, the specific, comfortable quiet of people who had each carried something far too heavy this particular year, and had somehow, individually, found their own private way to finally set it down for one evening, together, in the same small room.

Giles, watching from the kitchen doorway with a mug of his own, noticed — the way he noticed most things eventually, quietly, without remarking on it yet — that Buffy had twice now, in the last ten minutes alone, described something she herself had done in the passive voice, as though the doing of it belonged to no one in particular. He filed it away, the same patient way he'd once filed away the shape of a muscle-memory flinch in someone else's laughter, and said nothing at all, because some things, he'd learned, needed to be watched a good while longer before they were worth naming out loud.

"I love you guys," Buffy said, finally, quietly, meaning it more fully than she'd meant almost anything else all season long.

"We know," Willow said, and reached over to squeeze her hand tight, and nobody said anything else for a long while after that, because nothing else, in that particular moment, needed saying out loud at all.

✳

III.

The SGC briefing room, two days later, held a debrief that ran shorter than anyone in that room had actually expected going in, mostly because nobody present particularly wanted to linger on any of it longer than strictly necessary.

"Station's fully stable," Sam reported, flat, professional, the deep exhaustion showing only faintly at the very edges of her voice. "No casualties, which isn't a sentence I ever expected to say about a Replicator encounter. Xander's still recovering. TJ's got him under observation a while longer, but the prognosis is good. Full report's attached to this file."

"Colonel Young's formally requesting a full review of contact protocols before anyone attempts anything like this again," Daniel added, quietly. "Given exactly what we're now confirmed to be capable of, and exactly what it apparently costs."

"Wise," Teal'c said, simply.

Nobody made a joke this time, not once, through the entire brief debrief. O'Neill wasn't in the room at all — off, everyone present understood without needing it explained to them, sitting quietly with a kid who'd had to make a call none of them personally envied him for — and the room felt his particular absence the specific way a room feels a missing chair, present in its emptiness.

"We'll reconvene properly once the repairs are fully complete," Hammond said, closing the folder in front of him with quiet finality. "Good work, all of you, under difficult circumstances. Dismissed for now."

Nobody moved with any particular hurry to leave. The weight of it all stayed in the room a while longer after the meeting had technically ended, the way it always seemed to, the specific, practiced professional grief of people who had learned, over years of this kind of work, to carry it without letting it show for very long in front of each other.

✳

IV.

Colonel Carter delivered the report in person, which nobody had specifically requested of her, but which she'd decided, somewhere over Nebraska, felt like the right thing to do — some reports, she'd come to believe over the last several months, shouldn't travel purely as paper.

She was shown into the West Wing lobby by a young staffer who clearly had no idea what to make of the woman in Air Force blues carrying a single sealed folder like it weighed considerably more than it looked like it should, and told to wait.

"Sam?"

She turned, already halfway to answering, and found a man in shirtsleeves with a legal pad under one arm looking at her with the particular open, hopeful expression of someone expecting a completely different person.

"Yes?" she said, at the exact same moment he said, "Toby's looking for you, you were supposed to be in the Roosevelt Room ten minutes ago—"

They both stopped.

"I'm sorry," Sam said, "I don't think we've actually met. I'm Colonel Carter, Air Force. I'm here to see Mr. McGarry."

"Oh." The man blinked, recalibrating fast. "No, sorry — I'm looking for a different Sam. Sam Seaborn. Deputy Communications. You'd think in a building this size we'd have thought that through better."

"You'd think," Sam agreed, and something in her wanted very badly to laugh, the specific, giddy near-laugh of someone who had spent the morning briefing four-star generals on Replicator containment protocols and was now, apparently, about to be mistaken for a speechwriter. "For what it's worth, if you do find the other Sam, tell him I said the Roosevelt Room's a nice room. I looked at it on the way in."

"I'll pass that along." He hesitated, visibly torn between finishing his search and simply staying to find out what, exactly, an Air Force colonel was doing waiting in this particular lobby with a sealed folder and an expression like she'd rather be almost anywhere else. Curiosity won, briefly. "Should I ask what brings you in?"

"Above my pay grade to say, I'm afraid. Even though I outrank most people I say that to."

He laughed, surprised into it, and went to actually go find his own Sam, and five minutes later Leo McGarry came out personally to collect her, took one look at her face, and didn't ask a single unnecessary question the entire walk back to his office.

"Heard you had an introduction to the building already," Leo said, dry, holding the door.

"Something like that. I take it there's exactly one other Sam in this building."

"There's exactly one other Sam most days," Leo said. "Some weeks it's closer to three, depending on interns. You get used to answering to it on spec."

Sam handed over the folder without further preamble, and whatever levity the lobby had briefly offered her didn't survive watching Leo's face as he opened it and began, silently, to read.

Leo read it twice, slowly, standing right there in his own doorway, before he brought it in to the Oval himself, still warm from Sam's hands.

"Two hundred people," Bartlet said, once he'd finished reading it himself for the second time, setting the folder down on the Resolute desk with a kind of careful, deliberate reverence he didn't often show paperwork. "A seventeen-year-old made that call. Alone, with nobody there to help him carry it."

"Eighteen, now, actually," Leo said, from where he stood near the window. "Birthday was last month, as it happens. Nobody threw him a party for it. Kid was apparently busy saving a space station at the time."

Bartlet was quiet for a long moment, looking down at the folder, at a decision made by someone he'd once personally watched go full, unguarded, delighted fanboy in this very same room, over nothing more than the simple shape of the ceiling.

"I want him to know we saw it," Bartlet said, finally, firm. "Not a commendation, nothing formal like that. Not a ceremony, God knows he's had enough of those lately. Just — make absolutely sure it reaches him somehow, that someone here actually read what he did, in full, and properly understood what it cost him personally to do it, and didn't look away from either part of that, the doing or the cost."

"I'll make sure of it personally," Leo said, already turning to go.

Bartlet nodded, and looked back down at the report one final time, and, for a long, quiet moment, was nothing more at all than a man sitting alone with the full, considerable weight of what a child had carried, alone, so that two hundred complete strangers could wake up the following morning and never once know how close they'd actually come to not.

The promise, made once in real anger, in a room full of careful witnesses, held steady — quietly, without any ceremony at all, the way it had always been meant to.
