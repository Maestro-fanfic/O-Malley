Chapter 1: The Costume

The first thing Xander noticed about Ethan's Costume Shop was that everything cost more than it looked like it should.

The second was that the place smelled like somebody had locked a theater wardrobe in a basement for twenty years and opened the door that morning.

The third was Buffy.

She stood in front of a long dress the color of old champagne, one hand resting on the sleeve as if it might bolt if she moved too quickly. Lace climbed the bodice. The skirt had enough fabric to hide a marching band.

Xander looked from the dress to the price tag, then back to Buffy.

“You're going to need a wider front door.”

“Isn't it beautiful?”

“That's one word. ‘Load-bearing’ is another.”

Buffy ignored him. She had the look she got whenever Angel entered a room: half dreamy, half determined not to be caught dreaming. Xander had learned that commenting on that look was like checking whether a stove was hot with his face.

Willow hovered at Buffy's shoulder with a sealed costume bag hugged against her chest. The smiling model on the insert wore a short black skirt, a fitted top, and enough confidence to qualify as protective equipment. A folded white sheet had been clipped to the outside of the bag.

“Still going with the ghost?” Xander asked.

Willow pulled the sheet higher. “Ghosts are traditional.”

“So are eye holes.”

“I intend to have those.”

Buffy looked away from the dress long enough to give Willow an encouraging smile. “The sheet is optional.”

“The sheet is foundational.”

Xander grinned. “Willow Rosenberg: bringing structural engineering to the afterlife.”

She bumped him with one shoulder. It was gentle, but it moved him directly into a rack of plastic swords.

None of the swords survived close inspection. Neither did the pirate coat with vinyl cuffs, the rubber monster feet, or a military uniform whose manufacturer had decided that every soldier in history wore the same shade of green. Xander had twelve dollars in his pocket, eight of which were supposed to last until Friday. His mother had said there was money on the kitchen counter. There had been an empty bottle, a grocery receipt, and thirty-seven cents.

He could have worn his own clothes and called himself a civilian casualty. It had thematic weight.

Buffy disappeared toward the changing rooms with the dress. Willow followed, still defending the tactical necessity of the sheet. Xander wandered toward the rear of the store, where the lighting weakened and the costumes stopped pretending anyone had organized them.

A cardboard box sat beneath a rack of mascot heads. Someone had written CLEARANCE—INCOMPLETE across one side in black marker.

That was more his neighborhood.

He found one roller skate, a foam crown, three left gloves, and something that might once have been the tail of a dinosaur. Beneath them lay a grey hooded sweatshirt. It was soft at the cuffs and a little too large, but unlike most of the stock it felt like clothing rather than an argument with petroleum.

Folded inside it were a pair of wire-framed glasses without lenses and a black object shaped vaguely like a remote control. The remote was smooth and oval, with several shallow keys and a dark circular lens at one end. It looked too well made for a toy and too useless to be anything else.

There was also a badge.

Xander wiped dust from the plastic with his thumb.

ELI WALLACE

DESTINY EXPEDITION

The photograph showed a round-faced young man in the same hoodie, wearing an expression Xander recognized. It was the expression of someone who had just made a joke in a room where nobody else had noticed they were supposed to laugh.

No character name. No show logo. No helpful picture of a spaceship.

“Eli Wallace,” Xander read aloud. “Boldly going to fix the printer.”

The black remote warmed in his hand.

One of its keys flashed green.

Xander nearly dropped it. He pressed the key again. Nothing happened.

“Battery,” he decided.

The word sounded thin in the quiet corner.

He put on the glasses. The empty frames changed nothing, but the badge had a lanyard and the remote fit comfortably in his palm. With the hoodie, it made a costume if a person was willing to grade generously.

More importantly, it looked like the costume of a guy who had reached the spaceship without needing a uniform, muscles, mystical birthright, or a family credit card.

The man had a name badge. That meant somebody had expected him to be there.

Xander tucked the remote into the sweatshirt pocket and carried the pieces to the register.

The shopkeeper watched him approach.

He was lean, dark-haired, and English in a way that made Giles seem almost insufficiently committed. His sleeves were rolled with care. The name printed on the brass plate beside the register was ETHAN RAYNE.

Ethan took the badge between two fingers.

For the first time since Xander had entered the shop, the man's pleasant expression became entirely still.

“Where did you find this?”

Xander pointed toward the clearance box. “Archaeological dig beneath the giant chicken.”

Ethan glanced toward the back corner.

“Interesting.”

“That's usually what adults say right before something becomes more expensive.”

Ethan turned the badge over. There was no manufacturer's mark on the back, only a pattern of fine lines almost hidden beneath the cloudy laminate. His thumb passed over them once.

“Who is he?” Xander asked.

“I haven't the faintest idea.”

“Strong sales pitch.”

“Some costumes tell you what a person wished to be.” Ethan placed the badge on the counter. “Others leave room for discovery.”

He picked up the black remote. Its green light did not answer him.

“How much room are we talking?”

Ethan looked at the register. No price appeared when he entered the tag number. He tried again, more slowly.

“Three dollars.”

Xander waited for the correction. It did not come.

“For all of it?”

“Consider it a contribution to the spirit of the evening.”

That sounded suspicious. Three dollars sounded stronger.

Xander paid before either could change his mind.

Ethan put the costume in a plain paper bag. As he handed it across the counter, the bell above the front door rang once.

The door had not moved.

Ethan's gaze shifted toward it.

Xander turned, but Buffy emerged from the changing room before he could decide whether a stationary doorbell ranked above or below spontaneous combustion on the Sunnydale Weirdness Index.

The dress fit her perfectly.

That should have been impossible. The sleeves had been too long on the hanger and the waist too narrow. Now the fabric sat as though someone had measured Buffy for it, and her face had gone soft with delight.

“Well?” she asked.

Xander made himself smile. “You're going to need your own weather system, but otherwise? Excellent.”

Willow stepped out behind her, saw Xander looking, and immediately pulled the folded sheet in front of her costume.

“Found something?” she asked.

He held up the bag. “Apparently I am Eli Wallace, brave warrior of technical support.”

Buffy examined the badge. “Destiny Expedition sounds important.”

“See? Finally, a costume with upward mobility.”

“You don't know what it's from?” Willow asked.

“Probably a show that aired opposite something people watched.”

Behind the register, Ethan was still looking at the bell.

Then he smiled again.

“Whoever he was,” Buffy said, handing the badge back, “at least he looks comfortable.”

That decided it.

By sunset, Sunnydale had filled with tiny superheroes, plastic witches, cowboys, princesses, and creatures whose taxonomy had been abandoned halfway through manufacturing.

Principal Snyder divided the high-school volunteers into groups with the grim satisfaction of a man assigning penal labor.

Xander pulled the grey hood over his head and clipped the badge to its cord. The black remote—Kino remote, according to a strip of faded tape on its underside—went into his pocket.

Buffy descended the Summers staircase in the champagne dress and briefly forgot how stairs worked. Willow followed beneath the white sheet. Only her red hair showed at the bottom.

“You know,” Xander said, “if the ghost plan is total anonymity, the hair may be a clue.”

“I can still change my mind about haunting you.”

Joyce took pictures. Buffy protested, Willow tried to hide behind a child dressed as a bee, and Xander smiled on reflex when the flash went off.

In the picture, discovered much later, Buffy looked toward a life she imagined had once been simpler. Willow's sheet concealed almost everything. Xander stood between them in a secondhand hoodie, holding a machine that would not be invented for years.

Its lens was glowing.

No one noticed.

Ethan locked the shop at eleven minutes past six.

The street beyond the glass remained busy with families and costumed children. He turned the sign to CLOSED, lowered the shade, and carried the Janus bust from his office to the center of the back room.

Two faces looked in opposite directions. One young, one old. Beginning and ending sharing a single stone skull.

Ethan lit the candles.

He had performed more difficult workings. More dangerous ones, certainly. This spell required breadth rather than force. Hundreds of little transformations, each shaped by expectation, vanity, fear, and the ancient human habit of becoming someone else behind a mask.

He cut his palm and let seven drops fall.

“The world that denies thee,” he began, “thou inhabit.”

The candles bent toward the statue.

“The peace that binds thee, thou corrupt.”

That was not the next line.

Ethan stopped.

The words in the open book had changed.

Ink moved slowly across the page, forming letters in a hand older than the paper.

THE THRESHOLD THAT CALLS THEE, THOU SHALT CROSS.

The young face of Janus watched the closed door.

The old face watched Ethan.

For perhaps the first time in his adult life, Ethan Rayne considered leaving well enough alone.

Then, because he was Ethan Rayne, he smiled and read the line aloud.

The bell above the front door rang.

Every door in the shop opened at once.

Chapter 2: Earthrise

Eli Wallace had thirteen days, six hours, and an increasingly theoretical number of minutes left to solve the problem of his own survival.

The number appeared in the corner of the display because he had put it there. He had removed it twice. Both times he had restored it within an hour.

Without the countdown, it was too easy to spend half a day following an elegant diagnostic path that ended at a dead control crystal. With it, every failed idea arrived with an invoice.

Around him, Destiny slept.

The ship was never silent. Air moved through ducts built before human beings had names for the stars. Pumps circulated coolant. Relays answered one another behind walls darkened by age. Somewhere far below, the main drive maintained a vibration so deep that Eli felt it through the soles of his shoes rather than heard it.

But the people were silent.

Young, Rush, Scott, Chloe, Greer, TJ, Camile—all of them lay in stasis while Destiny crossed the emptiness between galaxies. If Eli repaired the last pod, he would join them. If he did not, the ship would wake the others years later and find whatever remained of him.

He leaned closer to the open panel.

“Come on,” he muttered. “You are a box designed to make time stop. I am trying very hard not to take that personally.”

The bypass refused to initialize.

Eli stared at it. He had reached the stage of exhaustion where anger required planning.

A small black Kino floated near his shoulder, recording the work. Its lens adjusted as he sat back.

“Attempt eighty-three,” he told it. “Still bad.”

The deck moved.

Not the familiar shift of Destiny dropping from faster-than-light travel. That came with a pressure in the bones and the sense of a sound arriving before it was made. This was sharper. The ship twisted once around an axis that did not exist.

Every light in the compartment went white.

Eli struck the deck with one knee. The Kino bounced from the wall and righted itself.

Then the drive went quiet.

The silence was so complete that Eli forgot the pain in his knee.

“No,” he whispered.

Emergency lighting should have activated. Alarms should have filled the compartment. Instead, the main lights returned one bank at a time, steady and warm. The displays around the open stasis pod filled with Ancient status reports too quickly for him to follow.

The deep vibration returned beneath his feet.

Different.

Destiny was no longer at FTL.

Eli reached the nearest console. Navigation refused to show a destination. It displayed stars, discarded the solution, displayed them again. The pattern looked familiar in a way that made no sense. The ship had crossed more galaxies than Earth could see clearly. Familiar stars were not an available option.

Another light entered the room.

Sunlight.

It lay across the deck in a bright gold bar from the open hatch, clean and strong and wrong.

Eli followed it into the corridor.

Destiny's observation deck was three levels away. He ran the first flight of stairs and nearly fell on the second. The Kino pursued him, bumping the railing at each turn.

He passed no one.

He knew he would pass no one. The knowledge still hurt.

The observation-deck doors opened before him.

Earth hung outside the glass.

There was no moment in which Eli wondered what planet it was.

He knew the blue of the oceans. He knew the broad turn of cloud over the Pacific and the brown-green edge of North America coming into daylight. He knew the white curve of the atmosphere because every photograph of home taken from space had trained him to know it before he ever understood that home could become unreachable.

The Moon stood beyond it, small and hard and exactly where the Moon belonged.

Eli stopped breathing.

His hand found the glass.

“Mom.”

The word clouded the window in front of him.

For several seconds—or several minutes; time had become unreliable—he was certain the ship had done this to him. A simulation. A neurological event. A final kindness generated by a system that had watched him fail eighty-three times and calculated the humane response.

He laughed once. It broke in the middle and became something else.

Earth remained.

There were lights along the night side. Weather over the Gulf. Sunlight on the Sierra Nevada. There was California, impossibly small beneath him. Somewhere down there his mother was—or had been, or would be, depending on which impossible answer the universe had selected.

Eli pulled himself away from the glass.

Wonder could wait. Disbelief could wait. If this was real, every person in stasis needed him to prove it.

He reached the observation-deck console and requested an astronomical fix.

Destiny returned Sol immediately.

G-class main-sequence star. Eight planets. One inhabited world. One natural satellite. The system geometry matched.

He requested an orbital solution.

The display hesitated.

Destiny was thirty-five thousand kilometers above Earth's surface. Its velocity relative to the ground was effectively zero. Its position remained fixed above the western coast of North America—but not above the equator, where a geostationary orbit could have explained it.

The ship was centered over Southern California and maintaining its position through a field Eli had never seen.

Navigation attempted to classify the field, produced a column of symbols that did not belong to any subsystem he knew, and gave up.

“Okay,” Eli said. His voice shook. “Earth first. Impossible hovering later.”

He opened passive communications.

The display filled so quickly that he recoiled.

Radio. Television. Navigation beacons. Military radar. Civilian air traffic. Satellites trading timing data. Thousands upon thousands of human voices stacked across the spectrum.

After the sparse signals of distant settlements and the endless dark between galaxies, the noise of Earth was almost physical.

Eli selected a television carrier at random.

An advertisement appeared on the screen. The resolution was terrible. A man with too much hair held up a cellular phone the size of a brick.

Eli stared.

He changed channels.

A news presenter discussed negotiations in Washington. The graphic in the corner looked like it had been designed by someone discovering computer animation in real time.

He changed again.

A local station advertised Halloween programming for that evening.

The date ran beneath it.

OCTOBER 31, 1997.

Eli read it three times.

His mother was alive.

Not merely alive. Younger. Healthy, perhaps, depending on how early the illness had begun. Eli himself was somewhere below, a child who had never solved the problem that brought him to Icarus Base, never dialed the ninth chevron, never seen Destiny.

Young was serving somewhere on Earth. Rush had not lost Gloria. Scott, Chloe, Greer, TJ—every sleeping person on the ship had another life beneath them, unfinished and unaware.

Eli's relief folded in on itself.

If he woke them all, what happened?

If he contacted Earth, what happened?

If he did nothing, what happened when the field failed or Destiny moved or somebody on the ground noticed an Ancient ship parked above California?

He looked at the countdown still hovering in the corner of his display.

Thirteen days, six hours.

That problem had just become very small.

Eli ran.

The stasis bay doors opened on rows of green indicators.

He did not go to Rush. Rush might understand the ship faster than anyone alive, but understanding was not the first requirement.

He did not go to Chloe, though the desire to see her awake and breathing and able to see Earth hurt like a hand around his ribs.

He went to pod fourteen.

Colonel Everett Young lay behind the glass, face slack with suspended time.

Eli placed both hands on the controls.

“Destiny,” he said, “begin revival.”

The ship requested confirmation.

“Commanding officer. Everett Young. Do it.”

The indicator changed from green to amber.

Warmth entered the pod. Medical systems began the slow work of returning a human body to itself.

Only then did Eli turn toward communications.

“Now,” he said to the sleeping commander, “we call home.”

Chapter 3: The Commander

Revival required twenty-seven minutes under ideal conditions.

Pod fourteen had not experienced ideal conditions in several years.

Destiny estimated thirty-four.

Eli spent the first six confirming that Young's vital signs were rising normally. He spent the next four trying to persuade the ship to open a communications search without flooding the display with every radio station on Earth.

He spent another minute looking at the date.

1997.

Every time he looked away, some part of him expected the numbers to correct themselves.

They did not.

The SGC existed. It had been operating in some form since the first Abydos mission, though Eli did not know how much of its later communications architecture existed this early. The frequencies in Destiny's expedition files came from more than a decade in the future. The encryption did not exist yet. Some of the satellites they referenced were not in orbit.

He could call NORAD.

That sentence would have sounded cooler if the situation had contained ten percent less terror.

Destiny had already identified Cheyenne Mountain through military signals and public records. Eli built a narrow transmission aimed at the complex, then hesitated over the message field.

If he announced an Ancient warship to the wrong duty officer, he could start a panic before Young opened his eyes.

If he waited, Earth might detect them first.

The ship answered that question by highlighting three ground-based systems that had begun tracking the anomalous contact above California.

“Right,” Eli said. “No pressure.”

He prepared a short identification burst but did not send it. Young needed to own the contact. That was not only military protocol. It was the difference between a terrified civilian making claims and the commanding officer of the expedition reporting an impossible emergency.

Pod fourteen chimed.

Eli was back beside it before the second tone.

The amber light turned white. Frost retreated from the glass. Young's chest rose sharply and stopped.

“Come on.”

It rose again.

The pod opened with a rush of cold vapor.

Young convulsed once, dragging air into lungs that had forgotten urgency. Eli caught his shoulder before he could pitch forward.

“Easy. Colonel, easy.”

Young's eyes opened without focus.

“Eli?”

“Yeah.”

His hand closed hard around Eli's forearm. “What happened?”

“The pod is stable. You're okay.”

“Why am I awake?”

There was no useful gradual version.

“We're at Earth.”

Young stared at him.

“Destiny dropped out, or moved, or something moved us. I checked the stars, the Moon, the atmosphere, satellite navigation, broadcasts—it's Earth.”

Young's grip tightened. “When?”

“1997.”

For the first time, Young's expression lost all command shape.

Eli helped him sit on the edge of the pod. Young's muscles trembled beneath the thin stasis garment, but his voice had already begun flattening into the tone he used when emotion threatened to interfere with the next necessary decision.

“Crew status.”

“All pods green. Nobody else awake.”

“Ship status.”

“Life support stable. Main drive available. Navigation can't explain our position. We're holding about thirty-five thousand kilometers above Southern California without orbital velocity.”

“Holding over what?”

“A town called Sunnydale.”

Young absorbed that as if Sunnydale might be the familiar part.

“Hostile contacts?”

“None in space. Earth tracking stations are starting to notice us.”

“Why did you wake me?”

The question was not accusation. Young wanted Eli's reasoning.

“Because you're the commander.”

Something moved behind Young's eyes.

He looked past Eli at the rows of pods.

“You didn't wake Rush.”

“No.”

“Good.” Young tried to stand. His knees failed immediately.

Eli caught him again. “Still thawing.”

“I noticed.”

“I can bring the display here.”

“I want to see it.”

Eli knew what he meant.

They made the journey slowly. Young leaned on him for the first corridor, then used the wall. By the second stairwell he could walk without either, though his face had gone grey with effort.

At the observation deck, the doors opened.

Young stopped.

Earth had turned while Eli worked. The western coast lay closer to night. Cities had begun illuminating the land beneath them, faint nets of gold spread along roads that both men knew.

Young approached the glass.

He did not touch it. He stood at attention without appearing to realize he had done so.

“Show me.”

Eli brought the verification onto the main display: stellar fix, lunar position, atmospheric composition, geographic match, satellite identities, time signals.

Young read everything.

“Could be an alternate Earth,” he said.

“Could be.”

“Simulation.”

“I checked internal processing. If Destiny is generating it, it's hiding the resource use from itself.”

“Time travel.”

“The date says yes. Everything I know about physics says stop saying that.”

Young almost smiled. It vanished before it arrived.

“Our younger selves?”

“Down there, probably.”

“Do not access them. Do not search for families. Nobody else wakes until we establish command authority and understand what happened.”

Eli's mother rose in his mind so vividly that he could see her at the kitchen table, pretending not to watch him eat.

“Understood.”

Young looked at him.

Eli corrected himself. “Yes, sir.”

“You said Earth is tracking us.”

Eli opened the alert. “Three systems have partial returns. None of them agree about our size. We're too high for most radar and in the wrong place for every orbit they understand.”

“Stargate Command?”

“Active. General Hammond should be in command by now.”

“Should?”

“It's 1997. My historical briefings were less detailed than I suddenly wish they were.”

Young considered the planet again.

“If George Hammond commands the SGC, we report to him.”

Relief arrived so sharply Eli nearly swayed. “I have a transmission ready.”

“Let me see it.”

The draft appeared.

CHEYENNE MOUNTAIN COMPLEX. ATTENTION STARGATE COMMAND. REQUEST SECURE CONTACT. COLONEL EVERETT YOUNG, USAF. DESTINY EXPEDITION.

Young read it twice.

“Add Hammond's name. Include my service number and current biometric file. Mark the records as future-dated and therefore non-authenticating. Append a sample of the Ancient database and our sensor solution.”

“That's going to get attention.”

“That's the idea.”

Eli made the changes.

“What if Hammond isn't there?”

“Then the officer who is there will find him.”

Young straightened. He still wore the thin clothes issued for stasis. His hands shook slightly from revival. He looked exhausted, displaced, and completely certain of the next step.

“Send it.”

Eli touched the control.

The transmission left Destiny as a narrow beam aimed at Cheyenne Mountain.

At Stargate Command, a technician listening to a dead military channel sat upright so quickly that his headset cord caught beneath his chin.

The message repeated.

He called his supervisor. His supervisor called the control room. The control room confirmed that the transmission had arrived from above California, then failed to identify the object that had sent it.

Three minutes later, General George Hammond entered the briefing room with Colonel Jack O'Neill, Captain Samantha Carter, Doctor Daniel Jackson, and Teal'c behind him.

The identification packet glowed on the screen.

O'Neill read the dates attached to Everett Young's service record.

“Either Personnel has started predicting promotions,” he said, “or Colonel Young has twelve years of service we haven't lived through yet.”

Carter stood close to a second display filled with Ancient characters.

“Sir, this isn't a decorative sample. It describes modifications to a Stargate addressing system. Parts of it are consistent with the material we've recovered, but the gate design is older than anything we've seen.”

Daniel looked from the symbols to the image assembled from ground tracking: an immense, indistinct shape hanging above the western United States.

“How old?”

“Possibly millions of years.”

Hammond read the message again.

“Can we answer?”

The technician nodded. “They've provided a return channel.”

Hammond took the microphone.

“Unknown vessel, this is Major General George Hammond of Stargate Command. Colonel Young, if you can hear me, identify your situation.”

On Destiny, Earth answered.

Chapter 4: First Contact

Young took the microphone from Eli.

For a moment, he said nothing.

Earth filled the observation window. General Hammond waited at the other end of the channel. Every impossible part of the situation pressed toward that single point of contact, demanding an explanation Young did not have.

He began with the part he did.

“General Hammond, this is Colonel Everett Young, United States Air Force. Service number transmitted with my personnel file. I am the military commander of an international expedition aboard the Ancient vessel Destiny.”

The answer came after a brief delay.

“Colonel, your service number belongs to an active-duty officer currently stationed on this planet.”

Young closed his eyes once.

“Yes, sir. Based on the date we've received, that officer is a younger version of me.”

Eli watched the signal meter. The connection was strong enough that he could hear someone in the distant briefing room stop moving.

Hammond's voice remained level. “State the date according to your records.”

Young gave it.

“And your present date?”

“October thirty-first, 1997.”

“How did you arrive here?”

“Unknown, sir. Destiny was crossing intergalactic space with its crew in stasis. Mister Wallace remained awake to repair the final stasis pod. The ship experienced an event he cannot identify and appeared in its current position.”

Hammond did not ask whether Young understood how that sounded.

“Your crew?”

“Alive. In stasis. I am the only person Mister Wallace has awakened.”

“Why you?”

Young looked at Eli.

“Because I am his commanding officer.”

The simple answer landed harder than Eli expected. He turned back to the console before Young could see it.

“What is your ship's combat status?” Hammond asked.

Young's eyes narrowed slightly. It was the correct question, and it required an answer that could not be safely broadcast on a channel they had only invented minutes ago.

“Destiny is not hostile to Earth. I will provide a full operational assessment once we establish secure communications and verify identities on both sides.”

“Understood. Are you capable of moving away from the planet?”

“Unknown. Navigation reports that we are stationary relative to a point in Southern California despite having no valid orbit. We have not attempted maneuvering.”

“Do not attempt it until the consequences are understood.”

“Yes, sir.”

At Stargate Command, Hammond covered the microphone and looked at Carter.

“Can they be where they claim?”

Carter had divided the second display into three windows. One held the Ancient data sample. One showed the orbital solution assembled from military tracking stations. The third showed a magnified image that dissolved into interference whenever the computers attempted to sharpen it.

“Something is there, sir. The tracking data is inconsistent with any natural orbit, but all three stations place it over the same coordinates.”

“Size?” O'Neill asked.

“We don't have a clean return.”

“Estimate.”

“Several hundred meters at minimum.”

O'Neill stared at the indistinct shape. “That's a generous definition of something.”

Daniel remained focused on the Ancient text.

“The language is older,” he said. “Not simply an unfamiliar dialect. Some of the forms are closer to the oldest material from the cartouche room on Abydos.”

“Could the Goa'uld fake it?” Hammond asked.

“Not easily. They might copy symbols, but this describes a coherent addressing model. Sam?”

Carter enlarged one set of equations. “If I'm reading the mathematical portions correctly, Destiny uses a different generation of Stargate. This isn't a description of how our gate works. It's part of an engineering record written by people who assumed the reader already understood the basic technology.”

Teal'c stood with his hands behind his back, studying the future service record.

“If this is a deception,” he said, “it has been prepared with knowledge beyond that possessed by Apophis.”

“Not the same thing as impossible,” O'Neill said.

“No.” Teal'c looked at the dark shape above California. “It is not.”

Hammond uncovered the microphone.

“Colonel Young, I cannot authenticate a service history containing events that have not occurred.”

“I understand, sir.”

“Captain Carter is reviewing the technical information you sent. I want a current biometric transmission and an unedited visual channel.”

“Stand by.”

Young handed the microphone back to Eli. “Can we do that?”

“Biometrics, yes. Visual—probably. Destiny's communications system doesn't think in terms of NTSC.”

“Make it think smaller.”

“That's offensive to both the ship and television, but okay.”

The joke came automatically. Eli felt Young register it, not as humor but as evidence that something familiar remained in a world that had stopped making sense.

Eli opened Young's medical file and stripped everything except the current scan. The temporal metadata caused Destiny to attach warning symbols to every line. He sent it anyway.

The visual channel took longer. He built a converter from an old Kino-recording interface and instructed the system to compress the observation-deck feed into a format Earth's receivers could understand.

The main display changed.

For the first time, they saw the room beneath Cheyenne Mountain.

General Hammond stood at the head of a long table. Eli knew the face from photographs and memorial recordings made long after the man's death. Seeing him breathe was nearly as disorienting as seeing Earth.

Jack O'Neill stood to one side, younger and darker-haired than Eli remembered. Samantha Carter wore captain's bars. Daniel Jackson had longer hair. Teal'c bore the gold emblem of Apophis on his forehead.

They were all looking back.

O'Neill leaned toward the camera.

“You two always dress like that when you call a general?”

Young looked down at the thin stasis clothing he had forgotten he was wearing.

“We were awakened unexpectedly.”

“I've had mornings like that.”

Eli heard himself say, “Not like this one.”

O'Neill's attention moved to him. The change was almost invisible, humor giving way to assessment.

“Mister Wallace?”

“Eli.”

“You sent the Ancient material?” Carter asked.

“I selected it.”

“Can you read it?”

“Most of it.”

Carter looked at Daniel. Daniel looked offended on behalf of several years of study.

“Destiny's interfaces translate through context,” Eli explained quickly. “And I've had a lot of practice.”

“How much?”

“A few years.”

“Aboard that ship?”

“Yes.”

Carter glanced at the personnel summary. “Your file says you weren't recruited through the Air Force.”

“I solved a problem in a video game.”

O'Neill's expression suggested that deception had become more plausible again.

“It was a cover,” Eli said. “The game contained a mathematical proof related to the ninth chevron. I solved it. General O'Neill came to my house.”

The younger O'Neill pointed at himself. “I did?”

“You will.”

“Do I at least call first?”

“No.”

“That tracks,” Carter murmured.

Hammond's look ended the exchange.

“Mister Wallace, if your claim is accurate, you possess extensive knowledge of future operations.”

“Yes, sir.”

“Do not transmit any more of that information over this channel.”

“Understood.”

Young stepped closer to the console. “General, I request instructions.”

Hammond studied him through the imperfect image.

Chapter 9: Necessary Awakenings

Whatever had carried Destiny to Earth had acted on the ship as a whole. No occupant showed radiation injury, cellular disruption, or interrupted suspension.

That was the good news.

The bad news occupied every other system.

Destiny had arrived with the damage it already carried. Power remained sufficient for stasis and life support, but large sections of the ship were inaccessible. Several repair processes had stopped during the transit. The navigation system continued insisting that the vessel occupied a position forbidden by ordinary mechanics.

Chapter 18: The Ledgers

Chapter 20: The Exception

The Council had indexed Slayers by date, region, Watcher, and manner of death.

It had not indexed them by success.

It tracked how efficiently a Slayer maintained her assigned territory. It recorded every time she questioned the Council.

“I'm not him.”

“No.” Young turned to him. “You're the reason he had a door.”

Xander looked through Eli's window as himself.

Janus opened the door. Eli crossed the threshold. Everyone else chose what the new beginning would become.
