Chapter 17 of 59

The Afternoon Raid

At two minutes past three, every telephone in the Watchers Council's London headquarters lost its line.

At three minutes past three, the building's wards announced the presence of hostile intent.

At four minutes past three, Sir Basil Covington entered through the front door carrying the Queen's suspension order in one hand and his reclaimed umbrella in the other.

The receptionist rose.

“Sir, you cannot—”

“I assure you, I can.”

Behind him came officers of the Metropolitan Police, a Crown solicitor, two forensic-accounting teams, three archivists cleared for extraordinary material, and four men and women the Council's wards could not agree how to classify.

The last group concerned the receptionist most.

Basil placed the suspension order on her desk.

“No one leaves. No document, artifact, recording, or communication is to be removed, altered, destroyed, transferred, summoned, banished, or rendered illegible.”

She stared at him.

“The wording has been reviewed,” he added.

A bell began ringing deeper in the building.

Every member of Basil's team checked a synchronized watch.

Across London, simultaneous teams entered two Council residences, a private training facility, a warehouse listed as storing theological manuscripts, and a country property held through six layers of charitable trusts.

In Oxford, a Council archivist reached for a brass lever beneath his desk and found a police officer's hand already covering it.

In Bath, a Watcher attempted to burn three files. The flame froze blue above the paper until a Crown specialist closed her fist.

At the training residence outside London, seven girls between the ages of nine and sixteen were told to assemble for inspection. The eldest placed herself between the younger children and the strangers.

The child-protection officer lowered herself until they were eye to eye.

“We are not taking you to the Council,” she said. “We are here because no one outside it has ever asked whether you are safe.”

The girl did not move.

“May I tell you where we're going before you decide whether to believe me?”

After a long moment, the girl nodded.

✳

Quentin Travers received Basil in the Council chamber.

The room had been designed to make visitors feel temporary. Portraits of dead Watchers covered dark wood walls. A circular table occupied the center beneath a domed ceiling painted with constellations as they had appeared over Britain three hundred years earlier.

Travers stood at his place without offering the chair opposite.

“Sir Basil. This is highly irregular.”

“It ceased being irregular several centuries ago. That appears to be the difficulty.”

“The Council's charter protects its archives from civil intrusion.”

Basil gave him the signed order.

Travers read the first page and looked up.

“Her Majesty has been misinformed.”

“That possibility is among the reasons she wishes to inspect your records.”

“Our work cannot be exposed to ordinary police.”

“The ordinary police are securing the ordinary crimes. Specialists will handle the extraordinary ones.”

“You do not understand what is contained here.”

“Then you have done a remarkably poor job of fulfilling your duty to advise the Crown.”

Travers set the order on the table.

“The Council predates this government.”

“So does burglary. Age is not authority.”

“Without us, the Slayer would be alone.”

Basil's expression did not alter. “The present Slayer is in California with one underfunded Watcher and a group of schoolchildren.”

“Rupert Giles has allowed inappropriate attachments to compromise her discipline.”

“Those attachments have apparently kept her alive.”

“You have heard one American account.”

“I intend to read yours.”

The door opened. A police superintendent entered with a document case.

“Sir Basil, the lower archive is refusing access.”

Travers folded his hands. “Certain repositories answer only to the Council.”

“Not today.” Basil looked at the superintendent. “Bring the charter specialist.”

Travers stepped forward. “You risk damaging protections maintained since before the Conquest.”

“Then I suggest you provide the keys.”

“I will not permit—”

“You are not being asked to permit anything.”

Basil picked up the suspension order and placed it before Travers again, this time open to the final signature.

“The Crown privileges under which this institution holds property, conceals material, crosses borders, and claims exemption from ordinary scrutiny are suspended. You will cooperate, or you will be removed while others do.”

Travers looked at the signature.

His face changed by less than a degree.

“On whose allegation?”

“Buffy Summers.”

“The girl has no standing.”

“The allegation came from a colonel in the United States Air Force, was confirmed by a major general, transmitted by the President of the United States, and placed before Her Majesty by the Prime Minister.” Basil leaned slightly closer. “The girl has acquired standing.”

✳

The lower archive opened at twenty-seven minutes past four.

It occupied three underground floors, each larger than the building above suggested. Shelves moved on iron tracks. Temperature-controlled cabinets held paper older than several nations. Crystal cylinders whispered when living people approached. Bound diaries filled an entire room.

The oldest records had been kept because the Council valued continuity.

That instinct would condemn it.

Forensic teams photographed every shelf before touching it. Archivists assigned evidence numbers. Magical specialists placed containment seals around objects that reacted to human proximity.

One cabinet attempted to erase the names written inside it.

The specialist from Bath struck the lock with a silver rod. The letters returned.

“Attempted destruction,” she told the recorder. “Automatic upon unauthorized entry.”

“Can you preserve it?” Basil asked.

“Yes.”

“Do so. Then identify who authorized the ward.”

In another room, officers found personnel rosters for field teams that did not officially exist. Weapons inventories. Foreign travel papers issued under false names. Payments to coroners, police officials, and hospital administrators across four continents.

The Council's secrecy had not been passive.

At five fifteen, a child-protection team reported that all identified Potentials at the residences were accounted for. Several believed leaving without Council permission would cause the Slayer line to fail. Two had no legal identity outside documents held by the Council.

Basil ordered independent medical examinations and individual legal representation.

At five forty, police found three interior rooms secured from the outside.

One was empty.

One contained restraints sized for an adult.

The third contained restraints that were not.

At six o'clock, Travers requested counsel.

Basil granted it and ordered him separated from every other senior Council officer.

At six seventeen, the first accounting ledgers reached the examination room.

Basil removed his jacket, placed it over the back of a chair, and began to read.
