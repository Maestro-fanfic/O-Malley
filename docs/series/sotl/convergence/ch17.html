Chapter 17 of 27

Loose Threads

Marcus Whitfield had served the Council faithfully for eleven years before Travers's death, and he had spent every single one of those years believing that the old system was simply, straightforwardly how the world had to work. Slayers were assets. Watchers were stewards. The occasional necessary cruelty was the price of a system too vital to gamble away on softer feelings.

He was three weeks into the storage unit outside Fresno, cataloguing what remained of eleven years' worth of files by lamplight, when his hand closed around an old laminated ID badge he hadn't seen in years — his own face, considerably younger, above a name he barely recognized as his own anymore. He turned it over once in his fingers, and the present-day unit, the cardboard boxes, the hum of the single overhead light, all of it simply fell away.

✳

LONDON. EIGHT YEARS EARLIER.

A debrief room. Fluorescent lights humming steady overhead. A body bag, already zipped, waiting in the hall just beyond the half-open door.

"She's dead because she hesitated." Cordwell hadn't raised his voice. Cordwell never raised his voice; that was rather the point of him. "She voiced a field instinct against my authorization, and then she followed the authorization anyway, and the hesitation in between is what killed her. Do you understand what I'm telling you, Marcus?"

"I understand what you're telling me." Marcus was twenty-five, three years into the job, and had not yet learned to hear the wrongness in a sentence like that one. "I don't understand how that's the lesson."

"The lesson," Cordwell said, "is that structure exists precisely so a fifteen-year-old girl never has to make that calculation alone, under fire, with imperfect information. She wasn't equipped to weigh it. I was. That's the entire purpose of the chain of command you're currently frowning at." He looked at Marcus with something almost like patience, almost like kindness, and it was that almost-kindness, more than the argument itself, that did the actual work. "Corinne didn't die because the system failed her. She died because she stopped trusting it for four seconds. Don't you make her mistake."

✳

The badge was still in his hand. The lamp still hummed overhead, an ordinary storage-unit hum, nothing at all like the fluorescent one from that room, and yet his hand had gone tight enough around the laminate to crack it. Marcus set it down carefully on the folding table, breathing, and did not, even now, let himself go back far enough to notice that Cordwell had never actually answered the question he'd been asked.

He had not been in the building the night Giles came for Travers. He had been three miles away at the time, collecting a research package from a courier who'd insisted on an inconveniently late meeting time, and by the time he'd made his way back, the story was already moving fast through what remained of the loyal ranks — not told as tragedy, but as something closer to a natural disaster, an event that had simply happened, the way weather happens, to a man who had never once, in his whole long career, imagined himself capable of losing anything at all.

He had gone to ground after that, taking with him three encrypted drives of research the new organization's hurried asset transfer had somehow, in all the chaos, failed to properly account for, and a conviction, hardened rather than shaken by everything that had happened around him, that someone needed to remember what the Council had actually stood for, once all the sentimentalists finished dismantling everything his whole professional life had been built on.

He did not move quickly, in the months that followed. He had learned, watching Travers's own fall from a careful distance, that quickness got a man noticed, and noticed, in this frightening new world, meant either dead or considerably worse than dead. He worked in shadows, patient, methodical, cultivating a small, careful handful of contacts who shared his particular flavor of grievance, and he waited, telling himself the waiting was itself a kind of discipline, a kind of loyalty.

He was, for now, simply one more loose thread in a world that had recently sprouted a great many of them all at once — and he intended, in his own careful time, on his own chosen terms, to make that thread matter again, whatever it eventually cost him.

✳

Far across the galaxy, in a region of space no human sensor had yet properly mapped, something considerably older and less patient than Marcus Whitfield's own quiet grievance was beginning, very slowly, almost imperceptibly, to notice that the balance of power in a galaxy it had once very nearly consumed had shifted, recently, in ways it did not yet fully understand.

The Replicators had been quiet for some time now — fractured, scattered, licking old wounds inflicted by enemies who had believed, wrongly as it turned out, that the threat had mostly ended for good. But quiet, for something fundamentally built to replicate, to adapt, to learn from every single encounter, was never quite the same thing as gone.

A signal reached one of its scattered, patient fragments — faint, encoded in a frequency no organic mind would ever have properly recognized as language at all, but unmistakable to something built from Ancient technology recognizing its own distant cousin across the void: a vessel, Ancient in its own original construction, had recently surfaced. Fully crewed. Fully functional, running at capacities the fragment's own collective intelligence had spent considerable time and resources trying, and repeatedly failing, to fully reconstruct from whatever scavenged pieces it had managed to gather over the years.

It began, patiently, methodically, without hurry, to calculate a course.

✳

None of this reached Xander, three months into a life he was still slowly learning the true shape of, standing on Destiny's bridge with Rush at one console and Sam at another, watching a star chart slowly resolve into a shape neither of them fully recognized yet, though something in the pattern already made the back of Xander's neck prickle uneasily.

"Something's odd about this reading," Sam said, frowning at a signal that shouldn't have existed at all, in a part of space nobody had previously flagged as significant in any way.

"Odd how," Xander asked, feeling, without being able to properly name why, the old familiar hum of the ship under his feet suddenly carrying a weight it hadn't carried a moment ago.

"I don't know yet, exactly." Sam kept working, fast, careful, methodical. "But I don't like the general shape of it. It's too regular. Too clean."

Somewhere behind him, unnoticed by anyone else currently on the bridge, Rush went very still, staring down at his own readout with an expression that had nothing whatsoever to do with old grievances against a teenager who'd stopped needing his approval months ago, and everything to do with a pattern he recognized, distantly, from a life none of the Destiny crew had actually lived yet, in a future none of them could possibly know, yet, was already coming for them.

"Colonel Young should see this," Rush said, quiet, urgent, already moving fast toward the door. "Now. Immediately. This can't wait for the morning briefing."

Whatever this was, sitting patient and cold somewhere out past the edge of their current charts, it was only just beginning.
