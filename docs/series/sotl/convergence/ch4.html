The Man Who Wasn't Easy

Colonel Young stood over pod thirty-one for a long time before he gave the order, TJ Johansen at his other shoulder, arms crossed, a medical scanner dangling loose and ready in one hand the way it always seemed to be these days, whether or not anyone had actually asked her to bring it.

"You understand what you're asking me to authorize," Young said, to Xander, though his eyes never left the readout. "Rush isn't easy on a good day. He wasn't easy before any of this happened, and I've got no reason to think six months of stasis is going to have improved his disposition any."

"For what it's worth," TJ said, not looking up from her own instruments, "his vitals are solid. Better than solid, actually. Whatever this ship's been doing to keep them all stable in there, it's doing it well. Medically, there's no reason not to wake him, if that's the part you're actually asking me about."

"It's not the only part I'm asking about," Young said, dry, "but I appreciate the professional opinion."

"I understand." Xander stood beside him, hands in his pockets, trying to look more certain than he felt. "But we need someone who actually understands the tech side of this, and out of everyone in this room, he's the only one who's spent his whole life doing that kind of work. I checked. Twice."

"And you're sure this can't wait," Young said. "Sure this actually needs him specifically, and isn't just you wanting to prove you can handle him."

Xander considered that honestly, for a moment, rather than answering fast. "Both, probably," he admitted. "But mostly the first one. The Hellmouth's not gonna hold off for my personal growth arc."

That got the ghost of something from Young: not quite a smile, but adjacent to one. He was quiet a moment longer, some old, hard-won calculation running behind his eyes, the weighing of a man who had learned, the difficult way, how much trust a person like Rush actually deserved, and how much you needed him anyway regardless. "Wake him," he said, finally. "But I want someone else on that bridge with you the whole time. I don't trust him not to test you the second his eyes open."

"Copy that," Xander said, and meant it more than he let on.

The pod's seal released with a soft hydraulic sigh, mist curling up and away into the cold air of the bay, and Nicholas Rush came back to consciousness the way he apparently did everything: fast, sharp-edged, cataloguing.

"Where," he said, before his eyes had even fully focused, voice rough with disuse, "and how long."

"Sunnydale, California," Xander said. "And it's been about seven months since you went under, give or take. There's a problem we need your help with. Big one. I'll get you a full debrief once you're steady on your feet, because there's no version of me explaining this one out loud from scratch that doesn't sound completely insane."

Rush's eyes found him properly then, and something in his expression did a very specific, very fast recalibration, the look of a man running an old, familiar equation and getting an answer that didn't match his memory of the variables.

"Eli," Rush said, testing the name out loud, the way you might test a weight-bearing beam before trusting it. "You look— " He stopped. Started again, sharper this time, the old reflexive needling arriving right on schedule. "You look considerably more composed than I'd expect, given you've apparently been running my ship for the better part of a year with no formal command training whatsoever. Tell me, did you at least manage not to break anything critical, or should I brace myself for a full damage report before we get to whatever crisis you're dragging me into now?"

"Ship's fine," Xander said, flat, no flinch at all. "Better than fine, actually. You'll see the logs. I'm not gonna stand here and defend six months of decisions to somebody who wasn't awake for any of them."

Something in Rush's face went very still.

It wasn't the words, exactly... Eli, in the life Rush remembered, might have said something almost identical, given enough provocation, given enough of a bad day. It was the shape of it. No waver underneath. No visible need for Rush's approval riding along beneath the flat refusal. Just a fact, stated once, and left there, uncontested, the way you'd state that the sky was a color.

"I see," Rush said, after a moment, in a voice that had lost some of its bite without anyone quite watching it go. "Well. I suppose we'll have quite a lot to discuss, once this crisis of yours is handled."

"Yeah," Xander agreed, already turning to go, moving on to the next thing that actually mattered right now. "I suppose we will."

Rush watched him leave the bay, and did not, for several long seconds afterward, move at all, turning something over, privately, that he had no immediate intention of naming out loud to anyone.

✳

TJ found Rush still sitting on the edge of the stasis pod twenty minutes later, tablet open in his lap, and set a cup of something warm down beside him without being asked.

"You gonna be okay working with him?" she asked, not unkindly, nodding in the direction Xander had gone.

"I have absolutely no idea," Rush said, and there was something almost like honesty in it, rare enough from him that TJ actually paused to register it properly. "I keep reaching for a version of that conversation I've had a hundred times before, and it simply isn't there to reach for. It's unsettling. I don't care for things being unsettling that I can't immediately explain."

"Welcome to the rest of your week, probably." TJ picked up her own scanner, moving toward her next patient. "For what it's worth, from where I'm standing, that's probably a good thing. Whatever version of Eli you used to know how to needle, sounds like he needed a version of you that didn't need needling quite so much."

Rush didn't answer that. He watched her go, and turned the tablet back on, and let the silence do whatever work it was going to do.

✳

Spike found the second death oddly anticlimactic, all things considered.

He and Dru had been holed up in an abandoned mausoleum on the edge of town for the better part of a week, feeding, staying quiet, waiting for whatever came next in whatever game Sunnydale's newly complicated cast of characters was currently playing. He had, if he was honest with himself, been rather looking forward to making an entrance eventually, something with a bit of theater to it, a bit of style, the kind of proper introduction a vampire of his reputation deserved. He'd even worked out a line or two, in the idle hours, the kind of thing you'd want quoted back at you for years afterward.

He never got the chance to use any of them.

Above them, sixty-two miles up, a targeting solution resolved with cold, mechanical precision, coordinates confirmed twice against sensor data that had spent three patient days tracking two moving, humanoid heat voids — shapes that walked and breathed out of old habit but carried no body warmth at all, standing out starkly against the ordinary thermal clutter of a small town at night, an absence so complete and so specific it had taken Destiny's systems the better part of those three days to be certain it wasn't simply an equipment fault. Neither vampire ever once suspected they were being watched. The strike that followed came down out of a clear night sky with no warning at all... no missile trail, no sound until the very instant of impact, just a sudden, total, absolute violence that turned the mausoleum and everything inside it into a smoking crater in considerably less time than it took most people to finish a sentence.

"Confirmed," Sam said, back on Destiny's bridge, watching the readout go dark. "Both voids gone."

"Well," Buffy said, arms crossed, staring at the display with an expression that mixed relief and a faint, uneasy discomfort she wasn't quite ready to examine too closely. "That's — that's certainly one way to do it."

"Couldn't happen to a nicer set of bloodsuckers," Rush observed, dry, not looking up from his own console.

"Feels weird," Buffy admitted, after a moment. "Not sad, exactly. Just — weird. Three years of build-up for those two, and it ends with a light on a screen going dark. No last words. No dramatic showdown. Just — off."

"Would you have preferred the showdown?" Sam asked, genuinely curious rather than needling.

"God, no." Buffy shook her head slowly. "I've done enough dramatic showdowns to last several lifetimes. I just didn't expect it to feel this — administrative."

"Get used to that," Rush said, not looking up. "Most problems, properly solved, feel administrative. The dramatic ones are usually the ones that weren't solved well enough the first time."

Nobody argued the point. Nobody, in the end, felt much like celebrating it either. The bridge went quiet again, the readout dark, two names crossed off a list none of them had ever wanted to be keeping in the first place.

✳

The list, as it turned out, had one name left on it.

"Richard Wilkins the Third," Rush said, three days later, pulling up a file that had taken Destiny's systems considerably longer to assemble than either vampire's had. "Mayor of Sunnydale. Also, per what I can only describe as a genuinely alarming quantity of buried municipal paperwork, something considerably less human than the job title suggests. He's been at this awhile — decades, near as I can tell, behind a rotating cast of assumed identities."

"How much less human are we talking?" Buffy asked, arms crossed, not liking where this was going.

"Presently? Difficult to say precisely. Old, powerful, patient, and by every account I can find, working steadily toward something considerably worse than what he currently is." Rush tapped the display. "The good news, such as it is, is that whatever he's building toward, he hasn't finished building it yet. There's a ritual clock running in the background of most of his correspondence — a hundred days, repeated often enough that I'm fairly confident it's load-bearing to whatever he's planning. We appear to have caught him well short of it."

"Meaning he's not invincible yet," Giles said, quiet, something old and grim moving behind his eyes. "Whatever he's planning to become at the end of that hundred days, he isn't it yet. Right now, he's simply a very old, very careful man who's gotten away with a great many things because nobody's ever caught him early enough to matter."

"We caught him early enough to matter," Xander said.

The strike came down two nights later, timed to an evening the Mayor spent alone in his office going over budget reports with the same patient, unhurried dedication he'd apparently brought to every other part of his long, careful life. There was no ceremony to it, no final speech, no chance for whatever he might have become to ever actually happen. Just a light on a screen, and then no light at all.

"That's three," Buffy said, afterward, standing on the bridge with her arms wrapped around herself, feeling something complicated that wasn't quite grief and wasn't quite relief either. "Three years of him being the guy nobody could touch, and it ends because we caught him before his own clock ran out."

"That is, in point of fact, the entire point of catching something early," Giles said, gently. "It very rarely feels as dramatic as the alternative. That does not make it the wrong choice."

"I know." Buffy let out a breath. "I just keep waiting for one of these to feel like it earned its ending. Maybe that's not actually how it's supposed to work anymore."

"Maybe not," Giles agreed. "I find I'm not yet certain whether that's a loss or a mercy. Possibly, in the end, it's simply both."
