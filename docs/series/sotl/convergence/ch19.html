Chapter 19 of 59

The Cruciamentum

Quentin Travers entered the Palace through a door not used for public audiences.

His solicitor entered with him. A police officer retained the solicitor's case long enough to examine it, then returned it without comment. Travers surrendered his watch, fountain pen, and a signet ring carrying a ward Basil's specialists had identified before dawn.

The ring went into a lead-lined box.

“An heirloom,” Travers said.

“Then your heirs may appreciate our care,” Basil replied.

They proceeded through two corridors and into a private council room whose only ornament was a portrait of a young Queen in uniform.

The present Queen sat at the end of the table.

Basil stood to her right. The Prime Minister sat farther down with the Crown solicitor and an official recorder. A second chair faced them.

Travers bowed.

“Your Majesty.”

“Mr. Travers.”

He glanced at the empty places around the table. “I had understood that the governing committee was summoned.”

“It was.”

“The others?”

“Will attend separately.”

For the first time, uncertainty touched his face.

The Queen indicated the chair.

“You will account for your own conduct. They will account for theirs.”

Travers sat.

The recorder began.

“Do you presently serve as Chairman of the Watchers Council?” the Queen asked.

“I do.”

“Do you accept that the Council's charter derives privileges from the Crown?”

“The Council predates the modern Crown.”

“That was not my question.”

Travers looked toward his solicitor. She gave the smallest nod.

“Yes, Your Majesty.”

“The charter requires the Council to identify, instruct, support, and advise the Slayer. Does it authorize ownership?”

“No.”

“Command?”

“The practical conduct of our duty has always required authority.”

“Does the charter grant it?”

“Not in that word.”

“Does it authorize the execution of a Slayer who begins questioning Council orders?”

The solicitor leaned forward. “Ma'am, my client is cooperating concerning the charter, but allegations of criminal conduct—”

“Will be determined by the courts,” the Queen said. “I am asking whether authority claimed from the Crown includes the power to kill a girl for questioning an order.”

Travers answered before his solicitor could stop him.

“Retirement is an extreme measure reserved for threats to the line and the secrecy that protects humanity.”

Basil wrote something down.

“You accept that ‘retirement’ means death,” the Queen said.

Travers realized what he had done.

“It may involve the permanent removal of a compromised Slayer.”

“You may use the word death in this room.”

He did not.

The Queen opened the procedure manual.

“Explain the Tento di Cruciamentum.”

Travers's composure returned. Here, at least, he stood on doctrine older than any person present.

“It is a test administered on the Slayer's eighteenth birthday. Her natural abilities are temporarily suppressed, and she is required to defeat a vampire through intelligence, courage, and resourcefulness.”

“How are her abilities suppressed?”

“A compound is administered by her Watcher.”

“With her knowledge?”

“The test would be invalid if she anticipated it.”

“Then without her knowledge or consent.”

“It is a confidential field protocol.”

“How long is she permitted to acclimate to the loss of abilities upon which her survival has depended?”

“She is not informed that they have been suppressed.”

“And her opponent?”

“A vampire selected by the Operations Committee.”

“A newly risen vampire?”

Travers hesitated. “The protocol specifies a master vampire.”

“Prepared in advance.”

“Contained by the Council, yes.”

“Armed?”

“The Slayer is permitted to locate or improvise weapons within the testing ground.”

The Queen looked down at the manual.

“You drug a girl without her knowledge. You give her no opportunity to understand the body you have forced upon her. You isolate her from assistance and place her within reach of a creature selected because it can kill experienced Slayers.”

“It tests whether she remains worthy of the calling.”

“The calling has already selected her.”

“The Council must ensure that power is accompanied by judgment.”

“By removing her power and arranging her death?”

“Those who succeed emerge stronger.”

“And those who fail?”

“Another is called.”

There it was.

Not regret. Not even denial. Continuity.

Basil turned a page in the Summers file.

“Why eighteen?” he asked.

“It is the traditional age.”

“It is the age at which she becomes legally capable of rejecting your authority.”

Travers said nothing.

“This is not an examination,” Basil continued. “An examination is designed to measure its subject. This is designed either to break her obedience or to remove her.”

“The Slayer must prove herself worthy.”

Basil closed the file.

“She has spent years keeping your species alive. It is the Council whose worth is presently under examination.”

The Queen looked at Travers.

“Did you authorize preliminary preparations for Buffy Summers?”

“The file was opened according to schedule.”

“Did you authorize them?”

“Yes.”

“Did you review the recommendation that her civilian relationships be used to increase psychological pressure during the test?”

His solicitor touched his sleeve.

Travers did not answer.

“Your refusal is recorded,” the Queen said.

The hearing continued for forty-three minutes.

When it ended, Travers bowed again. The gesture was as correct as when he entered. It no longer concealed the fact that he had been required to answer.

The police officer returned him to a separate waiting room where he could speak to none of his colleagues.

The next Council member entered through the same door.

✳

Six members of the governing and Operations Committees appeared that morning.

Lady Margaret Wyndam-Pryce denied knowing that field teams used lethal force during retirement operations. Basil produced three authorizations bearing her signature. She said the papers had been presented as secrecy measures. The Queen asked why she had signed orders she did not understand.

Dr. Alistair Haviland defended the Cruciamentum as necessary psychological conditioning. He admitted that the Council collected no long-term data from survivors because survival was uncommon.

Sir Malcolm Reeve had chaired the Operations Committee for nine years. He insisted that no individual member could be responsible for collective decisions. The Queen placed before him an authorization requiring his personal approval and asked whether the signature was his.

It was.

Two members described repeated attempts to reform field support and produced memoranda the governing committee had rejected. Their complicity did not disappear, but it acquired boundaries.

One member declined every substantive question upon advice of counsel.

His silence was recorded beside the payments he had approved.

No two accounts matched completely.

Together, they described the same institution.

✳

In Sunnydale, Giles read the Cruciamentum summary standing behind the library counter.

O'Neill had offered him a chair.

He had declined.

Buffy sat at the central table with Willow and Xander. Carter and Daniel stood near the secure facsimile machine the Air Force had installed that morning. Teal'c guarded the doors.

The document was only four pages. It contained no photographs, no death records, and no names beyond Buffy's.

It was enough.

“Is it real?” Buffy asked.

Giles lowered the page.

“Yes.”

“They were planning to drug me.”

“The test is not scheduled until your eighteenth birthday.”

“That wasn't the question.”

Giles looked at her.

“Yes.”

“And lock me in with a master vampire.”

“Yes.”

Willow stared at him. “You knew?”

The word cut more cleanly from her than it would have from O'Neill.

Giles placed the report on the counter.

“I knew the Cruciamentum existed.”

Buffy went very still.

“Since when?”

“Since my training.”

“Before you met me.”

“Yes.”

“Were you going to do it?”

Giles had faced vampires, demons, and the Master beneath the library. None had required the courage of answering without choosing the answer that might preserve Buffy's love for him.

“I had not decided what I would do when the order came.”

Xander stood up.

O'Neill moved one hand—not toward his weapon, only enough to remind Xander that anger did not need to become action.

Buffy did not look away from Giles.

“You had more than a year to decide whether to poison me.”

“I never intended for you to die.”

“That is not the same thing.”

“No.”

The answer hurt her more than an excuse would have.

Giles continued because stopping would make the confession another form of concealment.

“I believed I would refuse when the time came. I also believed there was time before I had to confront what refusal would cost. Those beliefs allowed me not to decide.”

“Cost you,” Buffy said.

“Yes.”

“Your job.”

“Yes.”

“The Council.”

“Yes.”

“While I got the master vampire.”

Giles closed his eyes.

“Yes.”

Buffy rose.

Willow reached for her hand. Buffy squeezed it once and let go.

“I need to leave.”

“I'll come with you,” Willow said.

“No. I need everybody not to come with me.”

She looked at Xander before he could object.

“Especially people who got back from space yesterday and are supposed to avoid stress.”

“That's not a real doctor's order.”

“It is now.”

Buffy walked toward the doors.

Giles found his voice. “Buffy.”

She stopped without turning.

“I am sorry.”

“I know.”

That was all she gave him.

The doors swung closed behind her.

Giles remained at the counter with the file bearing her name.

O'Neill waited until Buffy's footsteps had faded.

“You want me to tell you that knowing isn't the same as doing?”

“No.”

“Good.”

Giles looked at the doors.

“I want the chance to become the man who would have refused.”

O'Neill's voice was quiet. “That's her decision now.”
