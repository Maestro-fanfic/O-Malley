The Search Goes On

The coordinates Jack had given her led to a stretch of desert three hundred miles from anywhere at all, a place so empty and so quiet that the silence itself seemed to have its own particular texture, and Buffy stood at the edge of it for a long moment, engine ticking as it cooled behind her, before she let herself actually start walking.

This is familiar, Mike said — flat, quiet, entirely without the grief that colored almost everything else living inside her these days. Solitude. Distance. A signal, faint and patient, and the long walk toward it that always, eventually, comes next.

"You've done this before too," Buffy said out loud, to the empty desert stretching flat and gold in every direction, to whoever was listening from wherever they actually lived inside her now.

Many times, Artemis answered, and there was something almost fond threaded through it, laced through the ever-present ache underneath. It is, in its own strange way, the only thing I remember doing with any real consistency across everything else. Waking somewhere new, disoriented, uncertain. Walking toward the one single thing that still feels like mine, even when nothing else quite does anymore.

Can I push back on that a little? Mike's voice came in sharper this time, not unkind, but with an edge Buffy hadn't heard from him before, something that read almost like impatience with how comfortably Artemis kept settling into her own grief. Because from where I'm sitting, that sounds like a story you've told yourself so many times it's stopped sounding like a choice. "Waking up disoriented" isn't the only thing you remember doing consistently. You survived, every single time. That's the actual pattern. You just keep skipping straight past the part where you did something remarkable and landing on the part where it hurt. And I get why. I do. But you matter too much for me to just sit here quiet and let you keep doing that to yourself.

You do not know what you are talking about, Artemis said, and something colder moved through the words now, old and tired and not remotely interested in being argued with. You have carried this body for a matter of months. I have carried loss for longer than you have any framework to properly imagine. Do not mistake your own unfamiliarity with grief for some deeper wisdom about how it ought to be handled.

Maybe. Or maybe I've just spent enough of my own life watching people talk themselves into believing the worst version of their own story because it's the version that finally stopped surprising them. Mike didn't back down, exactly, but something in his tone gentled anyway, some old habit of de-escalation even here, even now, aimed at a grief that wasn't remotely a threat and didn't need defusing so much as company. I'm not saying you're wrong to grieve. I'm saying you don't have to let the grief write the whole story by itself. That's all. That's genuinely all I'm trying to say.

Buffy sat with both of them a moment, feeling the friction between the two voices settle, uneasy, into the same small space they were all three sharing, and found she didn't much want to referee it. "You're both allowed to be in here," she said, finally, quiet. "You don't have to agree with each other to both be right about something."

Perhaps, Artemis said, after a long pause, something in the single word considerably less certain than it had sounded a moment ago.

She found the ship two miles in, half-buried in drifted sand, its hull scorched in long, dark patterns that told a clear story of atmospheric entry gone violently, catastrophically wrong. It wasn't large — nothing at all like Destiny, nothing like the Anomaly's impossible scale — just a single-pilot craft, dented and dark and silent, sitting exactly where it had apparently been sitting for considerably longer than made any sense at all for nobody to have stumbled across it before now.

She approached slowly, Mike's trained instinct checking angles automatically, checking cover, checking for anything that might still, after all this time, pose a threat, even out here, even now, in the middle of so much empty nothing.

It is not a threat, Artemis said, gentle, though something in it now carried a little less certainty than it once might have. It is home. Or it was, once, a very long time ago. I no longer remember, honestly, which parts of that word still properly apply to it anymore.

Or maybe it's just a ship, Mike offered, careful now, gentler than before, clearly working to build back some of the ground his earlier bluntness had cost him. Old, quiet, waiting. Doesn't have to mean anything more than that unless you want it to. Sometimes a wreck's just a wreck, until somebody decides otherwise.

The Distress Beacon was buried under a collapsed panel near what had once, clearly, been the cockpit. Buffy worked it free with her bare hands, feeling something in her fingers move with a precision that wasn't entirely her own — Mike's careful, patient, procedural competence again, guiding her steadily through a repair she had no earthly business knowing how to perform.

The beacon flickered slowly to life, then steadied. A single, faint signal, pulsing patiently outward, carrying an identifier Buffy somehow already knew before she'd even finished reading it properly off the small cracked display.

Artemis, it said, over and over, patient and unhurried, the beat going on, on, on.

"I found you," Buffy said, to a ship that had been waiting, apparently, for this long, for someone like her. "Or — I mean. I guess I found me, technically. This is a weird sentence to be saying out loud to an empty desert."

It is not so strange as you might think, Artemis said. Every Traveler, eventually, finds the wreck they left behind somewhere. The waking is always the hardest part of it. The finding, by comparison, is relatively easy, once you've survived that first part.

Buffy sat down against the ship's cold hull, in the middle of an empty desert three hundred miles from anyone who loved her, and let herself, for a while, just be still with all of it — the ache, the grief for people who had never existed and the guilt for people who had, Mike's quiet, patient competence that had kept her safe all this time without her ever once asking it to, and now, this: a wreck, a signal, a small, solid proof that whatever she'd become on Halloween actually had a history behind it, even if she'd never get to fully know whose history it had originally been.

"So what happens now?" she asked, eventually, into the quiet. "Do I fix it? Fly it somewhere? Just — leave it here in the sand and walk away?"

You do whatever feels like yours to do, Artemis said. That has always, in the end, been the entire point of any of this. Nobody else gets to decide that part for you. Not even me.

Buffy looked at the ship a while longer, feeling the desert wind move slow around her, feeling something settle inside her that had been unsettled since Halloween — not resolved, not finished, but hers, finally, in a way nothing about this whole transformation had quite managed to feel before this exact moment.

She started working on the repairs before the sun went all the way down, alone, as she'd needed to be for this particular part of it.

It took three full days, working mostly with her bare hands and a small toolkit she'd bought at a hardware store in the nearest town, sleeping in her car at night under a sky that felt closer out here than it ever had back home, before the ship finally, properly powered up.

The cockpit, once she'd cleared enough sand and debris to actually climb inside, was smaller than she'd expected — a single seat, worn soft in a shape that didn't quite match her own body, controls laid out in a configuration her hands somehow already understood despite never having touched anything like them before in her life.

This was mine, Artemis said, quiet, something almost like wonder threading through the ache for the first time since Halloween. I had forgotten the shape of it. The exact weight of the seat. I did not know I still carried the shape of a memory like that until just now, sitting here with you.

"What do you want to do with it?" Buffy asked, hands resting light on the controls, not yet committing to anything.

I do not know yet, Artemis admitted. I think, for now, I would simply like to sit here a while longer. There has not been a great deal of simply sitting, in whatever life I lived before this one. I find I rather like it.

Buffy sat with her a while longer, engine idling low and patient beneath them both, watching the desert stars come out one by one overhead, and found, for the first time since a costume shop and a cracked stone idol had upended her entire life, that she didn't feel the need to rush toward whatever came next.

The beacon was still going, she noticed, somewhere around midnight, its small cracked display still steady, still faithfully pulsing that same patient identifier out into the dark, exactly the way it had been doing, apparently, for longer than either of them could properly account for.

Nada, Buffy thought, not quite meaning to send it anywhere in particular, simply thinking the name the way you think of anyone you trust to be listening.

The reply came instantly, the way it always did, no delay at all between the thought and the answer arriving fully formed. I hear it, Nada said, gentle, unhurried despite the immediacy of it. I have heard it since the moment it woke. Signals do not simply vanish out here, not truly. They travel a very long way, and they are heard by a great many more listeners than the small ones who send them ever imagine.

"You can actually do something about it?" Buffy asked, something in her chest loosening that she hadn't fully realized was still braced.

I do not need to do anything at all, small one. It has already been answered. Something warm moved underneath the words, patient and entirely certain. The Atlas hears every signal the instant it is truly sent, however faint, however old, however far from anywhere it happens to be calling from. It has never once, in the whole of my long existence, failed to answer immediately. The answer is already there, Artemis, has been there since the very moment you sent it out into the dark all those years ago. You have simply never before had anyone beside you willing to help you notice it arrive.

It arrived instantly, exactly the way Nada had promised it would, not as a voice at all but as text: stark, unadorned, scrolling into being across the inside of her own thoughts the way a status readout scrolls across a screen, machine-precise and entirely without inflection.

ANOMALY COMPLIANT. POSITION LOGGED.

SYSTEM INTEGRITY SCAN INITIALIZED.

NEW ITERATION DETECTED.

Three lines. Nothing more than that. And then, underneath the flat administrative certainty of them, something Buffy felt rather than read: warmth, enormous and entirely too large to fit inside three lines of system text, the shape of an old signal finally being closed out, correctly, completely, after far too long left open.

New iteration, Artemis repeated, quiet, something breaking gently open in the words. It does not mean only the signal. It means me. This version of me. It is telling me it sees who I have become, not only who I was when I first sent it.

Something in Artemis eased, all at once, in a way Buffy felt as much as heard: the quiet relief of discovering a question had been answered all along, faithfully, immediately, every single time it was asked, and that the only thing missing had ever been someone willing to help her actually notice.

Thank you, Artemis said, simply, and there was nothing guarded left in it at all.

The beat, as it turned out, could simply go on a while longer, as slow as it needed to, no longer waiting on anything at all.
