Chapter 18 of 59

The Ledgers

The Council kept two accounts of every expense.

The first was written for auditors who were never expected to ask what the words meant. It contained properties, salaries, archival preservation, travel, research grants, educational trusts, and field operations.

The second account was written for the Council itself.

Basil placed the volumes side by side.

Watcher salaries appeared in both. So did pensions, medical expenses, relocation allowances, and death benefits paid to surviving families. Senior personnel received housing. Administrative officers claimed meals. Members of the governing board were reimbursed for tailoring.

The active Slayer received nothing.

Her weapons appeared beneath her Watcher's field allowance. Her medical expenses, when recorded at all, were classified as incidentals. Funeral costs were charged to local disposal. No pension category existed because no Slayer had been permitted to reach one.

An accountant named Pembroke sat across from Basil beneath the supervision of a Crown solicitor and two police officers.

“The Slayer is not an employee,” Pembroke said.

“No. Employees have rights.”

“The calling is mystical. The Council does not create it.”

“The sea is also a natural phenomenon. That does not permit the Admiralty to stop paying sailors.”

Pembroke folded his hands.

Basil turned another page.

“Rupert Giles.”

“Field Watcher, North American assignment.”

“His salary is barely adequate for subsistence in his assigned location.”

“Field personnel receive standardized compensation.”

“Senior Council dining expenses exceeded his annual operational allowance last year.”

Pembroke said nothing.

“His position as school librarian is listed as external income.”

“It provides his cover.”

“And subsidizes the Council's deployment.”

“That is not how we classify it.”

“Classification appears to be your principal skill.”

Basil moved to the next column.

SLAYER RETIREMENT — CONTINGENCY RESERVE.

He read the associated figures.

“You have a budget for Slayer retirement.”

Pembroke's eyes shifted toward the solicitor.

“Answer,” she said.

“Yes.”

“Yet there are no pension disbursements, residences, annuities, or long-term medical provisions attached to it.”

“The terminology is traditional.”

Basil opened the operational ledger corresponding to the reserve.

Each expenditure carried a name, a field authorization, a team allocation, and a closure date.

Every closure date matched the death of a Slayer.

Some files used additional codes.

PERMANENT DISABILITY.

REFUSAL OF COUNCIL AUTHORITY.

ATTEMPTED WITHDRAWAL FROM ACTIVE SERVICE.

UNACCEPTABLE SECRECY EXPOSURE.

UNAUTHORIZED ALLIANCE.

Then:

INITIAL QUESTIONING OF COUNCIL ORDERS.

Basil read that line twice.

“Begins questioning Council orders.”

Pembroke swallowed. “Questioning frequently precedes disobedience.”

“It also frequently precedes the discovery that an order is wrong.”

“The Slayer cannot be permitted to undermine operational continuity.”

Basil turned the book so the solicitor could see the list of payments to field teams.

“So you kill her before she learns she is allowed to say no.”

“Retirement is authorized only after review.”

“By whom?”

“The Operations Committee.”

“How many Slayers have survived review?”

Pembroke did not answer.

“I see.” Basil closed the ledger. “And what charming traditional term do you use for murder?”

The first direct report reached the Queen shortly after eight that evening.

Basil stood in the same sitting room. He had recovered his jacket but not the untouched condition of his cuffs. A fine layer of archive dust had settled into the fabric.

The Queen read the one-page summary without expression.

“How many girls?”

“We do not yet know.”

“An estimate.”

“The indexed record extends beyond two thousand years. Portions are incomplete.”

“How many deliberate retirements?”

“We have identified thirty-seven modern files coded clearly enough to meet that definition. There will be more.”

“Modern?”

“The Council changed terminology repeatedly. Earlier cases require individual examination.”

She set the page down.

“Every single one.”

“Yes, Ma'am.”

“The Watchers?”

“Complicity varies. Some requested support repeatedly and were denied. Some falsified reports to protect their Slayers. Others signed retirement recommendations.”

“Then they are to be judged individually.”

“Yes, Ma'am.”

“And the Potentials presently in their care?”

“Accounted for and under independent protection. Several do not possess ordinary identity documents. We are correcting that.”

The Queen looked at the Council charter on her table.

“They made the children disappear before they had even been called.”

“In several cases, yes.”

“The Chairman and every member of the governing and Operations Committees will attend me tomorrow.”

Basil paused. “In person, Ma'am?”

“A personal accounting generally requires the presence of the person giving it.”

“Some are being treated as potential subjects of a criminal inquiry. Counsel may object to questioning.”

“I shall not try criminal charges in a sitting room. I shall require each person who exercised privileges derived from the Crown to explain how those privileges were used.”

“Together?”

“Separately. They have spoken collectively for long enough.”

“Their answers will need to be recorded.”

“Of course.”

“And if they decline the summons?”

The Queen looked at the suspended charter.

“Then their refusal will become part of the accounting, and they will never again exercise authority under that document.”

Basil inclined his head. “Yes, Ma'am.”

“Continue the investigation.”

“Directly, Ma'am.”

She dismissed him only after asking for the names of the seven girls removed from the training residence.

She wrote each one down herself.

At the Council, the next evidence cart arrived from the restricted archive.

Its top drawer contained procedures applied to Slayers who survived beyond the Council's preferred period of dependence.

Basil opened the first manual.

The title page read:

TENTO DI CRUCIAMENTUM

EIGHTEENTH-YEAR FIELD PROTOCOL

Beneath it lay a newly prepared folder.

SUMMERS, BUFFY ANNE

PRELIMINARY CANDIDATE ASSESSMENT
