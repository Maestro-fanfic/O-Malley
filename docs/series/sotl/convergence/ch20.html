Caught by His Own Petard

Daniel Jackson had spent three full weeks treating the question as a pleasant hobby, something to chew on during downtime between missions, and somewhere around the start of the fourth week, he was forced, gradually and then all at once, to admit it had become something closer to an outright obsession.

Chaos had never sat right with him, not from the very first night Giles laid out the surviving fragments of Ethan Rayne's testimony across the library table. Chaos gods were sloppy by their very nature — their myths, across every tradition Daniel had ever studied, described disorder for its own sake, entropy without any real direction or intent behind it. But everything about what had actually happened on that Halloween night showed exactly the opposite pattern, the more closely he examined it. Three costumes, three specific characters, three ships or stations or entities matched with uncanny, almost surgical specificity to the exact person wearing each one. That wasn't chaos, not by any definition he'd ever encountered in decades of study. That was selection, deliberate and precise.

He went back to the oldest sources he could find, working late into the night after every shift, pulling texts the SGC library hadn't properly catalogued in years — not the familiar Roman civic worship most people thought of first, but older still, the liminal-god traditions that predated even that, the ones that framed Janus not as chaos incarnate but as threshold itself. Doors. Beginnings that always implied their own eventual endings. A god who looked both ways at once because his entire nature was fundamentally about the precise moment of crossing, not about the disorder sitting on either side of that crossing.

He wrote his notes late into the night, more often than not in the SGC's own library, usually with Giles across the table doing his own parallel research on a separate but related thread, the two of them occasionally trading a source or a sudden insight without either one looking up from their own work. Daniel had noticed, without ever quite commenting on it, that Giles researched the way a soldier stood watch — relaxed on the surface, utterly still underneath, never once fully unaware of the exits.

"You've stopped calling him the chaos god," Giles observed, one such night, not looking up from his own book, the observation dropped casually into the quiet.

"Yeah." Daniel didn't look up either, still frowning at a page of translation notes. "I don't think that's actually what he is. I think that's just what he's been treated as, historically, by people who wanted a ready-made excuse for whatever they were already planning to do regardless. Costume magic, party tricks — of course you'd frame the god behind all that as pure chaos. It's a much better, tidier story for a Halloween shop than 'god of the specific moment you become something else.'"

"And what do you think he actually is, then, if not that?"

Daniel considered the question for a long moment, setting his pen down. "I think he's the reason a door can exist at all between two things that were never, by any normal rule, supposed to touch each other," he said. "I think what happened on Halloween wasn't an explosion, wasn't disorder let loose. I think it was an opening. A very deliberate, very precise opening."

He dreamed of him three nights later.

It didn't feel like a dream — that was the first thing he noticed, within the dream itself, with the particular unnerving lucidity dreams sometimes achieve right before you consciously understand you're actually having one. He was standing in a doorway. Not a room, not a hallway. Just the doorway itself, freestanding, wood and iron and something older than either material, open on both sides onto a darkness that didn't feel empty so much as waiting, patient, attentive.

"You've been paying attention," said a voice from somewhere within the doorway itself, and Daniel turned to find a face — two faces, actually, one looking toward him directly, one looking steadily away, both belonging, somehow, to the exact same head, both entirely, unnervingly real in the specific texture-heavy way dreams sometimes achieve and usually, frustratingly, don't. "Most people don't. Most people burn things in my name for centuries and never once wonder what they're actually asking for when they do."

"Janus," Daniel said, and it wasn't really a question at all.

"In one of my faces, yes." The face looking toward him smiled, easy, almost delighted, warmth crinkling at its eyes. "I know they say curiosity killed the cat, Dr. Jackson, but I just had to know about the man who finally put it all together properly. Chaos. Everyone always says chaos, every single time, for centuries. You said threshold. Do you have any idea how long it's actually been since someone bothered to do the reading properly, all the way through?"

"I want to understand the mechanism," Daniel said, because some deep scholar's instinct in him refused to waste a single second of this on anything but the actual, substantive question. "Not why. I already understand you're not going to tell me why, and I've made my peace with that. Just — how."

"Fair," Janus said, and something in the second face, the one looking steadily away, tightened almost imperceptibly — an acknowledgment of the boundary, drawn silently, without a single word needed. "Here's what I'll give you, freely, as a gift. Magic and what you people insist on calling technology were never separate things to me. Never have been, not once, not in all the ages I've watched mortals draw that particular line. They're both simply doors, dressed up differently depending on who's doing the building. A costume is a door between a person and a character. A myth is a door between a name and whatever that name actually, truly points toward underneath the story. I didn't build your friend's ship, Dr. Jackson. I didn't build the station either, or the wreck the Slayer's out there in the desert fixing with her own two hands right now, this very night. I simply opened doors wide enough that each of those things — already entirely real, already existing somewhere in some other reality — could step through toward the person wearing its particular shape."

"That's not a small thing to give away for free, all things considered."

"It's not free," Janus said, and both faces smiled now, in unsettling, perfect unison. "You're paying for it in understanding, Dr. Jackson. That's the only currency I've ever actually wanted from any mortal, in all my long history. Everything else — the burnt offerings, the careful little rituals, the half-remembered prayers — that's all just noise to me. Understanding is the only coin that's ever actually mattered."

Daniel woke with the dream already fading fast at its edges the way dreams always do, but he remembered enough of it — remembered doors, dressed differently — and wrote it down immediately, hand shaking slightly with the effort of catching it before it slipped away, already composing, in his own head, the next question he fully intended to ask.

The rewards kept coming after that, small and irregular, spread out over the following weeks. A second dream, shorter than the first, that gave him a fragment about why the ships had arrived already crewed and fully fueled rather than empty — because a door, once opened, always opens onto a whole thing, never merely a piece of one, and Janus, in all his long existence, had simply never once learned to do it any smaller than that. A third, stranger dream that gave him almost nothing in the way of words at all, just a single unsettling image held for what felt like a very long time: two faces, reflected infinitely in a pair of facing mirrors, each one looking, forever, at a slightly different version of the exact same unanswered question.

Daniel kept researching, methodically, patiently. Kept writing everything down, cross-referencing, building. Kept, without ever quite admitting it plainly to himself in so many words, playing along with something he'd slowly come to understand was a genuine, ongoing game — because somewhere around the third or fourth exchange, he'd understood, with the same strange lucid clarity the dreams themselves always carried, that this had never once been an interrogation. It was a game, deliberately structured. And, more than that, underneath the game: a lesson, offered patiently by a teacher who'd apparently been waiting a very, very long time for a student he judged genuinely worth the considerable effort.

He put the final piece together, on an entirely ordinary Tuesday afternoon, in the middle of a completely unrelated briefing about supply logistics.

"It's not just that he opens doors," Daniel said, out loud, mid-sentence, cutting himself off, the realization arriving faster than his own internal filter could properly catch and contain it. "He's been testing me, this whole time, seeing how far I could actually follow the logic on my own. Each dream's been a reward for whatever piece I got right the time before. He's not just answering my questions as I ask them. He's teaching me, deliberately, the way you'd teach a promising student."

The air in the briefing room changed before anyone else had time to even ask what he meant by any of that.

It didn't announce itself with light, or sound, or fanfare of any kind. It simply opened — a shape appearing in the middle of the room that hadn't been a doorway even half a second earlier, wood and iron and something older than either, and through it stepped a figure with two faces, one turned toward the startled, silent room, one turned steadily away toward whatever lay on the other side of wherever he'd just stepped through from.

"Caught," Janus said, delighted, spreading both hands wide in an expansive, theatrical gesture, "by my own petard, no less. Well done, Dr. Jackson, well done. I did not expect you to catch the actual shape of the game itself, not this quickly. That's rather the entire point, you understand — nobody, in all my long memory, has ever managed to get quite that far before."

Nobody in the room moved for a long, frozen moment.

"Right," O'Neill said, finally, recovering fastest, the way he generally, reliably did in situations this strange. "So. God. Standing in the briefing room. Pay no attention to the man behind the curtain, except, wait, there's two of him, and he's not behind anything, he's just standing right there. That's — yeah, that tracks pretty well with the general trend of my week so far." He didn't reach for a weapon at his hip. Some old, reliable soldier's instinct told him clearly it wouldn't matter one bit if he did.

Sam had her tablet up and scanning before she'd even consciously decided to raise it, running every single sensor sweep she had available, watching every one of them return absolutely nothing at all — no energy signature, no particle trace, no data of any kind where a two-faced god was standing in plain, undeniable sight in front of her.

"Indeed," Teal'c said, with the same flat, total economy of language he brought to nearly everything, and did not otherwise visibly react at all.

Daniel was the last one in the room to find his own voice. He'd spent three weeks building a private, careful, developing relationship with this exact presence through dreams and scattered fragments and late-night library sessions, and none of that careful preparation had made the actual, physical, undeniable fact of him standing in the SGC briefing room any less thoroughly staggering to witness in person. He opened his mouth twice before anything at all came out.

"You're—" Daniel managed, finally, voice unsteady. "You're actually here. Really here."

"I am," Janus agreed, both faces turning fully to look at him now, warm, delighted by the reaction. "Consider it a graduation gift, Dr. Jackson. There aren't many students, across all my long memory, I've ever bothered to give one to. You should feel appropriately honored."

He didn't stay long — a few more words, mostly directed at Daniel specifically, mostly untranslatable in any way that would have made proper sense to anyone else currently in the room. Then he turned to go, both faces swinging back toward the doorway in perfect unison — except that only one of them actually got there.

The second face didn't turn away with the first. It turned, instead, somewhere else entirely — toward nothing anyone in that room could have pointed to, toward whoever happened to be watching all of this unfold from outside it altogether — and it grinned, wide and delighted and entirely, knowingly aware of being watched, the particular grin of a man who has just gotten away with something and wants you, specifically, to know he noticed you noticing.

Then it was gone, following the first face back through the door, and the door closed behind both of them exactly the way it had opened: without sound, without light, simply, quietly no longer there at all.

Hammond, arriving moments too late to witness any of it firsthand, took one long look at the room's collective expression and made a decision, right there on the spot, that would hold for the entire remainder of his tenure.

"This does not go in the official record," he said, flatly, already turning to leave, already moving on to the next crisis waiting for his attention. "Not after everything we've already weathered with leaks in this program. Whatever just happened in this room, gentlemen, Major Carter — it stays in this room, and it stays between the five of us."

Nobody argued the point. Daniel, still staring at the empty space where the doorway had briefly stood, found, turning it over privately in his own mind, that he didn't especially want to argue it either.
