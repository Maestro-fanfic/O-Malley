Chapter 22 of 59

Power Remaining

Rush put the power figures on every functioning display aboard Destiny.

He did not soften them for Young, and he did not explain them twice.

The ship had crossed into Earth's sky with the same damage it had carried between galaxies: entire sections sealed, distribution networks operating through improvised bypasses, and systems older than human civilization consuming power no one aboard fully understood. The transit had not restored anything. It had only interrupted the slow process by which Destiny preserved what remained.

Now that process was accelerating.

“The field is drawing energy,” Rush said.

Young stood at the central console. Behind the glass, Earth filled half the observation deck. California lay in darkness beneath them, its cities scattered across the coast in bright constellations.

“From the ship?”

“Not directly. At least, not in any way the power grid can measure.” Rush enlarged the diagram. “But every system maintaining our position is compensating for it. Navigation makes a correction, the field negates it, and then the ship corrects for the correction. Guidance, inertial control, structural integrity, propulsion readiness—they're all cycling.”

TJ studied the lines descending across the display.

“How long?” she asked.

Rush gave her the look he reserved for questions whose answers had too many variables.

Young waited.

“At current consumption, our reserves fall below safe stasis margin in months, not years.”

The observation deck went quiet.

TJ looked toward the corridor. Hundreds of people slept beyond it. They had entered stasis believing the ship would cross the gulf between galaxies while Eli repaired the final pod. None had consented to wake in their own past, but none had consented to die quietly above it either.

“Define safe margin,” Young said.

“Destiny defines it as the point at which it can no longer guarantee uninterrupted support to every pod.”

“That's not what I asked.”

Rush's mouth tightened.

“We don't know. The ship may begin selective shutdowns. It may attempt to wake the crew. It may preserve some compartments at the expense of others. The relevant systems were damaged before we arrived, and several automatic repairs stopped during transit.”

“Worst case.”

“A power fluctuation interrupts stasis without completing revival.”

TJ's expression gave the clinical sentence a human meaning.

Young looked at the rows of names on the status panel.

“And the solution?”

Rush changed the display. Destiny appeared beside a star, its silhouette swallowed by a corona.

“The same solution the ship has used for millions of years. We enter a star and recharge.”

“The Sun.”

“Unless you have another suitable star hidden nearby.”

Young ignored the tone. “Can Destiny reach it?”

“The engines can. The ship cannot.”

Rush brought up the navigational plot. Every projected course away from the fixed point above Sunnydale bent back toward it. Some ended in errors. Others folded into impossible loops before the computer rejected them.

“Destiny is capable of reaching the Sun,” Rush continued. “It is also being held here by something the ship cannot identify and cannot navigate through. Remaining in position kills us slowly. Attempting to leave without understanding the restraint may kill us quickly.”

Young opened the channel to the ground.

Hammond appeared on one screen from the conference room at Cheyenne Mountain. Carter appeared on another from the Sunnydale High library. O'Neill, Daniel, Teal'c, Giles, Buffy, Willow, and Xander stood behind her.

Xander looked pale.

“Mister Harris,” TJ said, “any symptoms?”

“Headache. Not terrible. It feels like somebody tied a string behind my eyes and keeps winding it around a pencil.”

“Sit down.”

“Already sitting.”

Buffy put a hand on his shoulder as if to prove it.

Carter studied the telemetry Rush had sent.

“The field contraction began when the solar angle placed Sunnydale between Destiny and the Sun.”

Rush nodded reluctantly. “Yes.”

“Could the ship be trying to initiate a recharge?”

“It would have to move approximately one astronomical unit in a direction it currently cannot manage by a meter.”

“That wasn't my question.”

Rush paused.

“It is possible that Destiny's automatic systems selected the Sun and that the restraint reacted to the attempt.”

“Then whatever holds the ship may tighten whenever it tries to leave,” Daniel said.

Giles removed his glasses. “A binding.”

“A tether,” Carter said.

Xander shook his head.

“No. A coordinate.”

Everyone looked at him.

He pressed two fingers above his right eye.

“I don't know how I know. Destiny isn't being pulled down. It's being told that this is where it is. Every time navigation tries to define somewhere else, the answer gets overwritten.”

Rush moved closer to the screen.

“Can you see the mechanism?”

“No.”

“Then what precisely are you perceiving?”

“A headache, mostly.”

“Xander,” Young said.

The use of his name, rather than Eli's, cut through the beginning of the argument.

“Don't reach for it,” Young continued. “Tell us what comes without pushing.”

Xander let his hand fall.

“Destiny knows where she is because I know where she is. And I know where she is because I'm here.”

Willow's eyes widened. “You're part of the address.”

“Maybe.”

“That's enough,” TJ said. “No more testing him tonight.”

Rush turned from the screen. “The distinction between testing and allowing the only human being connected to the phenomenon to describe it—”

“Is one I am making as his doctor.”

Hammond spoke before Rush could answer.

“Colonel Young, your recommendation.”

Young had already reached it.

“Wake the engineers. Brody, Volker, Park, and the minimum personnel Rush and Carter require to verify propulsion, power distribution, and structural integrity. Lieutenant Johansen supervises every revival.”

“You have authorization.”

“I also recommend a controlled propulsion test after they complete their inspection.”

Hammond considered the dark lines on the power display.

“Submit the test parameters before execution. No change in position until I approve them.”

“Understood.”

Young looked through the observation glass again.

The planet outside was home. That truth had stunned everyone awake, divided loyalties, and created possibilities none of them had dared imagine.

It did not change the arithmetic.

“General,” Young said, “I need the record to show that the crew is in danger.”

Hammond's expression did not shift.

“It will.”

“If we can't change the anchor, the people in those pods die.”

“Then we change it.”

The answer was not reassurance. It was an order to everyone listening.

Young accepted it as such.

“Begin revival,” he said.
