Four Conversations

Sam stayed two hours longer than she'd planned to, and told herself the whole time it was purely for the data.

Xander walked her and Jacob back to the airlock himself, past the same stretch of oversized corridor that had nearly broken O'Neill an hour earlier, apparently unbothered now by just how much ceiling the ship insisted on having.

"You'll want this." Sam pressed a small drive into his hand before she could think better of it. "Standard diagnostic suite, nothing classified. Just enough that if something in there starts throwing errors you don't understand, you'll know it's throwing them before it turns into an emergency."

"I've got a ship that basically diagnoses itself," Xander said, turning the drive over once before pocketing it anyway.

"Basically isn't the same as actually, and I don't like betting ninety lives on basically." She said it lightly, but something underneath it wasn't light at all, and Xander, for once, didn't reach for a joke to smooth it over.

"Copy that," he said instead. "Thanks."

Jacob lingered a moment at the airlock threshold, looking back down the corridor the way a man looks at something he already knows he'll spend the rest of the week failing to properly describe to anyone who wasn't standing here for it.

Four hundred years, Selmak said, quiet, mostly to Jacob and only a little to the boy who could apparently hear him anyway. And I have still never once stood somewhere quite like this.

"Yeah," Jacob said. "Me neither, buddy. Me neither."

The scout ship peeled away slow, matching Destiny's orbit a long moment before finally committing to the burn that would carry it home, and Xander stood at the observation window and watched it go until it was nothing but one more point of light indistinguishable from the rest: the whole trip back to Earth stretched across hours, instead of the half-second it had taken Jack and Teal'c to simply not be there anymore.

Two very different ways to leave the same room, he thought, and wondered, distantly, which one he'd have picked, if anyone had ever bothered to ask which way he wanted to go.

✳

Jack and Teal'c had been back eleven hours, showered, and debriefed once, in the sparse, preliminary way that mostly amounted to sir, you are not going to believe this, by the time Sam and her father made it down through Cheyenne Mountain's outer checkpoints.

General Hammond met them at the base of the ramp himself, which told Sam most of what she needed to know about how seriously this had been taken before she'd said a single word.

"Major." A brief pause, entirely professional. "Selmak."

"General." Jacob inclined his head, Selmak's presence settling visibly behind his eyes for the greeting before receding again to let Jacob do the talking. "You're going to want the long version of this. I'd recommend a chair."

"I've had eleven hours of Colonel O'Neill's version," Hammond said, dry, already turning to lead them deeper into the mountain. "Believe me, General Carter, I'm ready for someone who uses complete sentences."

The briefing ran long. Sam laid out sensor data that resolved into nothing, hull composition readings that matched no catalogued database, a boy who'd absorbed nine years of a dead engineer's expertise in a single night with no clean explanation for how, ninety sleeping strangers with futures still unclaimed on Earth right now, walking around with no idea what they'd eventually become. She kept her voice level through all of it, the same careful, methodical cadence she used for any report that mattered, and only once, near the end, did something slip past the professional distance: a small, unguarded pause before she said, "Sir, I don't have a framework for this. I want that on the record before we go any further."

Hammond didn't answer right away. He'd gone very still at the head of the table, the same effortful stillness Sam had seen from him exactly twice before, both times when the truth in front of him was too large to react to honestly without losing something he couldn't afford to lose in front of his own people.

"Understood," he said finally, and the word carried more weight than its size should have allowed. "What do you recommend?"

"Contact. Careful, controlled contact, through people he already trusts." Sam glanced at her father, who nodded once. "He's seventeen, sir. He performed a flawless deep-space rescue on pure instinct and then apologized for the soup. Whatever else he is, he's not a threat that needs managing. He's a kid who needs someone competent standing next to him before Washington finds out and decides otherwise."

Hammond was quiet a moment longer, weighing something Sam couldn't fully see the shape of.

"Get some rest, Major," he said. "Both of you. I'll want a team ready to move by morning."

✳

"You're going," Hammond said, before any of them had finished sitting down, which was as close to a briefing as this meeting was ever going to get. He stood at the head of the table with a folder under one arm that he didn't bother opening, because everything in it, at this point, was old news to him. "I don't care what regulation says about civilian towns and unsanctioned contact protocols. There is an Ancient warship and something the size of a small moon parked over a town in California, and the only human being who's spoken to either of them is seventeen years old and personally responsible for saving two of my people's lives. You will go, you will assess, and you will report back, before Washington decides to handle this with rather less finesse than I trust the four of you to bring to it."

He looked around the table once, slow, deliberate, daring any single one of them to argue. Nobody did. Sam had already closed her laptop. Daniel had already reached for his bag. Teal'c hadn't moved at all, which was, everyone at the table understood by now, its own form of agreement.

"Wheels up in two hours," Hammond said, and that was that.

✳

Nobody slept on the flight, though several people made a reasonable effort at it.

"So," O'Neill said, somewhere over the Rockies, staring at a folder full of satellite imagery that told him almost nothing useful beyond the fact that something enormous was, indeed, sitting in orbit exactly where three separate agencies kept insisting it was. "Kid flies an invisible spaceplane, has ninety people in cryo-sleep, and knew my second-in-command's future rank before she did. Anybody want to take a swing at a working theory here, or are we just gonna wing it the way we usually do?"

"I don't have a theory," Sam said, not looking up from her own laptop, where the same handful of sensor readings had been open for the better part of forty minutes without yielding a single new insight. "I have several theories, and every one of them falls apart the second I try to reconcile it with the fact that he called me Colonel three ranks early, using a rank I don't actually hold in this timeline, in this reality, as far as I can currently tell."

"Time travel," Daniel offered, mostly, it seemed, to have something in the pool.

"Time travel doesn't explain the second impossible object sitting in the sky next to the first one."

"Alternate timeline, then. Parallel reality bleed-through."

"That doesn't explain it either," Sam said, patient in the specific way of someone who'd run this exact argument with herself several times over the last five days. "It just moves the impossibility somewhere I don't have the instruments to measure it."

"I am simply relieved," Teal'c said, from across the aisle, with the flat, total gravity he brought to everything, "that Colonel O'Neill and I did not perish in a manner that would have required more paperwork than our current situation promises."

"See, now that's a working theory I can get behind." O'Neill pointed at him with two fingers, approving. "Rule one of this mission: nobody dies, it upsets the accountants."

"I'll add it to the official briefing," Sam said, dry enough that Daniel actually laughed out loud, the sound swallowed almost immediately by the drone of the engines.

"I'm serious," O'Neill went on, warming to it now, the way he tended to when the alternative was sitting with how strange this whole thing actually was. "Rule two: if the kid offers you soup, and he apparently will, it's not soup, it's paste, and you should still eat it, because apparently it works."

"You're building an entire protocol around one rescue," Daniel said.

"I'm building an entire protocol around the fact that none of this makes sense and I'd like at least two rules I can actually follow." He leaned his head back against the seat, closing his eyes, though nobody at the table believed for a second he intended to actually sleep. "Kid's seventeen, Daniel. Flying a ship older than recorded history, with ninety people frozen in the basement, and he still stopped to make sure Teal'c and I had blankets. I don't need a theory for that part. That part I understand just fine."

Nobody had a response to that. The plane droned on, and they landed no closer to an actual working answer than they'd taken off, which, by that point, nobody found especially surprising.

✳

Sunnydale did not look, from the ground, like a town sitting directly beneath two alien structures. That was the first thing that struck all four of them, stepping off the plane onto a runway that had very clearly never been designed to receive a C-130: ordinary strip malls stretching off toward the horizon, ordinary traffic humming along an ordinary boulevard, an entirely unremarkable Californian afternoon unfolding underneath a sky that had stopped being ordinary five days ago.

Then all four of them actually looked up, and the ordinary afternoon stopped mattering at all.

"Okay, no." O'Neill stopped dead on the tarmac, head tipped all the way back, one hand coming up to shade his eyes like that was going to help. "No, that's — Carter, tell me I'm not seeing what I'm seeing, because what I'm seeing is a moon that wasn't there last Tuesday."

"You're seeing it." Sam had gone very still beside him, laptop bag slipping half off one shoulder, entirely forgotten. "I'm seeing it too. I've been seeing it on satellite photos for five days and it is worse in person, sir, it is so much worse in person."

"That's no moon," O'Neill said, slowly, unable to help himself, the grin already fighting its way onto his face despite the circumstances.

"Indeed," Teal'c said, flatly, tilting his own head back, his usual composure fracturing for one visible, startling second into something that, on anyone else, would have simply been called gawking — and delivering the word with the exact, practiced precision of a man who understood the reference completely, had understood it the instant O'Neill opened his mouth, and had chosen, deliberately, to give it nothing at all in return. "A moon does not possess visible architecture."

"It's got — are those docking bays?" Daniel had his hand shielding his eyes too now, squinting hard, already fumbling for the small notebook he carried everywhere out of habit. "Sam. Sam, is that thing rotating?"

"Parts of it, yeah." Sam's voice had climbed half an octave without her seeming to notice. "Different sections on different rotational periods, near as I can tell from the photos, which, again, did not remotely prepare me for actually standing under it."

"And the ship." O'Neill pointed, jabbing a finger at the smaller, closer shape hanging matte-grey against the blue sky, his voice rising with something that wasn't quite panic and wasn't quite delight and was mostly just pure, undiluted disbelief. "The ship I already met. The ship, I have personally been inside of, dying, very recently. And it is still doing this to me every single time I look at it."

"This is the part where I remind everyone," Daniel said, not looking away from the sky, "that we work for the government, and the government's official position, as of this morning, was that we were coming here to 'assess a situation.'"

"This isn't a situation, Daniel, this is a skyline." O'Neill finally tore his eyes away long enough to glare at the rest of the tarmac, at the strip malls, at the woman walking her dog past a Starbucks without so much as glancing upward, her attention fixed entirely on her phone. "How is nobody — how does an entire town just not look up? I've been standing here forty seconds and I already want to lie down."

"Denial's a hell of a drug," O'Neill added, watching the same woman disappear around the corner, still resolutely not looking up.

"There's likely a compulsion involved," Daniel said, finally managing to drag his own attention down from the sky, scanning the rest of the skyline with the reflexive, cataloguing attention of a man who'd spent a career learning to notice what everyone else had trained themselves to ignore, though his eyes kept drifting helplessly back upward every few seconds despite himself. "A town built on active supernatural convergence points usually develops some kind of collective blind spot. Self-preserving denial, or something actively suppressing awareness. I'd want to study it properly, given time."

"You'll get your chance," Sam said, checking her own equipment with hands that weren't quite as steady as she probably wanted them to be. "We've got about six hours of daylight and apparently four separate directions to go looking."

They split off from there, each toward a different piece of a puzzle nobody had been able to properly brief them on, because nobody — not Hammond, not the analysts back at the SGC, not even Sam herself, armed with the best data anyone currently had — actually could have.

✳

Teal'c found Oz behind the Bronze, sitting on an overturned milk crate in the gravel lot where the band loaded in, tuning a guitar with the unhurried focus of someone who had decided that whatever else was happening in the sky above him, the show was still happening tonight, on schedule, as promised.

"You are Oz," Teal'c said. It was not, in any real sense, a question.

"Yep." Oz didn't look up from the tuning peg, gave it a careful quarter-turn, listened to the note settle with an expression of total, undivided concentration.

Teal'c watched him work for a moment, taking in the details the way he took in everything: the guitar case propped against the wall, the amp half-loaded through the club's back door, a young man unbothered by two impossible objects hanging visibly in the sky not sixty feet above his head.

"I require the location of Xander Harris."

Oz considered this with the same unhurried focus he'd given the tuning peg, plucked the low E string once more, satisfied now, and finally looked up. "No clue," he said.

Teal'c inclined his head, accepting the answer with the same gravity he'd have given a considerably more complicated one. "I see."

"He moves around a lot lately," Oz added, after a beat, apparently deciding the silence had earned itself a small amount of further elaboration. "Has a spaceship now."

"So I am given to understand."

"Changes things. Schedules, mostly." Oz set the guitar down across his knees, looked up at Teal'c properly for the first time, taking his measure with the same unbothered directness Teal'c had given him. "You're not from around here."

"I am not."

"Figured." Oz nodded slowly, like that settled something. "You want a soda? We've got a cooler."

"I do not require refreshment."

"Cool." Oz picked the guitar back up. Neither of them said anything else. Teal'c stood a moment longer, taking in the club's dim, unhurried interior through the propped-open door: string lights, a stage half set, the particular ordinary calm of a place getting ready for an ordinary Tuesday night — and then, satisfied the conversation had reached its natural conclusion, went to look elsewhere.

✳

Giles had four seconds of warning before Daniel Jackson appeared in the library doorway looking like a man who had found a second home before he'd even finished crossing the threshold.

"You have primary sources," Daniel said, by way of introduction, already halfway to the nearest shelf, fingers reaching. "Actual primary sources — is this Sumerian? This can't be Sumerian, nobody's ever properly catalogued a demonology text in Sumerian, the field's spent thirty years assuming most of that material was lost—"

"It's a later transliteration," Giles said, standing, reaching for his glasses with the specific eagerness of a man who very rarely got to have this conversation with anyone who understood what he was talking about. "Though the original source material is, I believe, older. I've long suspected a connection to some of the pre-dynastic fragments you people apparently have access to, if even half of what's rumored about your program's holdings is accurate."

Daniel turned to look at him properly, fully, for the first time since entering the room. "You know about the program?"

"I know a great deal more than I'm meant to, about a great many things," Giles said, and something that might, in a considerably less careful man, have been called a grin tugged at the corner of his mouth. "Sit down, Dr. Jackson. I have a very strong feeling we're going to be here a while."

They were. Books came off shelves in stacks. Coffee went cold, untouched, on the corner of the table. Somewhere in the second hour, Daniel produced a small leather notebook from his bag and began cross-referencing a symbol Giles had never managed to place against something he'd apparently seen carved into a temple wall on another planet, and the conversation, from there, simply never stopped building on itself.

"This glyph," Daniel said, tapping a page, "this exact configuration. I've seen variants of it. Not identical, but close enough that I don't think it's coincidence. There's a theory some of us have been developing, that certain terrestrial mythological traditions may have been seeded, or at least influenced, by contact with—"

"The Ancients," Giles finished, and something in his own voice had gone quiet and careful, the voice of a man circling a theory he'd never had anyone credible to test it against. "I've suspected something of the sort for years. There are fragments — pre-Sumerian, even — that describe beings who arrived from the sky and departed again, leaving behind knowledge nobody quite knew what to do with."

"Can I see them?"

"You may see all of it," Giles said, already pulling another volume from the shelf. "I rather suspect neither of us is leaving this room particularly soon."

Neither of them noticed the light changing outside the windows, gold sliding slowly toward orange, then toward the deep blue of early evening, absorbed in a conversation neither one had realized, until this exact afternoon, he'd been waiting years to have.

✳

Sam found Willow in an empty classroom two doors down from the library, and stopped dead in the doorway, one hand flying up flat against the frame like her body had made the decision to brace for something before her brain had finished processing what it was bracing against.

"Oh, what—" The word was out before she could stop it, sharp and startled, entirely unguarded. Nothing in six years of gate travel, host bodies, or alien technology had built her a reference point for what was sitting at that desk: geometric, faceted, faintly luminous in the low evening light, a presence that read, at the most basic instinctive level a human brain could offer, as not organic in any sense Sam's training had ever needed to categorize before. It took her a visible, deliberate effort to keep her hand from drifting toward a sidearm she wasn't even carrying, and a full three seconds before she trusted her own feet enough to finish the step she'd started.

"That's—" She heard her own voice trying to climb back down out of the register it had just jumped into, working hard to sound like a person who did this sort of thing every day. "Okay. That's a new one. Even for me. And I've had a genuinely strange couple of years."

Willow's whole geometric frame went very still, some of the light behind her eyes dimming half a shade, the closest thing to visibly flinching a body built like hers could apparently manage. "Yeah," she said, quiet, "I get that reaction a lot from people who aren't from around here."

"I didn't mean it as—" Sam stopped herself, made herself actually look, properly, rather than simply cataloguing the wrongness of it. Underneath the strangeness, something in the tilt of the head, the patient way she was waiting for Sam's verdict, still read, unmistakably, as a teenage girl bracing for rejection. "Sorry. That was — I've walked into some genuinely bizarre rooms in my life, and that was still a new personal record. Can I come in anyway?"

"Yeah." Some of the tension eased out of the geometric shoulders. "Most people just leave."

Sam came in, closed the door gently behind her, and made herself sit down in the chair across the desk like it was the most ordinary thing in the world, even though every scientific instinct she owned was still quietly screaming that it categorically was not. "I'm told you might be able to explain some things I've spent the last several days failing to explain to myself."

"Maybe." Willow gestured at the chair, some of the visible effort easing once it was clear Sam intended to stay. "Fair warning, though, I don't really have a mechanism to give you. I'm guessing that's the part you actually want."

"It's usually the part I want, yeah." Sam set her bag down at her feet, deliberately, methodically, giving her hands something ordinary to do. "Try me anyway. I've been wrong about not having a framework before. Recently, actually."

Willow talked. She talked for longer than she'd expected to, about Nada, about the Anomaly, about the strange doubled feeling of thinking a thought and not being entirely certain, in the moment, whose it had started out as. Sam listened with the quality of attention she gave any new set of data: the shock from the doorway folding itself, piece by visible piece, into something closer to careful, patient cataloguing, the occasional clarifying question asked in a tone that made it clear she was actually listening rather than simply waiting for her turn to speak.

"There's no energy signature I can point to," Sam said, eventually, setting down the small scanner she'd been quietly running, its readout stubbornly, consistently blank. "No waveform. No particle interaction. Nothing that would show up on any instrument I've ever built or used in fifteen years of doing this. That should mean, by every rule I've built my entire career on, that it isn't real."

"And now?"

"And now I'm sitting across from someone who's clearly not lying to me, describing something detailed and internally consistent and emotionally coherent that I have absolutely no framework for at all." Sam was quiet a moment, turning the scanner over in her hands, not looking at it. "I don't like not having a framework. I want you to know that's not a criticism of you. It's just — an uncomfortable place for me to be standing."

"Yeah." Something in Willow's tone warmed, close to a real laugh, small but genuine, standing in for an expression she no longer had a face left to make. "Welcome to my whole week, basically. I don't have a framework either. I just have — her. Whatever her actually turns out to mean."

"I'm not saying I believe in magic," Sam said, carefully, testing the shape of the sentence like she was handling it for the first time. "I'm saying I believe you. Those might not be the same thing yet. I'd like to keep talking, if that's alright, not to explain it away, not to reduce it to something I can put in a report. Just to actually understand it, on its own terms, for as long as that takes."

"I'd like that," Willow said, and meant it more than the three words quite managed to carry. "Nobody's really asked to understand it before. Mostly people just want to know if it's dangerous."

"Is it?"

Willow considered the question honestly, for a long moment, feeling Nada's quiet presence settle somewhere behind her own thoughts, patient, listening too.

"I don't think so," she said, finally. "I think it's just — big. And I'm still figuring out how to carry something that big without it changing who's doing the carrying."

Sam nodded slowly, and didn't offer easy reassurance, which Willow found, somehow, more reassuring than easy reassurance would have been.

✳

Xander found Jack O'Neill already sitting on the school's front steps when he got there, like he'd known exactly where to wait without anyone telling him.

"Nice campus," O'Neill said, not looking up, squinting out at an unremarkable stretch of parking lot and flagpole. "Very 'ordinary town, nothing to see here.' I respect the commitment to the bit."

"You get used to it." Xander sat down beside him, close enough for conversation, careful enough to leave real room between them. "So. You wanted to talk, or you just genuinely like sitting on concrete steps outside high schools like some kind of off-brand John Bender."

"Bit of both, honestly." O'Neill studied him for a moment, the kind of look Xander was starting to recognize as distinct from most of the looks he got these days: not suspicious, not wary, just thorough, unhurried, the look of a man taking his time deciding something important. "You did good out there. With the rescue. I don't say that a lot, so you might wanna write it down somewhere."

"Noted. Framing it, actually. Might get it laminated, put it right next to my participation trophy from Little League and my third-place ribbon from the Sunnydale science fair, which, side note, should've been first, my volcano was objectively superior."

"You should also know," O'Neill went on, easy, conversational, the kind of tone that made whatever came next land harder for the setup, "that I've met a lot of people over the years who talk fast and joke a lot because it's just funnier that way, better company, keeps things light. And I've met a smaller handful who do it because if they slow down for even one second, something underneath's gonna catch up to them faster than they can outrun it." He let that sit in the air a moment, watching Xander's face now, without pretending not to. "Which one are you?"

Xander felt the automatic deflection queue up in his throat, fast and easy and well-worn, ready to fire before he'd even consciously chosen it: some version of both, obviously, I contain multitudes, it's very Walt Whitman of me — and something in O'Neill's steady, patient, unbothered silence made the joke die before it fully formed, left him sitting there with nothing but the plain, undressed truth of it.

"I don't actually know," he admitted, quieter than he'd meant to say anything out here. "Some days it's both. Some days I can't tell which one's driving. I used to think I was just — the funny one. The guy who wasn't good at anything except making everyone laugh so nobody noticed I wasn't good at anything. And now I've got this whole other set of memories where I was good at something, actually good, and I don't know how to hold both of those at the same time without one of them feeling like a lie."

O'Neill nodded slowly, like that was more or less exactly the answer he'd expected. "Don't kid a kidder, kid," he said, quiet, almost gentle, and didn't push any further into it. "That tracks."

He didn't say anything else about it. Didn't offer advice, didn't reach for a hand on the shoulder, didn't try to turn the moment into a Moment. Just sat there beside him on the concrete steps for a good long while, comfortable in the silence, watching the flagpole rope tap gently against metal in a breeze that smelled like cut grass and somebody's distant barbecue, filing something away behind his own easy grin for a much later conversation: one about a version of himself, twenty-some years younger, considerably angrier, more broken than he generally let himself remember being, standing at the edge of a mission he'd fully expected to be one-way.

"So," Xander said, eventually, grateful for a change of subject even as some part of him understood, dimly, uncomfortably, that nothing he'd just said had actually gone unnoticed. "Wanna see the spaceship? Fair warning, it's less Millennium Falcon, more — I don't actually have a good comparison, it's kind of its own thing, which I realize defeats the purpose of a comparison, but here we are."

"Thought you'd never ask," O'Neill said, standing, halfway to a grin that reached all the way up into his eyes this time.

✳

They reconvened at dusk outside the school, gathering in ones and twos on the front steps as the sky overhead went from gold to a deep, bruised purple, all present except one.

Willow arrived first, coming around the corner of the building still faintly luminous in the fading light, and O'Neill, who had been in the middle of a sentence to Sam about something entirely unrelated, simply stopped talking mid-word.

He stood up. He pointed, with the full length of his arm, the exact gesture of a man flagging down an aircraft carrier from several miles off. "Okay," he said, loud, delighted, entirely unguarded, "so it's not just the one of you."

"Yep," Willow said, coming to a stop a few feet off, visibly bracing for whatever came next.

"No, this is — this is genuinely great, actually, I want that on the record. Carter." He didn't look away from Willow, snapping his fingers twice in Sam's general direction. "Carter, are you seeing this. This is a whole situation. There's a geometry situation happening right in front of us."

"I noticed earlier, sir, yes."

"You noticed." O'Neill finally tore his eyes away long enough to give Sam a look of pure betrayal. "You had this information and you let me walk out here unprepared? For cryin' out loud, Carter, that's the kind of thing you lead with."

"I wasn't sure how to bring it up naturally."

"There is no natural way to bring this up, Carter, that's rather the point of it." He turned back to Willow, who had gone, by this point, from bracing to something close to genuinely delighted by the sheer scale of the reaction. "I apologize for the pointing. I've been reliably informed the pointing is rude. I'm doing it anyway because I think the situation calls for it."

"The pointing's fine," Willow said, and found, to her own quiet surprise, that she meant it, and that something in her chest had eased considerably at being met with noise and delight rather than the careful, controlled composure most new people brought to this exact moment. "Everybody's real quiet about it, mostly. This is kind of refreshing, actually."

"Well, I contain multitudes," O'Neill said, "and apparently none of them do subtlety."

He was still recovering from that, more or less, dropping into an easy, delighted orbit around the whole fact of her, peppering her with genuinely curious questions about geometry and light and whether she got cold, when Buffy came around the same corner a few minutes later, and the entire performance started over again from scratch, louder.

"Oh, come on—" O'Neill was on his feet again before he'd finished the sentence, both hands coming up in a gesture that took in the whole of her at once. "There's two of you doing this? Is this a theme? Am I gonna keep finding more?"

"Just the two of us," Buffy said, dry, coming to a stop beside Willow with the specific ease of someone who'd clearly heard the first round of this happening from around the corner and had decided, wisely, to simply let it happen rather than try to soften the landing.

"Thank God, honestly, I don't think my heart could take a third one today." He sat back down heavily, shaking his head, grinning the whole time despite himself. "You two are gonna be a genuine highlight of my year, I want you to know that. I've fought Goa'uld System Lords with less presence than you're currently bringing to a high school parking lot."

"I'll take that as a compliment," Buffy said.

"It absolutely was one." O'Neill glanced between the two of them, something warmer than the joking settling in underneath it now. "For what it's worth — and I know it's probably not worth a ton, coming from a guy who's known you both for a grand total of four minutes — you're gonna be just fine. Both of you. I've got a pretty good radar for this sort of thing."

Neither of them had a ready answer for that. It sat there in the gathering dark a moment, warm and a little surprising, before O'Neill, visibly uncomfortable with his own sincerity, cleared his throat and reached for a joke to cover the retreat.

"Anyway. Does either of you know where Xander's spaceship keeps the pie situation, because I've heard rumors and I intend to investigate them personally."

✳

"Where's Daniel," O'Neill said, a while later, looking around the small assembled group with the weary, practiced patience of a man who had asked this exact question, in this exact tone, on more missions than he cared to accurately count.

Nobody answered right away. Sam checked her watch. Teal'c said nothing, which was its own kind of eloquent shrug. Then, from the direction of the library, faint but unmistakable, drifting out through a window someone had left cracked open against the evening warmth, came the sound of two voices arguing — happily, enthusiastically, at considerable volume — about the precise etymology of a word neither of them had known existed three hours earlier.

"Ah," O'Neill said, already turning that way. "There he is."

Nobody moved to interrupt them right away. It seemed, somehow, to everyone standing there in the gathering dark beneath two impossible shapes crossing a purple sky, like the kindest thing they could do: to just let it run a little longer, this small, ordinary, delighted noise, before the whole rest of it caught up with all of them again.
