Ship of the Line - Convergence

entry one



Chapter 1: The Costume



The first thing Xander noticed about Ethan's Costume Shop was that everything cost more than it looked like it should.

The second was that the place smelled like somebody had locked a theater wardrobe in a basement for twenty years and opened the door that morning.

The third was Buffy.

She stood in front of a long dress the color of old champagne, one hand resting on the sleeve as if it might bolt if she moved too quickly. Lace climbed the bodice. The skirt had enough fabric to hide a marching band.

Xander looked from the dress to the price tag, then back to Buffy.

“You're going to need a wider front door.”

“Isn't it beautiful?”

“That's one word. ‘Load-bearing’ is another.”

Buffy ignored him. She had the look she got whenever Angel entered a room: half dreamy, half determined not to be caught dreaming. Xander had learned that commenting on that look was like checking whether a stove was hot with his face.

Willow hovered at Buffy's shoulder with a sealed costume bag hugged against her chest. The smiling model on the insert wore a short black skirt, a fitted top, and enough confidence to qualify as protective equipment. A folded white sheet had been clipped to the outside of the bag.

“Still going with the ghost?” Xander asked.

Willow pulled the sheet higher. “Ghosts are traditional.”

“So are eye holes.”

“I intend to have those.”

Buffy looked away from the dress long enough to give Willow an encouraging smile. “The sheet is optional.”

“The sheet is foundational.”

Xander grinned. “Willow Rosenberg: bringing structural engineering to the afterlife.”

She bumped him with one shoulder. It was gentle, but it moved him directly into a rack of plastic swords.

None of the swords survived close inspection. Neither did the pirate coat with vinyl cuffs, the rubber monster feet, or a military uniform whose manufacturer had decided that every soldier in history wore the same shade of green. Xander had twelve dollars in his pocket, eight of which were supposed to last until Friday. His mother had said there was money on the kitchen counter. There had been an empty bottle, a grocery receipt, and thirty-seven cents.

He could have worn his own clothes and called himself a civilian casualty. It had thematic weight.

Buffy disappeared toward the changing rooms with the dress. Willow followed, still defending the tactical necessity of the sheet. Xander wandered toward the rear of the store, where the lighting weakened and the costumes stopped pretending anyone had organized them.

A cardboard box sat beneath a rack of mascot heads. Someone had written CLEARANCE—INCOMPLETE across one side in black marker.

That was more his neighborhood.

He found one roller skate, a foam crown, three left gloves, and something that might once have been the tail of a dinosaur. Beneath them lay a grey hooded sweatshirt. It was soft at the cuffs and a little too large, but unlike most of the stock it felt like clothing rather than an argument with petroleum.

Folded inside it were a pair of wire-framed glasses without lenses and a black object shaped vaguely like a remote control. The remote was smooth and oval, with several shallow keys and a dark circular lens at one end. It looked too well made for a toy and too useless to be anything else.

There was also a badge.

Xander wiped dust from the plastic with his thumb.

**ELI WALLACE**

**DESTINY EXPEDITION**

The photograph showed a round-faced young man in the same hoodie, wearing an expression Xander recognized. It was the expression of someone who had just made a joke in a room where nobody else had noticed they were supposed to laugh.

No character name. No show logo. No helpful picture of a spaceship.

“Eli Wallace,” Xander read aloud. “Boldly going to fix the printer.”

The black remote warmed in his hand.

One of its keys flashed green.

Xander nearly dropped it. He pressed the key again. Nothing happened.

“Battery,” he decided.

The word sounded thin in the quiet corner.

He put on the glasses. The empty frames changed nothing, but the badge had a lanyard and the remote fit comfortably in his palm. With the hoodie, it made a costume if a person was willing to grade generously.

More importantly, it looked like the costume of a guy who had reached the spaceship without needing a uniform, muscles, mystical birthright, or a family credit card.

The man had a name badge. That meant somebody had expected him to be there.

Xander tucked the remote into the sweatshirt pocket and carried the pieces to the register.

The shopkeeper watched him approach.

He was lean, dark-haired, and English in a way that made Giles seem almost insufficiently committed. His sleeves were rolled with care. The name printed on the brass plate beside the register was ETHAN RAYNE.

Ethan took the badge between two fingers.

For the first time since Xander had entered the shop, the man's pleasant expression became entirely still.

“Where did you find this?”

Xander pointed toward the clearance box. “Archaeological dig beneath the giant chicken.”

Ethan glanced toward the back corner.

“Interesting.”

“That's usually what adults say right before something becomes more expensive.”

Ethan turned the badge over. There was no manufacturer's mark on the back, only a pattern of fine lines almost hidden beneath the cloudy laminate. His thumb passed over them once.

“Who is he?” Xander asked.

“I haven't the faintest idea.”

“Strong sales pitch.”

“Some costumes tell you what a person wished to be.” Ethan placed the badge on the counter. “Others leave room for discovery.”

He picked up the black remote. Its green light did not answer him.

“How much room are we talking?”

Ethan looked at the register. No price appeared when he entered the tag number. He tried again, more slowly.

“Three dollars.”

Xander waited for the correction. It did not come.

“For all of it?”

“Consider it a contribution to the spirit of the evening.”

That sounded suspicious. Three dollars sounded stronger.

Xander paid before either could change his mind.

Ethan put the costume in a plain paper bag. As he handed it across the counter, the bell above the front door rang once.

The door had not moved.

Ethan's gaze shifted toward it.

Xander turned, but Buffy emerged from the changing room before he could decide whether a stationary doorbell ranked above or below spontaneous combustion on the Sunnydale Weirdness Index.

The dress fit her perfectly.

That should have been impossible. The sleeves had been too long on the hanger and the waist too narrow. Now the fabric sat as though someone had measured Buffy for it, and her face had gone soft with delight.

“Well?” she asked.

Xander made himself smile. “You're going to need your own weather system, but otherwise? Excellent.”

Willow stepped out behind her, saw Xander looking, and immediately pulled the folded sheet in front of her costume.

“Found something?” she asked.

He held up the bag. “Apparently I am Eli Wallace, brave warrior of technical support.”

Buffy examined the badge. “Destiny Expedition sounds important.”

“See? Finally, a costume with upward mobility.”

“You don't know what it's from?” Willow asked.

“Probably a show that aired opposite something people watched.”

Behind the register, Ethan was still looking at the bell.

Then he smiled again.

“Whoever he was,” Buffy said, handing the badge back, “at least he looks comfortable.”

That decided it.

---

By sunset, Sunnydale had filled with tiny superheroes, plastic witches, cowboys, princesses, and creatures whose taxonomy had been abandoned halfway through manufacturing.

Principal Snyder divided the high-school volunteers into groups with the grim satisfaction of a man assigning penal labor.

Xander pulled the grey hood over his head and clipped the badge to its cord. The black remote—Kino remote, according to a strip of faded tape on its underside—went into his pocket.

Buffy descended the Summers staircase in the champagne dress and briefly forgot how stairs worked. Willow followed beneath the white sheet. Only her red hair showed at the bottom.

“You know,” Xander said, “if the ghost plan is total anonymity, the hair may be a clue.”

“I can still change my mind about haunting you.”

Joyce took pictures. Buffy protested, Willow tried to hide behind a child dressed as a bee, and Xander smiled on reflex when the flash went off.

In the picture, discovered much later, Buffy looked toward a life she imagined had once been simpler. Willow's sheet concealed almost everything. Xander stood between them in a secondhand hoodie, holding a machine that would not be invented for years.

Its lens was glowing.

No one noticed.

---

Ethan locked the shop at eleven minutes past six.

The street beyond the glass remained busy with families and costumed children. He turned the sign to CLOSED, lowered the shade, and carried the Janus bust from his office to the center of the back room.

Two faces looked in opposite directions. One young, one old. Beginning and ending sharing a single stone skull.

Ethan lit the candles.

He had performed more difficult workings. More dangerous ones, certainly. This spell required breadth rather than force. Hundreds of little transformations, each shaped by expectation, vanity, fear, and the ancient human habit of becoming someone else behind a mask.

He cut his palm and let seven drops fall.

“The world that denies thee,” he began, “thou inhabit.”

The candles bent toward the statue.

“The peace that binds thee, thou corrupt.”

That was not the next line.

Ethan stopped.

The words in the open book had changed.

Ink moved slowly across the page, forming letters in a hand older than the paper.

**THE THRESHOLD THAT CALLS THEE, THOU SHALT CROSS.**

The young face of Janus watched the closed door.

The old face watched Ethan.

For perhaps the first time in his adult life, Ethan Rayne considered leaving well enough alone.

Then, because he was Ethan Rayne, he smiled and read the line aloud.

The bell above the front door rang.

Every door in the shop opened at once.



Chapter 2: Earthrise



Eli Wallace had thirteen days, six hours, and an increasingly theoretical number of minutes left to solve the problem of his own survival.

The number appeared in the corner of the display because he had put it there. He had removed it twice. Both times he had restored it within an hour.

Without the countdown, it was too easy to spend half a day following an elegant diagnostic path that ended at a dead control crystal. With it, every failed idea arrived with an invoice.

Around him, Destiny slept.

The ship was never silent. Air moved through ducts built before human beings had names for the stars. Pumps circulated coolant. Relays answered one another behind walls darkened by age. Somewhere far below, the main drive maintained a vibration so deep that Eli felt it through the soles of his shoes rather than heard it.

But the people were silent.

Young, Rush, Scott, Chloe, Greer, TJ, Camile—all of them lay in stasis while Destiny crossed the emptiness between galaxies. If Eli repaired the last pod, he would join them. If he did not, the ship would wake the others years later and find whatever remained of him.

He leaned closer to the open panel.

“Come on,” he muttered. “You are a box designed to make time stop. I am trying very hard not to take that personally.”

The bypass refused to initialize.

Eli stared at it. He had reached the stage of exhaustion where anger required planning.

A small black Kino floated near his shoulder, recording the work. Its lens adjusted as he sat back.

“Attempt eighty-three,” he told it. “Still bad.”

The deck moved.

Not the familiar shift of Destiny dropping from faster-than-light travel. That came with a pressure in the bones and the sense of a sound arriving before it was made. This was sharper. The ship twisted once around an axis that did not exist.

Every light in the compartment went white.

Eli struck the deck with one knee. The Kino bounced from the wall and righted itself.

Then the drive went quiet.

The silence was so complete that Eli forgot the pain in his knee.

“No,” he whispered.

Emergency lighting should have activated. Alarms should have filled the compartment. Instead, the main lights returned one bank at a time, steady and warm. The displays around the open stasis pod filled with Ancient status reports too quickly for him to follow.

The deep vibration returned beneath his feet.

Different.

Destiny was no longer at FTL.

Eli reached the nearest console. Navigation refused to show a destination. It displayed stars, discarded the solution, displayed them again. The pattern looked familiar in a way that made no sense. The ship had crossed more galaxies than Earth could see clearly. Familiar stars were not an available option.

Another light entered the room.

Sunlight.

It lay across the deck in a bright gold bar from the open hatch, clean and strong and wrong.

Eli followed it into the corridor.

Destiny's observation deck was three levels away. He ran the first flight of stairs and nearly fell on the second. The Kino pursued him, bumping the railing at each turn.

He passed no one.

He knew he would pass no one. The knowledge still hurt.

The observation-deck doors opened before him.

Earth hung outside the glass.

There was no moment in which Eli wondered what planet it was.

He knew the blue of the oceans. He knew the broad turn of cloud over the Pacific and the brown-green edge of North America coming into daylight. He knew the white curve of the atmosphere because every photograph of home taken from space had trained him to know it before he ever understood that home could become unreachable.

The Moon stood beyond it, small and hard and exactly where the Moon belonged.

Eli stopped breathing.

His hand found the glass.

“Mom.”

The word clouded the window in front of him.

For several seconds—or several minutes; time had become unreliable—he was certain the ship had done this to him. A simulation. A neurological event. A final kindness generated by a system that had watched him fail eighty-three times and calculated the humane response.

He laughed once. It broke in the middle and became something else.

Earth remained.

There were lights along the night side. Weather over the Gulf. Sunlight on the Sierra Nevada. There was California, impossibly small beneath him. Somewhere down there his mother was—or had been, or would be, depending on which impossible answer the universe had selected.

Eli pulled himself away from the glass.

Wonder could wait. Disbelief could wait. If this was real, every person in stasis needed him to prove it.

He reached the observation-deck console and requested an astronomical fix.

Destiny returned Sol immediately.

G-class main-sequence star. Eight planets. One inhabited world. One natural satellite. The system geometry matched.

He requested an orbital solution.

The display hesitated.

Destiny was thirty-five thousand kilometers above Earth's surface. Its velocity relative to the ground was effectively zero. Its position remained fixed above the western coast of North America—but not above the equator, where a geostationary orbit could have explained it.

The ship was centered over Southern California and maintaining its position through a field Eli had never seen.

Navigation attempted to classify the field, produced a column of symbols that did not belong to any subsystem he knew, and gave up.

“Okay,” Eli said. His voice shook. “Earth first. Impossible hovering later.”

He opened passive communications.

The display filled so quickly that he recoiled.

Radio. Television. Navigation beacons. Military radar. Civilian air traffic. Satellites trading timing data. Thousands upon thousands of human voices stacked across the spectrum.

After the sparse signals of distant settlements and the endless dark between galaxies, the noise of Earth was almost physical.

Eli selected a television carrier at random.

An advertisement appeared on the screen. The resolution was terrible. A man with too much hair held up a cellular phone the size of a brick.

Eli stared.

He changed channels.

A news presenter discussed negotiations in Washington. The graphic in the corner looked like it had been designed by someone discovering computer animation in real time.

He changed again.

A local station advertised Halloween programming for that evening.

The date ran beneath it.

**OCTOBER 31, 1997.**

Eli read it three times.

His mother was alive.

Not merely alive. Younger. Healthy, perhaps, depending on how early the illness had begun. Eli himself was somewhere below, a child who had never solved the problem that brought him to Icarus Base, never dialed the ninth chevron, never seen Destiny.

Young was serving somewhere on Earth. Rush had not lost Gloria. Scott, Chloe, Greer, TJ—every sleeping person on the ship had another life beneath them, unfinished and unaware.

Eli's relief folded in on itself.

If he woke them all, what happened?

If he contacted Earth, what happened?

If he did nothing, what happened when the field failed or Destiny moved or somebody on the ground noticed an Ancient ship parked above California?

He looked at the countdown still hovering in the corner of his display.

Thirteen days, six hours.

That problem had just become very small.

Eli ran.

The stasis bay doors opened on rows of green indicators.

He did not go to Rush. Rush might understand the ship faster than anyone alive, but understanding was not the first requirement.

He did not go to Chloe, though the desire to see her awake and breathing and able to see Earth hurt like a hand around his ribs.

He went to pod fourteen.

Colonel Everett Young lay behind the glass, face slack with suspended time.

Eli placed both hands on the controls.

“Destiny,” he said, “begin revival.”

The ship requested confirmation.

“Commanding officer. Everett Young. Do it.”

The indicator changed from green to amber.

Warmth entered the pod. Medical systems began the slow work of returning a human body to itself.

Only then did Eli turn toward communications.

“Now,” he said to the sleeping commander, “we call home.”



Chapter 3: The Commander



Revival required twenty-seven minutes under ideal conditions.

Pod fourteen had not experienced ideal conditions in several years.

Destiny estimated thirty-four.

Eli spent the first six confirming that Young's vital signs were rising normally. He spent the next four trying to persuade the ship to open a communications search without flooding the display with every radio station on Earth.

He spent another minute looking at the date.

1997.

Every time he looked away, some part of him expected the numbers to correct themselves.

They did not.

The SGC existed. It had been operating in some form since the first Abydos mission, though Eli did not know how much of its later communications architecture existed this early. The frequencies in Destiny's expedition files came from more than a decade in the future. The encryption did not exist yet. Some of the satellites they referenced were not in orbit.

He could call NORAD.

That sentence would have sounded cooler if the situation had contained ten percent less terror.

Destiny had already identified Cheyenne Mountain through military signals and public records. Eli built a narrow transmission aimed at the complex, then hesitated over the message field.

If he announced an Ancient warship to the wrong duty officer, he could start a panic before Young opened his eyes.

If he waited, Earth might detect them first.

The ship answered that question by highlighting three ground-based systems that had begun tracking the anomalous contact above California.

“Right,” Eli said. “No pressure.”

He prepared a short identification burst but did not send it. Young needed to own the contact. That was not only military protocol. It was the difference between a terrified civilian making claims and the commanding officer of the expedition reporting an impossible emergency.

Pod fourteen chimed.

Eli was back beside it before the second tone.

The amber light turned white. Frost retreated from the glass. Young's chest rose sharply and stopped.

“Come on.”

It rose again.

The pod opened with a rush of cold vapor.

Young convulsed once, dragging air into lungs that had forgotten urgency. Eli caught his shoulder before he could pitch forward.

“Easy. Colonel, easy.”

Young's eyes opened without focus.

“Eli?”

“Yeah.”

His hand closed hard around Eli's forearm. “What happened?”

“The pod is stable. You're okay.”

“Why am I awake?”

There was no useful gradual version.

“We're at Earth.”

Young stared at him.

“Destiny dropped out, or moved, or something moved us. I checked the stars, the Moon, the atmosphere, satellite navigation, broadcasts—it's Earth.”

Young's grip tightened. “When?”

“1997.”

For the first time, Young's expression lost all command shape.

Eli helped him sit on the edge of the pod. Young's muscles trembled beneath the thin stasis garment, but his voice had already begun flattening into the tone he used when emotion threatened to interfere with the next necessary decision.

“Crew status.”

“All pods green. Nobody else awake.”

“Ship status.”

“Life support stable. Main drive available. Navigation can't explain our position. We're holding about thirty-five thousand kilometers above Southern California without orbital velocity.”

“Holding over what?”

“A town called Sunnydale.”

Young absorbed that as if Sunnydale might be the familiar part.

“Hostile contacts?”

“None in space. Earth tracking stations are starting to notice us.”

“Why did you wake me?”

The question was not accusation. Young wanted Eli's reasoning.

“Because you're the commander.”

Something moved behind Young's eyes.

He looked past Eli at the rows of pods.

“You didn't wake Rush.”

“No.”

“Good.” Young tried to stand. His knees failed immediately.

Eli caught him again. “Still thawing.”

“I noticed.”

“I can bring the display here.”

“I want to see it.”

Eli knew what he meant.

They made the journey slowly. Young leaned on him for the first corridor, then used the wall. By the second stairwell he could walk without either, though his face had gone grey with effort.

At the observation deck, the doors opened.

Young stopped.

Earth had turned while Eli worked. The western coast lay closer to night. Cities had begun illuminating the land beneath them, faint nets of gold spread along roads that both men knew.

Young approached the glass.

He did not touch it. He stood at attention without appearing to realize he had done so.

“Show me.”

Eli brought the verification onto the main display: stellar fix, lunar position, atmospheric composition, geographic match, satellite identities, time signals.

Young read everything.

“Could be an alternate Earth,” he said.

“Could be.”

“Simulation.”

“I checked internal processing. If Destiny is generating it, it's hiding the resource use from itself.”

“Time travel.”

“The date says yes. Everything I know about physics says stop saying that.”

Young almost smiled. It vanished before it arrived.

“Our younger selves?”

“Down there, probably.”

“Do not access them. Do not search for families. Nobody else wakes until we establish command authority and understand what happened.”

Eli's mother rose in his mind so vividly that he could see her at the kitchen table, pretending not to watch him eat.

“Understood.”

Young looked at him.

Eli corrected himself. “Yes, sir.”

“You said Earth is tracking us.”

Eli opened the alert. “Three systems have partial returns. None of them agree about our size. We're too high for most radar and in the wrong place for every orbit they understand.”

“Stargate Command?”

“Active. General Hammond should be in command by now.”

“Should?”

“It's 1997. My historical briefings were less detailed than I suddenly wish they were.”

Young considered the planet again.

“If George Hammond commands the SGC, we report to him.”

Relief arrived so sharply Eli nearly swayed. “I have a transmission ready.”

“Let me see it.”

The draft appeared.

**CHEYENNE MOUNTAIN COMPLEX. ATTENTION STARGATE COMMAND. REQUEST SECURE CONTACT. COLONEL EVERETT YOUNG, USAF. DESTINY EXPEDITION.**

Young read it twice.

“Add Hammond's name. Include my service number and current biometric file. Mark the records as future-dated and therefore non-authenticating. Append a sample of the Ancient database and our sensor solution.”

“That's going to get attention.”

“That's the idea.”

Eli made the changes.

“What if Hammond isn't there?”

“Then the officer who is there will find him.”

Young straightened. He still wore the thin clothes issued for stasis. His hands shook slightly from revival. He looked exhausted, displaced, and completely certain of the next step.

“Send it.”

Eli touched the control.

The transmission left Destiny as a narrow beam aimed at Cheyenne Mountain.

---

At Stargate Command, a technician listening to a dead military channel sat upright so quickly that his headset cord caught beneath his chin.

The message repeated.

He called his supervisor. His supervisor called the control room. The control room confirmed that the transmission had arrived from above California, then failed to identify the object that had sent it.

Three minutes later, General George Hammond entered the briefing room with Colonel Jack O'Neill, Captain Samantha Carter, Doctor Daniel Jackson, and Teal'c behind him.

The identification packet glowed on the screen.

O'Neill read the dates attached to Everett Young's service record.

“Either Personnel has started predicting promotions,” he said, “or Colonel Young has twelve years of service we haven't lived through yet.”

Carter stood close to a second display filled with Ancient characters.

“Sir, this isn't a decorative sample. It describes modifications to a Stargate addressing system. Parts of it are consistent with the material we've recovered, but the gate design is older than anything we've seen.”

Daniel looked from the symbols to the image assembled from ground tracking: an immense, indistinct shape hanging above the western United States.

“How old?”

“Possibly millions of years.”

Hammond read the message again.

“Can we answer?”

The technician nodded. “They've provided a return channel.”

Hammond took the microphone.

“Unknown vessel, this is Major General George Hammond of Stargate Command. Colonel Young, if you can hear me, identify your situation.”

On Destiny, Earth answered.



Chapter 4: First Contact



Young took the microphone from Eli.

For a moment, he said nothing.

Earth filled the observation window. General Hammond waited at the other end of the channel. Every impossible part of the situation pressed toward that single point of contact, demanding an explanation Young did not have.

He began with the part he did.

“General Hammond, this is Colonel Everett Young, United States Air Force. Service number transmitted with my personnel file. I am the military commander of an international expedition aboard the Ancient vessel Destiny.”

The answer came after a brief delay.

“Colonel, your service number belongs to an active-duty officer currently stationed on this planet.”

Young closed his eyes once.

“Yes, sir. Based on the date we've received, that officer is a younger version of me.”

Eli watched the signal meter. The connection was strong enough that he could hear someone in the distant briefing room stop moving.

Hammond's voice remained level. “State the date according to your records.”

Young gave it.

“And your present date?”

“October thirty-first, 1997.”

“How did you arrive here?”

“Unknown, sir. Destiny was crossing intergalactic space with its crew in stasis. Mister Wallace remained awake to repair the final stasis pod. The ship experienced an event he cannot identify and appeared in its current position.”

Hammond did not ask whether Young understood how that sounded.

“Your crew?”

“Alive. In stasis. I am the only person Mister Wallace has awakened.”

“Why you?”

Young looked at Eli.

“Because I am his commanding officer.”

The simple answer landed harder than Eli expected. He turned back to the console before Young could see it.

“What is your ship's combat status?” Hammond asked.

Young's eyes narrowed slightly. It was the correct question, and it required an answer that could not be safely broadcast on a channel they had only invented minutes ago.

“Destiny is not hostile to Earth. I will provide a full operational assessment once we establish secure communications and verify identities on both sides.”

“Understood. Are you capable of moving away from the planet?”

“Unknown. Navigation reports that we are stationary relative to a point in Southern California despite having no valid orbit. We have not attempted maneuvering.”

“Do not attempt it until the consequences are understood.”

“Yes, sir.”

At Stargate Command, Hammond covered the microphone and looked at Carter.

“Can they be where they claim?”

Carter had divided the second display into three windows. One held the Ancient data sample. One showed the orbital solution assembled from military tracking stations. The third showed a magnified image that dissolved into interference whenever the computers attempted to sharpen it.

“Something is there, sir. The tracking data is inconsistent with any natural orbit, but all three stations place it over the same coordinates.”

“Size?” O'Neill asked.

“We don't have a clean return.”

“Estimate.”

“Several hundred meters at minimum.”

O'Neill stared at the indistinct shape. “That's a generous definition of something.”

Daniel remained focused on the Ancient text.

“The language is older,” he said. “Not simply an unfamiliar dialect. Some of the forms are closer to the oldest material from the cartouche room on Abydos.”

“Could the Goa'uld fake it?” Hammond asked.

“Not easily. They might copy symbols, but this describes a coherent addressing model. Sam?”

Carter enlarged one set of equations. “If I'm reading the mathematical portions correctly, Destiny uses a different generation of Stargate. This isn't a description of how our gate works. It's part of an engineering record written by people who assumed the reader already understood the basic technology.”

Teal'c stood with his hands behind his back, studying the future service record.

“If this is a deception,” he said, “it has been prepared with knowledge beyond that possessed by Apophis.”

“Not the same thing as impossible,” O'Neill said.

“No.” Teal'c looked at the dark shape above California. “It is not.”

Hammond uncovered the microphone.

“Colonel Young, I cannot authenticate a service history containing events that have not occurred.”

“I understand, sir.”

“Captain Carter is reviewing the technical information you sent. I want a current biometric transmission and an unedited visual channel.”

“Stand by.”

Young handed the microphone back to Eli. “Can we do that?”

“Biometrics, yes. Visual—probably. Destiny's communications system doesn't think in terms of NTSC.”

“Make it think smaller.”

“That's offensive to both the ship and television, but okay.”

The joke came automatically. Eli felt Young register it, not as humor but as evidence that something familiar remained in a world that had stopped making sense.

Eli opened Young's medical file and stripped everything except the current scan. The temporal metadata caused Destiny to attach warning symbols to every line. He sent it anyway.

The visual channel took longer. He built a converter from an old Kino-recording interface and instructed the system to compress the observation-deck feed into a format Earth's receivers could understand.

The main display changed.

For the first time, they saw the room beneath Cheyenne Mountain.

General Hammond stood at the head of a long table. Eli knew the face from photographs and memorial recordings made long after the man's death. Seeing him breathe was nearly as disorienting as seeing Earth.

Jack O'Neill stood to one side, younger and darker-haired than Eli remembered. Samantha Carter wore captain's bars. Daniel Jackson had longer hair. Teal'c bore the gold emblem of Apophis on his forehead.

They were all looking back.

O'Neill leaned toward the camera.

“You two always dress like that when you call a general?”

Young looked down at the thin stasis clothing he had forgotten he was wearing.

“We were awakened unexpectedly.”

“I've had mornings like that.”

Eli heard himself say, “Not like this one.”

O'Neill's attention moved to him. The change was almost invisible, humor giving way to assessment.

“Mister Wallace?”

“Eli.”

“You sent the Ancient material?” Carter asked.

“I selected it.”

“Can you read it?”

“Most of it.”

Carter looked at Daniel. Daniel looked offended on behalf of several years of study.

“Destiny's interfaces translate through context,” Eli explained quickly. “And I've had a lot of practice.”

“How much?”

“A few years.”

“Aboard that ship?”

“Yes.”

Carter glanced at the personnel summary. “Your file says you weren't recruited through the Air Force.”

“I solved a problem in a video game.”

O'Neill's expression suggested that deception had become more plausible again.

“It was a cover,” Eli said. “The game contained a mathematical proof related to the ninth chevron. I solved it. General O'Neill came to my house.”

The younger O'Neill pointed at himself. “I did?”

“You will.”

“Do I at least call first?”

“No.”

“That tracks,” Carter murmured.

Hammond's look ended the exchange.

“Mister Wallace, if your claim is accurate, you possess extensive knowledge of future operations.”

“Yes, sir.”

“Do not transmit any more of that information over this channel.”

“Understood.”

Young stepped closer to the console. “General, I request instructions.”

Hammond studied him through the imperfect image.

“Maintain your present position. Do not awaken additional personnel or attempt to contact anyone outside this facility. Provide passive sensor data only. We will establish a secure link and determine how to reach you.”

“Yes, sir.”

“You will also refrain from contacting your present-day counterparts or families.”

Eli's stomach tightened.

Young answered without hesitation. “Understood.”

Hammond heard the silence beside him anyway.

“Mister Wallace?”

Eli looked at the blue curve beyond the glass.

“Yes, sir.”

“I recognize what I am asking.”

That nearly undid him.

“Thank you,” Eli said. “I won't contact her.”

Hammond inclined his head. He did not ask who she was.

Carter adjusted something outside the camera's view. “Destiny, can you transmit a wider navigational data set?”

Eli assembled it. “Sending.”

New figures appeared on the SGC display.

Carter read them, frowned, and moved closer.

“Your latitude isn't compatible with a stationary orbit.”

“We noticed.”

“There has to be continuous thrust or another field holding you there.”

“The drive isn't producing it. Destiny can't classify the field.”

“Can you send the raw readings?”

“Already attached.”

She opened them. The screen broke into interference.

On Destiny, every light dimmed.

The observation glass reflected Eli's face over Earth.

For a fraction of a second, another face occupied the same reflection: younger, narrower, wearing wire-framed glasses that had no lenses.

Eli jerked backward.

“What?” Young asked.

The lights returned.

The reflection was his own.

“Nothing.”

The word came with a memory of a woman taking a photograph in a house Eli had never visited.

“That wasn't nothing.”

Before Eli could answer, the communications display filled with static.

Far below them, in a costume shop built over the oldest kind of doorway, Rupert Giles put his shoulder through Ethan Rayne's locked door.



Chapter 5: The Change



Willow had lost Xander twice that night.

The first time happened when the world lurched and every child on the street stopped pretending.

One moment Xander stood beside her in the grey sweatshirt, trying to persuade a six-year-old astronaut that four chocolate bars counted as a successful mission. The next, something without shape opened behind him.

Willow saw it because she was dead.

Not dead in the ordinary way. She had no heartbeat, no weight, and no body beneath the sheet crumpled on the pavement behind her. She could pass through walls. She could see the thin luminous trails left by frightened spirits and the darker stains gathered around things that should not have survived burial.

The opening behind Xander was none of those.

It was a line drawn through the world. On one side stood Xander Harris beneath a California streetlamp. On the other waited a corridor older than human civilization.

A man in a grey sweatshirt turned toward them from inside it.

Then Xander was gone.

The second time Willow lost him happened when she became herself again.

There had been too much between those moments: children transformed into monsters, Buffy terrified of automobiles, Cordelia inexplicably unchanged, Spike enjoying the evening far too much, and Giles going white when Willow managed to explain who owned the costume shop.

Now Willow stood inside Ethan's back room, alive and breathless, with the white sheet clutched around the outfit she had spent the entire evening pretending she had not purchased.

The statue of Janus stood between Giles and Ethan.

Every candle leaned toward it.

“You don't know what you've done,” Giles said.

His voice frightened Willow more than the things outside had. It contained none of the careful patience he used in the library. This was the man Ethan called Ripper—the part of Giles that knew exactly how much pain a human body could hold and had already decided Ethan's could hold more.

Ethan wiped blood from his mouth and smiled from the floor.

“On the contrary. Half the town is having a splendid identity crisis.”

“Xander disappeared.”

The smile weakened.

“People transformed.”

“He didn't transform here,” Willow said. “Something opened. It took him somewhere.”

Ethan looked at her properly for the first time.

“You saw it?”

“I was a ghost.”

His gaze went to the statue.

“What was his costume?”

“You sold it to him.”

“I sold hundreds tonight.”

“Grey hoodie. Glasses. A black remote. The badge said Eli Wallace.”

Ethan stopped smiling.

Giles seized his shirt and pulled him upright. “Where is he?”

“I don't know.”

“Ethan.”

“That one wasn't mine.”

Giles drove him against the wall.

“It was in your shop.”

“Which is not the same thing.” Ethan glanced toward the statue again. “The invocation changed.”

“End it.”

“By all means.”

“Now.”

Ethan's amusement returned in a smaller, more brittle form. “Break the image, break the spell. You remember enough for that.”

Giles released him and reached for the statue.

“Wait,” Ethan said.

Giles's hand stopped an inch from the stone.

“What?”

Ethan rose carefully. “If the boy merely changed, breaking it will restore him. If he crossed—”

“Then what?” Willow demanded.

The two faces of Janus looked past each other.

“Closing a door does not drag back whatever has already gone through.”

“You said you didn't know what happened.”

“I don't. That's why this is suddenly interesting.”

Giles picked up the statue.

“You have always confused interesting with unforgivable.”

He brought it down against the stone floor.

---

The first crack crossed Janus's younger face.

Destiny screamed.

The sound did not come through the air. It entered through the deck, the consoles, and Eli's bones. Ancient status text flooded every display on the observation deck.

The connection to Stargate Command vanished beneath static.

Young caught Eli before he fell.

“Report.”

Eli tried.

A boy's life opened inside him.

He remembered a yellow crayon breaking in Willow Rosenberg's hand. He remembered Jesse laughing in the Bronze, then remembered Jesse with a vampire's face, then remembered driving a stake through Jesse's heart without meaning to. He remembered a father passed out before dinner and a mother turning up the television. He remembered Buffy dead in a shallow pool and the resistance of her chest beneath his hands.

He remembered none of those things.

He remembered all of them.

“Eli.”

Young's voice came from very far away.

Eli looked at his hands. They shifted as he watched—his fingers thinner for an instant, knuckles rearranging beneath the skin, then normal again.

“There's someone else,” he said.

“On the ship?”

“In me.”

The static cleared enough for Hammond's voice to break through.

“Destiny, respond. Colonel Young, report.”

Young pulled Eli toward the console. “General, the ship is experiencing an unknown event. It began during transmission of the positional data.”

Carter answered. “We're seeing an energy surge at the point beneath you. It began at the same time as your communications disruption.”

“Source?”

“A commercial address in Sunnydale. We're trying to identify it.”

Eli knew the answer.

Not because he had ever seen the shop. Because Xander had.

Dust. Rubber. A dress too expensive for Buffy. Willow holding a white sheet. Ethan watching a black remote that did not belong in his inventory.

“Ethan's,” Eli gasped.

Young steadied him. “What?”

“Costume shop. Ethan Rayne. Xander bought—”

Pain interrupted the sentence. Eli folded against the console.

On the SGC screen, his image split into overlapping frames. One showed Eli Wallace. The other showed a dark-haired teenager in the same sweatshirt.

Daniel stepped closer. “Who's Xander?”

Eli forced himself upright.

“The person whose body this is. Or whose life. I don't know.”

“You said your name was Eli Wallace,” Hammond said.

“It is.”

The certainty lasted only until another memory arrived: Xander writing his name on a school form, pressing hard enough to tear the paper because the emergency-contact line had been left blank.

“It was,” Eli whispered.

Young turned him away from the console and looked directly into his face.

“Stay with me.”

“I don't think I can.”

“Then tell me what you know.”

Eli gripped Young's forearms. “The ship crossed. I don't know how, but it came through something connected to him. The field is centered where the spell happened.”

“Spell?” O'Neill said over the channel.

“Halloween costumes. Everybody became—” Eli laughed breathlessly. “This is going to sound insane.”

No one in either room commented.

“Transmit everything,” Young ordered.

Eli reached for the console.

Destiny no longer recognized his hand consistently. The interface brightened, faded, and returned. He opened his working directory and pushed every relevant record into the command archive: the failed pod repairs, the transit event, the Earth verification, the positional field, the communications bridge.

Then he copied command access to Young.

“You already had access,” Eli said. “But the systems I modified were still routed through me. They're yours now.”

“Eli—”

“Don't wake everybody until TJ checks what the trip did to the pods. Rush needs to see the field data, but don't let him touch the engines first.”

“I'm not taking a turnover from you.”

“You need one.”

The ship screamed again.

Earth blurred beyond the glass. For one impossible instant, Eli saw it with two sets of eyes: a home lost across billions of light-years, and a world a seventeen-year-old boy had never left.

“Tell my mom—”

He stopped.

His mother was alive below. Another Eli was alive below. Any message would become a hand reaching into a childhood that had not happened yet.

“No,” he said.

Young understood the word he had taken back.

“I'll remember her,” he said.

Eli nodded.

The camera Kino floated into the observation deck, still recording. Eli took it in both hands.

“If any of this stays,” he told it, “this is Eli Wallace. Destiny is at Earth. Colonel Young is awake and in command. Stargate Command knows we're here.”

His face flickered.

“The person coming after me is Alexander Harris. Xander. He's seventeen. This isn't his fault.”

Young moved into view beside him.

“Eli.”

“Don't make him prove he's me.”

The second crack split the older face of Janus.

Eli set the Kino down and turned toward the window.

Earth waited beyond it.

He had imagined this view in a thousand different ways. Destiny arriving to celebration. The crew descending through atmosphere. His mother seeing him step onto a stage with people who finally understood where he had been. None of those endings had included 1997, magic, or a stranger's childhood spreading through his mind.

It did not matter.

Young was awake.

The SGC knew.

The crew would not drift into the dark unwitnessed.

Eli placed his hand against the glass.

“We made it,” he said.

Giles shattered the statue.

The boy reflected in the observation window became the boy standing before it.

Xander Harris screamed.



Chapter 6: Convergence



Young caught a stranger wearing Eli's clothes.

The change happened between one breath and the next. Eli's weight vanished from Young's hands. The body that replaced it was shorter and leaner, all sharp shoulders and adolescent angles inside the grey sweatshirt. The glasses remained, though their lenses had disappeared. The identification badge struck the deck and swung from its lanyard.

**ELI WALLACE.**

The boy's scream stopped when he ran out of air.

Young lowered him before his legs could collapse.

“Medical,” Young called.

Destiny displayed the same answer on three nearby panels: no active medical personnel.

Of course.

“Colonel Young?” Hammond's voice came through the restored channel. “What happened?”

Young kept one hand on the boy's shoulder and checked his pulse with the other. Fast. Strong. Human.

“Mister Wallace is gone.”

The words resisted leaving his mouth.

“The other individual has physically replaced him.”

The boy opened his eyes.

For one terrible instant, Young expected Eli to look out through them.

Instead he saw naked panic.

“Where—” The boy turned his head and found Earth outside the glass. “Oh, God.”

“Can you understand me?”

“Colonel Young.”

Young's hand tightened involuntarily.

The boy saw it.

“I know you,” he said. “He knew you. I—” He pressed both hands against his temples. “There are too many things in my head.”

“What's your name?”

“Xander.”

“Full name.”

“Alexander Lavelle Harris.”

“Age?”

“Seventeen.”

Young looked toward the camera.

O'Neill had lost every trace of humor. Carter was speaking to someone beyond the frame, ordering a search. Hammond remained at the head of the table.

“Mister Harris,” Hammond said, “do you know where you are?”

Xander looked around the observation deck.

The answer came in layers. Destiny. An Ancient seed ship's successor. A vessel launched before Atlantis left the Milky Way. A home, a prison, a promise, a machine with failing seals in hydroponics and a damaged relay two corridors below the mess.

“Destiny,” he said.

Young's face closed.

Xander corrected desperately, “I remember it. That doesn't mean I'm him.”

“Who is ‘him’?” Hammond asked.

Xander looked at the badge on the deck.

“Eli Wallace.”

The name hurt. It did not feel like losing a fictional character or waking from a dream. It felt like discovering that someone he loved had died and that the someone was him.

Not him.

Both statements were true. Neither fit inside his head.

Young helped him sit against the base of the console.

“What is the last thing you remember as Xander Harris?”

“Halloween.”

“Be specific.”

“I was escorting kids. Buffy and Willow were with me. We bought costumes at Ethan's. There was—”

Xander closed his eyes.

He could see the street from above his own shoulder. Willow stood outside her body beneath the sheet. Buffy stared at a passing car as if it were a dragon. Behind Xander, a doorway opened where no doorway belonged.

“A spell,” he said. “Ethan did something to the costumes. Everybody turned into what they were wearing.”

O'Neill glanced at Daniel. “Any chance ‘costume spell’ means something less weird in Ancient?”

“No.”

“Didn't think so.”

Carter returned to the camera. “General, local power and communications across Sunnydale are normalizing. The anomalous energy source has collapsed.”

“And the vessel?” Hammond asked.

“Still there.”

On Destiny, Young looked toward Earth.

“The spell ended,” Xander said.

“You are certain?”

“Buffy and Willow changed back. I remember—no. I don't remember seeing it. I know it happened.” He swallowed. “That's different.”

Young crouched in front of him.

“Is Eli still present?”

Xander wanted to give him the answer he was asking for.

He searched.

Eli's memories surrounded him: his mother's kitchen, the proof hidden inside a game, Icarus Base collapsing, Rush's fury, Chloe's hand, Ginn's smile, the endless observation window. Xander could follow any of them until he forgot which life he had entered from.

But memory was not a voice. No one answered when he called Eli's name inside his own mind.

“No,” Xander said.

Young looked away.

“I'm sorry.”

“Don't.”

The word was harder than Young intended. He drew a breath.

“Don't apologize for something you didn't do.”

Xander nodded.

The kindness made the guilt worse.

Hammond gave them several seconds before speaking.

“Colonel Young, does the ship recognize Mister Harris?”

Young looked toward the console. “Can you access it?”

Xander reached up.

His fingers found a sequence he could not have explained yesterday. The interface awakened beneath his hand, displayed Eli's authorization, then overlaid it with an amber medical warning.

**IDENTITY CONFLICT. BIOLOGICAL VARIATION. COGNITIVE KEY ACCEPTED.**

Young read the Ancient text.

“It recognizes part of him.”

Carter leaned toward the screen. “Can you send that record?”

Young's expression turned flat.

“Not until he is medically stable and capable of consent.”

Carter blinked. “Yes, Colonel. Of course.”

Hammond stepped in before the moment could harden. “Mister Harris, are you injured?”

“I don't think so.”

“Do you know anyone aboard who can examine you?”

“TJ. Lieutenant Johansen. She's in stasis.”

Young's gaze moved toward the door.

“General, I recommend waking Lieutenant Johansen. We have experienced an unknown transit and a full physical transformation. I need medical support before I can certify the condition of the crew.”

Hammond considered it. “Approved. Lieutenant Johansen only. No one else until she completes her assessment.”

“Yes, sir.”

The exchange settled something in the room. Young had asked. Hammond had decided. An impossible future officer and a commander from the past had found the first intact piece of the institution they shared.

“Mister Harris,” Hammond said, “we are locating your family and associates in Sunnydale.”

Xander went cold.

“Don't call my parents.”

Hammond's eyes sharpened. “Why?”

“Because they'll either be drunk, fighting, or both. Call Rupert Giles at Sunnydale High School. Or Buffy Summers. Willow Rosenberg.”

O'Neill wrote the names down.

“Your parents don't know where you are?” Hammond asked.

“My parents don't always know when I'm in the same house.”

No one made the obvious joke. Xander wished they would.

“We will contact Mister Giles first,” Hammond said.

“Thank you.”

“Until we understand the situation, you will remain aboard Destiny under Colonel Young's protection. That is not a detention. We currently have no means of bringing you safely to the ground.”

“There are shuttles.”

The answer escaped before Xander consciously searched for it.

Young noticed.

“You can fly one?”

“Eli could.” Xander looked toward the corridor, knowing exactly how many turns separated the observation deck from the shuttle bay. “I remember how. That's not the same as having done it.”

“Then you won't be doing it tonight,” Young said.

“No argument.”

Hammond nodded. “We will determine a safe means of boarding and return as soon as possible. Colonel, wake Lieutenant Johansen and report when she is functional. Maintain current position. Passive sensors only. Keep this channel open.”

“Yes, sir.”

Young started to rise.

Xander caught his sleeve.

“Wait.”

Young looked down.

“The Kino.”

It floated near the glass, still recording.

Young retrieved it. The device displayed the last entry automatically.

Eli appeared above its projector, translucent and small. His face flickered as Xander's tried to replace it.

“If any of this stays,” the recording said, “this is Eli Wallace. Destiny is at Earth. Colonel Young is awake and in command. Stargate Command knows we're here.”

The recording continued through Eli's final warning.

“The person coming after me is Alexander Harris. Xander. He's seventeen. This isn't his fault.”

Young stopped the playback.

For a while, no one spoke.

---

Willow ran back to the street where Xander had disappeared.

Buffy followed, carrying the shoes that had become impossible to run in the instant she became herself again. They found the children restored and crying, parents arriving from every direction, and Xander's assigned group clustered around the adult volunteer from the next block.

They did not find Xander.

His paper candy bag lay beside the curb. A miniature astronaut guarded it with both arms.

“He went through the light,” the child told Buffy.

“What light?”

The astronaut pointed up.

There was nothing visible in the night sky except stars.

Willow knew better.

“He's alive,” she said.

Buffy turned toward her. “How do you know?”

Willow tried to explain what she had seen as a ghost: the corridor behind him, the older boy turning, the sense of a path completing itself. Human language had not been designed for remembering the geometry of the dead.

“I don't know where he went,” she finished. “But it wasn't death. Death didn't look like that.”

Buffy picked up the candy bag.

“Good.”

It was not relief. Not yet. It was a promise to the universe that death had better not be what happened.

Giles emerged from Ethan's shop alone.

“He's gone,” Willow said.

“Ethan?”

“Xander.”

Giles looked at the place she pointed, then at the unbroken night sky.

“The spell has ended.”

“He didn't come back.”

Behind Giles, the bell above Ethan's door rang once.

The door remained closed.

---

Young helped Xander stand.

Earth waited beyond the observation glass, unchanged by the fact that two different lives now remembered loving it.

“You asked who I am,” Xander said.

Young did not tell him that he had already answered.

Xander picked up Eli's badge. He turned it over once, then closed his hand around it.

“Xander Harris,” he said. “And I think Eli just brought you home.”



Chapter 7: Quarantine



The first rule of coming home was that nobody was allowed to call home.

Hammond delivered the order without ornament.

“Until we know what has happened, Destiny is under communications quarantine. No transmissions except through the channel to this facility. No contact with family members, present-day counterparts, civilian authorities, or other military commands.”

Young stood before the observation-deck console, one hand resting beside the microphone.

“Understood, sir.”

“Passive sensors only. Captain Carter will coordinate the data requests. Do not transmit future operational history unless I specifically authorize it.”

“Yes, sir.”

“I am assembling a boarding team. That will take time.”

Young glanced toward Xander. “We will wake Lieutenant Johansen and complete an initial medical assessment.”

“Report as soon as she is functional.”

“Yes, sir.”

The visual link closed, though the encrypted carrier remained open. Earth continued turning beyond the glass.

Xander stared at the place General Hammond's face had been.

“He seems nice.”

Young gave him a look.

“In the sense that he didn't order anyone to shoot us.” Xander rubbed both hands over his face. The glasses shifted against his fingers. “My standards have adjusted.”

“Can you walk?”

“Probably. Is this a medical question or an order?”

“Both.”

Xander pushed himself upright. The deck remained beneath him, which felt like cooperation.

Young watched every movement. Xander knew why. Eli had occupied the same space a few minutes earlier. Every time Young looked at him, recognition began and failed.

Xander wished he could stop noticing.

He knew Everett Young's command voice. He knew the man's silences, his anger, the way exhaustion drew a deeper line beside his mouth. He remembered arguments he had never witnessed and forgiveness Eli had never managed to request cleanly.

He also remembered meeting Young less than an hour ago.

Both memories insisted they were first.

“Which way?” Young asked.

Xander pointed toward the door without thinking.

“You know the route.”

“Yeah.”

“Lead.”

The corridors lit ahead of them. Destiny recognized Eli's habitual path even when the person walking it did not match the stored body. Each open door felt like a question the ship was waiting for him to answer.

They reached the stasis bay.

Xander stopped at the threshold.

Rows of pods curved away beneath amber light. Faces waited behind glass: people Eli loved, disliked, mistrusted, depended upon, failed, saved. The emotional weight arrived without context, a crowd of feelings searching for owners.

Young stepped past him.

“Johansen's pod.”

Xander knew the number. He hated knowing it.

“Thirty-two.”

Young found it. Tamara Johansen lay inside with her hands relaxed at her sides. A medical officer in suspension, unable to give consent to being woken into her own past.

Young placed his palm on the controls.

“Destiny, begin revival of Lieutenant Johansen.”

The ship requested confirmation from its commanding officer.

Young gave it.

The indicator turned amber.

“Twenty-nine minutes,” Xander said as the estimate appeared.

“Sit down.”

“I'm okay.”

“That wasn't the question.”

There were benches along the center of the bay. Xander sat.

Young remained beside TJ's pod, reading the diagnostics as if attention could accelerate them.

“Colonel.”

“What?”

“Eli meant what he said.”

Young did not turn.

“About me.”

“I heard him.”

“Right. I just—he knew you'd think this was my fault even if you knew it wasn't.”

Young faced him then. “Is that what you think?”

Xander looked down at Eli's badge in his hand.

“I put on the costume.”

“Did you cast the spell?”

“No.”

“Did you bring the ship here?”

“Not on purpose.”

“That isn't what I asked.”

Xander tried to separate memory from inference. He found the instant of transformation: a street, Willow's sheet, a child in an astronaut helmet. Then Destiny, Earth, Eli's hand on the glass. Between them lay a doorway and something vast enough to decide that distance did not matter.

“I don't know.”

“Then neither do I.” Young looked back at TJ. “Until we do, you are not responsible for Eli.”

The wording left room for a future verdict. Oddly, that made it easier to believe.

---

Hammond waited until Young's image disappeared before issuing the next order.

“Nothing from this room leaves this facility.”

The technicians answered in near unison.

“The transmission is compartmented effective immediately. All tracking data comes here. If another command asks what is above California, they are to be told the contact is under NORAD review.”

O'Neill remained beside the table. “That answer has a shelf life.”

“I am aware of that, Colonel.”

“Something that size doesn't stay a radar glitch forever.”

Carter shook her head. “It may be harder to detect than its size suggests. The hull absorbs or scatters most active returns. The stations that found it only did so because it appeared inside volumes they were already tracking.”

“So our giant ancient spaceship is stealthy by accident.”

“Not stealthy. Difficult.”

“Much better.”

Hammond looked at the map. A line descended from Destiny's impossible position to a town on the California coast.

“Sunnydale.”

Daniel read from a second screen. “Founded late nineteenth century. Spanish settlement before that. The mission records are inconsistent.”

“In what way?”

“Populations disappear from several early surveys. A church was built and abandoned without diocesan explanation. There are references to earthquakes that don't appear in regional geological records.”

Carter opened municipal statistics. “The current mortality rate is also anomalous.”

“How anomalous?” Hammond asked.

She gave him the figure.

O'Neill stopped smiling.

“That's not a town,” he said. “That's a combat zone with property taxes.”

“Mister Harris provided three names,” Hammond said. “Find them. Quietly.”

O'Neill picked up the list.

Rupert Giles. Buffy Summers. Willow Rosenberg.

“The high-school librarian, a seventeen-year-old girl, and another seventeen-year-old girl.”

Hammond's expression did not change. “That is what he gave us.”

“I'll make the calls.”

“You will not mention the Stargate or the vessel until their identities are confirmed.”

“And if they ask where Harris is?”

Hammond looked toward the tracking image.

“Tell them he is alive. For now, that is the only fact we can give them with confidence.”

---

TJ woke fighting.

Her hand struck Young's chest. Her other arm caught the edge of the pod and nearly pulled her to the floor. Young held her upright without trying to restrain her wrists.

“Lieutenant. TJ. Look at me.”

Her eyes found him.

“Colonel?”

“You're safe.”

“Why—” Her gaze moved past him to the rows of pods. “What failed?”

“Nothing in stasis. I woke you.”

TJ tried to stand. Young supported her while she tested her legs.

“How long?”

“We don't know.”

That answer reached her. “Where's Eli?”

Xander had been waiting near the far console, where she would not see him immediately. Young's eyes moved toward him.

TJ followed.

For one second her face opened with hope.

Then she saw him clearly.

“Who are you?”

Xander stood. “My name's Xander.”

“Where is Eli?”

Young answered. “Gone.”

“Gone where?”

“We don't know.”

TJ looked between them. “You woke me because Eli is missing?”

“I woke you because the ship underwent an unknown transit, Eli was physically replaced by a minor, and we are at Earth in 1997.”

She stared at him.

“I need you to perform medical assessments on all three of us and verify the stasis system.”

“You said Earth.”

“Yes.”

Young did not try to soften it. He helped her to the observation deck and let the planet do what explanation could not.

TJ stood before the glass for a long time.

When she finally turned, her eyes were wet and her expression was professional.

“Medical bay,” she said. “Both of you.”

---

Destiny's medical equipment had been designed by people who regarded written instructions as evidence of insufficient intelligence.

TJ understood more of it than she had when the expedition began. Eli understood most of what remained.

Xander remembered both facts and possessed neither skill cleanly.

He sat on an examination platform while pale light moved from his head to his feet. Ancient symbols collected above him.

“Don't translate unless I ask,” TJ said.

“I wasn't.”

“You were reading ahead of me.”

“Sorry.”

“Don't apologize. Just let me determine what you know without assuming.”

Young stood near the door wearing clothes taken from an equipment locker. They fit badly but provided more dignity than stasis garments.

TJ reviewed the scan.

“Human male. Approximately seventeen years old. No evidence of stasis damage. No indication of radiation exposure from the transit. Elevated stress markers, mild dehydration, bruising consistent with recent physical activity.”

“Halloween involved running,” Xander said.

“It usually does?”

“In Sunnydale? More than you'd think.”

TJ adjusted the neurological display. “Destiny has Eli's previous scans. Your brain isn't his.”

Young's jaw tightened.

“But?”

“But the ship is registering activity in response to Eli's personal access patterns. I can't tell whether that's memory, learned behavior, or something its systems don't have a category for.”

“Can you tell if he's in there?” Young asked.

TJ looked at Xander rather than the scan.

“No machine can answer that question.”

“I did,” Xander said quietly.

“You answered whether you could hear him. That isn't the same as proving what remains.”

She lowered the display.

“Medically, you are Xander Harris. I will treat you as Xander Harris.”

He released a breath.

TJ turned to Young. “Your turn.”

“I'm functional.”

“That wasn't the question, Colonel.”

Xander looked at Young.

Young looked back.

Neither of them smiled.

He sat on the platform.

---

The telephone rang in Giles's apartment shortly after midnight.

Buffy reached it first.

“If this is my mother, we're studying.”

There was a pause.

“This is Colonel Jack O'Neill, United States Air Force. I am trying to reach Rupert Giles.”

Buffy covered the receiver. “It's the Air Force.”

Willow stopped pacing.

Giles took the phone.

“Rupert Giles.”

“Mister Giles, I need to confirm the identity of a young man named Alexander Harris.”

Giles went still. “Where is he?”

“Alive.”

“That was not my question.”

“It's the answer I'm authorized to give until I know who I'm speaking to.”

Giles's hand tightened around the receiver.

“Then tell me how you obtained this number.”

“Harris gave us your name. We found the number ourselves.”

“Let me speak to him.”

“Soon. First, tell me what happened in Sunnydale tonight.”

Giles looked at Buffy and Willow.

“You would not believe me.”

“Mister Giles, I have spent the last hour talking to a future Air Force colonel on a prehistoric spaceship. Take your best shot.”



Chapter 8: Boarding Party



The shuttle landed at Vandenberg before dawn.

Its approach had been hidden beneath a temporary closure of military airspace, three conflicting radar notices, and a weather report promising coastal fog that never arrived. The craft descended without wings, rotors, or any visible concern for the aerodynamic opinions of Earth.

It settled inside a cleared hangar on four broad landing assemblies.

O'Neill watched from behind a line painted on the concrete.

“You know,” he said, “I've always felt spaceships should make more noise.”

The shuttle's hull ticked softly as it cooled.

Chapter 9: Necessary Awakenings

The bad news occupied every other system.

Chapter 10: The Duplicate Problem



They began with Young.

The roster contained military personnel, civilians, international partners, and several people whose legal categories had become complicated even before time travel entered the discussion. Destiny had carried survivors of the Icarus evacuation, members of the Lucian Alliance, and people added or lost across years of travel.

They started with the original expedition.

**LIVING MATCH.**

**LIVING MATCH.**

**NO CONFIRMED MATCH.**

**LIVING MATCH.**

Scott existed below them, too young to know he would wear an Air Force uniform. Greer existed. TJ existed. Brody, Volker, and Park existed. Chloe Armstrong existed somewhere in the United States, years before her father would help send her through the gate.

**ELI WALLACE — LIVING MATCH. MINOR.**

Below that:

**MARION WALLACE — LIVING MATCH.**

Chapter 12: The Accord



The interview established three things.

Second, authorization did not equal understanding.

Third, Rush considered that distinction a challenge.

The fifth brought Eli's memory forward so violently that he answered in Ancient and did not realize it until Carter touched his shoulder.

He recoiled.

“Xander.”

The observation deck returned around him.

TJ stood on his other side. Young had moved between him and Rush without anyone asking.

Chapter 13: The Town Beneath



The shuttle landed six miles outside Sunnydale on a strip of cracked concrete the county still listed as an emergency airfield.

The field had not handled an aircraft in eleven years. Its windsock had become a nest. Grass grew through the taxiway in pale, stubborn lines.

Two Air Force vehicles waited beside a temporary communications van. The personnel wore no identifying unit patches and had been told only that SG-1 was arriving aboard an experimental craft.

They obeyed the order not to stare with varying success.

The shuttle settled without sound. Dust spread outward beneath it, flattening the grass. Its hatch opened.

Xander stood at the top of the ramp.

Home smelled like dry earth, gasoline, and eucalyptus. After Destiny's endlessly recycled air, the wastefulness of wind felt impossible. It touched every part of his face at once and continued past him as if air were something a planet could afford to lose.

He descended slowly.

O'Neill waited at the bottom rather than hurrying him. Carter remained close enough to catch him without advertising the fact. Daniel was already looking toward the hills. Teal'c watched the security personnel watching the ship.

Xander reached the ground.

Nothing happened.

He remained Xander. The shuttle remained a shuttle. Destiny did not pull him back into the sky.

“One small step,” he said.

O'Neill glanced at his shoes. “Looked medium-sized from here.”

The shuttle closed itself after them.

“Maintain scheduled check-ins.”

“Yes, Colonel.”

Young hesitated for less than a second. “Xander.”

Xander touched the radio. “Still here.”

“Good.”

The connection ended.

One of the drivers opened the rear door of the first vehicle. Xander did not move.

Above them, the sky was empty blue.

He knew exactly where Destiny hung. The knowledge had nothing to do with sight. If he extended the line of his body upward, through atmosphere and sunlight and thirty-five thousand kilometers of distance, it ended at the observation deck.

Carter followed his gaze. “Can you feel the ship?”

“I know where it is.”

“Like direction?”

“Like remembering where my room is when I'm downstairs.”

She accepted the description without reaching for an instrument.

“Tell TJ if that changes.”

“And you.”

“And me.”

They drove into Sunnydale.

When Xander turned, he introduced everyone. The names sounded absurd in the school parking lot: Colonel Jack O'Neill, Captain Samantha Carter, Doctor Daniel Jackson, Teal'c. “No?”

O'Neill kept his eyes on Giles. “I want to hear him say it.”

Chapter 14: The Field Report



Buffy refused to sit in the library's only comfortable chair.

That would have made the cut on her arm the center of the room, which was ridiculous. It was barely a cut. Giles had cleaned worse injuries while she complained about algebra.

She perched on the edge of the central table instead.

Carter had bagged samples of the vampire's clothing and what little dust remained. Her instruments confirmed that the material contained organic traces while refusing to explain how a human-sized body had become less than a kilogram of residue.

Daniel filled three pages with questions and stopped asking them when Giles glared.

Teal'c stood beside the weapons cage.

O'Neill faced Giles across the table.

“Start at the beginning.”

Giles folded a clean bandage around Buffy's arm. “There is no single beginning.”

“Pick the useful one.”

“Into each generation, a Slayer is called. One girl in all the world, granted the strength and instinct necessary to fight vampires, demons, and other forces of darkness.”

O'Neill glanced at Willow and Xander. “One girl.”

“Traditionally.”

“Traditionally?”

Buffy looked at Giles.

He secured the bandage. “Buffy died last spring.”

Carter stopped labeling the evidence bag.

“For how long?”

“A few minutes,” Xander said. “Maybe less.”

O'Neill looked at him. “You were there?”

“Angel and I found her. I did CPR.”

“The Slayer line passed to another girl,” Giles continued. “Xander revived Buffy, but the calling had already occurred.”

“So there are two,” Daniel said.

“There are.”

“Where is the other one?” O'Neill asked.

“The Council has not informed me.”

“The Council,” O'Neill repeated. “That's the adult responsible for this?”

“The Watchers Council is an organization, not an individual.”

“Where?”

“England.”

O'Neill's face became very still.

“A British organization has a seventeen-year-old conducting armed patrols in California.”

“The Slayer's calling is not created by the Council.”

“Didn't ask who created it. I asked who is running the operation.”

Giles removed his glasses and began cleaning them.

Buffy had watched him do it a thousand times. This time she recognized it as an attempt to find language that would sound less terrible to someone who had never agreed the terrible parts were normal.

“I am Buffy's Watcher,” Giles said. “I train her, provide research, and advise her in the execution of her duties.”

“Who pays you?”

“The Council provides a salary.”

“Enough to run this operation?”

Giles looked around the library: public-school books, privately purchased crossbows, bandages from the chemist, weapons repaired by hand.

“Barely enough to maintain myself,” he said. “The position here provides cover and additional income. The Council reimburses very little of what is required in the field.”

O'Neill looked at Buffy.

“And what do they pay her?”

Giles stopped cleaning his glasses.

“Nothing.”

Buffy folded her arms. “I get invaluable experience for my résumé.”

No one laughed.

She hated them a little for that.

“Weapons?” O'Neill asked.

“Primarily supplied by me.”

“Medical care?”

“I provide what I can.”

“Insurance?”

“No.”

“Disability?”

Giles did not answer.

“Survivor benefits?”

“Colonel,” Giles said, “there has never been a retired Slayer.”

The sentence changed the air.

“Because they can't retire?”

“Because another is called only when the current Slayer dies.”

O'Neill looked at the shelves filled with Watcher diaries.

“And the Council is comfortable with that arrangement.”

“The Council considers the Slayer sacred.”

O'Neill glanced toward Buffy. “Do they?”

Giles did not answer immediately.

“They consider the line sacred,” he corrected. “The individual Slayer is... replaceable. When one dies, another is called.”

“Replaceable,” O'Neill repeated.

“That is the practical assumption underlying much of Council doctrine.”

“Disposable, then.”

Giles removed his glasses again but did not contradict him.

Teal'c regarded him steadily.

“They do not support a warrior. They expend one.”

Buffy slid down from the table.

“Okay, that's enough.”

O'Neill turned toward her. “Is anything we've said wrong?”

“You don't get to walk in here, see one fight, and decide you know my life.”

“No.”

The agreement stopped her.

“I know one fight,” he continued. “I know you took point because you were the most qualified. I know everybody else followed your orders when contact happened. I know you got hurt and expected to keep going because nobody was coming to relieve you.”

“There is nobody else.”

“That's the problem.”

“It's my job.”

“Jobs pay.”

“This isn't—” Buffy stopped.

She had no word that made it better.

Duty. Destiny. Calling. None explained why Giles received a cheque and she received another grave to climb into.

“If I don't do it, people die,” she said.

O'Neill's anger softened. “I believe you.”

That was worse too.

“I'm not asking permission.”

“I'm not offering it. I'm asking where the people are who should have been making sure you came back alive.”

Buffy looked at Giles, Willow, and Xander.

“They're here.”

O'Neill followed her gaze.

He saw the answer. He also saw three civilians building with library books and first-aid supplies what an ancient institution had declined to provide.

“Yeah,” he said. “They are.”

O'Neill looked toward the cemetery beyond the school.

“The local mortality rate isn't bad record keeping. It's a body count.”

Hammond closed the folder on his desk.

“Return to the library. Maintain liaison with Miss Summers and Mister Giles.”

“Yes, sir.”

“Colonel.”

“Sir?”

“Good report.”

The screen went dark.

Hammond picked up the red telephone.



Chapter 15: The Red Phone

Hammond looked at the report.

“Mr. President, there is one point I recommend you state exactly.”

“Which point?”

“An organization based in Britain has deployed an American child into an undeclared war on American soil. If it claims authority derived from the Crown, then Her Majesty needs to know what has been done in her name.”

The President repeated the sentence once, committing it to memory.

“Anything else?”

Hammond thought of Buffy returning a stake to her jacket and expecting to continue patrol while she bled.

“Yes, sir. Colonel O'Neill does not believe this is a case in which the Council attempted to support a Slayer and failed.”

“What does he believe?”

“That supporting the individual girl was never the system's purpose.”

The President's answer was immediate.

“Then we find out what was.”

The line disconnected.

Hammond remained at his desk for several seconds, one hand resting on the red telephone.

In Washington, another secure call crossed the Atlantic.



Chapter 16: Direct Means Direct



The Prime Minister had been in office for less than six months when the President of the United States informed him that an ancient British organization was deploying children into an undeclared war.

The call had begun with Destiny.

That alone would have justified waking every relevant minister in Whitehall. A vessel built by a civilization millions of years old had appeared above California carrying an international expedition from the future. Its American commander had placed himself under the authority of a secret military program operating inside Cheyenne Mountain. The ship was fixed over a town containing what the President described, with audible distaste, as an interdimensional rupture.

Then he reached the girl.

“Her name is Buffy Summers. She is seventeen years old, an American citizen, and she has been fighting since she was fifteen.”

The Prime Minister looked across the secure cabinet room at the Foreign Secretary and Cabinet Secretary. Neither man appeared to have heard the name.

“Fighting whom?”

“Vampires, primarily. Other hostile nonhuman entities as they appear.”

The Prime Minister waited for the qualification.

None came.

“Your people have confirmed this?”

“Direct observation by a United States Air Force team. Instrumental evidence. Historical corroboration. The officer in command is not given to imaginative reports.”

“And the British connection?”

“A body calling itself the Watchers Council trains and directs the girl through a British national assigned to her. It pays him. It does not pay her.”

The Cabinet Secretary stopped writing.

“Does Miss Summers serve voluntarily?” the Prime Minister asked.

“She believes that if she stops, people die. That is not the same question.”

No, it was not.

“What does the Council provide?”

“One underfunded Watcher. Books. Training. Orders.”

“Medical care?”

“No.”

“Insurance?”

“No.”

“Protection for her family?”

“Her mother does not know.”

The Prime Minister placed one hand flat on the table.

“How long has this organization operated?”

“Possibly longer than either of our governments. Its own doctrine treats the succession of adolescent girls as continuous. General Hammond reports that most die in service in relatively short order.”

“And another is chosen.”

“Called, according to the terminology. The Council considers the line permanent and each individual girl replaceable.”

The President allowed that to settle before continuing.

“Prime Minister, an organization based in your country has deployed an American child into an undeclared war on American soil. If it has done so under authority derived from the Crown, then Her Majesty needs to know exactly what has been done in her name.”

The Prime Minister looked at the Cabinet Secretary.

The older man had gone pale.

“One moment, Mr. President.”

He muted the call.

“You know the name.”

The Cabinet Secretary removed his glasses. “I know that a sealed brief concerning the Council passes from one Cabinet Secretary to the next. I was instructed that it was not to be opened except upon request from the Sovereign or in response to an event designated Black Crown.”

“A child fighting vampires does not qualify?”

“Until this moment, Prime Minister, I had no reason to believe vampires were real.”

“Get the brief.”

It arrived nine minutes later in a locked red case carried by an official who surrendered it without asking for a receipt.

The case contained a charter copy, a list of properties, the name of one royal adviser, and a handwritten instruction renewed during the first year of the present reign.

**If Council action threatens the security of the Realm, the welfare of the Slayer, or the honor of the Crown, inform the Sovereign directly.**

The Prime Minister read it twice.

He returned to the call.

“Mr. President, this matter is older than my government. I shall have to involve the Palace.”

“I was afraid you might say that.”

“I rather think the Palace was afraid we might someday have to.”

---

Sir Basil Covington arrived through the Privy Entrance carrying a battered leather case and an umbrella despite the clear sky.

He was seventy-one, silver-haired, and built with the narrow economy of a man who had spent a lifetime declining second helpings without appearing to notice food had been served. His public biography described advisory work for three ministries, two embassies, and the Royal Household. It omitted the werewolf negotiations in Northumberland, the sealed rooms beneath the British Museum, and the reason certain bishops returned his calls before those of the Archbishop.

The equerry took his umbrella.

“Her Majesty is waiting.”

“Then someone should have telephoned me sooner.”

He entered the private sitting room and bowed.

The Queen sat beside a small writing table. The Council charter lay open before her. The Prime Minister stood near the window with the Cabinet Secretary.

“Sir Basil.”

“Ma'am.”

“You know why you are here?”

“I have read the American preliminary report.”

“Have you read the Council's reports concerning Miss Summers?”

“No, Ma'am. The Council has not offered them.”

“Did you ask?”

Basil met her eyes. “Not before today.”

The answer did not excuse him. It was not intended to.

The Queen touched the charter with one finger.

“Does this document permit the Council to command the Slayer?”

“It recognizes the Council's duty to identify, instruct, support, and advise her. The language of command is notably absent.”

“And does it permit them to send children abroad without compensation or protection?”

“No.”

“To conceal their work from the government of the country in which the child lives?”

“No.”

“To treat her death as a method of succession?”

Basil paused. “No, Ma'am.”

“Yet we do not know what they have done.”

“Not yet.”

The Queen closed the charter.

“You will report to me directly, Sir Basil.”

“Of course, Ma'am. I shall keep your Private Secretary fully—”

“Direct means direct, Sir Basil.”

He became very still.

“Yes, Ma'am.”

“If children have been sent to die under privileges granted by the Crown, I will not receive a summary after someone has decided which details I ought to know.”

“No, Ma'am.”

“You will investigate the Council as an institution. You will also examine every case individually.”

“The archive may extend back centuries.”

“Then you had best begin.”

“You want the record of every Slayer?”

“Every Slayer. Every Potential taken into Council custody. Every Watcher responsible for her welfare. Every official who authorized a test, concealed a death, denied assistance, or ordered a girl removed.”

Basil inclined his head.

“I will not have murdered girls reduced to a historical average,” the Queen said. “Nor will I have innocent Watchers condemned merely because their superiors failed them. The institution and the individuals will answer for what they actually did.”

“Yes, Ma'am.”

“Every single one.”

She moved the American report onto the charter.

“I want a raid on the Council's records this afternoon.”

The Prime Minister spoke for the first time. “The Home Secretary and Metropolitan Police are prepared to act once we establish the necessary authority.”

Basil considered the hour. “The Council maintains protected archives at more than one location. If they receive warning, material will disappear.”

“Then they are not to receive warning,” the Queen said.

“They have friends throughout government.”

“Which is why you are not reporting through them.”

“We will need specialists for the wards.”

“Take them.”

“Child-protection teams for the training residences.”

The Prime Minister nodded. “Already being assembled.”

“And authority to suspend privileges attached to the charter.”

The Queen picked up her pen.

“You have it.”

Basil bowed again.

“I will make the arrangements.”

“Sir Basil.”

He stopped.

“The first report comes to me.”

“Directly, Ma'am.”

The smallest acknowledgment passed across her face.

“This afternoon,” she repeated.

In Sunnydale, O'Neill answered his secure telephone.

Hammond did not waste words.

“The British government is acting against the Council. Effective immediately, no one in your group contacts Council headquarters or any affiliated office.”

O'Neill looked through the library doors. Giles stood at the table with an open book. Buffy and Willow were arguing about whether a secure military radio counted as an accessory. Xander was asleep in a chair beneath a jacket someone had placed over him.

“Giles isn't going to like being treated as a leak.”

“This is a records-preservation measure, not a judgment of guilt. Explain that distinction.”

“And if he calls anyway?”

“Prevent the call.”

“Understood.”

Hammond's voice lowered. “The order comes with presidential authority and the cooperation of the Crown.”

O'Neill looked at Giles again.

“That ought to get his attention.”



Chapter 17: The Afternoon Raid



At two minutes past three, every telephone in the Watchers Council's London headquarters lost its line.

At three minutes past three, the building's wards announced the presence of hostile intent.

At four minutes past three, Sir Basil Covington entered through the front door carrying the Queen's suspension order in one hand and his reclaimed umbrella in the other.

The receptionist rose.

“Sir, you cannot—”

“I assure you, I can.”

Behind him came officers of the Metropolitan Police, a Crown solicitor, two forensic-accounting teams, three archivists cleared for extraordinary material, and four men and women the Council's wards could not agree how to classify.

The last group concerned the receptionist most.

Basil placed the suspension order on her desk.

“No one leaves. No document, artifact, recording, or communication is to be removed, altered, destroyed, transferred, summoned, banished, or rendered illegible.”

She stared at him.

“The wording has been reviewed,” he added.

A bell began ringing deeper in the building.

Every member of Basil's team checked a synchronized watch.

Across London, simultaneous teams entered two Council residences, a private training facility, a warehouse listed as storing theological manuscripts, and a country property held through six layers of charitable trusts.

In Oxford, a Council archivist reached for a brass lever beneath his desk and found a police officer's hand already covering it.

In Bath, a Watcher attempted to burn three files. The flame froze blue above the paper until a Crown specialist closed her fist.

At the training residence outside London, seven girls between the ages of nine and sixteen were told to assemble for inspection. The eldest placed herself between the younger children and the strangers.

The child-protection officer lowered herself until they were eye to eye.

“We are not taking you to the Council,” she said. “We are here because no one outside it has ever asked whether you are safe.”

The girl did not move.

“May I tell you where we're going before you decide whether to believe me?”

After a long moment, the girl nodded.

---

Quentin Travers received Basil in the Council chamber.

The room had been designed to make visitors feel temporary. Portraits of dead Watchers covered dark wood walls. A circular table occupied the center beneath a domed ceiling painted with constellations as they had appeared over Britain three hundred years earlier.

Travers stood at his place without offering the chair opposite.

“Sir Basil. This is highly irregular.”

“It ceased being irregular several centuries ago. That appears to be the difficulty.”

“The Council's charter protects its archives from civil intrusion.”

Basil gave him the signed order.

Travers read the first page and looked up.

“Her Majesty has been misinformed.”

“That possibility is among the reasons she wishes to inspect your records.”

“Our work cannot be exposed to ordinary police.”

“The ordinary police are securing the ordinary crimes. Specialists will handle the extraordinary ones.”

“You do not understand what is contained here.”

“Then you have done a remarkably poor job of fulfilling your duty to advise the Crown.”

Travers set the order on the table.

“The Council predates this government.”

“So does burglary. Age is not authority.”

“Without us, the Slayer would be alone.”

Basil's expression did not alter. “The present Slayer is in California with one underfunded Watcher and a group of schoolchildren.”

“Rupert Giles has allowed inappropriate attachments to compromise her discipline.”

“Those attachments have apparently kept her alive.”

“You have heard one American account.”

“I intend to read yours.”

The door opened. A police superintendent entered with a document case.

“Sir Basil, the lower archive is refusing access.”

Travers folded his hands. “Certain repositories answer only to the Council.”

“Not today.” Basil looked at the superintendent. “Bring the charter specialist.”

Travers stepped forward. “You risk damaging protections maintained since before the Conquest.”

“Then I suggest you provide the keys.”

“I will not permit—”

“You are not being asked to permit anything.”

Basil picked up the suspension order and placed it before Travers again, this time open to the final signature.

“The Crown privileges under which this institution holds property, conceals material, crosses borders, and claims exemption from ordinary scrutiny are suspended. You will cooperate, or you will be removed while others do.”

Travers looked at the signature.

His face changed by less than a degree.

“On whose allegation?”

“Buffy Summers.”

“The girl has no standing.”

“The allegation came from a colonel in the United States Air Force, was confirmed by a major general, transmitted by the President of the United States, and placed before Her Majesty by the Prime Minister.” Basil leaned slightly closer. “The girl has acquired standing.”

---

The lower archive opened at twenty-seven minutes past four.

It occupied three underground floors, each larger than the building above suggested. Shelves moved on iron tracks. Temperature-controlled cabinets held paper older than several nations. Crystal cylinders whispered when living people approached. Bound diaries filled an entire room.

The oldest records had been kept because the Council valued continuity.

That instinct would condemn it.

Forensic teams photographed every shelf before touching it. Archivists assigned evidence numbers. Magical specialists placed containment seals around objects that reacted to human proximity.

One cabinet attempted to erase the names written inside it.

The specialist from Bath struck the lock with a silver rod. The letters returned.

“Attempted destruction,” she told the recorder. “Automatic upon unauthorized entry.”

“Can you preserve it?” Basil asked.

“Yes.”

“Do so. Then identify who authorized the ward.”

In another room, officers found personnel rosters for field teams that did not officially exist. Weapons inventories. Foreign travel papers issued under false names. Payments to coroners, police officials, and hospital administrators across four continents.

The Council's secrecy had not been passive.

At five fifteen, a child-protection team reported that all identified Potentials at the residences were accounted for. Several believed leaving without Council permission would cause the Slayer line to fail. Two had no legal identity outside documents held by the Council.

Basil ordered independent medical examinations and individual legal representation.

At five forty, police found three interior rooms secured from the outside.

One was empty.

One contained restraints sized for an adult.

The third contained restraints that were not.

At six o'clock, Travers requested counsel.

Basil granted it and ordered him separated from every other senior Council officer.

At six seventeen, the first accounting ledgers reached the examination room.

Basil removed his jacket, placed it over the back of a chair, and began to read.



Chapter 18: The Ledgers



The Council kept two accounts of every expense.

The first was written for auditors who were never expected to ask what the words meant. It contained properties, salaries, archival preservation, travel, research grants, educational trusts, and field operations.

The second account was written for the Council itself.

Basil placed the volumes side by side.

Watcher salaries appeared in both. So did pensions, medical expenses, relocation allowances, and death benefits paid to surviving families. Senior personnel received housing. Administrative officers claimed meals. Members of the governing board were reimbursed for tailoring.

The active Slayer received nothing.

Her weapons appeared beneath her Watcher's field allowance. Her medical expenses, when recorded at all, were classified as incidentals. Funeral costs were charged to local disposal. No pension category existed because no Slayer had been permitted to reach one.

An accountant named Pembroke sat across from Basil beneath the supervision of a Crown solicitor and two police officers.

“The Slayer is not an employee,” Pembroke said.

“No. Employees have rights.”

“The calling is mystical. The Council does not create it.”

“The sea is also a natural phenomenon. That does not permit the Admiralty to stop paying sailors.”

Pembroke folded his hands.

Basil turned another page.

“Rupert Giles.”

“Field Watcher, North American assignment.”

“His salary is barely adequate for subsistence in his assigned location.”

“Field personnel receive standardized compensation.”

“Senior Council dining expenses exceeded his annual operational allowance last year.”

Pembroke said nothing.

“His position as school librarian is listed as external income.”

“It provides his cover.”

“And subsidizes the Council's deployment.”

“That is not how we classify it.”

“Classification appears to be your principal skill.”

Basil moved to the next column.

**SLAYER RETIREMENT — CONTINGENCY RESERVE.**

He read the associated figures.

“You have a budget for Slayer retirement.”

Pembroke's eyes shifted toward the solicitor.

“Answer,” she said.

“Yes.”

“Yet there are no pension disbursements, residences, annuities, or long-term medical provisions attached to it.”

“The terminology is traditional.”

Basil opened the operational ledger corresponding to the reserve.

Each expenditure carried a name, a field authorization, a team allocation, and a closure date.

Every closure date matched the death of a Slayer.

Some files used additional codes.

**PERMANENT DISABILITY.**

**REFUSAL OF COUNCIL AUTHORITY.**

**ATTEMPTED WITHDRAWAL FROM ACTIVE SERVICE.**

**UNACCEPTABLE SECRECY EXPOSURE.**

**UNAUTHORIZED ALLIANCE.**

Then:

**INITIAL QUESTIONING OF COUNCIL ORDERS.**

Basil read that line twice.

“Begins questioning Council orders.”

Pembroke swallowed. “Questioning frequently precedes disobedience.”

“It also frequently precedes the discovery that an order is wrong.”

“The Slayer cannot be permitted to undermine operational continuity.”

Basil turned the book so the solicitor could see the list of payments to field teams.

“So you kill her before she learns she is allowed to say no.”

“Retirement is authorized only after review.”

“By whom?”

“The Operations Committee.”

“How many Slayers have survived review?”

Pembroke did not answer.

“I see.” Basil closed the ledger. “And what charming traditional term do you use for murder?”

---

The first direct report reached the Queen shortly after eight that evening.

Basil stood in the same sitting room. He had recovered his jacket but not the untouched condition of his cuffs. A fine layer of archive dust had settled into the fabric.

The Queen read the one-page summary without expression.

“How many girls?”

“We do not yet know.”

“An estimate.”

“The indexed record extends beyond two thousand years. Portions are incomplete.”

“How many deliberate retirements?”

“We have identified thirty-seven modern files coded clearly enough to meet that definition. There will be more.”

“Modern?”

“The Council changed terminology repeatedly. Earlier cases require individual examination.”

She set the page down.

“Every single one.”

“Yes, Ma'am.”

“The Watchers?”

“Complicity varies. Some requested support repeatedly and were denied. Some falsified reports to protect their Slayers. Others signed retirement recommendations.”

“Then they are to be judged individually.”

“Yes, Ma'am.”

“And the Potentials presently in their care?”

“Accounted for and under independent protection. Several do not possess ordinary identity documents. We are correcting that.”

The Queen looked at the Council charter on her table.

“They made the children disappear before they had even been called.”

“In several cases, yes.”

“The Chairman and every member of the governing and Operations Committees will attend me tomorrow.”

Basil paused. “In person, Ma'am?”

“A personal accounting generally requires the presence of the person giving it.”

“Some are being treated as potential subjects of a criminal inquiry. Counsel may object to questioning.”

“I shall not try criminal charges in a sitting room. I shall require each person who exercised privileges derived from the Crown to explain how those privileges were used.”

“Together?”

“Separately. They have spoken collectively for long enough.”

“Their answers will need to be recorded.”

“Of course.”

“And if they decline the summons?”

The Queen looked at the suspended charter.

“Then their refusal will become part of the accounting, and they will never again exercise authority under that document.”

Basil inclined his head. “Yes, Ma'am.”

“Continue the investigation.”

“Directly, Ma'am.”

She dismissed him only after asking for the names of the seven girls removed from the training residence.

She wrote each one down herself.

At the Council, the next evidence cart arrived from the restricted archive.

Its top drawer contained procedures applied to Slayers who survived beyond the Council's preferred period of dependence.

Basil opened the first manual.

The title page read:

**TENTO DI CRUCIAMENTUM**

**EIGHTEENTH-YEAR FIELD PROTOCOL**

Beneath it lay a newly prepared folder.

**SUMMERS, BUFFY ANNE**

**PRELIMINARY CANDIDATE ASSESSMENT**



Chapter 19: The Cruciamentum



Quentin Travers entered the Palace through a door not used for public audiences.

His solicitor entered with him. A police officer retained the solicitor's case long enough to examine it, then returned it without comment. Travers surrendered his watch, fountain pen, and a signet ring carrying a ward Basil's specialists had identified before dawn.

The ring went into a lead-lined box.

“An heirloom,” Travers said.

“Then your heirs may appreciate our care,” Basil replied.

They proceeded through two corridors and into a private council room whose only ornament was a portrait of a young Queen in uniform.

The present Queen sat at the end of the table.

Basil stood to her right. The Prime Minister sat farther down with the Crown solicitor and an official recorder. A second chair faced them.

Travers bowed.

“Your Majesty.”

“Mr. Travers.”

He glanced at the empty places around the table. “I had understood that the governing committee was summoned.”

“It was.”

“The others?”

“Will attend separately.”

For the first time, uncertainty touched his face.

The Queen indicated the chair.

“You will account for your own conduct. They will account for theirs.”

Travers sat.

The recorder began.

“Do you presently serve as Chairman of the Watchers Council?” the Queen asked.

“I do.”

“Do you accept that the Council's charter derives privileges from the Crown?”

“The Council predates the modern Crown.”

“That was not my question.”

Travers looked toward his solicitor. She gave the smallest nod.

“Yes, Your Majesty.”

“The charter requires the Council to identify, instruct, support, and advise the Slayer. Does it authorize ownership?”

“No.”

“Command?”

“The practical conduct of our duty has always required authority.”

“Does the charter grant it?”

“Not in that word.”

“Does it authorize the execution of a Slayer who begins questioning Council orders?”

The solicitor leaned forward. “Ma'am, my client is cooperating concerning the charter, but allegations of criminal conduct—”

“Will be determined by the courts,” the Queen said. “I am asking whether authority claimed from the Crown includes the power to kill a girl for questioning an order.”

Travers answered before his solicitor could stop him.

“Retirement is an extreme measure reserved for threats to the line and the secrecy that protects humanity.”

Basil wrote something down.

“You accept that ‘retirement’ means death,” the Queen said.

Travers realized what he had done.

“It may involve the permanent removal of a compromised Slayer.”

“You may use the word death in this room.”

He did not.

The Queen opened the procedure manual.

“Explain the Tento di Cruciamentum.”

Travers's composure returned. Here, at least, he stood on doctrine older than any person present.

“It is a test administered on the Slayer's eighteenth birthday. Her natural abilities are temporarily suppressed, and she is required to defeat a vampire through intelligence, courage, and resourcefulness.”

“How are her abilities suppressed?”

“A compound is administered by her Watcher.”

“With her knowledge?”

“The test would be invalid if she anticipated it.”

“Then without her knowledge or consent.”

“It is a confidential field protocol.”

“How long is she permitted to acclimate to the loss of abilities upon which her survival has depended?”

“She is not informed that they have been suppressed.”

“And her opponent?”

“A vampire selected by the Operations Committee.”

“A newly risen vampire?”

Travers hesitated. “The protocol specifies a master vampire.”

“Prepared in advance.”

“Contained by the Council, yes.”

“Armed?”

“The Slayer is permitted to locate or improvise weapons within the testing ground.”

The Queen looked down at the manual.

“You drug a girl without her knowledge. You give her no opportunity to understand the body you have forced upon her. You isolate her from assistance and place her within reach of a creature selected because it can kill experienced Slayers.”

“It tests whether she remains worthy of the calling.”

“The calling has already selected her.”

“The Council must ensure that power is accompanied by judgment.”

“By removing her power and arranging her death?”

“Those who succeed emerge stronger.”

“And those who fail?”

“Another is called.”

There it was.

Not regret. Not even denial. Continuity.

Basil turned a page in the Summers file.

“Why eighteen?” he asked.

“It is the traditional age.”

“It is the age at which she becomes legally capable of rejecting your authority.”

Travers said nothing.

“This is not an examination,” Basil continued. “An examination is designed to measure its subject. This is designed either to break her obedience or to remove her.”

“The Slayer must prove herself worthy.”

Basil closed the file.

“She has spent years keeping your species alive. It is the Council whose worth is presently under examination.”

The Queen looked at Travers.

“Did you authorize preliminary preparations for Buffy Summers?”

“The file was opened according to schedule.”

“Did you authorize them?”

“Yes.”

“Did you review the recommendation that her civilian relationships be used to increase psychological pressure during the test?”

His solicitor touched his sleeve.

Travers did not answer.

“Your refusal is recorded,” the Queen said.

The hearing continued for forty-three minutes.

When it ended, Travers bowed again. The gesture was as correct as when he entered. It no longer concealed the fact that he had been required to answer.

The police officer returned him to a separate waiting room where he could speak to none of his colleagues.

The next Council member entered through the same door.

---

Six members of the governing and Operations Committees appeared that morning.

Lady Margaret Wyndam-Pryce denied knowing that field teams used lethal force during retirement operations. Basil produced three authorizations bearing her signature. She said the papers had been presented as secrecy measures. The Queen asked why she had signed orders she did not understand.

Dr. Alistair Haviland defended the Cruciamentum as necessary psychological conditioning. He admitted that the Council collected no long-term data from survivors because survival was uncommon.

Sir Malcolm Reeve had chaired the Operations Committee for nine years. He insisted that no individual member could be responsible for collective decisions. The Queen placed before him an authorization requiring his personal approval and asked whether the signature was his.

It was.

Two members described repeated attempts to reform field support and produced memoranda the governing committee had rejected. Their complicity did not disappear, but it acquired boundaries.

One member declined every substantive question upon advice of counsel.

His silence was recorded beside the payments he had approved.

No two accounts matched completely.

Together, they described the same institution.

---

In Sunnydale, Giles read the Cruciamentum summary standing behind the library counter.

O'Neill had offered him a chair.

He had declined.

Buffy sat at the central table with Willow and Xander. Carter and Daniel stood near the secure facsimile machine the Air Force had installed that morning. Teal'c guarded the doors.

The document was only four pages. It contained no photographs, no death records, and no names beyond Buffy's.

It was enough.

“Is it real?” Buffy asked.

Giles lowered the page.

“Yes.”

“They were planning to drug me.”

“The test is not scheduled until your eighteenth birthday.”

“That wasn't the question.”

Giles looked at her.

“Yes.”

“And lock me in with a master vampire.”

“Yes.”

Willow stared at him. “You knew?”

The word cut more cleanly from her than it would have from O'Neill.

Giles placed the report on the counter.

“I knew the Cruciamentum existed.”

Buffy went very still.

“Since when?”

“Since my training.”

“Before you met me.”

“Yes.”

“Were you going to do it?”

Giles had faced vampires, demons, and the Master beneath the library. None had required the courage of answering without choosing the answer that might preserve Buffy's love for him.

“I had not decided what I would do when the order came.”

Xander stood up.

O'Neill moved one hand—not toward his weapon, only enough to remind Xander that anger did not need to become action.

Buffy did not look away from Giles.

“You had more than a year to decide whether to poison me.”

“I never intended for you to die.”

“That is not the same thing.”

“No.”

The answer hurt her more than an excuse would have.

Giles continued because stopping would make the confession another form of concealment.

“I believed I would refuse when the time came. I also believed there was time before I had to confront what refusal would cost. Those beliefs allowed me not to decide.”

“Cost you,” Buffy said.

“Yes.”

“Your job.”

“Yes.”

“The Council.”

“Yes.”

“While I got the master vampire.”

Giles closed his eyes.

“Yes.”

Buffy rose.

Willow reached for her hand. Buffy squeezed it once and let go.

“I need to leave.”

“I'll come with you,” Willow said.

“No. I need everybody not to come with me.”

She looked at Xander before he could object.

“Especially people who got back from space yesterday and are supposed to avoid stress.”

“That's not a real doctor's order.”

“It is now.”

Buffy walked toward the doors.

Giles found his voice. “Buffy.”

She stopped without turning.

“I am sorry.”

“I know.”

That was all she gave him.

The doors swung closed behind her.

Giles remained at the counter with the file bearing her name.

O'Neill waited until Buffy's footsteps had faded.

“You want me to tell you that knowing isn't the same as doing?”

“No.”

“Good.”

Giles looked at the doors.

“I want the chance to become the man who would have refused.”

O'Neill's voice was quiet. “That's her decision now.”



Chapter 20: The Exception



The Council had indexed Slayers by date, region, Watcher, and manner of death.

It had not indexed them by success.

Basil noticed the absence on the second day.

The archive contained detailed measures of obedience: instructions followed, unauthorized alliances, deviations from patrol schedules, emotional attachments, disputes with Watchers, and suspected threats to secrecy. It tracked how efficiently a Slayer maintained her assigned territory. It recorded every time she questioned the Council.

It did not consistently record the people she saved.

“Build a second index,” Basil told the archival team.

“By what criteria?”

“Threats defeated. Civilian casualties prevented. Years survived. Apocalyptic events averted. Requests for assistance and the Council's response.”

The senior archivist looked at the shelves. “That will take months.”

“Begin with the active Slayer.”

They reconstructed Buffy Summers from sources the Council had kept separate.

Giles's official reports described a gifted but undisciplined Slayer whose emotional attachments repeatedly complicated field operations. The Operations Committee recorded her friendship with Willow Rosenberg and Alexander Harris as security vulnerabilities. Angel was classified as evidence of compromised judgment. Her mother's ignorance was considered useful for maintaining secrecy.

Buffy returned.

She killed the Master, closed the Hellmouth, and resumed active service.

Chapter 22: Power Remaining



Rush put the power figures on every functioning display aboard Destiny.

He did not soften them for Young, and he did not explain them twice.

The ship had crossed into Earth's sky with the same damage it had carried between galaxies: entire sections sealed, distribution networks operating through improvised bypasses, and systems older than human civilization consuming power no one aboard fully understood. The transit had not restored anything. It had only interrupted the slow process by which Destiny preserved what remained.

Now that process was accelerating.

“The field is drawing energy,” Rush said.

Young stood at the central console. Behind the glass, Earth filled half the observation deck. California lay in darkness beneath them, its cities scattered across the coast in bright constellations.

“From the ship?”

“Not directly. At least, not in any way the power grid can measure.” Rush enlarged the diagram. “But every system maintaining our position is compensating for it. Navigation makes a correction, the field negates it, and then the ship corrects for the correction. Guidance, inertial control, structural integrity, propulsion readiness—they're all cycling.”

TJ studied the lines descending across the display.

“How long?” she asked.

Rush gave her the look he reserved for questions whose answers had too many variables.

Young waited.

“At current consumption, our reserves fall below safe stasis margin in months, not years.”

The observation deck went quiet.

TJ looked toward the corridor. Hundreds of people slept beyond it. They had entered stasis believing the ship would cross the gulf between galaxies while Eli repaired the final pod. None had consented to wake in their own past, but none had consented to die quietly above it either.

“Define safe margin,” Young said.

“Destiny defines it as the point at which it can no longer guarantee uninterrupted support to every pod.”

“That's not what I asked.”

Rush's mouth tightened.

“We don't know. The ship may begin selective shutdowns. It may attempt to wake the crew. It may preserve some compartments at the expense of others. The relevant systems were damaged before we arrived, and several automatic repairs stopped during transit.”

“Worst case.”

“A power fluctuation interrupts stasis without completing revival.”

TJ's expression gave the clinical sentence a human meaning.

Young looked at the rows of names on the status panel.

“And the solution?”

Rush changed the display. Destiny appeared beside a star, its silhouette swallowed by a corona.

“The same solution the ship has used for millions of years. We enter a star and recharge.”

“The Sun.”

“Unless you have another suitable star hidden nearby.”

Young ignored the tone. “Can Destiny reach it?”

“The engines can. The ship cannot.”

Rush brought up the navigational plot. Every projected course away from the fixed point above Sunnydale bent back toward it. Some ended in errors. Others folded into impossible loops before the computer rejected them.

“Destiny is capable of reaching the Sun,” Rush continued. “It is also being held here by something the ship cannot identify and cannot navigate through. Remaining in position kills us slowly. Attempting to leave without understanding the restraint may kill us quickly.”

Young opened the channel to the ground.

“Already sitting.”

“The field contraction began when the solar angle placed Sunnydale between Destiny and the Sun.”

“It would have to move approximately one astronomical unit in a direction it currently cannot manage by a meter.”

“That wasn't my question.”

“It is possible that Destiny's automatic systems selected the Sun and that the restraint reacted to the attempt.”

Xander shook his head.

“No. A coordinate.”

“I don't know how I know. Destiny isn't being pulled down. It's being told that this is where it is. Every time navigation tries to define somewhere else, the answer gets overwritten.”

“A headache, mostly.”

Chapter 24: The God at the Door



The Watchers Council had classified Janus incorrectly for three hundred years.

Daniel found the first error in a seventeenth-century translation. Giles found the second in a commentary copied from it. By noon, they had assembled a chain of scholarship in which each Watcher cited the last until repetition became authority.

The earliest texts said nothing about chaos.

They spoke of gates. Thresholds. Departures and returns. The first step of a journey and the last step home. The instant in which one condition ended and another began.

Past and future looked in opposite directions through the same opening.

“Janus presides over change,” Daniel said. “Not random change. Chosen passage. The Romans invoked him at beginnings, at endings, at war, at peace—whenever something crossed from one state into another.”

Chapter 47: The Agreement



The provisional alliance required eleven drafts.

The first failed because the Tok'ra used *access* where Hammond required *specific requested access under supervision*.

The third failed because the American draft described hosts and symbiotes together as Tok'ra personnel without explicitly preserving the host's independent personhood.

The fifth failed when the Tok'ra reserved the right to destroy captured operatives who might reveal resistance cells.

“No.”

“Then the agreement says protection whenever possible and requires somebody to prove when it wasn't.”

The seventh draft failed because the Tok'ra described Destiny as a jointly relevant strategic resource.

“Destiny is my ship and my crew.”

Garshaw's image regarded him across the briefing room.

“It is an Ancient vessel whose existence affects every power in this galaxy.”

“That makes it strategically relevant. It doesn't make it yours.”

The phrase seemed absurd until everyone remembered how often powerful institutions described people as resources.

**Selmak.**

Chapter 48: The Fleet Moves

Chapter 50: The Defense Plan



The plan depended upon Janus believing he had already won the important argument.
