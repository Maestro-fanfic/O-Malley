Chapter 5 of 27

The Delivery Truck

Rush walked into the library expecting a briefing. What he got, instead, was several very long seconds standing frozen in the doorway, notes forgotten in one hand, doing the one thing he genuinely hated doing in front of an audience: visibly failing to process something in real time.

"What in the—" It came out sharper and louder than he clearly intended, notes nearly slipping from his grip entirely, before he caught himself hard, visibly, and dragged his own voice back down by main force. "Right," he said, considerably quieter, in the flat, overcareful tone of a man rebuilding his composure one syllable at a time. "Nobody mentioned that part."

"Which part," Xander asked, not looking up from the table.

"The part where two of the three people I'm meant to be building a magic-technology hybrid device with are not, by any definition I currently possess, actually human." Rush's eyes moved from Willow's faceted, faintly luminous frame to Buffy's sealed visor and back again, cataloguing, the scientist in him fighting the rest of him for control of the reaction. "I read the debrief. The debrief undersold this considerably."

"Yeah, people do that a lot," Buffy said, dry, entirely unbothered, the visor tilting slightly in his direction. "You get used to it. Or you don't, and it's fine either way, honestly, we've had worse reactions."

"I am not having a reaction," Rush said, having a reaction. "I am recalibrating."

"Take your time," Willow offered, not unkindly.

He took, in the end, a little under a minute — standing very still, breathing, visibly filing the entire fact away into whatever category of his mind was reserved for things too large to process properly on a deadline — and then simply picked his notes back up and crossed to the table like nothing at all had happened, because there was, in the end, a wound in the ground with fourteen centuries of momentum behind it, and Nicholas Rush had never once in his life let something merely astonishing get in the way of something that actually needed solving.

"Right," he said again, considerably steadier this time, pulling up his waveform analysis. "Shall we begin."

The planning took two full days, longer than anyone had expected going in, mostly because nobody in that library had ever tried to make magic and Ancient technology genuinely cooperate before, and it turned out neither discipline was especially interested in sharing the room.

Jenny herself had needed exactly one evening with Giles, several months back now, before the softened, half-resolved version of Buffy and Willow her own mind had been quietly supplying her finally gave way to the plain, unfiltered truth of it — Giles sitting her down at her own kitchen table, saying it plainly, watching her go pale and then, gamely, recover, because that had always rather been Jenny's way with things too large to process properly the first time through. She'd needed a good stiff drink and a full night's sleep before she trusted herself to look either girl in the eye again, and had done so anyway, the following morning, with a directness that both of them had quietly, gratefully appreciated. By the time she found herself in a high school library arguing frequency theory with a scientist from another galaxy, none of it startled her especially anymore. It had simply become, with time and effort, the shape of her actual life now.

"It's not a matching problem," Jenny said, for what felt like the fourth time, spreading old diagrams across the table beside a laptop displaying Rush's own waveform analysis. "You keep treating the wound like a lock that needs the right key. It's not a lock. It's a wound. Wounds don't respond to precision, they respond to care. You can't just fire the correct frequency at it and expect it to close."

"With respect," Rush said, not looking up, "care is not a measurable quantity, and I would very much like something measurable before I ask this ship to route a significant fraction of its power reserves into a working I cannot verify by any instrument I trust." He said it with the edge of a man who had spent every spare hour of the last several weeks running power budgets that never quite balanced the way he wanted them to. "We are not, at present, in a position to spend reserves carelessly on anything, magical or otherwise. Until we find a proper Zero Point Module, or several, everything this ship does costs us something we can't easily get back."

"Then trust the people in the room instead of the instrument," Giles said, mild, though something under the mildness had an edge to it lately that hadn't quite been there before.

"I trust competence. I'm not yet convinced this qualifies."

"You're not yet convinced because you keep refusing to actually read what I gave you," Jenny said, sharper now. "There's fourteen hundred years of precedent in these texts for exactly this kind of closing. It's not guesswork. It's a different discipline, with its own rigor, that you've decided not to respect because it doesn't come with numbers attached."

Rush's jaw worked. He didn't have an immediate answer for that, which Xander, watching from across the table, privately filed away as the closest thing to a win Jenny was likely to get out of him today.

Willow spent most of the first day quietly cross-referencing both approaches on her own, saying little, until she finally set her own notes down in the middle of the table like a peace offering nobody had quite known to ask for.

"You're both right, and you're both missing the actual point," she said, when the room finally paused long enough to notice she'd spoken. "Jenny's right that the wound needs care, not just a matched frequency. Rush is right that care alone, at this scale, isn't going to be enough — fourteen centuries of a Hellmouth being open is a lot of wound to close with just intention. What if the frequency match isn't the thing that closes it? What if it's just the thing that lets the wound finally hear the care in the first place?"

The room went quiet for a moment, both Jenny and Rush turning that over from their own separate directions.

"That's..." Jenny said, slowly. "That's actually rather elegant, Willow."

"It is not unreasonable," Rush allowed, which from him was practically a standing ovation.

"Here's what I think actually works, building off that," Xander said, before the momentum could stall out again. "Jenny and Willow handle the wound itself — the listening part, the part that needs actual care. Rush, you and Sam build the delivery mechanism — something that can carry what they're doing at the actual scale this needs, because I don't think either of them can push fourteen centuries of stubborn on their own, no offense."

"None taken," Willow said, mild.

"So it's not tech instead of magic, and it's not magic instead of tech. It's tech carrying magic somewhere magic can't get on its own. Like — a delivery truck. A really, really overqualified delivery truck."

Rush was quiet a moment, turning that over with visibly more patience than he'd shown the rest of the conversation. "That," he admitted, grudging, "is not an unreasonable framing."

"I have my moments."

"You have approximately one every few days. I'll take it."

They spent the rest of the second day building the actual rig — Rush and Sam wiring together a piece of Destiny's own diagnostic array with roughly four hundred feet of cable that had never been designed for a high school basement, while Jenny and Willow worked through the invocation itself, line by line, checking each phrase against three separate sources before either of them would commit to it out loud.

Buffy spent most of that second day simply watching, mostly quiet, feeling Artemis's own strange, patient attention settle over the whole process like a second set of eyes. She'd expected to feel useless, standing at the edges of a plan built by people who understood the mechanics far better than she did. Instead, watching Rush and Jenny slowly stop talking past each other and start actually building something together, she found herself thinking of it less as a plan and more as a kind of repair — not just of the wound in the ground, but of something smaller, quieter, happening in the room around it.

"They're actually working together," she said, quietly, mostly to herself, watching Rush hand Jenny a length of cable without being asked and Jenny take it without complaint.

Small victories, Artemis observed, something almost wry in it. I have found, across a great many travels, that they tend to matter more than the large ones, in the end. Large victories close wounds. Small ones teach people how to close them together, the next time.

By the time the light outside the library windows had gone properly dark, twice over, there was nothing left to plan. There was only the wound itself, waiting, the way it had been waiting for fourteen hundred years for someone to finally get the approach right.
