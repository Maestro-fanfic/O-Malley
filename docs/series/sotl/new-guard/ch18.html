Chapter 18

He found her in the observation lounge, the one with the cracked viewport they hadn't been able to replace. Ice crystals spread in a fine web across the glass, and she was watching the replicator work through a cup of coffee she wasn't drinking. Most of the overhead panels were dark to conserve power, so what light there was came mostly from the console glow and the pale, endless white outside.

"Hey," Xander said, from the doorway.

"Hey yourself. How was Washington?"

"I told the President no."

That got her attention. She turned, one eyebrow up, the coffee pausing halfway to her mouth. "No to what?"

"All of it. The job, the money, the whole Superman-for-hire thing." He shrugged, a little embarrassed by how big it still felt to say out loud. "I'm going home. Finishing school. Being Xander, mostly, and doing the other thing when it actually matters."

"That's a real decision," Jenny said, her voice quiet but firm. "Not the easy one. The easy one would've been letting them decide for you."

"Yeah, well." He watched her turn back to the viewport, her reflection faint against the ice. The composed stillness she wore, the coffee going cold in her hands—it wasn't just about Data. It was a choice, too. "Which is why I came to ask you the same thing."

"Ask me what?"

"What you're going to do. Now that this isn't just about surviving anymore."

She was quiet a long moment, the ship humming faintly around them, some emergency system cycling through its rounds somewhere below decks, and then she set the mug down, deliberate, like letting go of it took more effort than it should have.

"Do you know what a replicator actually is," she said, not really a question, eyes on the machine as it idled, producing nothing. "Doctor Crusher's memory tells me. Molecular resequencing, patterns held in a buffer, converted back to matter on command. I could recite the theory and it wouldn't mean anything, because it isn't the theory that's strange. It's that I know how to use it the way you'd know how to use a can opener. Reflexively. Because somewhere in me, I've done it ten thousand times."

"Jenny..."

"I have a son," she said, flat, careful, the way something comes out when saying it any other way might break it open entirely. "Wesley. I remember teaching him to walk, one hand under his arms, on a carpet in a room that doesn't exist here. I remember the exact sound he made the first time he called me Mom instead of Mommy. I remember his father's hands. I remember the day Jack died, and the exact weight of the silence in the room afterward." Her gaze dropped to the mug in her hands, her thumbs tracing its rim. "And every single funeral I stood through in the years after that, for people I served with for decades, people whose names I could list for you right now.

"I remember Wesley leaving to travel with the Traveler. I remember telling him I understood when I wanted to ask him to stay. I remember the first patient I lost and the first one I saved when everyone else had given up. I remember Jean-Luc coming to sickbay with some injury he insisted was minor, lying badly about how it happened. None of those are just facts. They arrive with everything attached. Pride, anger, boredom, grief. Thirty years includes ordinary Tuesdays, too."

"And now all those people are gone."

"I don't know what they are." Jenny looked toward the cracked viewport. "Maybe they never existed. Maybe the spell copied a real universe and this ship was pulled out of it. Maybe Jean-Luc and Wesley are alive somewhere wondering what happened to the Enterprise. Every answer hurts somebody."

"That's not fair."

"No. It isn't."

The ship made a soft sound around them, metal contracting against the cold. Jenny glanced toward the door before she caught herself.

"I keep expecting people," she said. "Footsteps outside sickbay. Jean-Luc calling from the bridge. Alyssa Ogawa bringing me a report. I know this ship's crew manifest, Xander. More than a thousand names. I know who was allergic to what, who hid injuries because they were afraid of losing duty status, who needed to be distracted before a hypospray. The corridors are empty, and some part of me is surprised every time."

"Do you think they're dead?"

"A doctor isn't supposed to declare anyone dead without evidence. Beverly believed that. I believe it. So I have a thousand missing people and nowhere to search for them."

"None of that happened here," Xander said, quiet.

"No. None of it happened here." She looked at him, more open than he'd seen her all evening, some careful wall finally coming down. "But it happened to me. Do you understand the difference? It's not a memory I have. It's a life I lived. Thirty years, crammed into whatever I am now, and I can't set it down because the calendar says it never occurred. I buried a husband, Xander, and I raised a child into a grown man who did brave, foolish things and made me proud and terrified every single time. I can't go back to being Ms. Calendar, computer science teacher, grading essays and taking attendance, like none of that is sitting right behind my eyes every single night."

"Did you like teaching? Before this?"

The question surprised her. "Yes. More than I expected to when I took the position. The students could be impossible. The administration was worse. But yes, I liked watching somebody understand something they had been certain was beyond them."

"Then going back wouldn't be pretending that part didn't matter."

"No," she said after a moment. "It wouldn't. That's what makes the choice difficult. Jenny's life did not become false because Beverly's appeared inside it. I still remember my first day at Sunnydale High. I remember you and Willow trying to correct my computer terminology from the back row."

"Willow was correcting you. I was providing moral support."

"You were playing Minesweeper."

"Moral support takes different forms."

She laughed, and the sound caught halfway through, turning briefly into something else before she controlled it.

"For what it's worth," Xander said, after a moment, "I get a little of it. Not the same, not even close. But I've got all these instincts now that aren't mine either. Like I know how to land a punch I never learned how to throw. I know how fast I can run before it's too fast. Nobody taught me any of it. It's just there."

"That's not the same as thirty years and a dead husband, Xander."

"I know it's not," he said, rubbing the back of his neck. "I just mean I get a little of what it's like—having something in you that you didn't ask for and can't just set down because it'd be easier."

She considered that, something in her expression softening. "That's more than most people would think to say."

"So what do you do with it? The having-it part."

"I stay," she said, simply, like she'd only just said it out loud but had known it for days underneath. "Not because the tech back home doesn't fit anymore—and it doesn't, I'd feel it every day like a coat two sizes too small. Because this," she gestured, at the ship, at the ice outside the cracked viewport, "is closer to who I actually am now than a classroom full of teenagers ever could be again. I'm not Jenny Calendar anymore, not really. I'm not Beverly Crusher either, she never existed, not the way I did, not the way I still do. I'm something in between the two of them, something new, and the only place that makes sense for that is here, doing this work, with these people."

"What work? Fixing the ship?"

"That. Keeping the recovery teams alive. Teaching your people which parts of the Enterprise can help without killing anyone who tries to use them. Finding out whether Data can be restored. Making sure the technology on this ship doesn't become the cause of the very war it's supposed to prevent." She looked down at the Starfleet uniform she still wore. "And if any part of the crew exists somewhere, finding a way to learn that. I may never be able to. It remains my responsibility to try."

"That's a lot to choose."

"So was going home."

"Home's Buffy and Willow and Giles. School too, I guess. It's not just the house."

"No. Home rarely is."

"Maybe this is yours, then. Not because Beverly lived here. Because you're choosing it."

Jenny looked around the dark observation lounge. "The Enterprise was never Beverly's home by herself. It was the people. Without them, it's only a ship."

"There are people here now. Ferretti, Warren, all the recovery crews. Carter nearly moved into sickbay after one tour."

"She did look alarmingly comfortable."

"I'm just saying. Maybe home gets new people."

"Perhaps it does."

"Does anyone else know? What you just told me, I mean. The whole of it."

"No. And I don't think I'm going to tell them. Not the whole of it. Carter knows I'm carrying something. She doesn't know what, not really, not the specifics. Some things lose their shape the moment you try to put them into words."

"That's a hell of a thing to figure out by yourself."

"I'm not sure I have figured it out, not fully. I've just stopped pretending I haven't already chosen." A small, real smile, the first one he'd seen from her all evening. "You didn't come here to fix this for me, did you."

"No. I don't think I could if I tried. Wouldn't know where to start."

"Good. I didn't need fixing. I just needed someone who actually knew the truth of it to hear me say it out loud, all of it, without having to translate it into something smaller first." She picked the coffee back up and drank some, grimacing at the cold of it. "Thank you for that."

"Anytime," Xander said, and meant it. He stayed a while longer in the low light, neither of them saying much else, both of them looking out at the same endless white through the cracked glass.

The daily briefing began with the weather.

"The front passed south of the main site," Carter reported, one hand holding the radio handset and the other moving down her clipboard. "We had eleven hours of high winds and less accumulation than forecast. The exterior crews recovered all marked debris before visibility dropped. Two storage tents were damaged. No injuries."

"And the ship?" Hammond asked.

"The scaffolding held. The secondary hull shifted another four centimeters toward the original impact trench, then stopped. Warren's team added anchors this morning, but I don't want anyone in the exposed decks until the structural model is updated."

"Agreed. The international observers reached McMurdo this morning. Doctor Weir's office has confirmed one each from Russia, China, and Australia, plus a British engineering liaison. I want site rules in writing before they board any aircraft for the wreck."

O'Neill took the handset. "Already working on it, sir. Restricted sections stay restricted, everyone travels with an escort, and nobody puts a souvenir in their pocket unless they want to explain alien theft charges to five governments."

"Use more diplomatic language in the written version, Colonel."

"Carter's writing it."

"Then I have every confidence. What is the status of the altered section?"

Carter turned to a second page. "Deck Seven remains isolated from the ship's power grid. The optical monitor recorded one movement after shutdown, less than an inch of retraction in a single filament. Nothing since. That means the material retained some internal power after we cut the grid, or the shutdown response was delayed. We still can't call it inactive."

"Can it reach an occupied section?"

"Not without crossing two sealed bulkheads and an unpowered compartment. We've added independent motion and thermal sensors at both approaches. Commander Calendar is checking the computer logs for any attempt to reroute power."

"No foreign observer goes near that deck," Hammond said. "Doctor Weir will be informed that an active technological hazard is under containment. The existence of the Borg is not part of their briefing."

"That will raise questions," Carter said.

"Then we answer the questions we can without giving three governments a reason to demand samples of self-repairing hostile machinery."

"Yes, sir."

"Commander Data?"

"Stable, in the sense that his condition isn't changing. We've moved his components into a secured medical storage room with independent temperature control. I have sent all non-classified observations to Doctor Fraiser and the engineering group at the SGC. Nobody has identified a safe repair path."

"Understood. Continue documentation only."

O'Neill looked toward Jenny, who stood with Ferretti near the open hatch. She had declined to join the call herself, saying there was no point making a personal choice twice for two different audiences.

"Commander Calendar has decided to remain with the recovery operation," O'Neill said. "Indefinitely. She isn't putting a date on it."

"Her decision has been logged," Hammond replied. "Please extend my thanks for her continued cooperation, and let her know the offer of formal SGC support stands whenever she's ready to discuss what that might look like."

"Will do, sir."

Teal'c stepped closer to the radio with the latest sensor printout in one hand.

"The sensors are unchanged," Teal'c reported. "The signature beneath the ice has not fluctuated in the three days since it was first detected. Not by any measurable degree."

"Understood," Hammond's voice came back, tinny with distance. "For now, that's exactly where it stays. I've flagged it for the Joint Chiefs, but between the recovery operation and the situation with China, this is not the week to open a second investigation at that site. I trust you understand the reasoning, Teal'c, even if you don't care for it."

"I understand it. I do not entirely agree with it, but I understand it."

"Noted," Hammond said, dry, not unkind. "For what it's worth, neither do I, fully. But we don't have the resources to do this properly right now, and doing it improperly seems considerably worse than waiting."

"Yes sir," O'Neill said, next to Carter, arms crossed against the cold. "We'll keep an eye on it. Won't touch it, won't poke at it, just watch."

"Then that's everything for today. Hammond out."

The line clicked dead, static cutting to silence, and for a moment nobody said anything—then Ferretti turned back to his manifest, Casey and Sands hauled cable toward the scaffolding, grunting under the weight of it after hours of the same work, the low mechanical hum of the ship's emergency systems doing exactly what they'd been doing for three unbroken days.

Teal'c stood a while longer at the console, looking at the readout that hadn't changed since the moment he'd first found it. It did not spike. It did not fade. It simply persisted, steady, indifferent to the attention now being paid to it, to the sensors trained on it, to the questions being asked about it three thousand miles away in rooms he would never see.

Whatever waited down there was in no hurry.

He did not think that boded well for whoever eventually went to find it, and he said as much to no one at all, quiet, before switching off the console and going to help with the cable.

✳

The observers arrived in an Enterprise shuttle because the weather had grounded the helicopters.

That fact did not improve anyone's mood.

Major Ferretti waited inside the main shuttle bay with O'Neill, Jenny, and two security officers while the craft settled onto the deck. Warren had flown it under Jenny's supervision, a decision made after McMurdo reported that the approaching wind would leave the delegation stranded for at least another day. The shuttle's side door opened, and four people stepped out wearing identical borrowed cold-weather gear over four very different sets of expectations.

"Welcome to the Enterprise," Jenny said. "I am Commander Calendar. Before we go any farther, I need to know that each of you received the site restrictions at McMurdo."

The British engineer, Commander Hale, held up a stapled packet. "Received and signed."

The Australian environmental officer did the same. The Russian observer nodded without producing his copy.

Doctor Jian Zhao, representing China, looked around the shuttle bay before answering. "I received a list of areas the United States has declared inaccessible. I did not agree that the United States possesses the authority to make that declaration."

"It doesn't," Jenny said.

Zhao turned back to her.

"I declared those areas inaccessible. This is my ship. The United States and United Kingdom are coordinating recovery with my permission. You are welcome here under the agreement Doctor Weir negotiated, but you are not authorized to enter a secured deck because your government disputes who typed the notice."

O'Neill kept his face neutral. Ferretti became suddenly interested in the shuttle's landing struts.

"Your status is itself disputed," Zhao said. "We have been told you crossed from another reality with no surviving government capable of recognizing your commission."

"True. I also know which damaged systems can flood a corridor with radiation before you reach the second door. If you prefer to base your safety on jurisdiction, Major Ferretti can take you back to McMurdo."

"The shuttle cannot safely make another flight," Warren said from the cockpit.

"Then Doctor Zhao can enjoy our guest quarters until the weather improves."

The Australian officer coughed into one glove, poorly concealing a laugh.

Zhao studied Jenny for several seconds. "I will observe the restrictions while reserving my government's position."

"That's all the agreement requires," Jenny said. "Major Ferretti will issue your badges. Red markings indicate escorted access only. Black indicates no access. If a door does not open, you do not force it. If you see black cabling, exposed machinery, green illumination, or anything moving without an obvious cause, you step away and call security."

"The technological hazard mentioned in our briefing," the Russian observer said. "What is its nature?"

"Self-repairing cybernetic material integrated into a damaged section of the ship," Jenny answered. "It is isolated and under observation. We do not yet know whether it is capable of spreading."

"You expect us to accept that description without inspection?" Zhao asked.

"I expect you to remain alive long enough to inspect the rest of the ship."

Commander Hale looked past them at the supports running along the shuttle-bay wall. "This compartment has shifted since the schematics we received were recorded."

"Four millimeters," Jenny said. "The hull settled during the storm. The occupied section is stable, but you'll wear a locator and remain with your escort."

The Australian officer opened her case and withdrew a compact sampling kit. "I need exterior ice samples from both impact trenches and beneath the debris staging area. Your report lists no detected release, but several compounds in the materials inventory have no terrestrial exposure standard."

"Carter will help you choose safe collection points," O'Neill said. "Nothing comes inside until Commander Calendar scans it."

"Agreed. I also want baseline water samples from the nearest downstream ice movement."

"Put it in the work plan," Ferretti said. "We'll get you transport when the wind drops."

The Russian observer had moved no farther than the edge of the shuttle's marked safety boundary. "And the artificial crewman? Commander Data."

Jenny's expression closed. "His remains are secured."

"Our cybernetics institutes may be able to assist."

"So may China's," Zhao said.

"Nobody examines him today," Jenny replied. "Captain Carter has completed a preliminary assessment. I will consider written proposals once I know who would perform the work, what they intend to do, and whether they understand that Commander Data is a person, not recovered equipment."

"A non-functioning machine may still be a person?" Zhao asked.

"Yes. If that prevents your institute from assisting, it saves both of us time."

Ferretti handed out the first badge. "We start with the saucer's forward compartments. Inventory teams work in pairs, one international observer and one recovery specialist. Nobody removes anything. If you find an object that isn't already tagged, mark the location and call it in."

The four observers accepted their badges. None looked satisfied. That, O'Neill thought, was probably the closest thing to proof that Weir's agreement was working.

As the group left the shuttle bay, Zhao slowed beside him.

"There are rumors of another discovery beneath the ice," she said quietly.

O'Neill did not look at her. "There are rumors of a lot of things."

"My government will ask formally."

"Then somebody considerably better dressed than me will answer formally. Stay with your escort, Doctor."

She moved on without another word.

O'Neill watched her join the others and wondered which satellite pass, intercepted report, or careless sentence had already made the buried structure a rumor. They had kept it secret for less than a week.

In his experience, most secrets collapsed under their own weight long before that.

"So that's it, then," Casey said, the next morning, watching Xander zip the satchel closed. "You're actually leaving."

"Gotta go be a normal person for a while. Turns out I've got homework."

"Homework." Sands shook his head. "Guy juggles a Humvee for fun and he's worried about homework."

"I'm not worried about it. I'm resigned to it. There's a difference."

Warren came over from the makeshift workbench where he'd been cataloguing pieces, wiping his hands on a rag that had clearly given up being useful several days ago. "Hey, before you go. Thanks. For the scaffolding, I mean. You probably saved us two weeks of work."

"Least I could do. You guys have been out here freezing your butts off for way longer than I have."

"Speak for yourself," Casey said. "Some of us have a healthy amount of insulation."

"Some of us also snore loud enough to register on the ship's sensors," Sands shot back.

"That was one time."

"It was every night, Casey, I have witnesses."

Ferretti appeared in the doorway, arms crossed, something almost fond in his expression. "You heading out, Harris?"

"Yes sir. Figured I'd say bye first, though. Seemed rude not to, after everything."

"Appreciate that." Ferretti held out a hand, and Xander shook it, careful, the way he'd learned to be careful with ordinary hands. "For what it's worth, kid, I've served with a lot of people. Most of them wouldn't spend their downtime hauling steel for a crew they just met."

"Figured I might as well be useful while I was here."

"You were. More than useful." Ferretti nodded toward the hull rising behind them. "Come back and visit sometime. I got a feeling this place isn't done being interesting."

"Yeah," Xander said, glancing at the ice, at the place where the readout had sat unchanged for days now, something he still didn't fully understand and hoped he wouldn't have to for a while yet. "Yeah, I get that feeling too."

After a last round of handshakes and one more bad joke from Sands, he walked past the scaffolding and the makeshift camp. Then he rose into the pale Antarctic sky, the wreck, the ice, and the patient mystery beneath it all shrinking away behind him.

The Enterprise repeated its deep scan after midnight.

The pulse passed through damaged hull plating, temporary scaffolding, wind-packed snow, and nearly two miles of ice. It touched the buried structure and returned to the ship in broken pieces, distorted by the same interference that had defeated every earlier attempt.

The computer compared the result against the previous scan.

Structure dimensions unchanged.

Energy field unchanged.

One life sign.

No measurable variation.

The result entered the sensor log. Carter's independent monitor copied it to the equipment bank outside Deck Seven. A communications program marked it for inclusion in the next report to the SGC. No alarm sounded because the system had been told that change was the danger, and nothing beneath the ice had changed.

Above, the recovery camp slept in shifts. The ship continued breathing recycled air through empty corridors. Snow moved over the debris field one grain at a time.

Far below all of it, the signal remained exactly where it had been.

✳

The first tabletop exercise removed Xander from the board on purpose. Carter put a blank square where he would have been and asked the room to defend the Pacific without him.

The answer failed in eleven minutes. They reassigned the gaps to Enterprise sensors, SGC intelligence, Navy evacuation, and allied continuity. Xander’s name went on a separate line marked AVAILABLE CAPABILITY, never REQUIRED.

“We do not build around the strongest person we have,” Carter said. “We build one that survives when he is across the planet, injured, refusing, or simply not there.”
