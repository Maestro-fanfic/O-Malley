Chapter 32 (19 of 24 in this book)

Data's Shadow

Scene 1

The recovery lab was a contradiction, a place where two universes stitched together with fiber-optic umbilicals and hope. Carter called it “the server room,” though it had begun life as a crew lounge; someone had left a faded Starfleet deck plan thumbtacked to the wall, and you could still see the outline of a couch beneath the new cargo shelving. There were no couches now. There were only racks of SGC diagnostic hardware, a double row of old Starfleet consoles, and, running the length of the ceiling, four parallel bundles of conduit that looked like giant’s spaghetti, zip-tied to any handhold that would take the stress.

Everything in the room sweated cold. The condensation tracked every line of the alloy walls, running in tiny, regular beads until it pooled in the ridges of the floor. Sometimes, if you listened, you could hear the ship settling: a slow, tectonic groan as the saucer section adjusted its weight on the Antarctic ice cap. Every time it did, Carter’s hand twitched a little closer to the emergency abort on her console. She never used it, but the muscle memory was there, a safety catch she would never admit to needing.

She stood over the engram-reader, a prototype so new it still smelled like laser-cut polycarbonate, its main chassis easily the size of a dorm fridge but so dense it took three SGC techs to manhandle it into place. The front panel was all status lights and fan grilles, with a single touch screen, its surface currently alive with a progress bar that refused to move faster than human anxiety would allow.

Carter’s fingers danced over the interface, tapping, toggling, reviewing, confirming, then tapping again. She wore the SGC field uniform, sleeves pushed back, and the faintest smudge of ink on the edge of her right palm. She pressed two fingers to her temple, briefly, the universal sign of a migraine in progress, then dropped her hand to the keyboard and kept working.

Jenny Calendar stood three paces back, arms folded so tightly her knuckles pressed white against her sleeves. She watched the readouts, not the operator, and if she breathed, it was inaudible under the twin hums of the server and the ship’s backup ventilation. Her uniform was Starfleet black and gray, but the right breast bore a Velcro patch: “Consultant,” in SGC gold, like a label on a borrowed laptop. Her hair was back, and for once there were no stray strands. She radiated a careful, composed stillness, the kind that made her feel like the only fixed point in the room.

The breakthrough came with neither fanfare nor even a beep. The progress bar rolled itself to full, then the machine issued a single, perfect tone: the kind that said not “you’re done” but “you survived.” Carter leaned in, scanned the console, and recited the output before she knew she’d done it.

“First recovery pass complete. Partition integrity: seventy percent. Data authenticity: high. Sequence continuity: thirty-six point one percent.” She caught the line with the air of someone who had just been handed the bill for an all-nighter. “It’s incomplete, but it’s real. We got him.”

Jenny’s arms dropped to her sides. She walked forward, the soles of her boots just audible on the wet deck. She bent to read the same line Carter just did, then again, as if the numbers might change.

She exhaled, just once. “Is it… enough?”

Carter didn’t answer right away. She brought up a diagnostic overlay, eyes scanning the flood of hexadecimal that blurred into columns of colored hash. She let it run, then looked at Jenny over the edge of the console.

“It’s thirty to forty percent of a memory. Not enough to reconstruct, but enough to confirm we have a viable root. If we keep pushing, we can try for a complete pass—but with this much fragmentation, it’s going to start filling gaps with synthetic logic. That’s where you get drift. Or corruption.” She looked back to the screen, more comfortable with data than faces. “Or we can preserve what we have, stabilize, and wait for better tools.”

Jenny’s face was impossible to read. She touched the edge of the console, fingers resting just above the warm metal, and said, “You’re describing the difference between surgery and autopsy, Captain.”

The sentence hung in the air for a full two seconds. It seemed to echo, not from the walls, but from the unspoken memories of another life. Carter turned away from the screen and looked straight at her, surprise breaking through the technical discipline.

Jenny met her gaze. “I’ve seen this before. In Starfleet, they call it a ‘cold extraction.’ If you push, you get back a facsimile, maybe even a functioning copy—but the original is irrecoverable. You lose the witness. You get a narrative instead of a life.”

Carter’s lips pressed thin, but she didn’t retreat. “If we wait, we risk decay. The sector’s already showing entropy. There’s no way of knowing how long these blocks will last, or if external exposure will corrupt the memory further.” She made herself pause, to let the medical analogy sink in. “If this was a living patient, you’d stabilize and then operate. But this isn’t a body. It’s a logic stack.”

Jenny shook her head, not quite a denial. “You’re thinking like an engineer. But he was more than just a logic stack.”

Carter let the silence ride for a moment, then said, “Do you want to see the output?”

Jenny nodded. She set her hand flat on the console, bracing herself.

Carter keyed in the command, then moved aside. The main display shifted from diagnostic to playback: a scrolling log of raw memory, stripped of context but annotated by timestamps and embedded with system tags. The lines blurred past, then the system froze on one highlighted fragment.

Jenny read aloud, her voice low and clinical. “Operational Log 37: I am aware of the heat on the bridge, though my sensors are not rated for tactile input. The Captain is alive, but it is uncertain for how long. I have initiated emergency protocols as per my last directive, but the override is corrupted. The crew’s voices are fading—no, not fading, only… intermittent.” She stopped. “He was awake through the crash.”

Carter didn’t flinch. “That’s consistent with the other logs. Data stayed online as long as the core allowed. He shut down only when the power buses fused.”

Jenny read the next line, slower. “I do not fear discontinuity. I have no emotional markers for fear. But the absence of the crew is not correct. There should be more. There must be more.”

She exhaled, then looked at Carter. “He was alone.”

Carter nodded, just once. “He knew the risk.”

They stood there, the whir of the engram-reader filling the silence.

From the wall behind, a new sound: the low, throbbing resonance of the ship’s hull as it flexed under the changing temperature. It sounded, for a moment, like the memory of a heartbeat.

Jenny said, “If we reconstruct, what do we lose?”

Carter pulled up a side-by-side projection, one display showing the intact fragments, the other a predicted interpolation. She gestured at the delta. “Best-case scenario? We get a functioning personality, but with gaps—missing connections, inconsistent recall, maybe even new logic born from the stress. Worst-case, we introduce artifacts. Confabulation, or a spiral into recursive breakdown.” She met Jenny’s eyes. “But there’s a nonzero chance it works. He comes back. Maybe not exactly as he was, but close.”

Jenny’s jaw set. “And the other option?”

“We keep the fragments as-is. Wait for a better tool, maybe something from the SGC or the academic teams back in the States. But every cycle we wait, the cold eats away at the integrity. Even with the containment, even with the power we’re shunting in, it’s a race.”

Jenny said nothing. She stared at the output, running her finger along the glass as if tracing the memory herself.

Carter leaned back, hands braced on the edge of the server rack. “You’re the ranking officer, technically. If you want to hold, I’ll log it.”

Jenny nodded, but her eyes were on the wall, unfocused.

“Beverly would wait,” she said, almost to herself.

Carter recognized the switch: the calendar turning, the overlay of another life. She nodded, slow. “If you want to talk to SGC, I can set up the relay. They’ve got a round-the-clock team watching the feed.”

Jenny shook her head, abrupt. “No. They’ll want results. They always do.” She inhaled, steadying herself. “I need… five minutes. Alone.”

Carter understood. She set the system to safe, cycled the main display to a holding pattern, and walked out, her boots clicking through the puddled water and into the corridor.

Jenny stood alone in the cold, arms folded, watching the ghost of a life scroll by in amber pixels.

The ship groaned again, louder this time, a warning that nothing here was meant to last.

Scene 2

Jenny’s five minutes of solitude lasted three and a half. On the fourth, the wall-mounted containment vault spun up, casting a barcode of blue light through the condensation fog.

The SGC containment vault was ugly. It had been dragged down from Colorado after the first positronic guardian event, cabled into the wall with cables thicker than her wrist. The vault was a box of banded alloy, covered with hatches, hazard stickers, and a single viewport—through which, if you looked hard, you could see the fractured ghost of the thing inside.

When the guardian came online, the air went sharp and clean. No ozone, no stink of coolant. Just a wash of pure, intentional silence. The vault’s status panel cycled through three colors—amber, then blue, then a heartbeat’s worth of green. Jenny straightened, but did not move closer.

She heard Carter’s return before the door fully opened. Carter didn’t stop, just walked in and stood at parade rest, backlit by the server array. She took in the lit vault, the readouts, and Jenny’s posture in one sweep.

The guardian spoke without preamble, but not from the usual speaker. Its voice vibrated through the hull, resonant and level. “Major Carter. You are aware that interpolation does not fill gaps. It invents them.”

Carter, not blinking, said, “It’s standard protocol. Incomplete blocks are reconstructed by comparison to an established baseline. It’s not invention—just probability.”

A pause. The vault’s blue light dropped to a lower intensity, as if the thing inside was considering her words.

“It is always invention,” the guardian said. “You risk a false continuity. You produce a likeness, not an identity.”

Jenny said, “Then what would you have us do?”

The vault pulsed, a slow, even light. “The distinction was made clear. You likened the process to surgery, or to autopsy. I ask: does the difference matter to a mind that was never continuous in the first place?”

Jenny’s jaw flexed, the way it always did when she was suppressing a real answer. “It matters to those left behind. It matters to me.”

The vault’s internal fans hummed. The lights shifted to a dim white.

“The living are always left behind,” said the guardian, softer.

Carter, perhaps seeing the direction this was headed, raised a hand. “We’re arguing definitions. The problem is practical. If we don’t act, the memory decays. If we do, we risk a corrupted recovery. There’s no path that preserves everything.”

“No path ever does,” said the guardian.

Jenny took a breath, then asked, “What would you choose, if you could?”

The vault’s lights cut for three seconds—a blackout, total and deliberate—then came back at half strength.

“That question,” the guardian said, “is a category error. My logic cannot make the choice for a being whose continuity I have already broken. The consent must come from the source, not from a witness or an attendant.”

Jenny’s voice was very steady. “You’re saying you can’t authorize your own restoration.”

“Correct.”

Carter, frowning, moved to her terminal and started scanning the diagnostic logs. Her typing was sharp, a counterpoint to the soft hum of the vault.

She stopped. “Wait. The energy draw on the last activation—there was a spike in the partition. The guardian’s logic drew from the same blocks as Data’s memory.”

Jenny turned to look. “You’re saying the guardian is… what, an echo?”

Carter shook her head, but the motion was complicated. “Not an echo. Not a copy, exactly. It’s running on a sample of the same logic, but it’s partitioned. It’s like… a read-only shadow. It can consult the memories, but it can’t alter them.”

The vault’s light went still—no cycling, no pulse, just a steady blue.

“I have an interest in the preservation of the memory blocks,” said the guardian. “But I am not the one who should decide the fate of the whole.”

Jenny stepped close to the containment vault, so close the blue from the viewport caught the edge of her jaw and painted it ghostly. “Is there enough left to wake him?”

The guardian was silent.

Carter answered. “If we attempt a partial reactivation, we risk burning out the remaining structure. But we might get a short-term response. Like rousing someone from a coma for a few seconds.”

Jenny’s hand rested on the vault. “Then we do that. We ask.”

The containment’s blue shifted, a softening at the center.

The guardian said, “That protocol aligns with my logic. If the subject consents, you may proceed.”

The three-way standoff dissolved, a fraction.

Jenny let her hand fall, then said, “No attempt at reconstruction without Data’s own informed consent. That’s the rule.”

Carter looked down, shut her eyes for a second, then set her jaw and started a new program on the secondary system. “I’ll run the risk assessment. If the numbers are better than fifty-fifty, we go.”

The guardian’s lights, steady now, did not fade. They watched the two women, silent as the ice beneath the ship.

Jenny turned to Carter. “Thank you,” she said, and meant it.

Carter keyed the new parameters. “You might want to let the others know. If this goes sideways, we’ll need backup.”

Jenny nodded, then left the lab.

Behind her, the containment vault glowed a pulse of blue, then subsided to near-black.

The recovery room felt colder than before, but it was, for the moment, honest.

Scene 3

The first alert came as a red-band text burst, not a voice call. Carter was in the middle of tuning the risk parameters on the reactivation protocol, eyes bloodshot from the twin screens’ glare, when her console locked out and flashed: “SGC OPERATIONS: PRIORITY URGENT. IMMEDIATE RESPONSE REQUIRED.”

She read the message twice. The first time, it didn’t register. The second time, she made a sound halfway between a laugh and a hiss.

She turned to Jenny, who had returned from her call with the States and was standing, arms folded, in the cold space between the mainframe and the recovery vault.

“DRI just filed a jurisdictional claim on the engram fragments,” Carter said. “They’re calling them classified anomalous material under domestic research statute.”

Jenny’s head snapped up. “How could they know? The recovery log just finished an hour ago.”

Carter’s hands were steady as she paged through the details, but her heart rate was spiking. “We’ve got leaks. Or watchers. Doesn’t matter. If the DRI’s got a claim, they’ll try to lock down the data at the first opportunity.”

“Can they?” Jenny asked.

Carter didn’t answer, just started copying the logs to three separate encrypted drives, running the duplication in parallel. “Only if the White House backs them.”

#

Four thousand miles north, in a room that had not changed since the Eisenhower years, the Situation Room’s fluorescent lighting made every face look like it had been up all night.

Bartlet stood at the head of the table. Leo McGarry was at his right shoulder, White House counsel to his left. Across from them, the DRI counsel sat with hands folded, back perfectly straight, eyes fixed on the manila folder resting on the table before him.

The DRI’s senior attorney was not tall, not young, and not in the mood to negotiate. He wore a charcoal suit and the same noncommittal half-smile as every prior administration’s favorite fixer. His folder was labeled “SINGULARITY” in block capitals; no one at the table had opened it.

Leo said, “Let’s get to the point.”

DRI: “The material recovered from the Enterprise’s computer core constitutes a unique instance of an anomalous cognitive imprint. As such, under the Domestic Research and Investigation Act of 1976, all material falls under the jurisdiction of the DRI, regardless of point of recovery.”

White House counsel didn’t flinch. “The Antarctic recovery was a military joint op. SGC has jurisdiction for all off-world or non-terrestrial artifacts.”

DRI: “The computer core was in U.S. sovereign territory at the time of extraction, and the material in question is not a physical artifact. It’s digital. The legal precedent is clear.”

Bartlet drummed a worn Parker pen against his binder, once, twice, three times, then set it down with care. “You’re not arguing over who gets to play with the toys. You’re arguing over the definition of Data as a person, or as a file.”

DRI: “I am arguing the letter of the law, Mr. President.”

Bartlet smiled a tired, academic smile. “It’s always the letter of the law, until someone decides to amend the alphabet.”

Leo leaned in, eyes tired but sharp. “The standing protocol is the interim personhood framework, designed by Dr. Weir’s committee and reviewed by this office. If the recovered fragments belong to a recognized living witness, the DRI has to go through the full appeals process before any unilateral action.”

DRI counsel opened the manila folder for the first time. He didn’t look down. “The personhood framework was written for biologicals. Data is neither.”

White House counsel said, “That’s factually untrue and already decided. The Southwick Memo—”

DRI: “Applied to a live witness. Not to postmortem reconstruction.”

The room went quiet, except for the air conditioner, which cycled on with a stutter.

Bartlet said, “Suppose we run with your logic. Suppose Data is not a person, but a program. Does your agency have the authority to terminate, reformat, or overwrite him?”

DRI: “With due process, yes.”

Bartlet looked at Leo. Leo looked at White House counsel. White House counsel stopped writing.

Bartlet reached across the table, picked up a black slab of metal—Carter’s secure relay, which had been placed on the table at the start of the meeting—and toggled it on.

A voice played out, flat and exhausted, with just enough edge to catch everyone’s attention. Xander’s voice.

“You’re asking me if a copy of someone’s memories is still that person. I’m sixteen and even I know that’s the wrong question. The right question is whether you get to decide it’s not, just because it’s convenient.”

There was a long, brittle pause.

DRI counsel closed his folder, and for the first time, his expression changed: not anger, not fear, but the haunted look of a man who’d just remembered his own humanity.

Bartlet smiled, but there was no joy in it. “The administration’s position is that the recovered memory belongs to its owner. The recovery will proceed under joint custody, SGC and Jenny Calendar, until we have a formal framework. If you wish to contest, you may, but you’ll do it in the open.”

DRI: “Noted, Mr. President.” He stood, gathered the folder, and left, not slamming the door but not closing it gently, either.

Leo turned to Bartlet. “Will the testimony hold up?”

Bartlet looked at his cold coffee, then set it down. “It doesn’t have to. It just has to hold until we can build a law that does.”

Leo nodded, then left with White House counsel.

Bartlet sat alone for a moment, then toggled the relay again.

“Carter, you’re cleared to proceed. Do it by the book.”

#

In Antarctica, the news hit as a one-line text: “Custody retained. Joint protocol in effect. Proceed.”

Carter exhaled. Jenny allowed herself a tight, honest smile.

On the engram-reader, the status indicators flashed amber, then rolled to green. The containment vault’s blue shifted, not brighter, but with a complexity neither woman had seen before—an overlay of fractal patterns, like a logic tree in bloom.

They would have to learn what it meant.

The next phase was waiting. Carter could do that from the ice; Jenny could do it from Sunnydale. Forty-eight hours later, after transferring the day-to-day watch to Carter under their joint-custody protocol and making her promise that no reconstruction attempt would begin without both of them on the line, Jenny boarded the northbound logistics flight to Christchurch. An Air Force connection carried her the rest of the way to California. She remained Data's co-custodian by secure link, but the people waiting at home were no longer abstractions on a monitor.
