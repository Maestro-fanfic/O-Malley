Chapter 25 (12 of 24 in this book)

"Ripples"

Scene 1

Second period at Sunnydale High. Three hundred and twenty-seven feet from the main entrance to the farthest classroom, if you counted the route taken by the popular kids and not the one that cut through the outdoor breezeway past the custodian’s cart. Xander sat at the battered desk he’d inherited from a guy two grades ahead who once carved a seven-inch tally mark down its center, as if measuring how long until parole. The surface of the desk was a micro-history of the town’s hormonal urges and generational drift: initials, phone numbers, “SUCK IT, SNYDER,” a four-digit locker combo, a still-wet gob of blue gum pasted under the metal bracket. There were worse places to be, he supposed.

He drummed his pencil next to the watch on his wrist, a plain Casio with no visible buttons but more classified technology than the entire math wing. The second hand ticked, one-two, perfectly synchronized to the school’s analog clock. Every seventeen ticks, the watch’s face did a micro-flicker, unreadable unless you looked for it, a heartbeat for the program that Carter’s people had installed, or, as O’Neil put it, “your government-mandated conscience.” He hadn’t triggered the panic function all week, but it was nice to know it was there.

From the back row, he could see Willow’s red hair bent over her spiral notebook, her handwriting so precise it looked forged. Two seats up and one to the left, she glanced at him, then slid a folded note across the aisle at shoe level. He snagged it with the tip of his sneaker, opened it in his lap, and read: “Any sign of last night’s patrol? Didn’t see the duster after 11. -W.”

He grinned, and wrote back: “No movement since, but I think the civvies on perimeter are Navy. How’s your end?” He sent it down the row via the time-honored flick-and-catch technique. The normalcy was almost suspiciously thick.

Principal Snyder’s voice burst from the PA with the dry precision of a man who considered every syllable ammunition. “Attention, students. After-school detentions will be held in room 214 today. Attendance is not optional. The cafeteria will close at 2:30. Please do not linger.” The words had the effect of sandpaper on the classroom’s collective nerves. The substitute teacher, a twitchy adjunct with the complexion of skim milk, paused in his reading of World History: Modern and checked the clock, as if worried detention might be contagious.

Xander kept his gaze fixed on the hallway window, a two-inch slit in the reinforced classroom door. He could see Snyder’s head bobbing past, then pausing, the pale blot of his face aligned with the window just long enough to clock who was paying attention and who was reading the notes passed under desks. The sight of him made the room’s temperature drop a full five degrees.

Then something went wrong.

It wasn’t an alarm or a gunshot or the thud of a vampire in broad daylight. It wasn’t even the low-grade paranoia of someone watching through an unfamiliar lens. It was a pressure, localized, like the air on one side of your face getting dense and heavy while the other stayed normal. A weirdness with no name, the kind of thing Giles would call “an ontological precursor event” and Willow would call “something with a signature.” For Xander, it was like walking into a spiderweb you couldn’t see and suddenly realizing the world had corners that you’d never noticed before.

He raised his hand to sharpen his pencil, because it was what a real student might do when encountering existential dread. The wall-mounted sharpener by the window had been broken since the Carter Administration, but the ritual was important. As he stood, the pressure increased—not painful, not even uncomfortable, just present, directional, like the feeling of a heavy blanket being pulled over your side of the bed. It came from the outside, past the parking lot, in the direction of City Hall. That part was crystal clear.

He looked back at his desk, considered the pros and cons of returning to it, and then did so without sharpening the pencil. The pressure abated a little, like it was disappointed he hadn’t tested the boundary.

Willow had already clocked the entire sequence. When he looked at her, she held up the next note, already written: “You okay?”

He drew a line down the page, turned it so she could see, and wrote: “No idea.”

Snyder, still framed by the hallway window, seemed not to blink for a full minute. He simply watched, hands clasped behind his back, as if willing one of them to try for the door. Xander resisted the urge to wave.

On the intercom, the PA resumed: “Students, please note that any unauthorized departures from the premises will be recorded and referred to the appropriate authority. Repeat: unauthorized departures will be referred.” Snyder’s version of a love letter.

Xander closed his eyes and thought about the watch on his wrist, the perimeter it represented, the invisible fences that kept certain people in and others out. The boundary line, whatever it was, had gotten smarter.

He opened his eyes, saw Willow scribbling equations into the margin of her notebook, and for a moment he wanted to believe in the comfort of a world where the only things that mattered were grades and gossip and the last period of the day.

Instead, he looked at the watch, at the second hand clicking inexorably forward, and waited for the next shift in the air.

Scene 2

The archive room was twelve degrees colder than the rest of City Hall, and Rook wondered if that was the insulation or a byproduct of the shelves. A half-century of municipal records lined the metal uprights: tax ledgers, planning maps, event programs from civic celebrations dated in fading fountain pen. They’d dedicated the lowest level of the building to boxes, and each box to a moment of the town’s supposed history, as if the act of shelving could save anything from being re-written.

Rook did not bother with gloves. She’d checked the humidity on her first visit and found it safe enough for bare skin, and there was a certain pleasure in touching the artifacts directly, even when she suspected most of them were ghostwritten or redacted to serve someone else’s narrative. She wore dark slacks and a navy blazer, the sleeves shoved up past her elbows, a button-down shirt in a pattern only she would notice, and the reading glasses, always, on their chain. There was nothing occult in her appearance. She could have been an auditor, or a mid-level compliance officer. Most days, she was.

She opened the box labeled INCORPORATION DOCUMENTS 1841 — ORIGINAL SURVEY, set the lid aside, and thumbed through the folders. The page she wanted was folded in thirds, the way an architect would file a blueprint for long storage. She set it on the table, careful with the edges, and examined the lines: the original plat of Sunnydale, town lots and drainage and all the gentle subterfuge of a settlement intended for more than settlers. There was a faint scent of beeswax, not from the archive but from the disc she withdrew from her own pocket. The disc was flat, half the diameter of a coffee mug, grey-black with swirls of silver inside. Its weight was modest, but its presence was deliberate.

She sandwiched it between the vellum and the cardboard backer. The two did not fit perfectly, but that was the point. A sympathetic material had to be visible, but not dominant; the working would pull energy from the point of friction. She closed the box, slid it back into place, and wrote the time and location in her ledger with the same pen she used for airline receipts and parking validation.

Wilkins watched from the threshold, expression one step past cordial but still in the range of “mayor at a ribbon-cutting.” His suit was charcoal, tie in a shade of blue that would play well on television. He looked less like a person than a stock photo for “leadership.”

“Will it hold?” he asked.

Rook closed her ledger. “It held before I placed it. This is reinforcement.”

He strolled into the archive, hands in his pockets, voice soft enough not to echo. “The distinction is recognized?”

“Yes. The working now identifies Alexander Harris as a resident with standing, and the alternate as an external entity. The overlap is preserved, but the system will route pressure accordingly.”

Wilkins grinned. “Wonderful. That will spare us the embarrassment of a more physical escalation. And the school record?”

Rook glanced at her watch. “Two hours, plus a stop for access at the main office. The registrar is meticulous.”

“Then the final anchor?”

She nodded. “Restfield. The marker is undisturbed, and I’ll be there before sunset.”

Wilkins rocked back on his heels, as if considering a joke and then deciding against it. “I appreciate your efficiency, Dr. Rook.”

She returned the compliment by not smiling. “You paid for professional grade.”

He gave a two-finger salute and retreated, but lingered in the corridor, not quite leaving her alone in the archive. Rook closed her bag, checked for the next disc, and made a mental note to sterilize the writing hand before her next client appointment. Some energies had a habit of tracking.

She let herself out, careful to keep the swing of the heavy door within the normal range for a public building. The hinges were steel, recent, and greased, but the threshold still groaned with a rebar undertone, as if the building itself minded the transaction.

The lights flickered once, a stutter in the cheap fluorescent, and she caught Wilkins looking up at the tubes with that same faint amusement, as if he’d seen it all before and liked the tune.

He left the corridor first, his step unhurried. Rook watched the shadow he cast slide along the far wall, then moved to the exit herself, counting the turns and blind corners until she was back in the ordinary world.

Above ground, in the gentle morning sun, Sunnydale’s new line of separation snapped into place with the kind of silence that a mathematician would appreciate, if not anyone else.

Scene 3

Giles’s office smelled like fresh copy paper and whatever tea Willow had steeped in the microwave last week and then forgotten behind the microfiche reader. Xander leaned against the mesh cage door, arms folded over a backpack he’d never gotten around to emptying. After hours, the library felt more like a dead mall than an academic sanctum: fluorescent lights half-buzzed overhead, the floor was marked with a road map of scuffs and pitted tile, and in the center of the room, Giles’s desk hosted three open books, a loose sheaf of printer paper, and one empty mug labeled “Watchers Drink and Know Things.” Willow typed in short, violent bursts at the main computer terminal, hair hiding her face. Buffy occupied the office doorframe, her jacket slung over her shoulder, face set in that don’t-waste-my-time mask she used for both vampires and adults.

“So,” Buffy said, “tell it again. Only this time, skip the part where it’s a ‘vibe’ and use actual words.”

Xander took a breath, exhaled, tried again. “It wasn’t a curse, exactly. Or a ward. It was like… you know when you’re about to step off a curb, and your brain expects a certain distance, but then it’s two inches lower than you thought, and your skeleton does that full-body lurch?” He pantomimed it, almost losing the backpack. “Except this was aimed at me specifically, and it came from City Hall. I know it sounds—”

Willow, still typing, said, “No, that tracks. If it was just a suppression working, you’d have gotten the standard static headrush. This was probably a geas or a contract construct, which would fit the Mayor’s legal style. Let me see if there’s a public records anchor.” She clicked into the city’s database, muttering “no proxy, no VPN, come on…” and squinted at the screen.

Buffy looked at Giles. “You buying this?”

Giles, who’d been polishing his glasses with a microcloth that looked like it had been laundered as often as his shirts, said, “Given the events of last month, it’s not impossible. Principal Snyder has been taking a particular interest in both your behavior and your attendance, Xander. The authorities here have a… talent for codifying what should be ephemeral.”

Buffy arched an eyebrow. “So bureaucracy is the new black magic?”

Xander grinned. “Makes sense. The first thing any evil empire does is paperwork.”

Willow snapped her fingers. “Here! 1992 municipal boundary re-survey. It references an older document from 1841, which is in the City Hall archive but—wait—‘restricted access, administrative hold.’ That’s not standard. Usually it would just say ‘offsite storage.’”

Giles replaced his glasses, his focus laser-straight on Willow’s monitor. “May I?” He crossed the space in three long steps, reading over Willow’s shoulder. “Oh dear. That’s very specific phrasing. They’ve marked it as both a historical document and an active boundary artifact.”

Buffy shifted, her voice half-sarcasm. “So you’re telling me Sunnydale’s magical problems are the result of… city planning?”

Willow nodded. “It’s a thing, actually. There’s a whole subfield in digital forensics called ‘jurisdictional geomancy.’ Some towns—usually really old or really cursed—encode protections right into their street grids, public utility records, stuff like that. If you want to keep a supernatural entity out, or in, you use city paperwork as a sort of operating system. The Mayor is really, really good at it.”

Xander said, “So that’s why it felt legal. It was a cease and desist order.”

Buffy, still in the doorway, looked unconvinced but interested. “Did it stop you from doing anything?”

“No. Not yet,” Xander said. “It just wanted me to know I was on notice. Like a warning shot. I tried to ignore it, but the pressure didn’t go away until I went back to my desk.”

Giles frowned, as if expecting an exam question. “Has Ethan Rayne visited since last week?”

“Not in person. Next training is tomorrow afternoon.” Xander felt a twist of irritation at that—like being benched when he’d finally leveled up. “Why?”

“It’s possible,” Giles said, choosing each word, “that this is a test run. The pressure may escalate, or it may be designed to force you into a reaction that triggers a more aggressive response. Either way, you’ll need to be ready for the full effect.”

Willow found a second document. “Here. City Hall filed an emergency session two months ago to update the boundaries of ‘affected parties’ in the case of a disaster event. The language is pure legalese, but they specifically carve out Alexander Harris as a resident of record, and—” She scrolled, then blinked. “They actually put ‘alternate identity: none on file’ in the minutes.”

Giles’s face went still, a mask he only wore when he’d just realized the puzzle pieces fit, but the picture was of something terrible. “A jurisdictional working anchored to the original incorporation would be exceptionally robust. It’s not a curse at all. It’s an operating system for the town.”

Buffy pushed off the frame, voice crisp. “So we can’t just punch it.”

Giles said, “No. We very much cannot just punch it.”

“Great,” Xander said, “my one skill, rendered decorative.” He slouched against the cage, waited for someone to say it would be okay. Nobody did.

Willow kept searching, her fingers moving faster as if she could outpace the slow weight building in the room. “If there’s an anchor, there’s a key. Even bureaucratic magic needs an access protocol. I just need to find the loophole.”

Buffy said, “When you do, make it punchable.”

Xander let the team’s noise fill the space, and for the first time all afternoon, the weird pressure at the edge of his senses faded. It was nice, he thought, to know that the normal rules of the world still bent a little for friends who cared enough to fight City Hall, even if City Hall had a mystical superuser account.

He just hoped the next version of the working didn’t come with an armed response team.

Scene 4

Ethan Rayne arrived twenty minutes ahead of schedule, an act of either desperation or high drama. The canvas bag slung over his shoulder clinked with glass vials and reeked of cedar resin and a hint of black pepper, a scent profile that had once cleared out the entire chem lab at Oxford. He swept into the library with a quick, birdlike scan of the room, then deposited the bag on the research table as if expecting someone to frisk him for contraband.

Giles met him with a tight-lipped nod and began briefing in clipped, bullet-point sentences: “Administrative working. Boundary effect. Origin point is City Hall, with secondary reinforcement at school records office. Intent is to constrain or expel a specific identity from jurisdiction. Target: Xander. Not the physical, but the public and private signatures.”

Ethan, to his credit, did not interrupt. He sorted his tools with a precision bordering on surgical: a compass with the directional letters replaced by runes; a hand mirror rimmed in blackened iron; a piece of notepaper densely crammed with minuscule handwriting, the margin stained with what looked like coffee but might have been blood. He nodded, once, to let Giles know he was following.

Xander hovered at the edge of the table, close enough to see but not to participate. Buffy had taken a seat on the upper steps, propping her feet on the railing and giving the impression of someone listening to an audiobook of the meeting while planning the grocery list for her mom. Willow held her laptop open but had paused in her typing to watch Ethan’s every move with open fascination.

When Giles finished, Ethan unfolded the notepaper, set it next to the mirror, and lined up the compass so the needle pointed precisely east. He held the note at a shallow angle over the mirror, sighted along the reflection, and blinked three times in quick succession. “It’s not a curse,” he said. “It’s a contract. Someone wrote this up like a deed of trust. There’s an offer, a signature, a beneficiary. Which means…”

He let the implication dangle. Buffy supplied: “Someone in town hired a magical lawyer?”

Ethan smiled, a flash of the old chaos. “The best. The clause structure is professional. The language of the working is careful, precise. Not homegrown. It’s a franchise operation. If I had to guess, I’d say our Mayor contracted this out, but the instrument is designed to outlast even him.”

Willow leaned forward. “Can you tell what the terms are?”

Ethan tapped the paper. “The party of the first part: Alexander Lavelle Harris, known resident, as defined by school records, household utilities, and municipal filings. The party of the second part: ‘external agent or entity with no standing in jurisdiction,’ a category that happens to fit our friend here whenever he puts on the suit. The consideration is ‘maintenance of civic order,’ and the term is ‘for the duration of the protected administration.’ Which is a fancy way of saying: the working will persist as long as the current Mayor is in office, and perhaps longer if he named a successor.”

Buffy said, “So what happens if Xander ignores it?”

Ethan gave a shrug. “It won’t hurt you, but it will make things… difficult. Any action you take as Superman inside town boundaries will trigger resistance. Redirection, sudden ‘accidents’ with official paperwork, or even a supernatural summons to report at City Hall. If you escalate—say, try to break the working by force—it will escalate in return.”

Xander frowned. “Is there a way to uninstall it? Wipe and reboot?”

Ethan shook his head. “Not like a computer virus. This is more like an employment contract you didn’t know you signed. To break it, you have to violate the central premise.”

Willow, who’d gone thoughtful, said, “What’s the contradiction?”

Ethan’s smile was genuine. “Exactly the right question. The entire working assumes that Xander Harris and Superman are two legally and mystically separable entities. It’s built on the idea that the resident can remain even if the power is expelled.”

Xander blinked. “But that’s… not true. It’s all me. I don’t have an off switch.”

Ethan looked at Giles, who said, “If the contract is based on a false dichotomy, then exposing that flaw at the anchor point could break the whole mechanism.”

Willow: “Which is the survey marker in Restfield. The original plat for the town.”

Buffy swung her legs off the railing, all business now. “So we need to go there, and do… what, exactly?”

Ethan packed his tools. “We disrupt the anchor, preferably while the system is watching. If the contradiction is large enough, the working will collapse under its own illogic. But whoever commissioned this will know, immediately, that we’re coming.”

Buffy smiled. “Let them. I like an honest fight.”

Xander watched the tools go back in the bag. He felt oddly proud that, out of everyone in the room, he was the only one whose mere existence could break a legal contract just by being himself. “Guess I’ll be the test case, then.”

Willow looked up at him, and for a second he saw the echo of their grade-school years, the two of them sneaking out to the playground after the bell, never quite sure if they were hiding or waiting to be found.

She said, “Just… be careful?”

Xander nodded. “That’s the plan.”

Ethan zipped the bag. “It’s settled, then. We meet at the survey marker tomorrow night.”

Giles said, “Thank you, Ethan. Try not to bring the rest of your baggage with you.”

Ethan offered a mock salute. “No promises.”

They all left the library in staggered intervals, as if the town itself was watching and it was safer not to be seen in a group.

As Xander walked home, he kept his hands in his pockets and his head down, but he was already thinking about what it would be like to walk through that boundary as himself, not as a power or a legend or a file in City Hall, but as a kid who could break the world simply by refusing to be divided.

Scene 5

They assembled at the library table, a geometry of half-filled mugs, dog-eared volumes, and the glowing rectangle of Willow’s laptop. The table itself had survived three years of late-night research, two minor arsons, and a number of magical mishaps that probably wouldn’t pass EPA standards. It was, Xander thought, an excellent place for a group project.

Willow set up her station first, flipping open the notebook and fanning out Ethan’s notation alongside the city records she’d printed in color. The public library’s ink budget would take a hit, but for a good cause. “Okay, so the working is distributed,” she said, voice clear and fast, “across three anchors: City Hall, the school records office, and the original survey marker at Restfield. Each one validates the others. If you kill just one, the rest get weaker, but it doesn’t fall until all three are offline or unsynchronized.”

She spoke like she was diagramming a logic circuit, hands tracing invisible lines on the tabletop. “The problem is, they have separate authentication pathways. Which means—”

“Which means,” Giles cut in, “that we will need to address each anchor simultaneously, or in close enough succession that the working cannot self-correct.”

He was already moving, drawing three books from the cage shelves. The first was a battered red volume stamped “CALIFORNIA: CIVIL GOVERNANCE, 1841-1920.” The second was a typeset monograph on magical contracts, with so many post-its it looked like a bird’s nest. The third was a slim, hand-bound pamphlet with Giles’s own name at the bottom, and a title that Xander read aloud before he could stop himself: “On the Legal Fictions of the Hellmouth: Precedents in Jurisdictional Magic.”

Buffy, perched on the counter near the phone, rolled her eyes. “Light bedtime reading?”

Giles didn’t look up. “If we are to succeed, we must know the exact terms. These contracts, for all their mysticism, are often more vulnerable to procedural contradiction than to raw force.”

Ethan, off to one side, had spread out his mirror, compass, and a small velvet pouch of objects that might have been magical foci or just a very weird dice set. He was using the compass to plot angles, making tiny marks on the mirror’s edge with a black Sharpie. “It’s elegant,” he said. “If you’re into this sort of thing. The working parses every intervention Xander makes through an identity algorithm. If he acts as himself, it logs the event as ‘local activity’—but if he uses the powers or the name, it triggers an escalation. The more public the action, the stronger the response.”

Xander watched Willow, and said, “So it’s a spam filter, but for heroics.”

Willow grinned. “Exactly. But it has to match you, legally and magically. If you could blur the boundary—be both at once, or neither—it might confuse the system.”

Buffy, after a brief and efficient call to the Navy’s local support desk, set the receiver down and announced, “Perimeter’s in place by ten p.m. They’ll run a silent cordon around the cemetery, and if anything civilian pops up, they’ll move it along. But they stay outside the fence unless we signal.”

Xander, who’d been enjoying the show, realized his role when Willow slid a folder toward him. “You’re the primary. You protect the site, respond to threats, and—if the working turns nasty—do the thing only you can do.”

He nodded, trying not to look like he didn’t know what that was yet. “Copy that.”

Willow continued, “Once we reach the marker, Ethan and Giles need two minutes to align the logic and disrupt the anchor. If Rook or whoever shows up, delay them. Don’t escalate unless you have to. Any external agents—demon, vampire, city employee—try to keep them away from the working.”

Buffy: “What about the other anchors? We can’t get into City Hall or the school records office at midnight.”

Willow: “That’s the trick. We don’t have to. If we introduce enough noise at the survey marker, it will trigger a self-check at the other nodes. It will either crash the system, or Rook will have to come out and defend the anchor herself.”

Ethan, never looking up from his mirror, said, “I hope she does. I want to meet the practitioner who wrote this logic.”

Giles muttered, “Try not to duel with the contractor in public, please.”

Ethan smiled. “I make no promises.”

Xander said, “I feel like a very expensive paperweight.”

Willow, without missing a beat: “You’re a very expensive paperweight who can catch a car. Stop complaining.”

He smiled, but there was a pulse of worry underneath. If the town was already this good at bending reality to its rules, what would happen if it decided that even the idea of Xander Harris was a liability?

He pushed the thought down, tried to focus on the page Willow had handed him. It was a floor plan of Restfield Cemetery, not to scale, but with every approach mapped and every possible civilian route highlighted in green. The marker was in a dead corner of the lot, up against the fence and invisible from any road. It was, he realized, the perfect place for an ambush or a legal summons. Or both.

Buffy closed her eyes, exhaled, and said, “We ready?”

Giles: “As ready as we can be, given the circumstances.”

Willow: “Just need to finalize the counter-logic. If anyone has a better word for ‘ontological continuity,’ now’s the time.”

Ethan, softly: “Persistence of self.”

Buffy: “Being a person.”

Giles: “Identity indivisible.”

Willow typed each in, not erasing the last until she saw which phrase the system would accept. She hit enter, watched the script run, and then said, “Got it. The working can’t parse a single entity with two legal faces. If we make you walk into the marker as both Alexander Harris and the superhero, it should force the working to reconcile, and break.”

Buffy, rising to her feet, said, “Let’s go then.”

Ethan packed his gear, Giles grabbed his books, Willow shouldered her laptop. Xander flexed his hand, just to make sure it still worked.

They left the library together, a party of five, armed with nothing more than paperwork, logic, and the idea that nobody could split you in half without your permission.

At the front doors, Willow turned and said, “Remember, if anything weird happens, don’t split up.”

Buffy said, “That’s your line.”

Willow smiled. “It’s a good line.”

They walked out, the fluorescent lights humming behind them, and stepped into the night.

Somewhere in the darkness, a boundary marker waited. So did the person who wanted to draw the next line.

Scene 6

Restfield Cemetery, night. The moon was a thin, indifferent quarter, shedding just enough light to make the fog look thicker and the markers older than they were. Xander followed the path through the east gate, the hard ground crunching under boots, Buffy setting the pace as if she had always known this place by heart. Giles and Ethan walked together, one hand on a book and the other in the pocket of a long coat, as if to keep a hidden flask or a last-ditch spell within reach. Willow moved behind them, her laptop zipped inside a blue backpack with enough stickers to make the NSA nervous. Xander brought up the rear, less to watch their six and more to scan the sky and fence line. The watch on his wrist was silent, but the second hand had gone full psycho—he counted three skips in the last minute.

The marker was exactly where Willow’s map said it would be: a knee-high block of granite wedged between two headstones so worn the names had blurred to lichen and cracks. The stone read S.D. CORP. 1841 in a blocky hand, and looked as if nobody had visited it in a century except, maybe, the groundskeeper or someone with a thing for historical trivia.

Two unmarked sedans were parked on the access road, headlights off, the Navy support crew holding a soft perimeter and trying very hard not to be seen. One guy in a blazer leaned against a trunk, squinting at a clipboard in the dark. A second car, windows rolled down just enough to catch a radio signal, had its hazard lights set to a dim, irregular blink. No one else in town would have noticed the pattern, but to Xander it said: “We’re here, but only if you need us. Otherwise, the dead can keep their secrets.”

Dr. Evelyn Rook was already at the marker, standing with her back to the approach, both hands resting lightly at her sides. Her hair was pinned up, blazer immaculate, slacks pressed, and her only visible luggage was the battered leather ledger balanced open on the granite. She did not glance up as they arrived.

“I wondered how long the mapping would take,” she said, projecting a conversational calm that was completely out of sync with the hour and venue.

Buffy stopped at six paces. The rest fell into a loose semicircle. Rook did not turn until she finished whatever she was writing. When she did, it was with the smallest of pivots, making eye contact with each member of the team as if to calibrate their threat level. Her eyes skipped past Ethan, barely registering him, then settled on Xander for a full beat.

She closed the ledger with a thumb and said, “You made good time.”

Buffy, arms folded, didn’t blink. “You expecting us?”

Rook tilted her head, considering. “It was inevitable. You’re here to contest the anchor. I’m here to witness and, if necessary, record the outcome.”

Xander stepped forward, keeping one foot on the packed dirt and one on the grass. “You’re not here to fight?”

“If it comes to that, I’ll defend myself. But the contract is not a force field, nor am I.” Rook gestured at the marker, then at Willow’s backpack. “The working is conditional. As long as the clauses are not violated, there’s no physical enforcement.”

Ethan, standing a little apart from the group, flipped open his own notebook and began writing, making a tiny notation after each sentence Rook spoke. He did not look up.

Giles cleared his throat. “Might we inquire, then, as to the exact terms you are defending tonight?”

Rook smiled without showing teeth. “A simple one: To maintain the distinction between the resident of Sunnydale and the power that operates without local standing. My client believes the latter to be a destabilizing influence. The contract aims to keep the two as separate as legal fiction allows.”

Buffy: “Your client being the Mayor?”

Rook said nothing, but did not dispute it.

Xander said, “So if I leave town, everything goes back to normal?”

“Normal is a slippery word here. But the system would prefer it, yes.”

He glanced at Willow. She mouthed: “Don’t.”

He said it anyway. “I’m not leaving. This is my home, too.”

Rook gave a tiny shrug. “Then we proceed.”

Buffy took one step forward. “Let’s just make this clear. He lives here.”

Rook: “Alexander Harris lives here. Superman does not. Not as the system sees it.”

Ethan, still writing, muttered to himself, “Interesting. The logic’s recursive. I’d almost call it elegant.”

Willow set her laptop on a flat-topped marker and powered it up, the glow from the screen momentarily casting all their faces in a shade of blue. “We’re ready when you are,” she said.

Giles opened a book, held it with one hand, and waited for the right moment to start reading. Buffy kept her stance, never breaking eye contact with Rook. Xander waited, feeling the weird sense that the next move belonged to him, but not knowing whether to push or hold.

Rook looked at him, then at Ethan. “You’ll want to begin. The longer we wait, the more likely we draw outside attention.”

Ethan grinned, the expression at once childish and ancient. “You do the magic, I’ll do the math.”

They began.

Scene 7

Willow ran her laptop off the battery, the screen’s glow washing out the contrast of the grass and headstones. She cross-referenced Ethan’s notes with the city’s public records database and a mirror of the school district’s server that, if anyone ever audited, would raise more questions than it answered. “Key phrase,” she said, voice flat: “outside power without standing.” She highlighted the clause on all three anchor nodes. “The language here assumes any power not registered at all three sites is an anomaly, and must be routed to the boundary.”

She set her fingers on the keys, eyes darting between lines of legalese and Giles’s reference material. “But the original definition of ‘resident’ is way broader than the city wants it to be,” she said. “It’s not about paperwork. It’s about behavior and persistent presence. There’s no role distinction.”

Giles, who had found the relevant passage in the 1841 charter, read it aloud, voice projecting enough to cut through the chill: “Resident shall mean any person maintaining continuous and unbroken existence within the boundaries of the corporation, irrespective of profession, origin, or function.” He pronounced the last words with the precision of a closing argument.

Ethan, meanwhile, was kneeling by the survey marker, the compass set flat, the mirror poised face-down on the granite. He tapped the compass needle, aligning it with a thin scratch on the stone, then said, in a voice almost too low to catch, a sequence of syllables that sounded like radio static or bad AM gospel. The effect was immediate. The air around the marker seemed to flex, like a heat mirage on a blacktop, and then everything went sharper than before.

Xander’s job was to keep watch, but when the wave hit, he could feel the working’s pressure spike, then falter, then go utterly limp. It was the reverse of the morning’s lurch—like a door you’d always known to be locked swinging wide on the first try. He blinked, almost surprised by the lack of resistance.

On the west side of the cemetery, two vampires made a move: both in full game-face, hair slicked back in last week’s nightclub fashion, hands low like they expected a gunfight and not a stake. Xander intercepted them before they were ten paces from the main path, one motion sending the first into a headstone with a crack, the second flat onto the grass. He snapped a stake out of his coat’s inside seam and dispatched both in under five seconds, neither even having time to hiss. He wiped the stake clean on the grass, checked for more movement, then returned to his post without comment.

On the east, Buffy handled a third, a heavier one in a bomber jacket, who went down with a single swing and an eye roll that suggested she was bored by the lack of variety.

The Navy team held the road. One of the support guys gave a thumbs-up in Xander’s direction, then went back to his clipboard. The medical kit, unopened, sat on the trunk like a polite promise.

Back at the marker, Willow typed her final line: “Continuous legal and civic person.” She hit enter, and all three anchor points lit green on her map overlay. “That should do it,” she said, voice shaky with adrenaline.

Giles, book still open, recited: “In all matters of standing, no distinction shall be made between identity and essence, save where provided by law.” He looked at Rook as he finished, waiting for the challenge.

Ethan, satisfied, packed the mirror and compass back into his bag, then turned to watch the sky, as if half-expecting fireworks.

But nothing exploded. The stone did not crack. There was only a faint pop, a static whine at the edge of hearing, and the feeling of pressure—already fading—vanished for good.

Rook, at the edge of the group, had not moved. She watched the dissolution with her pen uncapped, her notebook open and pages already filling with notations. Her expression never changed, but her hand moved faster, as if the process itself was more interesting than the result.

Buffy: “Status?”

Xander shrugged. “Feels… normal? Maybe better than normal.”

Willow, double-checking her screens, said, “It’s clean. The anchors are mutually recognizing. No split.”

Giles: “The precedent is established.”

Ethan: “Next round will be less fun.”

Xander took a breath, and for the first time in days, it didn’t catch on a buried snag. He stretched, felt his feet root into the earth, and realized he could walk in any direction without consequence.

He caught Rook’s eye, and she gave a bare nod, like a professor acknowledging a well-argued thesis.

The group lingered a moment, then packed their things and left. Rook remained, documenting the aftermath, her ledger now three pages thicker than when they’d arrived.

The cemetery was quiet as they made their way back to the main road, the moon painting their shadows in long, clean lines.

Some problems, Xander thought, could be solved with a little paperwork and a refusal to be split in half.

He liked that.

Scene 8

Rook made a final note in her ledger, closed it, and slipped the elastic band around the cover with a gesture that had the finality of a gavel strike. She packed the ledger and the mirror into her bag, slung it over her shoulder, and regarded the group with a gaze that was neither hostile nor warm—just the professional assessment of someone who kept very good records.

She addressed Xander first. “You’ve created a logical problem I don’t have a solution for yet. That’s not a threat. It’s a professional observation.”

He nodded, unsure if he was supposed to be proud.

She turned to Ethan and said, “You widened the flaw cleanly. I didn’t expect that.”

Ethan bowed with a flourish. “I had good material to work with.”

Rook’s lips twitched, the ghost of a smile, and she walked north through the cemetery, passing the government perimeter without a word. The support team watched her go, one of them making a quiet note on his own clipboard, then returned to their protocol as if this happened every Thursday.

Buffy watched her until she was out of sight, then turned to the group. “She’ll be back. With a better version.”

Giles, dusting off his hands, said, “Almost certainly. But she did say the contracting party was a local with standing under the original charter.”

Willow zipped up her laptop and said, “It’s the Mayor.”

Nobody disagreed.

Xander sat on the survey marker, which now felt nothing like an anchor and everything like an old, cold bench. He put his face in his hands, not in defeat, but as a way to let the exhaustion settle in. He stayed that way for a few seconds, then looked up and said, “So the Mayor tried to evict Superman using my own school records.”

Buffy, with something almost like pride: “Yep.”

Xander, shaking his head, “That’s actually kind of impressive.”

Buffy: “Don’t compliment the villain.”

Willow sat next to Xander on the stone, her knees tucked to her chest. She didn’t say anything, but her presence was more comforting than any pep talk he could imagine.

The government medical team approached, a man and a woman in matching windbreakers. They asked to check Willow and Giles for “possible exposure,” waved a handheld scanner over their forearms, and, satisfied with the readings, offered a granola bar and a bottle of water to each before retreating. Giles, amused, pocketed the granola bar; Willow broke hers in half and shared it with Xander.

Buffy gave the team lead a terse but complete debrief, ticking off each event in order and ending with, “No collateral, no injury, mission complete.” The officer nodded, took notes, and offered her a business card. She looked at it, shrugged, and slid it into her jacket.

When it was over, the group lingered by the marker, not in a hurry to leave, savoring the rare and quiet moment of having actually fixed something.

Willow rested her head on Xander’s shoulder, just for a second. “You know,” she said, “next time they try to split you, maybe tell them you come with friends.”

He laughed, real and low. “I’ll put it on my resume.”

They sat there for a long minute, letting the cemetery be what it was—a place for old stories and new names, and, for tonight at least, a place where you could just exist and not have to fight for it.

It was enough.

Scene 9

The library at midnight was a different animal. The day’s traffic had scuffed the linoleum to a faint shine, and the only sound was the hum of the vending machine, which hadn’t been restocked since the semester started. Xander sat at the research table, hands folded on the edge, opposite Buffy, who had her chair tipped so far back that it was more of a balancing act than a seat.

The remains of their effort covered the table: two annotated maps, three half-drunk sodas, and a pile of printouts that Willow had color-coded with pink and yellow highlighter. The government’s “Incident Support Framework” binder was open to a tabbed checklist titled AFTER-ACTION REVIEW.

Buffy set her heels on the edge of the table. “Okay. What worked?”

Xander flipped through his mental log. “Perimeter held. Support crew stayed out of the way, but were ready when called. Willow got the records access in forty minutes. Medical was… immediate.” He looked at the small scrape on his wrist, now bandaged and already healing.

Buffy nodded. “Nobody from the team entered the operational area without my go-ahead. No one tried to ‘take over’ the scene.”

Xander smiled. “They even brought snacks. Well, granola bars.”

Buffy smirked. “Admiral Chegwidden’s a man of his word.”

They sat in silence, the weight of the day sinking in. After a while, Buffy said, “It worked the way we said it would work.”

Xander: “Yeah.”

Buffy: “That doesn’t mean it’ll always work that way.”

Xander: “I know.”

She dropped her chair to all fours. “We keep the channel. But we need a better way to flag the difference between ‘hold position’ and ‘bring the medical kit now.’”

He nodded, already imagining what Willow would build for the next iteration. “I’ll write up the notes,” he said.

Buffy glanced at the checklist. “It’s still your town, you know.”

He laughed. “Doesn’t feel like anyone else’s.”

They sat for a while longer, going over details, refining the new protocols, drawing red lines around the few places they felt the support was too slow or too cautious. In the end, Xander said, “I keep waiting for the part where having help makes me feel less like it’s my problem.”

Buffy looked at him, not unkind. “It doesn’t. That’s not what help is for.”

He absorbed that, and, surprisingly, it made things easier.

Giles, who had been shelving the day’s returns in the cage, emerged with a mug of tea and set it beside Xander’s hand, just out of spill range. He did not say anything, but the gesture was the kind you didn’t need to name.

Willow had fallen asleep at the computer, her cheek pressed to the keyboard and one hand still half-closed around a highlighter. Xander shrugged off his jacket, walked over, and draped it over her shoulders before shutting down the monitor. She stirred, smiled in her sleep, and settled back in.

He walked out with Buffy, the night air outside cooler and somehow friendlier than the day. They didn’t talk. They didn’t need to. There would be another fight, another working, another way for the town to decide it wanted a different version of reality. For now, though, the air was clear, and the world was quiet, and there was the sense—brief, fragile, but real—that the problem had been solved.

At least for tonight.

Scene 10

Antarctica. Under the ice, in a structure that had never been part of Starfleet schematics, a pulse of artificial cognition threaded its way through a hundred meters of compressed water and stone, and answered back.

The makeshift medical bay had been adapted from sickbay on Deck 7, which now doubled as the main research and communications node. Jenny Calendar, wrapped in a thermal layer and a blue SGC jacket two sizes too large, stood at the diagnostic console, fingers poised above the haptic interface. The temperature inside was tolerable, but the taste of recycled air and chemical insulation clung to everything.

Sam Carter, in a similar getup, watched the progress bar on the secondary screen. “Diagnostic ready,” she said, and looked over at Jenny. “On your mark.”

Jenny toggled the signal generator, activating the custom pulse they’d built—patterned off Data’s own positronic signature, but narrower, more question than assertion. On the slab in the alcove, Data lay motionless, his synthetic skin retracted at the left temple to expose the dense logic of his cognitive hardware. A dozen leads bridged the gap from his skull to the console, each one glowing a faint, steady amber.

The signal fired. The console rendered it as a clean blue spike: a single, deliberate handshake, not a flood of information. The waveform tunneled down, through layers of hull, deck, ice, and into the abyss below, where the sensors said a different kind of machine intelligence had waited, unchanged, for nearly three months.

Jenny and Sam watched the screen. The return came in eleven seconds—long enough for a particle to cross continents, but short in the cosmic scale. The incoming signal was not an echo. It arrived as a dense, asymmetrical cluster, structured in the same frequency space as Data’s pulse but arranged in a new and distinct pattern.

Sam, frowning: “That’s not just a handshake.”

Jenny examined the display. She parsed the return, pulling up a three-dimensional model. “It’s not a reflection at all,” she said. “It’s a response keyed to Data’s own frequency. It read the diagnostic, recognized the signature, and answered.”

Sam, with a trace of awe: “It answered him specifically. Not the ship. Not the experiment. Him.”

Jenny looked over at the slab. Data remained motionless, but one eyelid flickered, and the readings spiked for half a second before settling.

“It knows what he is,” Jenny said, voice quiet.

Sam scrolled through the capture. “We still don’t know what’s down there. Or why it hasn’t done anything yet.”

Jenny: “Or what it will do if we go closer.”

They stood silent a moment, watching the signal phase shift on the console, as if the pattern itself was alive and thinking.

Sam, softly: “Next step?”

Jenny watched Data. His eyelids didn’t move, but the graphs behind his head glowed a steady, expectant yellow. She said, “We find out carefully.”

Sam nodded. “We run the diagnostic again, but next time we layer in a handshake protocol. See if it will talk.”

Jenny: “And if it tries something new?”

Sam: “Then we improvise.”

The signal trace continued, a faint but persistent thread in the noise floor of the planet’s southernmost continent. Data’s presence had changed the terms; whatever waited below was now aware of the person, not just the probe.

Jenny set the experiment to loop every four hours, then dimmed the workstation, casting the room into the familiar blue of anticipation.

She looked at Data, and for a moment, wondered if androids could dream about being recognized.

“Seventy-two hours until the next window,” Sam said.

“Nothing ever gets easier,” Jenny replied.

Sam looked at her, eyes tired but hopeful. “No, but you get used to the weird.”

They left the console humming, the logic thread looping through its cycle, and stepped into the corridor. The air was colder there, but Jenny found it easier to breathe.

Below the ice, the anomaly waited. The pulse of logic from above was not a threat. It was a signal. An overture.

Whatever came next, it would be mutual.

And somewhere, in the silent blackness of the Antarctic shelf, a new thing was counting down the days until its own freedom.

Jenny smiled, closed the hatch behind her, and left the light on.

She thought Data would appreciate it.
