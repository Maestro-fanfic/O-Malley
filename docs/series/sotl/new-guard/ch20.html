Chapter 20

The watch vibrated once against the inside of Xander's wrist during second-period homeroom.

One long pulse. Call.

He kept his eyes on the worksheet in front of him and wrote the answer to question seven as if a military satellite had not just reached into Sunnydale High to tap him on the arm.

Across the aisle, Willow looked up.

Xander finished the sentence, set down his pencil, and glanced at the clock above the chalkboard. Nine-fifteen exactly. Carter had said nine-fifteen, plus or minus thirty seconds. Right on schedule.

Willow raised her eyebrows.

He moved his wrist just enough for her to see the black digital face and gave the smallest possible nod.

At the front of the room, Mrs. Moran was trying to persuade thirty teenagers that writing three goals for the semester would improve their chances of achieving any of them. Xander had written pass chemistry, figure out where he was sleeping next week, and do not get a country invaded. The list felt less like a joke every day.

The bell rang four minutes later.

Willow was beside him before he reached the door. "Was that it?"

"Scheduled test. One long. Call when practical."

"And practical is now?"

"Practical is before Snyder decides hallway movement has become a privilege available only through written application."

Buffy fell in on his other side. She had heard enough to look at the watch rather than ask what they were discussing.

"It looks normal," she said.

"That's because Carter understands secret identities and also fashion accessories sold at the mall."

"Does it do calculator things?"

"No."

"Stopwatch?"

"Yes."

"Alarm?"

"Yes."

"Global crisis hotline?"

"Apparently."

"So it's less useful than a calculator watch but more likely to ruin lunch."

"That's pretty much the sales brochure."

They turned toward the library. Xander had the designated number memorized, but the administration kept the nearest student-accessible telephone in the main office, where a call lasting more than forty seconds would require an explanation. Giles's office had a private line and a door.

The library was empty except for Giles, who looked up from a stack of returned books as the three of them came in.

"The test?"

"Right on time."

Giles unlocked his office without comment. Xander dialed the long-distance number Carter had written on a card and made him burn after memorizing it. The line clicked twice before a woman answered without identifying any organization.

"Authorization."

Xander gave the six-digit code paired to the receiver.

"Hold."

There was no music. Willow leaned into the doorway. Buffy leaned against the opposite frame, watching the watch.

A man's voice came on. "Receiver test one. Identify the signal received."

"One long. Standard call request."

"Correct. Remain on the line for urgency test."

The watch gave one long pulse.

Three seconds later, it gave another.

Then a third.

"One long repeated three times," Xander said. "Urgent call. Leave what I'm doing and contact you immediately if I can do it safely."

"Correct. The test is complete. No response or travel is requested."

"That's it?"

"That's it. Thank you."

The line went dead.

Xander replaced the receiver. "Mountain people have really mastered the warm goodbye."

"Can you ignore it?" Buffy asked.

Buffy’s question hung in the air. Willow would have started with the technical specs—satellite footprint, signal interference, encryption protocols. But Buffy went straight to the heart of it: who was in charge? She wanted to know if the thing on his wrist was a tool, or a leash.

"Yeah. Off switch is underneath. They can't tell if I got anything, and they can't tell if I turned it off."

"What about the urgent one?"

"Still a request. Just one where waiting for the bell is supposed to be a genuinely bad idea."

Buffy considered that. "Who decides genuinely?"

"Duty officer has criteria. I have judgment. If they disagree, mine wins while I'm wearing it."

"Good."

Giles's expression eased by a degree.

"And the Superman one?" Willow asked.

Xander lowered his voice, though the library remained empty. "Flat receiver clipped inside the uniform. Same patterns, same address, separate off switch. I don't wear the same watch in both identities and hand Cordelia a photo comparison that ruins my life."

"Cordelia does have a remarkable eye for accessories," Giles said.

They all looked at him.

"I have supervised her in a library for more than a year. One learns things against one's will."

The warning bell sounded.

Xander pulled his sleeve over the watch. "Back to the part of my day where the worst thing that can happen is failing a chemistry quiz."

They stepped into the hall and nearly walked into Snyder.

He stood ten feet away, motionless amid the current of students, his attention fixed on Xander's covered wrist.

"Mr. Harris."

"Principal Snyder. Great morning for education."

"Why were you in the library?"

"Book emergency."

Snyder looked past him at Giles. "During passing period."

"The books get restless if you don't check on them."

"Get to class."

Xander went. He could feel Snyder watching until the crowd carried him around the corner.

The real signal came during sixth-period history.

Mr. Whitmore was drawing the Missouri Compromise on the board when the watch pressed one long vibration against Xander's wrist.

He looked down.

Three seconds.

Another long pulse.

A cold dread washed through him, tighter than any classroom anxiety.

Urgent call.

The morning’s test had been a game, a secret kept under a worksheet. This wasn't a game.

Xander raised his hand.

"Mr. Harris?"

"I need to go to the nurse."

"Can it wait twelve minutes?"

The third pulse ended.

"No. Sorry."

He was already gathering his books.

"Mr. Harris, I did not excuse you."

"I know."

He left anyway.

Half the class turned to watch. Mr. Whitmore called his name once more, sharper, but did not follow.

Xander walked until the door closed, then ran.

The library was occupied by three freshmen doing research at the tables. Giles was behind the counter. He saw Xander's face and crossed to the office before Xander needed to say anything.

"Urgent call," Xander said.

Giles shut the door behind him. Xander dialed.

"Authorization."

He gave the code.

This time the hold lasted less than two seconds.

“Superman response desk. We have an authorized international assistance request. Southern Brazil…”

The voice was calm, professional, and laid out the facts like a weather report: wildfire, trapped vehicles, forty-three people in immediate danger. The details blurred into a single, sharp point. Are you available?

The words came quickly, each one arranged to be answered, not obeyed.

"How long until the fire reaches them?"

"Local estimate is fourteen minutes before the eastern front crosses the trapped section. Conditions are changing."

"Coordinates?"

The officer read them. Xander repeated the numbers while Giles wrote them on a scrap of catalog paper.

"I'll go."

"Acknowledged. Incident commander is Captain Ana Ribeiro, Rio Grande do Sul fire service. She speaks English. Report to the command position south of the highway before entering the evacuation corridor. Do not use heat-producing abilities without her approval. Fuel tankers are operating in the area."

"Wasn't planning to."

"Call this number after release from local command."

The line disconnected.

Giles was already moving toward the locked cabinet behind his desk. The Superman uniform lay inside a plain canvas bag beneath three boxes of accession forms. Xander had complained about bringing it to school until Giles asked whether he preferred flying home in street clothes while news helicopters followed.

"Fourteen minutes," Xander said.

"Then I suggest you change."

The office door locked. Xander pulled the recessed switch beneath the watch face with the tip of a paper clip, watched the display go blank, and stripped off his shirt.

The uniform felt the same, fitting like it had always been his. The world, however, warped around it. In jeans and flannel, he was a kid ditching class, facing detention. In blue and red, he was a number in an equation, the variable that might stop forty-three people from burning to death on another continent.

He clipped the flat receiver inside the uniform at his waist and switched it on.

"Tell Buffy?" he asked.

"I will."

"And Whitmore?"

"I will attempt to produce an explanation that does not involve Brazil."

"Tell him I threw up. Nobody asks follow-up questions."

Xander opened the office window.

"Xander."

He looked back.

"Report to the incident commander first."

"I heard them."

"I know. Hear me as well."

Xander nodded and climbed through the window.

He kept below the roofline until the school buildings blocked the courtyard, then rose.

Sunnydale dropped beneath him, then the grid of streets dissolved into the brown sprawl of the valley, then the valley compressed into a crease between mountains, and the world narrowed to numbers Giles had written on catalog paper.

It lay across the southern horizon in a brown-black sheet, wide enough to make the problem look less like a fire than a change in the sky. Xander slowed hard as the ground came into focus. Roads. Fields. A line of red vehicles beside a cleared pasture. People moving around a temporary command post beneath two yellow canopies.

He landed outside the activity rather than in the middle of it.

Every head turned.

A woman in soot-streaked yellow gear came toward him. She was in her forties, compact, hair braided beneath her helmet. A radio snapped rapid Portuguese at her shoulder.

"Superman?"

"Captain Ribeiro?"

"Yes. Come."

She did not stare after that. She took him to a map weighted at the corners by water bottles and pointed.

"Fire crossed here twenty minutes ago. Convoy turned back. This truck rolled when the driver avoided a tree. It blocks both lanes. Coach here, coach here, ambulance between. Fire engine at the rear lost steering. We have crews trying from the west, but the wind shifted."

The radio at her shoulder spoke again. Ribeiro answered, listened, then pointed to a red line drawn across the highway.

"Eastern fire front is eight hundred meters. Maybe less. Can you move the truck?"

"How much fuel?"

"Diesel. Tank damaged, leaking. No fire yet."

"I can move it. Where do you want it?"

She looked at him for the first time as if recalculating. "Off the road, north side, thirty meters past the drainage cut. Keep it upright."

"People first or truck first?"

"Truck. My crews cannot reach people until road is open. Then the front coach. Its rear axle is caught under timber. Do not move the ambulance unless the medic tells you. One patient has spinal injury."

"Got it."

"You report when road is open. I decide what moves next."

"Got it," Xander repeated.

He launched.

The heat rose before the trapped convoy appeared. Trees burned on both sides of the road, not like campfires but like structures coming apart, branches throwing orange across the smoke. The highway vanished beneath a lattice of fallen trunks. Beyond them, a white fuel truck lay across both lanes, cab against the guardrail, rear wheels suspended over a ditch.

People were crowded inside the first coach. Faces pressed to glass.

Xander landed beside the truck. Diesel spread in a dark stream beneath it. He could hear metal contracting in the heat and, from inside the buses, the separate, crowded thud of dozens of pulses.

He crouched, put both hands beneath the tank where the frame would carry the load, and lifted.

The truck came up too easily. That remained the most dangerous part. A thing that weighed twenty tons felt no more informative in his hands than a chair. He had to watch the frame, watch the damaged tank, watch the driver's cab so it did not swing through the guardrail.

He rose three feet, moved north, and set the truck upright beyond the drainage cut.

The torn line kept leaking.

Xander tore a strip from the truck's rubber mudflap, folded it over the puncture, and crimped the surrounding metal against it with his fingers. The flow slowed to a drip.

He flew back to the command post.

"Roadblock moved. Fuel leak pinched off, not repaired."

Ribeiro relayed it. "Coach axle next. There are two firefighters at the engine. One has a broken leg. Front is now five hundred meters."

"Which first?"

She looked at the map, then at the smoke. "Firefighters. Engine fuel tank is protected but not forever. Bring both here. Then coach."

Xander went.

The fire engine stood at an angle against a burning embankment. One firefighter lay behind its front wheel while the other tried to drag him by the shoulders. Flames moved through dry grass toward the underside of the engine.

Xander landed between them and the fire.

"Captain Ribeiro sent me. I'm moving you both."

The standing firefighter stared once, then nodded and shouted something to the injured man.

Xander lifted them together, one on each arm. The injured firefighter screamed when his leg shifted. Xander froze.

"Sorry. Sorry."

"Go," the other man said in English. "Go now."

Xander held the broken leg against his side as carefully as he could and rose above the smoke.

At the command post, medics took them. Ribeiro pointed back before he landed fully.

"Coach."

The eastern fire front had become visible through the trees.

Xander cleared the timber from around the front coach in armfuls, throwing each burning trunk into an already blackened field where it could not start another line. The rear axle had bent around a fallen eucalyptus. He tore the trunk apart lengthwise rather than lifting the bus onto thirty frightened passengers.

The driver leaned out his window. "Can move!"

"Captain says go west! Stay behind the cleared lane!"

The coach started. Its damaged rear wheel screamed against the road but turned.

The second coach followed. The ambulance remained.

Xander flew back.

"Two coaches moving west. Ambulance still in place."

Ribeiro was listening to three radios now. "Ambulance cannot move. Fallen power pole ahead. Live line across road."

"I can move the pole."

"Line may be energized."

"That part's okay. Where?"

She gave him the location. "After line is clear, ambulance follows. Fire engine stays. We abandon it."

"I can move the engine too."

"If there is time."

There was time because he made it.

He lifted the power line by its wooden pole and carried the whole assembly away from the road, sparks snapping across his arms without sensation. He cleared the ambulance, then returned for the disabled engine after the last vehicle had begun moving. Fire rolled over the embankment as he lifted it. Paint blistered beneath his hands. He carried it above the trees and set it in the pasture beside the command post.

For a few seconds, everybody watched the fire engine settle onto the grass.

Then Ribeiro's crews went back to work.

The evacuees were transferred to waiting vehicles. The injured firefighter disappeared inside an ambulance. Radios crackled in a language he didn't understand. The machine of the disaster response ground on without him. He was a tool that had been used, and now it felt like he was being set aside.

Ribeiro came over after six minutes.

"The corridor is empty. Our crews are withdrawing to the southern break."

"Do you need me there?"

"Can you carry water?"

"A lot. But I don't know where you want it, and dropping it from high up might hurt people."

"Correct." She looked toward the smoke. "We have aircraft arriving. Wind is changing again. Your work here is complete."

The dismissal surprised him.

"That's it?"

"Thirty-seven civilians, six responders, two coaches, one ambulance, two engines, and a fuel truck are no longer inside my fire. That is not nothing."

"I could stay."

"You could. You do not know this fire, this terrain, my crews or our plan. Extraordinary strength does not make you incident commander."

There was no insult in it. Ribeiro said it as a fact she expected him to understand.

"Right."

She held out a gloved hand. "Thank you."

Xander shook it carefully.

"If your people ask," she added, "tell them the request was answered and you were released by local command at seventeen forty-three."

"They'll ask."

"Good."

He rose above the field.

The fire remained enormous. From the air, the corridor he had cleared was a thread at its edge. Aircraft approached from the south, orange and white against the smoke. Fire crews repositioned below according to a plan that had existed before he arrived and would continue after he left.

He had not saved Brazil. He had moved a handful of people out of the fire's path. It felt small against the smoke-choked horizon.

But it was the job they had called him for. And for today, that had to be enough.

The clock in Giles's office said 3:38 when Xander climbed through the window.

He stared at it for several seconds before remembering time zones.

His history class had ended twenty-three minutes ago.

The uniform smelled of smoke. Ash had worked into the seams at his shoulders and left a gray smear across the red on his chest. He switched off the concealed receiver, changed, and turned the watch back on.

Giles waited outside the office. Buffy and Willow were at the table behind him.

"Forty-three people," Xander said before anyone could ask. "All out. Local command released me. Nobody died while I was there. One firefighter had a broken leg."

Giles nodded once. "And you?"

"Smoke doesn't really bother me. Fire didn't either. I got sparks on me from a power line. Also fine."

Buffy came closer and pinched the sleeve of his flannel shirt.

"You smell like a barbecue."

"International rescue barbecue."

"Did you put out the fire?" Willow asked.

"No. I moved the people and the vehicles they couldn't reach. Captain Ribeiro said I didn't know enough to help with the rest."

Buffy smiled slightly. "I like her."

"You'd get along. She orders people around and gets annoyed when they think being strong makes them qualified."

"I really like her."

“Mr. Whitmore reported you for leaving class,” Giles said.

The words took a second to register. Brazil. The fire. The weight of the truck in his hands. It all dissolved, leaving him standing in a quiet library, smelling of ash, about to get detention. The world snapped back into place.

"Right. School."

"Snyder has requested you appear in his office tomorrow morning."

"I rescued a bus. Two buses."

"I doubt he will consider jurisdictional distance a defense."

Xander looked at the history book still lying where he had dropped it. "I went from the Missouri Compromise to Brazil and back before the last bell."

"Still have chemistry homework," Willow said gently.

"That seems personally insulting."

Buffy studied him. "You okay?"

"Yeah. I mean, yes. People were going to burn, and then they didn't." He rubbed soot between his fingers. "It was just a school day. Then it wasn't. Then it was again."

"That part doesn't get normal," Buffy said.

She sounded certain.

Xander looked at her. He saw the familiar exhaustion in her eyes, the set of her shoulders that was older than any teenager's. She had lived this weird, fractured life for years, fighting monsters in the dark and then sitting through algebra class like nothing had happened.

"No," he said. "I guess it doesn't."

Giles's apartment was quiet. Too quiet. Xander had showered until the water ran clear, but he could still smell smoke in the fabric of his clean shirt. He stared at his chemistry homework, the question about ionic bonds looking like a language from another planet.

He couldn't focus. He kept seeing faces pressed against a bus window.

He pulled an old grocery receipt from his wallet. Zoey's handwriting felt impossibly normal next to the routing phrases and verification codes for the White House switchboard. It had sounded so simple in Washington. Now it felt like crossing another world.

They sounded less straightforward when an operator answered, "White House."

"Hi. I need to speak to Zoey Bartlet."

Silence.

"May I ask who's calling?"

"Xander Harris. She gave me a routing thing. Georgetown Blue, then seven-one-four."

"One moment."

The line clicked.

Another operator asked his name, then his location, then the routing phrase again. Xander provided all three.

"Please hold."

Music came on this time. Instrumental, patriotic, and faint enough to make him wonder whether the White House had chosen it or whether every secure telephone system came with government-approved trumpets.

Four minutes passed.

The music stopped.

"Hello?"

Zoey sounded breathless.

"Hey."

"Xander?"

"Unless the switchboard rerouted me to another teenage boy who knows your private code."

"Give them a week. How are you calling me through the White House?"

"You told me to."

"I gave you the number because I thought you might call. I didn't think you would use it correctly on the first try."

"Your confidence is touching."

"Where are you?"

"Giles's sofa."

"Is that in California?"

"Most days."

"Most days?"

Xander looked down at the chemistry book. "I left history class for Brazil."

There was a pause.

"You left history class for Brazil."

"There was a fire."

"Of course there was."

"Two buses, an ambulance, firefighters. The watch did the urgent thing."

"The watch works?"

"Very works. Satellite reaches Sunnydale. Sunnydale reaches my wrist. My wrist ruins sixth period."

"Are the people okay?"

"Yeah. Broken leg on one firefighter. Everybody got out. The fire is still going, but people who actually know fires are doing that part."

"And you came back to school?"

"Technically school ended while I was over the Pacific. I came back to chemistry homework."

Zoey laughed. It was not a polite laugh or the careful one she used in rooms where people watched her father. Xander leaned back against the sofa and let the sound reach him across the country.

"How was your day?" he asked.

"Comparatively embarrassing."

"Try me."

"My detail followed me into the campus bookstore because they decided the upper floor had insufficient visibility. Then one of them stood in American Literature while I bought shampoo."

"Maybe he likes American literature."

"He was facing the shampoo aisle."

"Maybe he likes American shampoo."

"Then I got escorted to a seminar on democratic participation by three armed men who checked the room first."

"That feels participatory."

"I know why they're there. I know what happened the last time I slipped them. I know I was wrong. That doesn't make it less weird when somebody inspects the bathroom before I use it."

"I know."

The answer came out quieter than he intended.

Zoey went quiet too.

"Do you?"

Not the bathroom part. Pretty sure our school bathrooms would win that fight. But… yeah. The rest of it. It’s like, one thing happens to you, and suddenly your life has all these new rules. And the worst part is, they mostly make sense. So you can’t even get properly mad about it without feeling like a jerk.

"Exactly."

"I got a satellite watch."

"You said."

"I got two satellite watches, actually. One's not a watch. Secret identity thing. There are protocols and phone numbers and people on duty who can call me from anywhere on Earth. I left class, flew to another continent, and a fire captain told me where to put a fuel truck."

"That sounds organized."

"It is. That's the thing. It's really organized."

He picked at a scorch mark on the cuff of his flannel. It came away beneath his thumbnail.

"For me," he said. "After, what, eleven days? Two weeks? Everybody knows where I'm supposed to call. There are military satellites and international permissions and a person whose job is deciding whether something counts as urgent."

"You don't sound happy about that."

"I am. I think. Those people would've died if nobody could reach me. Captain Ribeiro knew what she was doing, and the guy on the phone gave me facts instead of ordering me around. That's all good."

"But?"

"But I came back and Buffy was in the library."

The sentence sounded stupid by itself.

Zoey waited.

"She does this every night," Xander said. "Not Brazil. Vampires. Demons. Whatever crawls out in Sunnydale. She has Giles and us, but we're mostly carrying pointy sticks and hoping the hospital doesn't ask why somebody has bite marks."

"The White House knows about that now."

"Yeah. That's why it feels worse. You know. Your dad knows. Fitzwallace knows. The mountain knows. Everybody had meetings about what resources I get, what job I should take, which country gets to ask me for things. Buffy has been doing it for years and nobody ever had a meeting about her."

"What does she have? Officially."

"A librarian."

"Giles."

"Yeah. He does everything he can. Research, training, patching people up. But if she gets hurt, he can't take her to some protected hospital and explain Slayer injuries. If she needs backup, it's me and Willow and whoever has a free night. If her jacket gets ripped, she buys another jacket. If a sword breaks, we find money for a sword."

"Does she get paid?"

Xander laughed once. "By who?"

"The Council?"

"They pay Giles. They don't pay Buffy."

"Not even hazard pay?"

"Not even regular pay. She spends all night keeping people from getting eaten, and if she needs money she's supposed to find an after-school job. Somewhere between school and graveyards."

The anger surprised him when it arrived. It was not clean anger aimed only at the Council. It included Snyder, the hospital, every adult who had looked at Buffy's exhaustion and decided it was attitude. It included the White House for learning the truth and still making the first giant machinery about him.

It included his parents. It included every offer that had treated his time as valuable enough to bid on while home remained a place where he had learned to be quiet.

"Sorry," he said. "I didn't call to yell about government policy."

"You aren't yelling."

"I'm angsting at noticeable volume."

"You're allowed."

"Am I?"

"Yes. So am I. We can make it a club."

"Membership requirement is adults making decisions about your life and then acting surprised when you have opinions."

"That's too broad. Half the country qualifies."

"Fine. Also armed escorts or secret satellite equipment."

"Better. Very exclusive. Terrible meetings."

Xander smiled despite himself.

"I'm angry for me too," he said. "That's the part that feels selfish."

"Why?"

"Because Buffy's got the bigger thing. Actual destiny. Chosen girl, one in all the world, nightly death schedule. My stuff happened last week."

"Your life doesn't have to be worse than hers before you're allowed to hate parts of it."

"Tell that to my guilt."

"Your guilt sounds exhausting."

"It contains multitudes."

It’s like, just because you can carry something heavy, everyone assumes you’re supposed to," Zoey said. "And once you pick it up, they all walk away because, hey, you’ve got it handled."

Xander stopped picking at his sleeve.

"Yeah."

"Security people say I understand the risks, so I should understand every restriction. Your military people say you can cross continents, so everybody gets to build plans around you. Buffy's Council says she's the Slayer, so apparently that means she doesn't need a salary or a doctor."

"Or a night off."

"Or privacy."

"Or a normal life."

"Like those are rewards you earn after doing the impossible correctly."

The line went quiet between them.

Xander looked through Giles's window at the courtyard below. A neighbor carried groceries from a car. Somebody's television laughed behind a wall. His chemistry book waited on the table, ordinary and unreasonable.

"My dad should hear this," Zoey said.

Xander straightened. "Zoey."

"Not from me. From you and Buffy."

"I was venting."

"You were. You also described a seventeen-year-old doing essential work without medical care, reliable backup, equipment money, or pay. Those can be both things."

"I can't offer Buffy to the government."

"I'm not asking you to. I'm offering to arrange a meeting. You, Buffy, and my father, in person. You make your own case. She makes hers. I get you through the door and then I stop."

"Your dad's the President."

"I had noticed."

"Buffy doesn't love institutions."

"Then she can tell him that."

"She might say no."

"Then the answer is no."

Xander rubbed the back of his neck, the motion automatic. The call had completely changed course. He’d dialed for a friendly ear, for someone who understood the weirdness of shampoo and bodyguards. Instead, Zoey had just casually offered him a door into the Oval Office. A door he hadn't even known existed.

"I have to ask her."

"I know."

"And if she says yes, it's her meeting too. Not me talking about her while she sits there."

"I know."

"And you're not going to tell your dad what we want before we know what we want."

"Xander."

"Right. You know."

"I do."

He could hear the smile in her voice.

"Thanks," he said.

"For what?"

"Listening to the angst."

"It's an exclusive club. We have obligations."

Somebody spoke near Zoey, too muffled for him to make out.

"I have to go," she said. "My detail has located a new hallway to stand in."

"Suspicious-looking hallway?"

"Very. Trench coat and everything."

"Stay on your side of the continent for a while."

"You too."

"I make no promises."

"Apparently neither does Brazil. Call me after you talk to Buffy."

"I will."

The line clicked off.

Xander held the receiver a moment longer before setting it down.

The chemistry book remained open to the same unanswered question.

He closed it.

Buffy heard him out without interrupting.

That was worse than if she had interrupted.

They were in the library after school the next day. Giles had locked the doors and put the CLOSED FOR CATALOGING sign in the glass, a phrase that had never once prevented a determined student from trying the handle but gave him grounds to look offended when they did.

Willow had gone to return a computer disk to the lab. Xander waited for her to leave before turning to Buffy. He'd mentioned Zoey's offer to Giles, but this next part had to be hers alone.

"She said she can arrange an in-person meeting," he finished. "You, me, President Bartlet. She gets us through the door. She doesn't make the case for us."

Buffy turned a stake between her fingers. "What case?"

"That's up to us."

"You called the President's daughter to complain about me?"

"I called the President's daughter to complain about me. You came up because my complaints are social and include friends."

Glad to know I made the cut for your social complaints tour.

"I told her the White House knows demons exist, but you still do this with pointy wood and Giles's first-aid box. She said her dad should hear it from us."

"Us."

"If you want. I told her I couldn't say yes for you."

Buffy looked at Giles. "Did you know about this?"

"Not until Xander told me before you arrived."

"And?"

"And I believe being offered access is different from being offered to anyone."

"That's a very Watcher answer."

"It was intended as a Buffy answer."

She put the stake down. "Government help comes with government. They don't send a doctor and forget you exist. They send forms and people who want reports and somebody who decides the forms mean they own the doctor."

"I know," Xander said.

"Do you? You got a watch yesterday. Today Snyder knows you left class, a military duty officer knows where you went, and somebody in Brazil knows how fast you can move a truck. That's help working correctly."

"Also why I asked you instead of telling Zoey yes."

Buffy walked to the cage and back. "What did you ask for?"

"Nothing. I was angsting."

"Professionally?"

"Teenage amateur division."

"And she heard policy."

"She has political ears."

Buffy looked at the table, at the stack of patrol maps, the dented first-aid box, the crossbow Giles had repaired twice because there was no money for another.

"The money is usable before we send her one dramatic piece of equipment. I don't want a vehicle, a rifle, and twelve men appearing in California while she still has to ask her mother for lunch money."

"I'll have an answer tonight."

"Good."

Fitzwallace began placing the call to the Navy command center. Leo collected his notes and headed for the door.

Bartlet looked down at the single crumb Buffy had left on the Resolute desk.

"And somebody tell Mrs. Landingham she was right about the cookies."

Leo stopped. "You can tell her yourself."

"I am the President of the United States."

"Then you have the authority."

"I was hoping to delegate."

"She has jurisdiction."

Leo left before Bartlet could answer.

✳

The document had required one hundred and fifty-six years to become valuable.

Holland Manners considered that a respectable rate of maturation for a contract.

The original agreement occupied four sheets of linen paper in a climate-controlled case beneath Wolfram & Hart's Los Angeles offices. Richard Wilkins had signed it in 1841, forty-eight years before the town called Sunnydale appeared on any map. The signature was unchanged from the one on the amendment now resting beside it.

The first agreement granted Wilkins exclusive occult jurisdiction within the described territory. In exchange for the firm's assistance securing that jurisdiction, Wolfram & Hart surrendered nearly every right to operate there. Clients could pass through. Employees could conduct ordinary business. The firm could not establish a significant magical presence, alter local thresholds, or contest obligations protected by Wilkins's office.

It had been an extravagant concession.

At the time, the territory had been mostly dust, missions, and cattle.

The amendment reopened it.

Not completely. Wilkins had survived too long to write carelessly. Wolfram & Hart received limited operating rights for the duration of one service and whatever enforcement the service required. In consideration, the firm would provide its highest-ranking sorcerer to prevent the newly manifested power called Superman from interfering with the Mayor's protected civic obligations.

Dr. Evelyn Rook read the final page twice.

She was in her early fifties, dressed in charcoal gray, and carried no visible charm, stone, bone, blade, or other object by which an inexperienced observer might identify a sorcerer. Her only jewelry was a plain gold watch and a small enamel pin on her lapel depicting a surveyor's transit, the kind used to establish boundary lines. Holland knew the watch clasp contained language taken from a Roman property judgment, and that the pin was not decorative, but that was the sort of detail which separated professional practice from costume.

"Well?" he asked.

"The amendment is adequate to permit a survey. It is not adequate to define completion."

"The Mayor wants Superman neutralized."

"The Mayor's stated wish is not the service's operative purpose."

Holland smiled slightly. "He was quite specific."

"Clients often are. Specificity and precision are different disciplines."

Rook placed the amendment beside the original agreement, aligning the bottom edges exactly.

"Superman need not die. His death would attract national attention, foreign intelligence services, several military commands, and every occult opportunist capable of reading a newspaper. Mr. Wilkins requires him to lack standing to interfere. That is a jurisdictional problem."

"And the two tests?"

"Successful."

Holland opened the thin field report. "Four vampires displaced, one nest abandoned. A footpath produced inconsistent exits."

"One test asked whether territorial pressure could redirect a supernatural population already bound to Sunnydale's ecology. The other asked whether civic routes would accept revision under the reopened rights. They are components of the same service."

"The Mayor may regard them as delays."

"The Mayor is welcome to practice magic himself."

Holland's smile grew warmer. "He has."

"Then he should understand the value of definitions."

Rook drew a legal pad toward her and wrote three headings in a compact hand.

SCOPE.

DURATION.

PROTECTED OBLIGATIONS.

"I require the precise acts Superman must be prevented from performing, the period during which the prevention must hold, and an exhaustive schedule of the civic obligations the working is permitted to protect. No implied additions. No later expansions by interpretation."

"You intend to bind him?"

I intend to determine who, precisely, is being bound. The world sees Superman, an outside power. But the town's legal records see Alexander Harris, a resident and high school student. The distinction is useless, unless I can teach the city's magic to read its own paperwork.

"And if it can?"

Rook closed the folder.

"Then Sunnydale will not need to hurt him."

Two floors below them, a records clerk entered the Laurel Park displacement and the altered Carver Avenue route into the same matter file.

"It will merely learn where he is allowed to matter."
