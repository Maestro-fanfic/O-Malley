Chapter 14

"Let's do this then," Leo said, holding the door for Doctor Weir and Lord Marbury as they stepped through into the Oval Office proper.

President Bartlet was already on his feet, coming around the desk to meet them, tie loosened, jacket over the back of his chair rather than on it, a legal pad open to a page dense with handwriting in three different colors of ink, and a coffee cup with a Post-it note stuck to the side of it that said, in someone else's handwriting, drink this. "Doctor Weir. Thank you for coming.

"Mr President." She shook his hand, firm, brief. "I understand there isn't much time before the others are seated."

"There never is anymore." Bartlet glanced at Marbury. "John, thank you for staying through all of this. I know it's not what you signed on for when you flew over."

"I signed on for whatever proves useful, Mr President," Marbury said, settling his coat over one arm. "Though I confess I hadn't imagined it would involve quite so much Egyptology."

"None of us had." Bartlet turned back to Weir. "You've read the packet Major Samuels gave you?"

"Twice. I confess I'd like a third read before I'm asked to stake anything on it."

"Wouldn't we all," Leo muttered, from over by the door.

"The short version," Bartlet said, "is that we're proposing a joint operation with the British to secure the crash site and manage recovery, under the Antarctic Treaty. Everyone else gets a seat at the table and no claim on the ship. I'd like you to sell that as the only sane version of this, because it is, and I don't think everyone in that room is going to want to hear it."

"I generally find that people already know when they're being told the sane version, Mr President. It rarely helps."

"No," Bartlet agreed. "It rarely does."

"There is another complication," Leo said. He picked up a sheet from the corner of Bartlet's desk and passed it across to her. "Superman arrived at the crash site a little over an hour ago."

Weir read the first three lines, then looked up. "Arrived how?"

"Flew," Bartlet said. "New York to Antarctica, apparently with a stop somewhere in between, because why should geography get to have rules when nothing else does?"

"Was he sent there by the United States government?"

"No," Leo said. "He left New York without telling the Air Force officer assigned to him. General Hammond didn't know where he'd gone until the team on the ice reported him standing in their shuttle bay."

"Does China know that?"

"They know he's there," Fitzwallace said from the doorway behind them. He came in carrying a red folder under one arm, having apparently abandoned his own jacket somewhere else in the building. "We don't know whether they know why. They have satellite coverage of the site. If they got a clear image of him in that suit, they'll assume he's operating for us no matter what we tell them."

"And is he?"

Fitzwallace paused at the question. "No."

"That pause is going to cost us," Weir said.

"The pause is me deciding how much detail you need before the meeting," Fitzwallace answered. "The honest version is that he isn't under anybody's command. He cooperates with Colonel O'Neill and his people. He has listened to requests so far. That is not the same thing as taking orders, and nobody in this building has settled what his legal status is."

"Then we don't describe him as an American asset," Weir said. "Not in the room and not in any written framework. If Ambassador Wen asks, he's a private individual who arrived independently and is currently assisting the people already on site."

"They're going to say that's convenient," Leo warned.

"It is convenient. That doesn't make it false." She glanced down at the report again. "Has he touched the ship? Removed anything?"

"Lifted one of the shuttlecraft upright," Fitzwallace said. "Nothing removed. Commander Calendar asked him to help secure wreckage before the weather buries more of it."

"Commander Calendar owns the ship?"

"Commands it," Fitzwallace corrected. "Ownership is one of the questions nobody has had enough sleep to answer."

"Then it is one of the questions we're going to be asked in there." Weir handed the report back. "We're proposing to recover a vessel that has a surviving commander. Has anyone asked for her consent?"

The men around the table found other things to look at.

"Before we promise transparency, operational control, or pieces of her ship to anyone," Weir continued, "someone needs to ask the woman who brought it down without killing her crew or anyone on the ground what she is prepared to permit. Otherwise this isn't a recovery framework. It's five countries arranging a salvage operation around a living witness who hasn't agreed that the ship is salvage."

Bartlet looked at Fitzwallace. "Can Hammond reach her?"

"Through McMurdo. Communications from inside the saucer are still unreliable."

"Have him ask," Bartlet said. "No legal language, no pressure. What does she need, what will she authorize, and what won't she?"

Fitzwallace nodded and stepped toward the phone on the side table.

"That answer won't be here before we sit down," Leo said.

"Then we don't promise anything that depends on it," Weir replied.

Bartlet checked the clock over the door. "They're waiting. One more thing before you go in. Kinsey's in there."

Weir stopped, halfway into her coat. "I wasn't told Senator Kinsey would be attending."

"He invited himself," Leo said. "Committee oversight, strategic asset, all the usual language. I couldn't keep him out without making it a bigger story than the story already is, and believe me, I tried for most of an hour this morning."

"What am I supposed to do with him?"

"Ignore him if you can. Outmaneuver him if you can't." Leo shrugged. "He's smart, he's persistent, and he genuinely believes every word he's about to say to you. That doesn't make him right."

"I see." She finished with the coat. "Then I'll deal with him same as I'd deal with anyone else in that room."

"I'd pay to watch that," Bartlet said, and something in it was almost fond. "Go on. I'll be along after the opening remarks. Try not to let them notice I'm not staying."

The private room off the Situation Room had been reserved for the part of the meeting nobody wanted recorded in the minutes. Giles arrived with Colonel O’Neill and Major Carter, carrying a thin black folder that looked too ordinary to contain the end of the world.

Bartlet looked at the folder, then at Giles. “I’m told this concerns Superman.”

“It concerns the reason he exists,” Giles said. “And the reason the arrangement around him can no longer remain private.”

For several months, the Scoobies had treated the information as something to be protected along with Xander’s identity. He had been a child with impossible abilities, and the first duty had been keeping governments, agencies, and opportunists from turning him into a resource. That logic had expired. Xander had crossed an ocean, intervened at an international crash site, and was now operating alongside military personnel without a shared threat picture.

Giles opened the folder. Inside was the message Xander had sent back in Ethan’s care, along with Ethan’s account of the terrible price he had paid to bring it, photographs of gold-and-black ships over ruined cities, and dated fragments of a war that had already happened somewhere else. “The source is not a prediction,” Giles said. “It is a report from Xander, carried backward by Ethan because his world failed before it understood what was attacking it.”

Carter studied the first photograph. “Orbital bombardment.”

“Before the armies landed,” O’Neill said. “The warning describes London, Manchester, New York, and Washington hit from orbit. Earth’s missiles could not penetrate the shields. The ground war lasted three years.”

Bartlet’s expression did not change, but the room did. Leo stopped turning pages. Weir lowered her hand from the folder she had been carrying. Fitzwallace’s attention fixed on the photographs as if the ships might move if he looked away.

“Casualties?” Fitzwallace asked.

“The note names two Slayers and a witch among the dead,” Giles said. “It also says the governments that survived spent the first phase arguing over whether the invasion was real. That is the mistake we are trying not to repeat.”

Weir looked at the costume report. “And the boy?”

“Xander Harris,” Giles said. “Superman is not an independent extraterrestrial asset. He is a sixteen-year-old carrying abilities future-Xander deliberately sent back to his younger self. The secrecy protected a child. It cannot be allowed to conceal a planetary assault from the people responsible for responding to one—and the response has to function even if he never joins it.”

Bartlet read the date at the bottom of the note. “March sixth.”

“Yes,” Carter said. “The exact fleet strength is unknown. The scale is not. This requires orbital warning, continuity planning, military coordination, and a ground response in Sunnydale. The first plan must assume no Superman: the Enterprise, the SGC, the Navy, and the allies build a defense with the resources already available. If Xander participates, that is an additional capability, not the foundation.”

Bartlet closed the folder. “Then this is the hand-off. The White House, the SGC, and the relevant allies receive the threat picture. We build the response without counting on Xander. He keeps his consent and his choices; if he joins an operation, it improves the plan rather than becoming the plan.”

Giles nodded, relief absent from his face but present in the loosened set of his shoulders. “That is all we should have asked for in the first place.”

The waiting delegations were on the other side of the door. Bartlet handed the folder to Leo. “Then let’s stop pretending today’s meeting is only about recovering a ship.”

Leo did not move toward the door. “If this goes into the multinational meeting, the recovery agreement stops being a recovery agreement. It becomes a defense pact made in a room where half the people will assume we have been hiding an army.”

“We have been hiding a child,” Giles said. “The army is the part we do not have.”

Fitzwallace opened the photograph packet again. “The Chinese and Russians will ask for every image, every weapons assessment, and every source. If we give them nothing, they assume we intend to monopolize the response. If we give them everything, we hand them the knowledge that made the original world lose three years.”

“Then we tell them enough to make the threat impossible to dismiss,” Weir said. “Not enough to give anyone a private weapons program. The distinction will not satisfy them.”

“It does not have to satisfy them,” Bartlet said. “It has to keep them from acting alone.”

That was the hornet nest: strategic control, operational authority, diplomatic disclosure, intelligence sharing, legal jurisdiction, civilian continuity, and whether a sixteen-year-old could be protected without turning him into the most valuable strategic asset on Earth. Every answer created two more disputes.

Carter set the note beside the photographs. “There is also the practical problem. Xander is already at the crash site. If this becomes public before we establish a command relationship he accepts, every intelligence service on the planet will start looking for him.”

“Which is precisely why the command relationship needs to exist,” Giles said. “Not ownership. Coordination. The secret protected his identity. It did not give us the right to keep the people responsible for civilian survival ignorant of the reason he was sent.”

Bartlet looked from Giles to the folder, then toward the closed door. “We disclose the invasion, the date, the photographs, and the operational consequences. We disclose that Superman is a local human asset under voluntary cooperation. We do not disclose his civilian identity beyond the people who need it to keep him alive.”

Leo’s expression tightened. “And when the delegates ask why we knew before today?”

“We tell them the truth,” Bartlet said. “We had a warning. We spent too long deciding whether protecting one boy justified withholding a planetary threat. It did not.”

The consequences would not stop at diplomacy. Buffy and Willow would have to learn that the information they had guarded as a promise to Xander was now moving through military channels. Ethan’s magic, the future note, and every recovered device would acquire owners, lawyers, classification markings, and people who believed urgency entitled them to skip consent.

Carter’s operational list was already growing: secure the photographs, validate the weapons data, establish an alert path Xander could refuse without being hunted, coordinate with Calendar before anyone treated the Enterprise as a weapons platform, and build a response that did not depend on one teenager being willing to die on command.

Giles looked at the folder one last time. “He will feel betrayed.”

“Possibly,” Bartlet said. “But if we keep the warning to ourselves and his future arrives on schedule, he will have been betrayed by our caution instead.”

No one in the room mistook that for a clean solution. It was a decision to let the argument become international, personal, operational, and unavoidable before the invasion made every argument irrelevant—and to build a defense that did not collapse if one extraordinary teenager was unavailable. Weir gathered the packet, Fitzwallace took the photographs, and Leo opened the door.

Weir took her seat at the head of the rearranged table, the untouched water glasses gleaming like a row of small, clear obstacles. Her eyes swept the room as the delegations settled. Volkov, the Russian, sat upright and patient—a man built for waiting. Wen, for China, had a folder open he never consulted, his stillness a performance. Fitzwallace was a solid presence at her right hand, but two seats down, Senator Kinsey radiated a smug satisfaction. He was, as Leo had warned, uninvited and entirely undeterred.

Bartlet's opening ran four minutes, no longer, a plain statement of the proposal and a promise that Doctor Weir would handle the details, and then he was gone, the door closing softly behind him, and the room's attention swung to her all at once.

"Gentlemen," Weir said, once the room quieted. "I am going to begin with the largest fact in the room. Earth is facing a planned extraterrestrial invasion. We have a date, a partial account of the last war, and no reliable estimate of the attacking fleet."

Volkov did not move. Wen’s folder closed with a quiet click. The Australian ambassador looked from one face to the next. Nobody reached for the water.

"The warning describes ships entering the solar system, orbital bombardment, and armies landing after the cities are struck," Weir continued. "London, Manchester, New York, and Washington were destroyed in the account we received. The war lasted three years. Conventional missiles could not penetrate the attackers’ shields."

"What is the source?" Wen asked.

"A message sent backward by a human survivor of that future, carried here by Ethan Rayne at terrible cost. We have photographs, dates, weapons descriptions, and casualty accounts. The source is extraordinary. The evidence is not a reason to wait."

Volkov’s voice remained level. "You expect four governments to reorganize their defense posture around a letter from a man who does not yet exist."

"No," Weir said. "We expect them to stop pretending that separate national responses are safer than coordinated ones. The warning gives us a deadline. The Enterprise gives us technology that may help us meet it."

The Australian ambassador leaned forward. "And what exactly are you asking this table to do?"

"Accept a joint emergency posture. Share detection and threat information. Establish continuity and evacuation planning. Prevent any one nation from stripping the crash site for weapons while the rest of us are still trying to understand what is coming."

"You are asking for military cooperation under the cover of salvage," Wen said.

"I am asking you to recognize that salvage is no longer the primary problem," Weir replied. "The primary problem is that we may have less than a year to prepare for an orbital assault. The ship is one resource in that preparation, not the prize at the center of it."

Volkov opened his folder again. "If the invasion is real, why has the United States not notified the world?"

"Because until today the information was held by a small group protecting the person who brought the warning into this timeline," Weir said. "That protection was once defensible. It is not defensible now that the threat intersects four nuclear powers and an international crash site."

The room changed at that. The delegations had come expecting a jurisdictional dispute over a starship. They were now being asked to decide whether their governments would prepare together for an enemy none of them could yet see.

"What proof can you provide today?" Wen asked.

Weir nodded to Fitzwallace. He placed copies of the photographs on the table: gold-and-black ships above ruined cities, yellow energy cutting through cloud, armored forces moving through smoke. He did not distribute the future-Xander note itself. That remained a protected source document.

"We are not claiming the photographs identify fleet strength or command structure," Fitzwallace said. "They establish the scale of the warning and the kind of threat it describes. Our own intelligence confirms a hostile extraterrestrial power exists. We have fought its representatives. We have not yet confirmed its arrival schedule."

"Then your certainty is incomplete," Volkov said.

"Our certainty is sufficient to justify preparation," Weir answered. "Waiting for certainty is how the future described in these photographs won."

The argument became specific. China demanded access to all sensor data gathered at the site. Russia demanded a veto over any transfer of alien technology. Australia demanded environmental command and a civilian safety perimeter. The United Kingdom offered engineers and airlift, but only if the operation had a written multinational mandate.

Weir let them spend their first anger. Then she put the framework on the table: observer access, shared inventories, joint safety briefings, no removal of functioning systems, and no unilateral military expansion at the site. Operational command would remain with the designated recovery team until a broader defense authority could be negotiated.

"And Commander Calendar?" Wen asked. "You have described her as a survivor, but you are treating her ship as a strategic resource."

"She is alive, in command of the vessel, and not in United States custody," Weir said. "No system is removed without her consent. If the Enterprise contributes sensors, shields, or communications capability, that contribution is negotiated with her. She is not cargo, and neither is her ship."

"What of Superman?" the Australian ambassador asked. "Is he part of this defense posture?"

"He is a voluntary local responder," Weir said. "The baseline plan cannot depend on him. If he participates, he adds capability. He does not replace an alliance, an evacuation system, or a defense force."

Kinsey leaned forward. "You are asking us to accept an invasion warning, a secret military program, an alien vessel, and a masked civilian asset in one sitting."

"No," Weir said. "We are telling you those facts already coexist. The question is whether we build a response while they are still separate problems, or wait until the first attack makes them one."

The room fell silent again. It was not agreement. It was the sound of governments recalculating what they could no longer safely ignore.

By the time the meeting adjourned, no nation had surrendered its legal position. Britain had offered airlift and engineering support. Australia had accepted a safety corridor. China and Russia had demanded written reservations and observer access. Everyone had accepted that the crash site was now part of a planetary defense problem.

It was not a settlement. It was the first layer of a response, and every delegation left knowing that the next meeting would concern not only who owned the ship, but who intended to survive what was coming.

✳

"You don't get to do that again," Leo said as soon as the door to the Roosevelt Room closed behind them.

The three of them had made it no farther than the empty office across the corridor. Weir shut the door while Leo faced Kinsey, his voice low enough not to carry and considerably more dangerous for it.

"I asked a question every person in that room was already thinking," Kinsey said.

"You answered it too," Weir replied. "You told them the United States intends to promise cooperation in public and reserve everything useful in private."

"I told them we have national interests. If that comes as a surprise to Doctor Weir's negotiating partners, perhaps they aren't qualified to be here."

"You gave Wen a justification to reject the agreement," she said. "He no longer has to explain why China is sending destroyers toward Antarctica. He can say a member of the United States delegation admitted the recovery framework is a cover for an American claim."

"Then correct the record. That's what you're here for."

That's not how it works," Leo snapped. "Someone else's options don't become your justification."

Kinsey looked from Leo to Weir. "The two of you seem very certain this agreement survives contact with Congress. It won't. The first time the administration proposes handing a propulsion system or an energy weapon to the Chinese government, I will have half the Senate standing beside me. The other half will be asking why they weren't invited sooner."

"Nobody is handing weapons to China," Weir said.

"Not today. That's the useful little phrase everyone keeps using. Not today. Observer status today, technical briefings tomorrow, a formal seat when we decide what the technology is worth. You think I didn't hear the progression?"

"I think you heard it and decided a room full of foreign diplomats was where you wanted to begin your reelection speech," Leo said.

Kinsey's expression hardened. "My concerns are on the record."

The inner door opened. Bartlet stepped into the corridor, and the air changed around him. Leo straightened. Weir stopped moving. Even Kinsey’s objection faltered—not because Bartlet raised his voice, but because the President had arrived, and nobody mistook that for anything other than command.

"Your departure can be too," Bartlet said. He opened the door. "You're done in that room."

"You can't exclude congressional oversight from a military operation of this scale."

"I'm not excluding oversight. I'm excluding you from a diplomatic negotiation after you deliberately contradicted my stated position. There's a complaint box in Leo's office if you have something to say. I'm told they're very responsive."

For a moment it looked as if Kinsey might refuse. Then he straightened his jacket, nodded once to Weir, and walked out without another word.

Leo waited until the senator had turned the corner. "He's going to make that expensive."

"He already has," Weir said. "Wen's minimum position is going to move."

Fitzwallace appeared in the doorway from the Roosevelt Room with three sheets in his hand. "It did. China now wants a binding guarantee that no recovered system will be transferred to American military custody without international inspection. Australia wants environmental command and a civilian scientific deputy at the camp. Russia wants identical technical access to whatever Britain receives."

"And Britain?" Leo asked.

"Johns wants one chain of command and no committee able to countermand a safety order from the site commander."

"All reasonable until you put them in the same document," Weir said.

"That's generally how this building works," Leo replied.

She took the sheets and read them quickly. "Okay, the principles are simple. One: custody is separate from inspection. Everything stays put unless it's a safety risk. Two: anything that moves gets logged for all observers. No distribution, no reverse engineering. Three: command is US and British, but Australia gets the environmental officer with veto power on treaty violations. Russia and China get observers on the inventory team. They can watch, they can object, but they can't give orders."

Fitzwallace considered it. "Johns can live with that."

"Australia probably can," Leo said. "China?"

"China can reject it without leaving the framework. We record their objection, continue negotiations, and include a clause saying nothing in the provisional agreement prejudices any nation's later claim. They keep their ships where they are while talks continue."

"And if they sail anyway?" Fitzwallace asked.

"Then they do it after rejecting an inspection seat they asked for, not because we refused them any role at all. That matters to every government deciding whether to support them."

Leo looked at the clock. "You've got seven minutes."

"Then someone find me a typist who doesn't ask what Enterprise means."

✳

When the meeting resumed, Kinsey's empty chair attracted more attention than anyone admitted. Weir did not explain it. She placed the revised two-page framework in front of each delegation and gave them time to read it before she spoke.

The Australian ambassador reached the second page first. "The environmental officer can suspend recovery operations?"

"For a documented safety or Treaty violation," Weir said. "The site commander can appeal the decision through the joint command, but cannot ignore it while the appeal is heard."

"Acceptable as a provisional arrangement. I'll need Canberra's approval before signing anything permanent."

"This isn't permanent," Weir said. "It gets us through the next seventy-two hours without five countries improvising around the same wreck."

Johns objected to the inventory observers entering damaged sections without permission. Volkov objected to Britain receiving technical briefings through the command staff before Russian observers saw them. They spent twenty minutes replacing simultaneous access with simultaneous release, and another ten defining emergency removal narrowly enough that it could not become a route for emptying the ship one classified truck at a time.

Wen read the entire document twice.

"China's request for operational participation is not met," he said at last.

"No," Weir agreed.

"Nor does this document recognize any special Chinese interest in the recovery."

"It doesn't recognize a special American, British, Russian, or Australian ownership interest either. It recognizes who can put a safe team on the ice today and gives every government here a means of verifying what that team does."

"Our vessels will not turn back on the strength of a provisional document."

"I'm not asking them to turn back today. I'm asking that they hold their present position while the next round of talks continues. No southward movement, no attempt to establish a separate camp, and no action that forces the joint naval group to intercept them."

"And in return?"

"Your inventory observer flies south with the first multinational contingent. Your government receives the same movement logs as mine. If the United States removes a piece of that ship without recording it, you will know."

Wen glanced toward the empty chair Kinsey had occupied. "And if Senator Kinsey prevents this arrangement from being honored?"

"Then you bring the evidence back to this table," Weir said. "But you don't get to use what he might do as permission for what your navy is already preparing to do."

Volkov leaned back, watching Wen rather than her. Marbury had gone uncharacteristically quiet. Even Leo stayed by the wall and allowed the silence to remain where it was.

"I can recommend that our vessels hold for twenty-four hours," Wen said finally. "I cannot promise longer without instructions from Beijing."

"Then we have twenty-four hours to make the next agreement better than this one," Weir replied.

It was not a settlement. No one signed. They initialed the framework, each man adding handwritten reservations like footnotes to a truce. Wen’s pen stroke was sharp, retaining China’s demand for operational authority. The Australian ambassador’s note was a careful defense of territorial claims. Volkov added his condition in a dense script: no private British access. Even Fitzwallace’s signature felt like an admission that they had accepted an operation they could no longer completely control.

It held, which was not the same as saying it would continue to hold.

✳

Kinsey didn't go far. He found a quiet corner of the corridor outside the Roosevelt Room, phone already to his ear before the door had fully closed behind him.

"She held the room better than I counted on," he said, low. "And Fitzwallace came in behind her, which I didn't see coming." A pause, listening. "No, I'm not finished. I said I didn't get what I wanted today. That's not the same thing as giving up on getting it."

Another pause, longer.

"The strategic value doesn't go away just because I lost one argument in front of a Russian and a Brit who both think this is funny. There will be other rooms. Smaller ones. Ones without a mediator running the meeting like she's brokering a hostage situation." He glanced back once at the closed door, something flat and patient settling into his face. "I'll call you when I have something worth calling about."

He closed the phone, straightened his jacket, and walked off down the corridor with the controlled, empty posture of a man who had just lost everything.

✳

Afterward, once the delegations had filed out, Wen the last to leave and pausing at the door just long enough to incline his head once more before he went, the room emptied down to Weir, Leo, Bartlet, and Fitzwallace.

"That went about as well as it could have," Fitzwallace said, dropping into a chair.

"A senator broke the American position in front of four foreign delegations, China gave us twenty-four hours, and I negotiated an environmental veto over a starship recovery without knowing whether a damaged starship can poison an ice shelf," Weir said. "If that was as well as it could have gone, I'd prefer not to see the other version."

"The other version had destroyers moving south before lunch," Leo said. "Take the win. It won't be here long."

The phone on the side table rang before anyone could answer. Fitzwallace picked it up, listened to the operator, then covered the receiver with one hand.

"Hammond. He has Calendar's answer and an updated site report."

Bartlet nodded toward the speaker controls. "Put him through."

Fitzwallace pressed the button. "George, you're in the Oval Office with the President, Leo McGarry, and Doctor Weir."

"Yes, sir," General Hammond's voice came through a wash of static. "Mr President, Doctor Weir. I'm calling from the command center. We're relaying through McMurdo, so the line may not hold."

"Then let's not waste it," Bartlet said. "Did Commander Calendar agree to the recovery operation?"

"Conditionally, sir. She welcomes engineering, medical, and security assistance. She does not consent to the removal of any functioning system from either section of the Enterprise without her approval. Personal effects and remains are to be catalogued and treated as belonging to her crew. She also wants final authority over access to secured areas aboard the vessel."

"Can we give her that?" Bartlet asked.

Fitzwallace frowned. "Security can't answer to a civilian commander we haven't vetted."

"She's the only person alive who knows which door opens into a laboratory and which opens into an armory," Hammond said. "At the moment, sir, my people consider her judgment part of site security. Major Ferretti reports she's cooperated fully."

"What exactly is still functioning?" Weir asked.

"More than we'd like and less than she needs," Hammond replied. "The saucer has intermittent power. Life support is holding in occupied sections, but computer access is spotty." Bartlet leaned forward. "The secondary hull is worse. We've got decks open to the weather, and the whole structure shifted in the last storm. Ferretti pulled his people back."

"Immediate environmental risk?"

"Calendar says the main power system shut down clean. No release detected." Weir let out a quiet breath. "The bad news is, she says there are materials on board our people won't know how to handle if containment fails. She's making a list."

"So the Australian demand wasn't ceremonial," Weir said.

"No, ma'am. Neither is their concern about the weather. A front is expected to reach the site within thirty-six hours. There is debris spread between the two sections, some of it already under fresh snow. Superman is helping move what Commander Calendar identifies as safe, but they cannot clear the field before the weather changes."

"Is he taking direction from Ferretti?" Fitzwallace asked.

"From Commander Calendar for the ship and Ferretti for everything outside it. So far. Colonel O'Neill and the rest of SG-1 are en route to McMurdo now."

Weir caught the qualifier. "So far?"

"He arrived on his own, Doctor. He is cooperating because he wants to help. Colonel O'Neill will have a better idea of his intentions after they speak."

"What else changed?" Fitzwallace asked. "You said this was an updated report."

The static deepened for a moment before Hammond answered. "They recovered another member of the crew from the debris field. An artificial life-form named Commander Data. His body was separated during the crash and is non-functional. Commander Calendar believes he may be repairable, but no one on site has the expertise."

Bartlet looked at Leo. "Artificial life-form meaning robot?"

"Android, sir," Hammond said. "A machine with a human appearance and what Commander Calendar describes as a positronic brain."

"Of course it is," Bartlet murmured. "Zoey is never going to forgive me for knowing this before she does."

"There is another concern," Hammond continued. "One section of the secondary hull appears to have been altered before the crash. Black structural growth, exposed conduits, machinery integrated directly into the ship's systems. Commander Calendar identified it as Borg technology."

"Borg," Leo repeated.

"A hostile cybernetic species from her universe," Hammond said. "She reports no living Borg aboard and no biological readings from that section. Major Warren observed cabling moving without anyone touching it, however. The area is now sealed."

Fitzwallace leaned toward the phone. "Moving how?"

"Attempting to reconnect itself, according to Commander Calendar. Self-repair. She believes the technology may still be following its last instructions even without anyone controlling it."

"Can it spread?"

"Unknown. She does not believe it can cross open ground or infect people without active drones, but she has been wrong about what survived twice already today. Ferretti is treating the entire section as hazardous."

Nobody spoke for a moment.

"General," Weir said, "the agreement I just negotiated places foreign inventory observers at that site. How much of this will they be allowed to see?"

"That's above my authority, ma'am. I would strongly recommend nobody enter that section until SG-1 and Commander Calendar agree it is safe. Russian and Chinese observers won't do us any good if the first thing they observe kills them."

"Agreed," Fitzwallace said. "We'll classify it as an active hazard, access by site command only. The observers get notice that an area is closed and the stated safety reason. They do not get a guided tour."

"That will look like the first secret removal before their aircraft even lands," Weir warned.

"Then you get to explain the difference between transparency and suicide," Leo said.

"There's one final item," Hammond said. "The Enterprise sensors detected a structure approximately two miles beneath the ice. Large, artificial, origin unknown. Commander Calendar also reports a faint life-sign reading from inside it."

Bartlet stared at the phone. "Two miles beneath the ice."

"Yes, sir. The reading is being distorted by some form of interference. They cannot identify the species or determine the condition of whatever is down there."

"Is it connected to the ship?" Fitzwallace asked.

"Commander Calendar says no. The structure was already there. Teal'c has reviewed the preliminary readings and does not recognize the technology as Goa'uld."

Weir looked from Fitzwallace to Leo. Both men had gone still at the name.

"Who is Teal'c?" she asked.

Hammond did not answer.

"George," Fitzwallace said, "hold further reports on the buried structure to this group, the site team, and your command staff. No mention in the international briefing yet. Stabilize the wreck, secure the altered section, and keep everyone away from whatever is under the ice. No drilling and no attempt to reach it without authorization."

"Understood, sir."

"We'll get additional engineers moving once the observers are selected," Bartlet said. "And General, make sure Commander Calendar knows her conditions were heard. Nobody strips her ship while she's helping us keep it from coming apart."

"Yes, Mr President. I'll inform her. Hammond out."

The line clicked dead. The room stayed quiet around it.

"China's not signing anything permanent tomorrow," Weir said eventually.

"No," Bartlet agreed, perched on the edge of the table. "But they didn't walk out today either. A week ago I'd have taken that as a win."

"I need to know what he meant by Goa'uld," Weir said. "And I need to know why a man named Teal'c is apparently your authority on their technology."

"Yes," Bartlet said. "You do. Sit. There's something else."

Weir sat. "Bigger than a scenario that invalidates every security treaty written in the last fifty years, involves four nuclear powers, and is happening in a jurisdiction we can't legally claim?

"Considerably," Bartlet said.

Fitzwallace slid a folder across the table. Weir opened it and found a photograph clipped inside: a ring of grey metal, standing upright in sand, unmistakably manufactured, unmistakably not built by anyone whose name would appear in any history she'd ever studied.

"In 1928," Fitzwallace began, "a device was found beneath the sands of Giza."

"I've already been briefed on the Enterprise," Weir said, slowly. "I assume this isn't the Enterprise."

"No. This is older."

"How much older?"

"Nineteen twenty-eight is when we found it," Leo said. "How old the device itself actually is, nobody's given me a straight answer to yet."

"It's a doorway," Fitzwallace said. "That ring generates a stable wormhole to a matching device on another world. Point A to point B, instantaneous, no travel time in between. We call it the Stargate."

"Another world."

"Hundreds of them, as far as we've mapped so far," Fitzwallace said. "Some habitable. Some not. Some with people on them who look exactly like us, because it turns out an awful lot of Earth's own history got seeded from the other side of that gate a very long time ago."

"Seeded how?"

"A race called the Goa'uld took humans from Earth as slaves, thousands of years ago, and scattered them across dozens of worlds," Leo said. "Some of what your ambassadors call ancient mythology, Egyptian, Norse, more than a few others, turns out to be first-contact reports somebody just never had the context to recognize as such."

"Ra," Weir said, slowly, remembering something from Major Samuels's own briefing packet.

"Ra," Fitzwallace confirmed. "Among others."

"How long has this program actually existed? Sending people through, not just having found the device."

"The device itself, since 1928," Leo said. "Actively sending people through it, since 1994. There's a team, SG-1, that's been running the program's front line since it started. Colonel O'Neill, the officer currently attached to Mr. Harris, commands it."

Weir sat back. "O'Neill's part of this program? He's not simply liaising with Mr. Harris on Superman's behalf?"

Both," Fitzwallace said. "It's how he got the assignment. When Superman appeared, we needed someone cleared for extraterrestrial matters to make first contact.

"How many people, total, know this program exists?"

"Several hundred know enough to keep the program operating," Leo said. "Most of them know one part of it, not the full picture. The number of civilians briefed at this level is considerably smaller. You're one of perhaps two dozen."

"And Congress?"

"A very small subcommittee," Fitzwallace said, "with a very large amount of trust placed in a very small number of people, myself included. I won't pretend that sits comfortably. It doesn't, some nights."

"Has something gone wrong out there? In three years, with a program this size, something must have."

Fitzwallace looked at Leo, and Leo looked away first.

"There's a species called the Goa'uld," Leo said. "Parasitic. They take hosts, they call themselves gods, and they have been doing exactly that to entire populations of humans on other worlds for thousands of years. We've lost people fighting them. Good people, career officers, the kind of loss that gets written up as a training accident because there's no honest way to write it up at all. It's an ongoing war, Doctor Weir. It's just one nobody back here has ever had to think about, because it's never once come looking for us directly. Until, we think, very possibly, now."

"You think the Enterprise's arrival is connected to that."

"We don't know," Fitzwallace admitted. "We think it's too large a coincidence not to be, but we don't have proof either way yet."

"Is Superman part of this?" Weir asked.

"No," Bartlet said. "That's the one thing everyone keeps assuming and everyone keeps getting wrong. Mr. Harris has no connection to the program. His people vouch for that, and I believe them."

"Then what is he?"

"We don't entirely know yet," Fitzwallace admitted. "Which is, I'm told, its own kind of answer."

"How many people know both halves of this? The Stargate, and Superman, together, as connected things?"

Not many," Leo said. "Now you're one of the not-many."

Weir closed the folder, opened it again, made herself read the next page properly instead of skimming, a diagram of the gate itself, its outer ring marked with symbols she didn't recognize, thirty-nine of them arranged around the circumference like a language nobody had ever taught her to read. "I have a meeting with three ambassadors tomorrow morning."

"You do."

"None of whom can know about this."

"Correct. Not the Stargate. Not the Goa'uld. The moment any of that becomes part of the Enterprise or Superman conversations, we lose the ability to control any of it, and I mean that in the most literal sense. This isn't politics, Doctor Weir. This is the single largest secret this country has ever kept, and I am asking you to help me keep it a little while longer."

"What happens if I say no? If I ask to be released from this and go back to Iraq and let someone else run tomorrow's meeting?"

Nobody answered right away.

"Then we find someone else," Bartlet said, finally, quiet, honest. "And we lose the best person I've seen in that room in a long time, at exactly the moment I need her most. I won't pretend that's not a real cost to me if you walk out that door. But it's your choice to make, not mine."

Weir looked at the three of them a long moment. "I'm not walking out the door."

"No," Bartlet said. "I didn't think you would."

"Am I allowed to ask why you're telling me tonight, then, and not after the ambassadors have gone home?"

"Because you're good in a room, Doctor Weir," Bartlet said. "And I have a feeling we're going to need you in a great many more of them before this is finished, rooms considerably harder than the one you were just in."

She let that settle a moment, the photograph of the ring still turned toward her on the table, the diagram beneath it, the sheer scale of what she was suddenly, unwillingly responsible for keeping quiet. "Then I suppose I should say thank you."

"Don't thank me yet," Bartlet said. "Wait until you've met the kid."
