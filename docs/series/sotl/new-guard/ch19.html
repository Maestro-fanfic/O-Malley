Chapter 19

Sunnydale High smelled exactly the way he remembered, and somehow that was the strangest part of the whole morning.

Floor wax and old paper and whatever industrial-strength cleaner the janitors used on the bathrooms that never quite masked what it was covering up. A freshman knocked into his shoulder apologizing without looking up from his own feet, Snyder's voice crackled over the PA about gum, strictly prohibited as ever, and he was just Xander Harris again, late for first period, backpack half-zipped, same as every morning before Halloween.

"You're staring at your locker like it insulted you," Willow said, falling into step beside him, her own bag clutched to her chest.

"I forgot the combination."

"You've had that locker since freshman year, Xander."

I know! It's just... I feel like I'm watching a rerun, but I'm the only one who knows the ending. Everyone here is the same, but I'm not. And nobody can see the difference. It's like I'm a ghost in my own life." He tapped his temple.

"I can tell," Willow said, quieter, studying his face. "You look tired. Good tired. But tired. Like you've been carrying something heavy for a really long time and finally got to set some of it down, but not all of it."

"Guess sleeping in a former alien starship'll do that."

"Xander!" She glanced around at the crowded hallway, and he grinned despite himself.

Buffy caught up to them by the library steps, backpack slung over one shoulder. Her eyes did a quick, professional sweep—checking his posture, looking for the strain around his eyes, the way she'd started doing since their last patrol.

"So," she said. "First day back. How's it feel?"

"Honestly? Kind of amazing. Kind of terrifying. Mostly just really, really normal, which is its own kind of weird. Like the normal is the thing I have to get used to now, not the other stuff."

"Enjoy it while it lasts," Buffy said, sounding like she'd learned that lesson the hard way more than once, and steered them both toward class before the bell finished ringing.

Normal lasted until fifth period.

He was at his locker again, trading out books, half listening to Willow rattle off something about a chemistry assignment, when a shoulder caught him hard from behind, the exact same shove he'd taken a hundred times since freshman year, Kyle Bittaker putting his whole weight into it the way he always did.

Nothing happened.

Xander didn't move. Not an inch, not a stagger. Kyle, who'd built the whole shove around the expectation of Xander going into the lockers, went stumbling sideways instead, off balance, catching himself against the row with a clatter that drew more attention than the shove itself ever had.

"What the hell," Kyle muttered, staring at his own feet like they'd betrayed him.

Xander forced his shoulders to jerk sideways a half second too late, faked a stumble he didn't need, let his books scatter across the floor.

"Ow," he said, unconvincingly, bending to gather them while a couple of students exchanged a look he pretended not to notice.

"You okay?" Willow asked, low, once Kyle had wandered off, baffled and unsatisfied.

"Yeah. Just gotta remember to actually fall down sometimes. Apparently. Who knew that'd be the hard part."

"That's going to get harder before it gets easier," Willow said. "You know that, right? The pretending."

"Yeah. I know."

"You could just tell people, you know. Some people. Me, obviously, but I mean people like Kyle even, if it stopped him from trying to shove you into lockers."

"And then it's not a secret anymore. Then it's a thing everybody knows, and it changes how everybody looks at me, and I go from being invisible to being the guy who used to be invisible and now everyone's watching. I kind of like that Kyle has no idea. It's the last normal thing left."

"That's fair," Willow said, and let it go.

Down the hall, Principal Snyder stood half-hidden by his office door. His attention had nothing to do with hallway discipline; his small eyes tracked every second of the non-fight. When the hall emptied, he turned back inside without a word, his hand already reaching for the telephone.

An hour later Xander sat between Buffy and Willow at the end of a cafeteria table, eating pizza that had somehow managed to be both burned and cold. Willow complained about chemistry. Buffy stole the least burned piece from his tray and denied it while chewing. Somebody dropped a carton of milk three tables away and half the room applauded.

Nobody asked him to lift anything. Nobody offered him a job. Nobody called him Superman.

For twenty-three minutes, the most important decision in his life was whether the cafeteria cookie was worth the risk.

It was the kind of awful he wanted to feel again.

✳

Giles asked him to stay in the library after the final bell.

Xander expected a lecture about the hallway, or a prophecy update from the Summers' kitchen. Instead, Giles locked the library doors, crossed to the table, and set his glasses down with a deliberate click, leaving them unpolished.

"Colonel O'Neill told me about the scars on your back," he said.

Every easy answer disappeared.

Xander stared at him. "He what?"

"He saw them during the medical testing in New York. He recognized what caused them."

"They're old."

"That is not a denial."

"It's not happening now."

"That is also not a denial."

Xander pushed his chair back, scraping it against the floor. "So what? It's over. He can't hurt me anymore, not unless he gets his hands on a tank. So it doesn't matter. It's handled.

"Your invulnerability does not make what he did acceptable."

"Didn't say it did. Just means it can't happen again."

"It means he cannot injure you in the same fashion. It does not make the house safe, and it does not require you to remain there."

"Where am I supposed to go?"

"You may stay with me."

The answer came so quickly that Xander had no joke prepared for it.

"Giles, your apartment has one bedroom."

"It also has a sofa, heat, and a notable absence of drunken men with belts. We can find something larger if the arrangement lasts. Buffy and her mother may be willing to help as well, though I have not told them why and will not without your permission."

"You already talked about this with O'Neill."

"We spoke about ensuring you had somewhere safe to go. We did not decide where you would live. That decision remains yours."

Xander looked down at the table. "If my parents find out the government offered money, they'll care where I live real fast."

"Then they need not find out. The White House is protecting your identity. As far as your parents are concerned, you have returned from an unusual school trip and are spending more time with friends. Given their previous level of attention, I doubt the change will be immediately challenged."

"And if it is?"

"Then I will speak to them. If that fails, Colonel O'Neill offered assistance that would be, and I quote, 'loud and obnoxious. Possibly violent.'"

A laugh escaped him before he could stop it. "He actually said that?

"With considerable menace."

"Sounds like him."

Giles put his glasses back on. "You do not have to protect your father from the consequences of his own behavior."

"I'm not."

"You are protecting everyone from the discomfort of knowing. That is not the same thing."

"What do you want, Giles? You want me to call the police and explain my dad used to hit Superman? You want Snyder hearing about it? Half the town?"

"No. I want you to pack what you need and come to my apartment tonight. Tomorrow, when there is time and no immediate danger, we decide what happens next. Quietly, if possible. Formally, if necessary."

"You make it sound easy."

"I assure you, I expect it to be extremely difficult."

"That's more honest."

"I am trying."

Xander pulled out a chair and lowered himself into it. "The worst part was the TV. Whenever he'd start, she'd just... turn the volume up. Like if it was loud enough, it wasn't happening."

Giles closed his eyes.

"Don't," Xander said. "Don't look like that."

"How should I look?"

"Like it isn't the end of the world. I've seen the end of the world. Or pictures of it, anyway. This isn't that."

"Pain need not be the end of the world to matter."

They sat in the library's after-school quiet. The only sounds were the clicking of old pipes in the walls and the rustle of a forgotten notice stirring in the air-conditioning vent.

"Your sofa's terrible," Xander said.

"I am aware."

"And you don't own a television."

"I own many books."

"Not helping your case."

"Will you come?"

Xander looked toward the doors, then back at the man who had offered without hesitation and still waited for an answer instead of deciding it for him.

"Yeah," he said. "For tonight. Then we figure it out."

"For tonight," Giles agreed.

It wasn't a solution, but it was a key in his hand. He wasn't sure he was ready to turn it, but for the first time, he knew the lock wasn't permanent. For now, that was enough.

✳

Buffy found her mother's old address book on the kitchen counter that night, flipping through it without really meaning to while she waited for the kettle, and stopped on a page she hadn't looked at in months.

Her own name was in there, in her mother's handwriting, birthday circled, a note in the margin about her shoe size from two years ago that nobody had ever bothered crossing out. Ordinary. The kind of thing a normal girl's information looked like.

She thought about what Xander's own page would look like now, if anyone bothered to keep one. Powers, unknown limit. Weaknesses, unknown. Whereabouts, wherever the world happened to need him.

She traced the edge of the counter, thinking of Xander. He'd had his destiny thrown at him, same as her. She knew what that cost. But a small, mean part of her couldn't ignore the difference: when the world noticed him, it sent presidents and generals. For three years, her only backup had been a library full of books.

That could change. Giles had mentioned the people in uniforms, the ones who knew monsters were real. People who probably had files on 'hostile sub-terrestrials' and other things she just called Tuesday. They knew about demons, about Giles. Sooner or later, they'd connect the dots to Sunnydale. They'd come looking for the person who kept the graveyards quiet.

Buffy wasn't sure if that sounded like long-overdue backup, or just footsteps getting closer in the dark.

"You're allowed to feel weird about it," Giles said, from the doorway, and she startled, not having heard him come in.

"Feel weird about what?"

"Whatever it is you were just thinking, that had you frowning at your mother's address book like it had personally wronged you."

She closed the book, a little embarrassed. "It's not a big deal."

"I didn't say it was. I said you're allowed to feel it anyway." He came in, put the kettle on properly since she'd forgotten it was still just sitting there. "You've carried this town on your shoulders for three years, Buffy, largely without recognition, largely without thanks, and largely without choice. It would be rather strange if some part of you didn't notice the difference now."

"I don't want what he's got. The Oval Office, the whole world knowing his face. I really don't."

I don't imagine you do. But has anyone ever offered? Has anyone ever asked what you needed?"

She was quiet a moment, turning that over. "Is that awful? That I feel that even a little?"

"No," Giles said, simply. "It's human. You're allowed to be human about this, Buffy, even you." He found two mugs, set them out. "For what it's worth, I don't believe Xander would trade places with you for a moment, if he actually understood what your three years have cost. I think if you asked him honestly, he'd tell you he'd rather have what you have. Friends who chose to fight beside you, rather than a world that simply decided it needed you."

"Maybe," she said, and let it go, mostly, and made the tea.

✳

Buffy peeled off after a vampire that came a little too close to the crowd outside the Bronze, sprinting into the dark, leaving Xander and Angel alone in the mouth of the alley, streetlight buzzing faintly overhead.

"You've been avoiding a question," Angel said.

"Have I?"

"Since the night you flew back into town. I asked what you were. You never actually answered, just deflected with a joke the way you always do."

Xander was quiet a moment, watching his own breath fog in the cold, and found, to his own surprise, that he wasn't inclined to deflect this time. "Kryptonian, I guess. Or whatever the Halloween spell decided that means, exactly. I don't fully understand it myself, if I'm being honest. I've got the powers, all of them, or most of them. I don't have the history, don't have the memories, don't have anything except what I figure out by trial and error."

"That's not a small thing to carry."

"No," Xander agreed. "It's not. Some days it feels like the only thing I'm carrying."

Angel looked at him for a long moment, something unreadable moving behind two hundred years of practiced stillness. "I've spent most of my existence not being human either. Not the way anyone standing near me is human. It doesn't stop mattering just because you get used to it, just because you learn to hide it well enough that people forget to ask."

"Does it get easier?"

"No," Angel said, honest in a way that cost him something. "You just get better at carrying it where people can't see it. That's not the same as easier. Don't mistake the two."

"Buffy know how you actually feel about all this? About what you are, day to day, underneath everything?"

"No. And I'd rather it stayed that way."

"Yeah," Xander said. "I get that more than I used to."

"Does Willow know? Buffy?"

They know I can lift a car. They don't know what it feels like to be the thing that can. Nobody gets that part." He shrugged, not looking at Angel. "Except you, maybe. Which is weird, since I'm pretty sure you'd still like to bite me most nights.

"Not tonight," Angel said, dry, and something in it was almost warmth.

They stood there a while longer, not friends, not quite adversaries either anymore, something quieter and more complicated settling between them instead.

"I still loathe you," Xander said, eventually, mostly to break the silence.

"I know," Angel said, and for just a second, something that might almost have been amusement crossed his face.

✳

"Celia," the Mayor said, into the intercom, pressing the button with a single, precise finger, "any word from our friends at Wolfram and Hart?"

They're drawing up the terms now, sir. Mr. Manners said they should be ready for you within the week, but I can press him if you need it sooner.

"Good, good." He straightened the student photograph on his desk, corners worn now from handling. "And our young Mr. Harris?"

"Back in Sunnydale as of this morning, sir. Full time, according to the attendance records Principal Snyder forwarded."

The Mayor considered that, folding his hands, patient as ever, though something in the set of his shoulders had sharpened just slightly. "Well," he said, gaze fixed on the photograph. "That does change the timeline." He reached for the antibacterial gel, working it carefully between his fingers. "Celia, please inform Mr. Manners that we will require his draft by tomorrow. And clear my schedule for the afternoon."

✳

Cordelia spotted him by the vending machines two days later, and the same nagging itch she'd been carrying since October flared up all over again.

"Harris," she said, sizing him up, arms already crossing. "You know you've got a real talent for looking exactly like a guy on the news I've been trying not to think about for days now."

"Small world," Xander said, feeding a dollar into the machine, not quite meeting her eyes.

"I'm serious. It's freaky. Same jaw, same everything, same stupid haircut even." She crossed her arms tighter. "You'd tell me, right? If you were secretly some kind of superhero. We're basically friends. Ish. Practically."

"Cordelia, if I were secretly Superman," Xander said, grabbing his soda, working to keep his voice light, "don't you think I'd have a cooler car by now? Because I'm still driving nothing, which seems like a real oversight on Superman's part."

That's a dodge, Harris, and a lame one. Try again.

"It's the only one you're getting."

"You know I'm going to figure it out eventually. I always figure things out eventually."

"Good luck with that."

She studied him a second longer, something in her expression not fully convinced, not fully willing to let it go, and then rolled her eyes and turned on her heel. "Whatever. Weirdo. Enjoy your gas station soda."

He watched her walk away, and only when she turned the corner did he realize his jaw had been clenched the whole time. She never did look fully satisfied. He filed that away next to a dozen other small worries, and didn't know yet what he was going to do about it.

✳

Ethan was waiting for him on Giles's fire escape, a bottle and two glasses set between them on the metal grating. The city spread out below, a scatter of ordinary lights. Ethan poured a measure into one glass, leaving the other empty.

"Sit," Ethan said, and Xander did, settling onto the cold metal steps beside him.

"This a social visit?"

"Not remotely." Ethan poured himself a measure and didn't offer Xander any. "I need you to understand something, and I need you to actually hear it, not simply nod along the way you're rather good at doing when you'd prefer not to think too hard about a thing."

"Okay."

There's a clock ticking. For you, for me, for this whole town. We don't have the luxury of failing, because there is no 'after' if we do. Understand?

"So what do we do?"

"We begin properly." Ethan set his glass down with a small, deliberate click. "I'm not a soldier, whatever you might have gathered from your own future. I can't teach you to throw a punch better than the Colonel already can, and I wouldn't insult either of you by trying. What I can teach you is how to feel magic coming before it arrives, how to ward against it, how to recognize when a spell aimed at you feels wrong before it lands, before it's too late to matter."

"Show me. Now, I mean. Something small."

Ethan considered him a moment, then held out his hand, palm up, a small, flickering light kindling above it, blue-white and cold. "Close your eyes. Tell me when you feel it change."

Xander closed his eyes. For a long moment, there was only the cold. Then a warmth bloomed against his eyelids, faint but distinct, like standing in a patch of sun.

"Now," he said.

"Good," Ethan said, and the light guttered out. "That was benign. Warmth, mostly harmless. Something aimed to actually harm you will feel different. Sharper. It won't be warmth on your skin, it'll be a needle pressing against it. You'll learn to feel the point before it breaks through. You'll learn the difference faster than you think. Everyone does, once their life depends on it."

"That's not exactly reassuring."

"No. It wasn't meant to be."

Ethan reached into his coat and produced a piece of white chalk, a length of red thread, and an iron nail darkened with age. He placed all three on the metal step between them.

"First lesson," he said. "Magic is not a color of light. It is not heat, electricity, or any other tidy force your Captain Carter can measure with a machine. The light was theater. What you felt was intention attached to it."

"Your intention?"

"A very small piece of it. Intention gives magic direction. Form gives it rules. Power makes it happen. Confuse those three and you'll mistake a bright display for a dangerous spell while the quiet one behind you takes your head off."

"Still doing great on the reassuring part."

"I contain multitudes."

Ethan drew a circle no wider than his palm on the step, then bisected it with three short lines. He placed the nail across the center and tied the red thread around it with a knot too complicated for Xander to follow at full speed.

"Watch again," Ethan said.

He untied the thread and repeated the knot slowly. Over, beneath, twice around the nail, then through a loop that appeared where Xander had not expected one.

"What does it do?"

"At the moment, nothing. Chalk is chalk. Thread is thread. Iron is useful because the supernatural world has spent several thousand years agreeing that it ought to be, but agreement alone doesn't make a ward."

He pressed two fingers to the chalk mark and spoke three words Xander did not recognize. The air above the step tightened. Nothing lit up. There was no sound. Xander felt pressure against his skin, faint but unmistakable, as if he had walked into a room where an argument stopped the instant he entered.

"Now touch the nail," Ethan said.

Xander reached for it. His fingers stopped half an inch above the iron.

There was no physical barrier. He knew that as certainly as he knew he could push his hand through the metal stairs if he wanted. Every instinct he had told him not to close the remaining distance.

"That's creepy."

"Good. Creepy keeps one attentive. Push through it. Slowly."

"You just said it was a ward."

"Against casual contact, not force. A locked door and a fortress are both barriers. Learning which stands before you is part of staying alive."

Xander lowered his hand. The pressure sharpened, climbed his wrist, and became a cold ache behind his eyes. He stopped.

"Could I break it?"

"Easily."

"Should I?"

"No."

Xander pulled his hand back. The discomfort vanished immediately.

"Second lesson," Ethan said. "Being capable of breaking a thing is not evidence that breaking it is wise."

"O'Neill's been teaching that one too."

"Then for once the Colonel and I are in complete agreement. A landmark occasion."

Ethan scuffed through one of the chalk lines with his thumb. The pressure collapsed.

"The ward had four anchors," he explained. "The circle defines the boundary. Iron gives the boundary weight. The knot holds the working after I stop feeding it power. The spoken phrase tells it what to resist. Break any one and the ward fails."

"So I look for the chalk."

You look for the anchors. Competent witches don't use chalk. They'll carve a symbol into the mortar of a brick, or tie the ward to a name in a book, or even a memory. Anything that lasts.

"A person?"

"Blood is traditional. Pain is cheap. Love is stronger than either and considerably harder to arrange."

Xander stared at him. "People use love to power spells?"

"People use everything to power spells. Magic is not made moral by the fuel."

Ethan handed him the chalk. "Draw it."

Xander copied the circle and lines, then wrapped the thread around the nail. His first knot came apart. The second held but left the loop on the wrong side. Ethan made him undo it without comment.

On the fourth attempt, the shape matched.

"Now the words," Ethan said, and taught them one syllable at a time.

Xander spoke the phrase. For a moment nothing happened. Then the air trembled and the chalk circle pushed against his awareness, weaker than Ethan's but present.

"I did it."

"You assembled a ward after copying every movement from a man who supplied the power and prepared materials. Try to contain your arrogance."

"You could just say good job."

I could. You'd spend half the time unlearning what I taught you."

But Ethan was smiling when he broke Xander's circle.

"And the other thing? The stuff you actually lived through?"

Ethan was quiet a moment, staring at the space where the light had been. "In the years I remember, I saw two kinds of people die. The ones who rushed in because they couldn't stand the waiting, and the ones who hesitated because they couldn't accept what they'd become. The war got them both. It hardens you, or it breaks you. I'd rather you learned that lesson from me than from it.

"Give me one. A mistake, I mean."

Ethan turned the silver ring on his finger once. "There was a shelter outside Carson City during the first winter. An old mine with one entrance, easily defended. We placed a ward over it that would stop a Goa'uld host, a Jaffa, or anything carrying a symbiote. Forty-three people lived behind it."

"What happened?"

"The ward worked. That was the problem. Everyone began treating it as if it could do more than we had told it to do. They stopped posting a proper watch. A group of human collaborators walked through carrying conventional explosives and collapsed the entrance."

Xander looked at the broken chalk circle between them.

"Forty-three?"

"Thirty-eight dead before we opened another route. Five survived. The people who built the ward blamed themselves because grief prefers a person to a principle. But the ward had done precisely what it was made to do. We failed because we began believing protection against one danger meant safety from every danger."

"So what's the lesson? Never trust magic?"

"Never ask a defense to make decisions for you. Know what it stops. Know what it permits. Keep watch anyway. That applies to magic, military plans, secret identities, and your new skin."

Xander rubbed one thumb across his palm. "You think I'm doing that. Treating this like it makes me safe."

"I think you could. I think everyone around you could. Invulnerability is a very seductive word, especially to frightened people."

"And you?"

"I know better. I helped bury the ones who believed it."

"How bad does it get? For me, I mean. In the version you remember."

Ethan didn't answer right away, turning the glass in his hand. "Worse than I'm willing to tell you tonight. Ask me again closer to March. There's an answer that will help you prepare, and there's one that will just scare you. I'm going to give you the first one, but only when it's time."

"That's not comforting."

"It wasn't meant to be comforting. It was meant to be honest." He looked out over the city a while. "We have time yet. Not as much as either of us would like, not nearly as much as I'd choose if I were given the choosing of it. But some. Enough, perhaps, if we don't waste it."

He turned the plain silver band on his finger, a gesture Xander was beginning to recognize. Some instinct told him not to ask about it, not tonight.

They remained there a while longer in the cold, the city quiet below them, until one of them finally exhaled and neither moved to leave.
