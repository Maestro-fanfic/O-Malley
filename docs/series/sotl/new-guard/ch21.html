Chapter 21

The third request to examine Lieutenant Commander Data arrived before breakfast.

Jenny rejected it before Commander Warren finished reading the routing information.

Warren ticked off the list on his tablet. "The French are sending a robotics specialist—says he can be aboard by eighteen hundred."

"Then their specialist can enjoy a very long flight home," Jenny said without looking up.

"The Japanese proposal is more detailed. It includes a full nondestructive protocol."

"A protocol written by people who have never seen a positronic brain. It's useless."

He sighed, lowering the tablet. "The British are being more diplomatic. They've only asked for copies of our existing scans."

"Which scans?"

"All of them."

Jenny's gaze finally met his, cold and final. "No."

Jenny stared past Warren, her focus settling on the frost tracing the edges of the conference lounge windows. The portable heaters worked hard enough to make the air smell metallic, but they couldn't fight back the Antarctic morning. Half the ceiling was open to the ship's wounded structure, bundled cables hanging like exposed nerves. Three decks away, Data lay just as broken, his eyes closed since recovery. Every government on the ice wanted a piece of him, a piece of her friend. Her own sudden interest in the word no felt less like a strategy and more like a shield.

Elizabeth Weir warmed both hands around a mug of coffee. "They'll argue that the recovery framework gives observers access to significant discoveries."

"Data isn't a discovery."

"I agree. The framework doesn't yet know how to agree."

"Then it can learn. He is a Starfleet officer and a person. Until we have rules that begin there, nobody opens his head because their government sent a well-qualified man on an airplane."

Weir set down her coffee. "I can circulate an interim personhood protocol today. No invasive examination, no allocation as recovered technology, no samples, and no national claim based on who transported him from the wreck. Technical access only by agreement of the medical and engineering custodians."

"And if someone argues he's property?"

"They can make the argument in writing and attach the name of the official willing to explain it publicly when he wakes up."

Warren glanced toward her. "You think he will?"

Weir did not look away from Jenny. "I think our rules should survive the possibility."

Jenny felt some small knot inside her loosen. "Add that no diagnostic can be used to develop a copy or derivative system."

"Without his consent," Carter said.

"Without his informed consent," Jenny corrected. "If we reach a point where he can give it."

Weir made the addition. It wouldn't settle the century-spanning legal questions, but Jenny saw the immediate, practical genius of it. The protocol shifted the burden. Now, anyone who wanted to touch Data had to start by justifying, on paper, why they should be allowed anywhere near him.

Carter looked up from the diagnostic plan spread across the table. "What we're proposing doesn't open anything."

"I know. That's why you're still here."

The corner of Carter's mouth moved. She turned the plan so Jenny could see the final revision.

The test would transmit less energy than an ordinary tricorder scan. It would map passive conductivity across the surviving positronic pathways, never issue an activation command, and stop at the first sign of power accumulation or internal state change. The diagnostic pattern came from Enterprise maintenance files and had been rewritten to avoid every sequence Jenny remembered as a wake command, repair instruction, or cognitive stimulus.

The word landed in him like something he hadn't expected to still feel.

They did not make her qualified.

They made her careful.

"One pass," Jenny said. "Passive map only. No repetition until we've reviewed every trace."

Carter nodded. "Agreed."

Weir said, "I'll make sure the observer log describes it as a safety assessment, not an examination available for replication."

"They'll still ask."

"Then I can develop an interest in the word no."

Jenny looked at her. "It's satisfying."

"I've noticed."

With the protocol agreed upon, there was no reason to wait. They proceeded to the diagnostic bay, arriving just as the ship's chronometer marked nine hundred hours.

Data rested beneath a clear sterile field. Carter took the engineering station. Jenny monitored internal power, temperature, and the neural pathways they could read without crossing the damaged interface. Warren handled the ship's remaining sensors, including the faint return two miles beneath the ice which had persisted through every attempt to classify it.

"Diagnostic field at one percent," Carter said.

"No response from Data," Jenny reported.

The first pattern passed across the positronic matrix.

Lines appeared on Carter's display, branching structures rendered in pale gold. Most ended abruptly at damaged nodes. Others continued deeper than the scan could resolve. No power built. No pathway activated.

"Two percent."

The buried return changed.

Warren leaned closer to his station. "Commander."

Jenny did not look away from Data. "Report."

"Subsurface signal amplitude is up four percent. Interference pattern narrowing."

Carter's hands stopped above the controls without touching them. "Any effect here?"

"Nothing," Jenny said. "Continue at present level. Do not increase."

The diagnostic advanced through its second internal sequence: branch, pause, comparison pulse, branch. Beneath the ice, the faint signal rose, paused, and changed again.

Warren looked from one display to the other.

"It's following us."

"Correlation isn't response," Carter said.

"The rise matched the branch pulse. The pause matched too."

"Shared sensor noise. Power coupling through the hull. An artifact in the restored array."

"Two miles down?"

"I said possibilities, not conclusions."

Jenny watched Data's internal temperature remain steady to a hundredth of a degree. "Complete the current sequence and terminate."

The final comparison pulse crossed Data's silent pathways. The signal below sharpened once, three narrow peaks inside a broader rise.

Carter ended the field.

The buried return dropped to its old ambiguous baseline.

The low hum of the ship’s systems filled the silence. Warren stared at the screen, his knuckles white where he gripped the console. Carter's breath misted in the cold air.

Warren brought the traces together on one screen. The diagnostic sequence appeared above, the subsurface fluctuation below it. They were not identical. They were far too similar for comfort.

"Run it again," he said. "Same power, same pattern. If the lower signal repeats, we can rule out coincidence."

"No," Jenny said.

"Commander, if something down there is reacting to Data's architecture, we need to know."

"We need to know what we just did to Data before we use him to ask it a second question."

"His readings never changed."

"The readings we understand never changed. That's not the same statement."

Carter saved both raw data streams and locked out the diagnostic controls. "She's right. We review first. We also reproduce every possible source of sensor coupling without Data in the circuit."

Warren looked again at the paired traces. "It followed the structure."

"I saw it," Carter said.

"What does that mean?"

Jenny looked at the unmoving gold face beneath the sterile field.

"At the moment, it means we don't do it again."

The isolation alarm sounded before anyone could answer.

The alarm cut through the sterile silence of the diagnostic bay—a sharp, two-tone warning. The first identified Deck Seven. The second was impossible: power on the wrong side of a dead isolation boundary.

They met Ferretti at the emergency turbolift. He wore insulated gloves and held a pulse rifle he couldn't reasonably expect to help, his face grim under the harsh corridor lights. "Junction seven-fourteen," he said without preamble. "Current on the safe side."

"Source?" Jenny asked, her mind already racing through schematics.

"There isn't one."

"There's always a source."

"Then this one didn't file a travel plan," Ferretti bit out. He led them into the corridor, stripped to bare structural ribs. A meter-wide gap, a firebreak carved by recovery teams, was supposed to separate the dead Deck Seven network from the rest of the ship. It wasn't empty anymore.

Something black and fibrous crossed the gap now.

It had grown through an environmental return duct, followed a seam behind the bulkhead, and emerged inside a conduit restored two days earlier to provide heat to an unrelated compartment. Metallic threads wrapped Starfleet cabling in a precise spiral. Tiny green indicators moved under the black surface like light beneath skin.

Carter aimed a scanner at it from behind the marked line. "No life signs. No active drone carrier."

"That make you feel better?" Ferretti asked.

"Not remotely."

Jenny called up the junction plan. "It bypassed seven-fourteen completely. The alarm found current because the new route touches the monitoring ground here."

"So if it hadn't?"

"We would have discovered it when it reached something noisy enough to notice."

Ferretti looked down the safe corridor, where lights and heat had only recently returned. "Every system we fix gives it another road."

"Yes."

"That's a lousy repair strategy."

"It's a worse survival strategy if we stop restoring life support."

They shut down the entire branch. Darkness took the corridor section by section until only the independent work lights remained. The green movement in the adapted cable slowed but did not stop.

"Stored power," Carter said. "Or local generation."

Jenny ordered the technicians back. The portable field generator established a containment plane across the safe end of the conduit. One technician cut out a three-meter section of ordinary cable beyond it. The second sealed both exposed ends inside layered isolation housings.

The Borg growth continued moving until it reached the physical absence.

Then it stopped.

Not died. Stopped.

Carter watched the last green point hold at the severed edge. "It's behaving like automated repair. Find damage, identify an available system, restore connection."

"Assimilation logic without anybody home," Ferretti said.

"Possibly."

"I liked the empty ship better when empty meant empty."

Jenny expanded the isolation boundary by two compartments. It cost them a secondary heat loop, one working replicator junction, and a sensor relay they had spent nineteen hours restoring. She ordered the entire new pathway removed rather than cut at one end.

Ferretti waited until the technicians began before asking, "What do we call this in the report?"

"Active contamination."

"It stopped."

"Because we took away the road. Dormant systems do not build new roads."

"People are going to want a better answer than that."

"So do I. We don't have one."

Behind the isolation field, the last green light remained fixed at the edge of the gap.

Waiting was too human a word for it.

Jenny found no better one.

Ethan placed a silver coin on the library floor and told Xander not to touch it.

"I wasn't going to."

"You were looking at it like a nail."

"It's a coin."

"And already we've established why this lesson may take all evening."

The library was quiet, empty for the day save for the sound of Giles working in his office. The late sun slanted through the high amber windows, catching the dust motes stirred by Ethan's movements.

Ethan had drawn no circle around the coin. There were no candles, bones, bowls of suspicious liquid, or Latin phrases taped helpfully nearby. The coin lay on worn wood and looked capable of purchasing half a cup of bad coffee.

"Tell me what it does," Ethan said.

Xander focused. The coin appeared to be solid silver. It carried a faint trace of Ethan's skin oil, chalk dust, and something herbal from the pocket where he had kept it.

"It's magic."

"Remarkable."

"You asked."

"I asked what it does."

Xander extended his senses carefully. There was pressure around the coin, weaker than the ward Ethan had built on the roof. It did not push outward. It seemed to wait for some condition he could not see.

"It reacts if I cross something."

"Which something?"

"The edge?"

"There is no marked edge."

"That was me noticing the problem."

Ethan crouched and rolled the coin three feet across the floor. The pressure moved with it.

"So the coin carries the spell," Xander said.

"Does it?"

"You just moved it. The pressure moved."

"An object can carry a rule without being the rule. What happens if you melt it?"

"We lose twenty-five cents."

"This is a shilling."

"Then we lose money I can't spend."

Ethan sighed. "The working permits a person carrying the coin to cross a threshold from which that person has been barred. Melt the silver and the permission may fail, transfer to the largest remaining piece, or attach itself to whoever destroyed the carrier. You do not know until you understand how permission was granted."

"So don't eye-blast the suspicious magic thing."

"Not until someone competent has examined it."

"And if the competent person is being eaten?"

"Then make the best poor decision available. The point of learning is to improve the selection."

Xander stepped closer without touching the coin. The pressure gathered against his awareness.

Something else passed through it.

Cold traced the outside of his skin from his shoes to the top of his head, not pain, not contact. For one strange instant the library seemed to measure him. The walls, floor, school records upstairs, and the town beyond them all felt like parts of the same question.

Then it was gone.

Xander flinched back, a sudden, violent movement that sent a deep groan through the floorboard beneath his heel.

Ethan moved at once. Two fingers touched the air over the coin, then the floor, then the wooden edge of the library doors.

"That wasn't yours," Xander said.

"No."

Giles came out of his office. "What happened?"

"Something passed a working across the boundary. Passive, I think."

"You think?" Xander asked.

"Professional confidence is knowing where certainty ends. I know it did not attempt to bind, injure, compel, or alter you. I think it measured."

"Measured what?"

Ethan examined the cracked board, though the damage had been Xander's. "Whether you belong. Whether your condition is maintained by enchantment. Perhaps whether the school recognizes you."

"And the answer?"

"I don't know."

Giles began checking the doors and windows for marks. "Could you trace it?"

"Not from residue this light. Whoever cast it expected someone might notice."

Xander looked at the ordinary walls. "Should I be worried?"

"Yes," Ethan said. "But not because I know what comes next. Be worried because someone is asking a careful question and has not yet shown us why."

In Los Angeles, Evelyn Rook watched a line of ink travel across prepared paper.

The working had touched no blood, taken no power, and left no mark on Alexander Harris. It had only asked the civic boundary to describe what crossed it.

The answer settled into three precise notations.

Permanent physical state.

No sustaining enchantment.

Resident recognized without dispute.

Rook turned past the page labeled REVERSAL. There was nothing to undo. No illusion to strip away, no continuing spell to starve, no foreign shape waiting to collapse.

She opened a clean file and wrote two headings.

ALEXANDER LAVELLE HARRIS: RESIDENT.

SUPERMAN: FOREIGN POWER.

Between them, she left a measured distance of blank paper.

Chegwidden found Fitzwallace studying the photograph behind his desk.

It showed six younger men on the deck of a carrier. Chegwidden was in flight gear, standing slightly apart from the others in a way that suggested he hadn't been invited into the frame so much as claimed a position in it. Fitzwallace had one hand raised, index finger extended, mid-point in what looked like a correction rather than a greeting. The remaining four had the look of men who expected to be somewhere else by dinner.

Fitzwallace turned. "About which thing?"

"Whatever you were arguing."

"History remembers me as persuasive."

"History wasn't trapped on that carrier for eleven weeks."

They shook hands, then abandoned the formality and embraced briefly. Chegwidden closed his office door.

"You look terrible," he said.

"You look like a lawyer."

"That's because I outrank the good tailors. Coffee?"

"If it's improved."

"It hasn't."

Chegwidden poured two cups anyway. Fitzwallace accepted his and moved to the visitor chair without asking. For several minutes they discussed people whose names did not appear in either man's current briefing books. One had retired to Annapolis and become intolerable about sailing. Another had married for the third time and claimed this represented optimism rather than poor target identification. Chegwidden asked after Fitzwallace's wife. Fitzwallace asked after Harm and Mac in terms which suggested he knew more about the admiral's office than the admiral preferred.

"They are excellent officers," Chegwidden said.

"That wasn't the question."

"It was the answer you were getting."

Fitzwallace drank the coffee and grimaced. "Still awful."

"It keeps visits short."

Neither man looked at the sealed folder Fitzwallace had placed on the corner of the desk.

Chegwidden asked, "How long has it been?"

"Since we had dinner? Two years."

"Three. You canceled two years ago."

"The President was trying to prevent a war."

"Presidents are inconsiderate that way."

"You canceled the year before."

"A pilot landed an aircraft where the Navy preferred there not be an aircraft."

"They still let you use pilots?"

"Against my advice."

The ease remained, though it felt thinner. Chegwidden found himself thinking of Rick's funeral last spring—an official notice was the only reason he'd known. They kept moving dinners to a calendar neither of them seemed to control anymore.

Chegwidden glanced once at the folder.

"You didn't cross town to tell me my coffee is bad."

"I might have. Principle matters."

"You would have sent a memo."

Fitzwallace set down his cup. "The President has a mission."

"For JAG?"

"For you. JAG is one reason."

Chegwidden drew the folder toward himself but did not open it until Fitzwallace nodded.

The brief began with maps.

The file started with geography—Sunnydale, California—then devolved into a litany of failures. Chegwidden’s pen paused over a list of hostile contacts, then moved to the section on fatalities hidden inside gang violence and missing-person reports. The category labels kept changing, as if the paper itself couldn't decide what kind of war this was. He kept his face neutral, a habit learned over decades of absorbing shocks.

"Local government compromised?" he asked, not looking up.

"Unknown extent. Assume leaks."

"Standing federal response?"

"None active."

Chegwidden flipped a page. The local response was a paragraph that read like a joke: patrols, a high-school library, a privately funded foreign national. He tapped the page. "This isn't a response team. It's a club."

"It's what they have," Fitzwallace said, his voice flat.

"Research support?"

"Two students and whatever books are in the library."

Chegwidden finally looked up from the folder, his expression hard. "That's unpaid intelligence analysis performed by the same people carrying the field mission. Who do they call when they need real assets?"

Fitzwallace’s silence was the answer. Chegwidden made a sharp note on his pad, the scratch of the pen loud in the quiet office.

Chegwidden turned a page. "Who has tactical authority?"

"The Slayer. Buffy Summers."

"Training?"

"Two years formal under the Watchers Council, active combat before and throughout."

"Prior service?"

"She's been the Slayer since she was fifteen."

Chegwidden stopped reading.

"Current age?"

Fitzwallace met his eyes. "Sixteen. Seventeen in January."

The admiral looked down at the casualty estimates again.

"You're telling me there's an active breach on American soil, and the entire standing response is a seventeen-year-old girl who buys her own bandages?"

"That's what I'm telling you."

Chegwidden closed the folder.

"All right. Start over. This time, tell me everything she doesn't have."

Fitzwallace did.

He described the absence of medicine, wages, logistics, legal protection, relief, records control, intelligence reach-back, and reliable backup. He described weapons stored in a school library and injuries explained to a mother who did not know vampires existed. He described the Oval conversation and Buffy's terms.

Chegwidden interrupted only to expose structure.

Chegwidden held up a hand, interrupting to expose the legal structure. "Does she request missions or accept them?"

"Neither. She identifies threats locally and acts."

"So we're a support element, not a command structure. Who defines a hostile?"

"She does."

"And if our intel sees a threat first?"

"We offer the intelligence. We don't launch our own hunt."

Chegwidden made a note. Jurisdiction. "Immediate mass casualty?"

"Standing exception. Clear and immediate threat."

He drew a box around the note. Boundaries. "Can she refuse personnel? Equipment?"

"Yes. Her call."

"And her pay is not tied to tasking. It's hazard pay, recognizing a condition of service, not a contract for it." He was outlining the charter in his head, a firewall of words. "Good. Now, what about the DRI?"

"Completely outside our chain."

"Good."

"You haven't read their file."

"I don't need to. Any organization that looks at this mission and begins with research instead of casualty reduction starts from the wrong verb."

Fitzwallace allowed himself the smallest smile. "That's why I'm here."

Chegwidden rose and walked to the map on his wall. "A team on the ground isn't a command. People use 'boots on the ground' when they mean the visible end of ten invisible systems. Operators need transport, communications, intelligence, medics, maintenance, supply, legal authority, a watch floor, and somebody awake at three in the morning who can move all of them."

"The President wants full command-and-control."

"Over our people."

"Only our people."

"Support role."

"Only. That does not mean passive. If we see something, we tell her. If we have an option, we offer it. If she asks for help, the answer cannot depend on whether the right clerk is awake."

Chegwidden turned. "If I take this, the charter protects her refusal in writing. Mission expansion comes back through me. No agency borrows the command as a collection platform. No doctor trades treatment for access. No one uses payroll to create an employment relationship she didn't accept."

"Agreed."

"I choose the personnel."

"Agreed."

"And if the White House wants a ceremonial task force that photographs well and cannot move a trauma patient at midnight, find another admiral."

"A.J., I came in person."

Chegwidden looked at his old friend. "Yes, you did."

He returned to the desk and opened a legal pad.

"I want every claimed authority the White House thinks applies, plus the opinions explaining why. I want the Summers file without psychiatric speculation presented as fact. I want the file on the officer you're considering for the advance element."

"Two personnel files," Fitzwallace said.

"I only named one."

"You said you choose the personnel. I brought options."

Fitzwallace removed two slimmer folders from his case.

Chegwidden read the names.

Lieutenant Jordan O'Neil.

Lieutenant Commander Lillian Urgayle.

He looked up. "Where does the President want headquarters?"

"You haven't asked."

"I asked for law and people first. Buildings are easy."

"Coronado."

Chegwidden nodded once. "That'll do."

"Is that a yes?"

"That's a conditional acceptance pending written authority."

"You became a lawyer."

"You noticed."

Fitzwallace stood to leave. Chegwidden came around the desk, stopping him with a hand on his shoulder. For a moment, they were just two old friends, the weight of the mission briefly set aside.

"Dinner," Chegwidden said. "Before another three years."

"You buying?"

"I'm making more coffee."

"Then I'll find a war to prevent."

They shook hands again.

By the time Fitzwallace reached the outer office, Chegwidden was reading the first personnel file.

The map on the library table was a confession laid out in four layers of transparency and three colors of marker.

The base layer showed current streets. Over it she placed an 1899 municipal map Giles had found in the archive, then property lines copied from county records, then the sightings. Red dots marked displaced vampires and other predators. Blue traced Buffy's wrong shortcut and Angel's impossible scent trail.

Every concentration leaned against the same crooked line.

"It isn't a street," Buffy said.

"Not anymore," Willow replied. "It was the western town boundary before the 1911 annexation. The road moved, but the property descriptions kept pieces of it."

Angel studied the marks nearest Laurel Park. "The vampires weren't driven west by something chasing them. They reached the line and chose anything else."

"Choice implies intention," Giles said. "The pressure at the nest may have been a territorial warning."

"It implied a direction," Angel said. "Not a source."

Ethan stood at the end of the table, turning the silver shilling between his fingers. "A rule need not hate you to harm you. The city may be telling them where they are permitted to go."

"Cities don't tell people things," Buffy said.

"This one elected Richard Wilkins. Its judgment is already suspect."

Xander leaned over the map. The old boundary ran past the school, cut behind Restfield Cemetery, and crossed three residential blocks before vanishing under newer development.

He felt the pressure when his finger reached the cemetery.

"There."

Everyone looked at him.

"Same thing as the library. Not as strong."

Giles frowned. "You can sense it through the map?"

"I don't think it's the paper. More like remembering where it was made me notice where it is."

The library phone rang, making them all jump. Giles snatched it up. His posture stiffened as he listened, his free hand grabbing for a pencil. "Restfield," he bit out, scribbling the address. "Trapped, car crash... under attack."

Buffy didn't wait for the rest. She was already at the weapons chest, pulling out stakes.

Willow's eyes flew to the map. She jabbed a finger at the top transparency, her voice tight. "Giles, it's on the line. It's right on the line."

"They're concentrating the effect," Ethan said, his voice grim. "Forcing a response."

Xander felt the boundary half a block before he reached it.

Cold pressure rested against his chest, too broad to be a wall and too exact to be weather. Ahead, a station wagon had jumped the curb and folded its front end around a utility cabinet. Power lines spat blue light across the pavement. A woman and two children were trapped inside. Three vampires circled beyond the arcing cable, flinching from the electricity but not from the people.

"I feel it," Xander said.

"Where?" Buffy asked.

"Everywhere past that mailbox."

A vampire smashed the rear window of the station wagon.

The woman screamed.

"We don't have time to admire it," Buffy said. "Angel, left. I'm going through the yard. Willow, stay behind the corner and mark anything that repeats. Xander, get the car."

He crossed the line at a run.

For six strides the street behaved normally. On the seventh, the air tightened around him. On the eighth, he passed the same blue mailbox again.

Xander stopped.

Buffy had crossed through the yard and reached the nearest vampire. Angel hit the second from behind. The third was still tearing at the broken window.

Xander ran straight toward the car.

Mailbox. Cracked sidewalk. Dead lawn. Mailbox.

He poured on speed, the world dissolving into a gray smear around him.

The block blurred. The route did not. Each burst returned him to the same patch of pavement, momentum folded into destination without impact or turn. He was not bouncing off anything. Nothing resisted him. The city simply completed his movement where it had decided he belonged.

He launched upward.

Stars opened above the rooftops. For one second he cleared the power lines, the trees, the entire affected block.

Then his boots struck pavement beside the blue mailbox.

"What the—" He stopped, shook his head. "No, I don't even know what question to ask."

The vampire at the car pulled the door frame outward. Metal shrieked.

Xander's eyes heated.

There was no target. No wall. No visible field to burn. Heat vision crossed the street and struck exactly what it had been aimed at. It could destroy the mailbox, the pavement, the houses, or the vampire if Xander ever obtained a clear line.

It could not make over there become here.

"Stop pushing it!" Ethan shouted from behind the boundary.

Xander tried once more, faster.

The pressure tightened until the whole block seemed to twist around him. He returned to the mailbox hard enough to crater the asphalt.

"I noticed that part!"

Buffy drove her stake into the first vampire and rolled under the second one's swing. Angel reached the car, caught the vampire by its coat, and threw it away from the window.

"Victims first," Buffy ordered. "Angel, doors."

The electrical cable snapped against the road between them. Angel recoiled from the sudden arc.

The children screamed again.

Willow crouched behind the corner, her gaze darting between the chaos on the street and the map spread on the pavement. "The seal!" she yelled, her voice thin against the shriek of metal. "The mailbox—it has a city seal on it! The storm drain, too! They're...

"Anchors," Ethan finished, his eyes fixed on the unfolding scene. "The spell is using the city's authority as its anchor points."

Xander seized the mailbox post.

"Don't," Ethan snapped.

"It's one mailbox."

"And if breaking it transfers the rule to the person applying force?"

Xander let go.

"Strongly dislike magic."

"A promising beginning."

Giles arrived at a run carrying the archive map and a flashlight. He checked the stamped seal on the mailbox, then the mark cast into the storm drain.

"These are modern. The boundary predates both."

"Utility cabinet," Willow said.

"Installed in 1978."

Angel ripped the bent rear door from the station wagon. Buffy pulled the first child free and carried him through a narrow strip of yard the loop did not control.

The remaining vampires shifted together. They had learned that Xander could not reach them.

"They look happier," he said.

"Fix your geography," Buffy replied, "and I'll fix their attitude."

Willow traced the old line past the modern symbols. "They repeat because they're all city property. The spell is borrowing anything that says Sunnydale owns this spot."

Giles followed Willow's logic, sweeping his flashlight beam across the modern fixtures. "She's right. The spell is using official markers as a conduit." His light caught on the utility cabinet, then traveled higher, searching for something older. He brushed away a layer of soot above the cabinet's frame, revealing a round iron plate nearly swallowed by layers of paint.

"There," he breathed. "A survey marker. Nineteenth century. This isn't just a carrier. This is the original anchor."

The crashed car had driven the cabinet sideways. Live current crawled across the iron housing and the flooded gutter beneath it. The old marker was wedged between the cabinet and a cracked masonry pillar. Any ordinary person trying to reach it would cross both the arcing line and the unstable weight of the vehicle.

"Can I break that one?" Xander asked.

Ethan examined the mark from beyond the line. "No. It has been instructed to hold the boundary while it remains in the city's keeping."

"Meaning?"

Willow saw it first. "Don't break it. Take it. If the city doesn't have it anymore, it can't hold anything."

"Ownership magic," Ethan said. "Appalling, but yes."

Giles pointed to the four old bolts. "Remove it intact. Do not damage the seal."

Buffy drew both vampires away from the car, moving backward through the yard. Angel carried the woman. The live cable fell across the only direct route to the marker.

Xander walked through it.

Blue-white current seized him, turning the air to ozone. His jacket crisped to ash and every fuse in the cabinet blew with a series of sharp cracks, but the voltage found nothing to burn on his skin. Bracing the crushed station wagon with one shoulder, he reached into the charged space between steel and masonry and began turning the rusted bolts, his touch careful enough not to shear them.

The pressure tried to return him to the mailbox.

He did not cross the boundary. He worked from the place it permitted him to occupy, following the rule instead of fighting its destination.

The final bolt came free.

Xander lifted the iron plate from its setting.

"This belongs to me now," he said, his voice cutting through the hum of the city.

The block released him.

"Not defeated," Buffy said.

"I ran around one block until you fixed it."

"We fixed it. You were the only person who could reach the marker without becoming extra-crispy. Willow found the rule. Giles found the right anchor. Ethan stopped you from making it worse. Angel and I got the family out."

"That's a lot of people required for me to cross a street."

"That's a team."

He looked at her.

Buffy glanced toward the uniform hidden in Giles's office. "China proves you need people around you when you can break everything. Tonight proves you need people around you when you can't. Neither one makes you useless."

Xander let that settle.

"Do teams get more cookies?"

Buffy checked the waxed packet. "Team consumed the final cookie."

"Mrs. Landingham would want us to requisition more."

"Mrs. Landingham has jurisdiction," Willow said.

Giles put his glasses back on. "Can we return to the magical assault on the town?"

"I contain multitudes," Buffy said.

Ethan almost smiled, then looked again at the dead survey plate.

"Enjoy the victory," he told them. "You earned it. But understand what it purchased."

"What?" Xander asked.

"The next working will be built for the people you proved yourselves to be tonight."
