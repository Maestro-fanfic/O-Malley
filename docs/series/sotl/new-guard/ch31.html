Countermeasures

Scene 1

Sunnydale High’s HVAC was on the fritz again, which meant the second-floor corridor baked in dry heat while the classrooms stayed refrigerator-cold. Xander Harris sat halfway back in American History, left arm tucked under his desk and jacket sleeve cinched to hide the watch that was not supposed to be a watch. Carter called it a “field monitoring array.” Buffy called it a government handcuff. He mostly just called it “buzzing,” because sometimes it would, and the only way to keep it from waking up the rest of the period was to keep his arm as motionless and low as possible, as if the limb wasn’t attached to him at all.

Today the buzz was new—sharp, high, as if someone had swapped the little motor inside for an electric razor. He glanced down at his wrist, careful to keep the angle below desk height. The display flickered, backlight pulsing in an anemic blue, then flashed three words in crisp red LCD:

CIVIDENTITY INVALID

The next instant, the display went dark. Not “off,” just dead, like a pet turtle you weren’t sure how to check for a pulse. He tapped the case twice—harder than Carter’s briefing recommended. Nothing.

Xander let his arm fall to his side and pressed his palm flat to his thigh. He counted the seconds until the pulse came back; it never did. He stared at the watch until it felt like someone was staring back. Finally, he tugged his sleeve down, tight as it would go, and tried to focus on the board, where Ms. Cavanaugh was diagramming the Missouri Compromise in four dry-erase colors. Not that the Compromise, or its inbred children, ever did a thing for him, but at least it obeyed the rules it was born with.

The bell shrieked, the classroom exhaled, and Xander faked a yawn just to remind his face how normal worked. He merged into the crowd in the hall, stayed close to the wall until the stream reached the admin wing. He ducked into the attendance office, the door’s pneumatic hinge giving him a full two seconds to shed any facial expression before entering the lair of Mrs. Pruitt.

Pruitt had run the front desk since before Xander learned to spell “truancy.” She was heavyset, with a hairdo that changed quarterly but always landed in the taxonomy of helmet. Her glasses looked like they’d been scavenged from a long-dead optometrist, and she wore a lanyard with a laminated photo of her own face, just in case you forgot who she was. She did not so much sit at her post as stake it out, eyes on every angle.

She didn’t bother with the pleasantries. “Late slip?”

“For Willow. She’s in Chem, but—”

“She’s never late.” Pruitt’s hand hovered over the keyboard, the confidence of a Vegas blackjack dealer.

“She got stopped by a guidance appointment. She just needs a pass.” He smiled, but it was thin and gone before she could counter.

Mrs. Pruitt started to type. “Full name?”

He spelled it out: “Rosenberg, W-I-L-L-O-W.”

Her fingers scurried, then stopped. Her nose scrunched. “No record of her missing first period.” She looked up, eyes small and suspicious. “Are you sure this isn’t a ‘friend favor’?”

He shrugged. “Scout’s honor.”

Pruitt’s mouth made a noise between a “tsk” and a snort. She cleared the search, reset the system, then said, “Your full name?”

“Harris, Alexander L.” He watched her screen this time. She pecked in the name, scrolled, and the results pane stayed stubbornly blank. She tried again, typing slower. Same outcome.

“Is your enrollment under a different name?” she asked.

“I’ve been Xander Harris since—” he started, but her eyes were on the monitor, not him.

She frowned, then checked a second system—this one with a yellowed CRT and a double boot-up. “Student ID?” she asked.

He recited it, number for number, not because he cared but because the last three years had drilled the sequence into the shape of his own phone number. She typed it in. Nothing.

She leaned back, the chair groaning in protest, and said, “Weird. You sure you haven’t moved recently?” The question landed with a flatness that made it sound like she didn’t expect an answer, only an excuse.

Xander kept his voice as even as he could: “Lived at the same address for, like, ever.”

“Parents’ names?” she asked. She typed them in, then shook her head. “This is a first.”

He leaned over the counter, careful to respect the boundary line—Pruitt policed that perimeter like it was international airspace. On the monitor, the search field still displayed “HARRIS, ALEXANDER L.” but there was a red error banner at the top: “NO MATCHES FOUND.” The same phrase repeated below, but smaller, as if it might become truer by shrinking.

She squinted at him, then at the monitor, then at him again. “I’ll print you a late slip, but you should have your parent come in with your birth certificate or a utility bill. Sometimes the system drops people. Usually it’s a typo, but sometimes—” She trailed off, and he could see the gears in her head labeling this as “not my problem.”

She clicked print, and a single blank page slid out of the tray. She handed it to him, no apology. He took it, looked at the whiteness, then folded it and slipped it into his pocket.

He said, “That’s my name. Been enrolled here since freshman year.”

She shrugged, arms folding to signal the end of the interaction. “Bring a parent, hon. It’ll sort itself out.”

He let himself out, shutting the door behind him without a backward glance.

In the hallway, the air was thick with disinfectant, layered over with the cheaper body sprays of a hundred students. He walked to his locker—number 342, bottom row. The combo spun true, the latch opened, and the inside was just as he’d left it: two textbooks, one beat-up spiral, the photo of Willow and Buffy at Homecoming. No sign of erasure there.

He stood, hand on the open locker, and tried to piece together whether this was an attack, a prank, or just the universe serving up another round of “Screw You, Alexander.” He checked the watch again, but the screen remained flat as a stone.

He closed the locker, checked both ends of the hall, and started for the library.

Somewhere behind him, the bell rang, but he was already gone.

Scene 2

The library’s main lights were still off, but the low beams above the research table made the whole room look like a nighttime airport lounge—calm, blue, impossible to nap in. Giles was already there, collating a stack of manila folders. Ethan occupied the opposite end of the table, his battered occult meter beside the municipal map they had been annotating for weeks. Both men looked up when Xander entered.

Willow was in her usual quadrant, curled over a section of table with her laptop open and a colored pen tucked behind one ear. She had a legal pad on the surface, but it was mostly scribbled with session times, equipment cross-checks, and, in one corner, a sticky note that read: “More cinnamon for Giles, less nutmeg.” Xander set his backpack down beside her, soft enough not to startle, and dropped into the chair.

He didn’t have to say much. Willow picked up on his mood, glanced at his hands (still trembling slightly from the weird), and closed her notebook mid-sentence.

“I’m not in the system,” Xander said, voice flat. “Carter’s watch glitched and my school record’s a ghost.”

Willow didn’t answer, just started typing. The first click was her unlocking the laptop, the next ten were her chaining through proxies, the next seventy were pure muscle memory. She didn’t look at the screen—her eyes followed the thought instead.

Giles, glasses off and pinched at the bridge of his nose, said, “You’re certain?” Not doubting—just checking the parameters.

“Mrs. Pruitt tried my name and my ID,” Xander said. “Got nothing. She told me to bring in a parent with documentation.” He let the bitterness coat the words, then set it aside.

Willow snapped her gum once (the fake grape kind she’d been nursing all week), then said, “I’ll check the actual database.” She moved her focus from the keyboard to the screen in a single, sliding motion.

A minute later: “I’m in the Sunnydale Unified, admin console. Searching ‘HARRIS, ALEXANDER L.’” She frowned, eyes narrowing. “It’s not a null. It’s not even an inactive record. It’s… not here.” She ran a macro—her own, judging by the speed—and brought up every archived transaction over the last year. “No deletion logs. No audit trail.” She started biting her lower lip, which meant this was less routine and more bad-news.

Giles leaned over the table, hands folded. “Try social?”

Willow nodded, pasted in the nine digits she’d memorized from Xander’s intake form last semester. Nothing. She did it again. “Zero. Like, literal zero.” She tried the home address, reading from her notes.

The address pinged, but the record attached was empty. “Occupant field is blank,” Willow said, tilting the screen to show him. “No resident, no parent or guardian attached. It’s like the house exists, but nobody’s ever lived there.”

Xander stared at the screen, as if expecting a name to reappear out of guilt. It didn’t.

Giles pushed his chair back and paced three meters, then forward again. He removed his glasses, set them on the table, then turned to Xander. “You haven’t changed anything—no enrollment forms, no phone number?” he asked.

“Not unless sleepwalking counts.”

Willow scrolled through the district’s admin logs. “It gets weirder,” she said. “Your parents’ records are still active. Your father’s got two parking tickets in the system, your mother signed you up for junior PE last August. But you—” She swiveled the laptop—“do not exist.”

Ethan leaned over Willow’s shoulder, reading the results without touching the computer. “Localized, or has someone widened the working?”

Willow was already on it, toggling through state-level logins. She hit the California DMV first. “Okay, your dad’s got a valid license, renewal pending in May. Your mom’s expired last year, still active as a non-driver. You?” She checked the database. “Huh. Your learner’s permit application is in ‘pending.’ Status: ‘applicant identity unverified.’ That’s not a normal status.” She tabbed to county birth records, then to the municipal voter roll. “You’re not in the county database,” Willow said. “But your parents are.” She flicked her hand over the trackpad. “This is surgical.”

Xander shrugged. “So, Rook.”

Willow hesitated, then said, “I don’t think it’s a direct hack. If it was, there’d be a backdoor or a ghost trail. This is cleaner. It’s like—someone with admin rights came in, drew a line around you, and wrote, ‘does not exist here’ on the inside. The data is still in the backup, but it’s black-boxed.”

Giles nodded, not in approval but in grim recognition. Ethan had already opened the sigil log to the pages on civic identity and was marking correspondences in the margin. “If the records are paper, can you check them?” Giles asked.

Willow shook her head. “That’s basement archive. Only the office staff can pull that, and not without a principal sign-off.”

“I’ll go look,” Xander said, standing up, but Giles stopped him with a flat, palm-down gesture.

“No,” Giles said. “We need to understand the parameters before we interact with the record directly. If this is Wilkins, it’s possible the effect propagates when someone of interest queries the data in person.” He looked at Willow. “You’re certain the digital access can’t be traced?”

“I’m using a VPN through SGC, with a double bounce off UCSD’s med school. If Rook’s watching, she’s seeing a lot of medical students with unhealthy levels of curiosity.”

Giles allowed the faintest smile. He glanced at the clock on the wall, then at the phone beside his desk. He picked up the receiver, dialed the number for Buffy’s house, and waited.

Two rings, then a woman’s voice, crisp and tired: “Summers residence.”

“Joyce,” Giles said, and explained the situation in exactly three sentences.

There was a silence on the line. “I’ll be there in twenty minutes,” she said, and hung up before Giles could reply.

Giles set the receiver down and looked at the table. Willow was still working the databases; Ethan had the municipal map open beside her; Xander waited with both hands flat on his backpack.

“Don’t touch anything,” he said, eyes locking on Xander. “We’ll wait for Joyce. If Wilkins is behind this, we’ll need to escalate through legal means, not just magical ones.”

Willow started typing again, running a recursive search for anything that might ping the edge of the deletion. Xander stayed seated, hands folded on the backpack, not trusting himself to move or speak.

Outside, the hallway was quiet except for the occasional bang of a locker. The clock ticked off the seconds in even rhythm. The waiting was worse than any monster.

Xander watched the second hand make its slow march, and wondered if time would forget him, too.

Scene 3

Joyce Summers didn’t enter the library; she strode in as if she’d never left campus, a manila folder under one arm and her car keys clicking with each step. She clocked the four of them—Giles, Ethan, Willow, Xander—in one practiced sweep, then set the folder on the table and began pulling out paperwork with the speed of a nurse prepping for triage.

She started with the birth certificate. “Certified copy,” she said, sliding it forward. “I got this for Xander last semester, when his parents forgot to send in the FAFSA form.” She spoke the last part without judgment, as if Xander’s parents were a regional weather phenomenon: sometimes inconvenient, rarely preventable.

Next came the original Sunnydale High enrollment card. The edges were rounded and there was a coffee ring half-shadowing the date, but the counselor’s signature was crisp in blue ink. “That’s freshman intake,” Joyce said, tapping the line with a well-manicured finger. “You can call the counselor, but I doubt she’ll remember three years ago.”

She produced a third sheet: “Recent utility bill, with the Harris address.” The envelope had been sliced open with a letter opener, not a key or a pen tip. She was a person who believed in proper tools.

Willow had her laptop up and camera ready. She angled the webcam and snapped a photo of each document, dragging and dropping them into the case folder she and Ethan had been maintaining since the first boundary test. While she ran the numbers—cross-referencing the birth certificate with county records, the address with the district’s GIS, the counselor’s signature against a scanned yearbook page—Ethan checked each page against the civic sigils in his log. “These are legit,” Willow said. “All the watermarks are there, even the weird fluorescent one the district started using after the city council scandal.”

Giles took the enrollment card and held it under the desk lamp. He traced the embossed seal, then squinted at the ID number in the upper right. “Curious,” he said, passing it back to Willow. “Check the student ID on the current database.”

Willow did, then shook her head. “Doesn’t exist.” She ran a compare against previous-year archives, then even the yearbook digitals. “It’s not a duplication glitch. This number just… isn’t in use.” She slid the card back to Xander, who stared at it like it might morph in his hand.

Joyce pressed her lips into a line, then snapped the folder shut. “I’m going to the records office on Maple. Someone will fix this in person, or at least explain why my paperwork isn’t enough.” She looked at Willow. “You coming?”

Xander started to stand, but Joyce fixed him with a look that belonged on a principal, not a parent. “You stay here,” she said. “If this is what I think, your face in that office is exactly what they want. Willow, with me.”

Willow nodded, grabbed her bag, and followed Joyce without hesitation. They left in a tangle of footsteps and determination, the folder like a baton in a relay.

Giles watched them go, then turned to the shelf behind his desk. He ignored the demonology for once, reaching instead for a narrow, dust-filmed row: “California Administrative Law, 1994 Revision.” He pried out three volumes and lined them up beside Ethan’s open sigil log, then pulled a slim, spiral-bound local government handbook for dessert.

He paged through the index while Ethan traced the reclassification pattern across the map. “The legal manipulation is carrying the working,” Giles said. “The supernatural component is only an accelerant.”

Xander, who had never seen Giles wield anything more dangerous than sarcasm in a meeting, watched as the older man annotated the first three pages, flagged two case studies with torn scraps of yellow legal pad, and started a list on the margin. “You ever sue a city before?” Xander asked, voice lighter than he felt.

“There are more ways to fight than with a stake, Xander,” Giles said. Ethan, still bent over the map, added, “And fewer than Rupert would like to admit that involve filing in triplicate.”

For a moment, the library felt like the safest place in the world. But only for a moment.

Xander watched the legal pad fill with Giles’s precise block capitals and wondered if Wilkins had ever lost a fight like this. He doubted it.

But it was nice to see someone trying.

Scene 4

The Sunnydale Unified School District records office looked like it had been built to resist optimism. The walls were painted in what must have once been “eggshell,” but now had the damp, uneven hue of year-old oatmeal. The only light came from a row of flickering fluorescents and the slow-cycling LED in the corner, which blinked an ambiguous green every five seconds. The air smelled faintly of toner, coffee dregs, and the sort of plastic that only ever exists as part of a municipal infrastructure order.

Joyce took a number from the dispenser—thirty-one—and glanced up at the digital readout. “Now serving: 27.” She sat on a hard chair by the door and opened her folder, reviewing each document as if prepping for a deposition. Willow hovered beside her, eyes on her laptop, fingers busy as they mapped the battle terrain.

At the counter, three clerks worked in slow tandem. One was on the phone, the second methodically sorting a stack of sealed envelopes, and the third (a young man with gelled hair and an allergy to eye contact) moved his mouse in smooth, lazy arcs across his screen.

Number 28, then 29, then 30—each called out with the flat, deadpan tone of an elevator voice, and each customer dispatched in under a minute. When the board reached “thirty-one,” Joyce stood and approached the counter, folder ready.

The clerk—“Delgado,” according to his nameplate—looked up with practiced neutrality. “How can I help you?”

Joyce said, “I need to restore an enrollment record. My neighbor’s son was administratively deleted from the district’s system. I have original documentation.” She placed the folder on the counter, then opened it, arranging the birth certificate, enrollment card, and utility bill in a precise fan.

Delgado did not appear surprised. He typed “Harris, Alexander L.” into the field, then checked the result. “No record on file,” he said. He tried the student ID, then the home address. Nothing.

“That’s why we’re here,” Joyce said, with a touch of steel. “There should be a record. He’s been enrolled continuously since freshman year.”

Delgado leaned in, examined the birth certificate, then the card. “This is all in order. But before I can restore the file, it needs to be compared to the master archive in the basement. That process takes four to six weeks.”

Joyce’s fingers tightened on the counter. “That’s not acceptable. I need it corrected now.”

Delgado shrugged, expression not unkind, but not yielding either. “District policy. The archive is only accessed once a month. If you want to escalate, you can speak to a supervisor.” He gestured at the seating area, where a sign said “Supervisor requests: Wait here.”

Joyce did not wait. “Please page Ms. Tran,” she said, reading the supervisor’s name off the wall behind the desk.

Delgado made the call. Within two minutes, a tall woman with reading glasses on a beaded chain appeared, smoothing the sleeves of her blazer as she walked. She took the folder, read the top page, and scanned the situation in the way a seasoned administrator knows exactly how much drama each case will entail.

Ms. Tran listened to Joyce’s summary, then ran the same search on a back-office terminal. “There is no Harris, Alexander L. in the active roster,” she confirmed. “However, this documentation is standard. It will be forwarded to the Verification Team, and, if validated, the record will be restored at the next archive access.”

Joyce kept her voice calm, but let the anger show in the set of her shoulders. “Four to six weeks, he’s in limbo?”

Ms. Tran nodded. “It’s how the process works.”

Willow, who had been quiet, stood and approached the counter, laptop open. “The California Education Code, Section 49068, says that districts must maintain continuous enrollment records and restore them within five business days of receiving original documentation. Subsection C, part one.” She read the line directly from the screen.

Ms. Tran’s face did not change, but her hand hovered over the folder a half second longer. “That’s the policy,” she said. “But the restoration still has to be approved by our office. We’ll submit this for expedited review.” She took the folder, closed it, and placed it on a tray marked “Action Required.”

Joyce wrote her phone number on a sticky note and pressed it to the front of the folder. She looked at Willow, then back at the clerk. “I want confirmation by Friday.”

Ms. Tran offered a tight, professional smile. “I’ll let you know as soon as we have anything.”

They left through the slow-moving glass door. The world outside was cold but at least slightly less hostile.

On the walk to the car, Willow said, “Three of the four data points I checked were altered inside of a forty-eight hour window. No external breach. This was done by someone with root access.” She shut her laptop and hugged it to her chest. “It’s not even erased—it’s reclassified as ‘non-resident, non-student.’ That’s a category they use for kids who move away or drop out, so it’s legal. More durable than a soft delete.”

Joyce’s hands were steady on the steering wheel, but her jaw was clenched. “We keep the pressure on. If they stonewall, we escalate. Next stop is the County Office.”

Willow nodded. “And I’ll keep digging. Ethan can compare the reclassification timestamps against the anchor log. Maybe there’s a pattern.”

The car started, the heater coughed up a weak plume of warm air, and the two of them sat in silence for a second before Joyce pulled into the street.

From the records office window, a clerk watched them go. He didn’t wave. He just made a note on a legal pad, then turned back to his work.

It wasn’t personal. But it wasn’t going to stop.

Scene 5

Giles and Ethan had claimed the entire main table, turning it into a war zone of legal texts, ruled pads, municipal maps, and sigil logs. Giles had arrayed the law books by topic—education law, administrative procedure, state-level code—while Ethan matched each statute to the branching civic working they had been mapping since the first anchor. Between them lay Giles’s battered spiral notebook, its spine held together with electrical tape and its cover labeled in tiny block letters: “Sunnydale — Bureaucratic Hostilities.”

Buffy had arrived ten minutes earlier, dropped her backpack by the door, and made herself at home sitting cross-legged on the research table. She didn’t touch the papers, but she did shift them around for Giles, spreading the pile for easier access. She was in training mode: hyper-alert, but pretending not to be.

Giles pushed the spiral notebook to the center and cleared his throat. Ethan turned his sigil log to the corresponding page without being asked. “We’ve confirmed it,” Giles said. “What’s been done to Xander isn’t a hack, at least not in the traditional sense.”

Buffy said, “I got that from the ‘bring your parents’ thing.” She glanced at Xander, who had his head down and was picking at the edge of a notebook page.

Giles continued: “It’s a reclassification. A deliberate, lawful reclassification of residency status. There’s no trace of deletion or fraud. Whoever did this worked through every municipal channel—paper, digital, probably even microfiche. Until the school accepts your reinstatement, you are, in the eyes of the district, a non-resident. Which is to say, you’re not here.”

Xander looked up, expression somewhere between “bored” and “planning an arson.” “So, what? I can’t play on the football team?”

Buffy rolled her eyes. “You barely show up for gym as it is.”

He shot her a look, but it was all surface, no venom.

Giles pressed on. “It’s not about the team. It’s about the legitimacy of your physical and, more importantly, metaphysical presence in the town. The law books confirm it: as far as the district is concerned, you are not eligible for services, not bound by student conduct, not protected by their insurance, not a legal actor.”

Buffy looked at the notebook. “And the magical angle?”

Giles nodded. “That’s where it becomes more than a bureaucratic headache.” Ethan tapped the same branching clause in his own notes as Giles opened the older cases. “Wilkins has always used legal facts as the substrate for larger workings,” Giles continued. “Think of the administrative reclassification as the skeleton of a spell. Once the paperwork says you don’t exist, it becomes much easier to implement a supernatural exclusion.”

Buffy frowned. “So it’s not the paperwork, it’s what the paperwork lets him do.”

“Exactly,” Giles said. “He’s making the absence of your legal self the anchor for a spatial working—an exclusion zone, if you like. The precedent is here.” He turned to a flagged page in the notebook, showing a diagram that looked like the world’s least-fun family tree. “He did it with condemned buildings in the sixties. Then with a section of the hospital in the eighties. Anyone not recognized by the bureaucracy just… couldn’t get in.”

Xander let out a low, “So, they’re using my own paperwork against me.”

“More precisely,” Ethan said, “the absence of your paperwork. Same grammar as the boundary contract, just moved inward and made personal. Clever, durable, and terrifyingly dull.”

Buffy set her jaw. “So what’s the counter?”

Ethan slid the sigil log into the center of the table. Several pages were already cross-referenced to Willow’s timestamps and Giles’s municipal cases. “We reinforce what they’ve thinned out,” he said. “Not just the records. The agreement between the records, the town, and Xander himself. We’ve been mapping that relationship since Restfield; this is the same working tightening its grip.”

Giles followed the marked lines with one finger. “A counter-working built from the documents Joyce recovered, the school records Willow traced, and the anchor sequence we already know.”

Buffy caught Xander’s eye. “So the evil paperwork has a sequel.”

Xander shrugged, but there was a tremor in it.

Ethan gave her a thin smile. “Same author. Same bad habits. The useful part of having watched every draft is knowing where he repeats himself.”

Buffy uncrossed her arms. “And you can make the counter-working stick inside the school?”

“We can,” Giles said. “Ethan knows the clause structure, Willow has the record trail, and we have the original documents. Wilkins has made them part of the same system. We may as well use it.”

They sat in silence for a minute, the only sound the rattle of the air vent and the faint, inescapable ticking of the main clock.

Buffy broke the silence: “So, worst case, Xander disappears from school and also, eventually, reality?”

Giles didn’t say “yes,” but he didn’t say “no.”

Xander reached for the spiral notebook, flipped it open, and studied the branching lines of Wilkins’s old cases. “If I just go home and hang out, what happens?”

Giles hesitated. “I don’t know. But I suspect the effect strengthens the longer you remain classified as a non-resident. The paperwork will be difficult to reverse. The metaphysical effects, harder still.”

Buffy said, “So it’s a race.”

Giles nodded.

Xander closed the notebook and set it aside. “If you’re all about to make my existence legally binding, can we order pizza first?”

Buffy smiled. “I’ll get it. But you’re paying.”

It wasn’t much, but it felt like a sliver of normal in a day engineered to erase it.

Giles looked across the table at Ethan, who was already revising the counter-sigil in the margin of Willow’s printout. For once, neither of them needed to argue about the next step.

Scene 6

Ethan cleared a working space with the ruthless economy of someone who had been living out of the same canvas duffel for weeks. The battered notebooks, masking tape, and hard plastic lunchbox with its faded Union Jack decal were already familiar fixtures at the end of Giles’s table; he rearranged them around the documents without ceremony.

He opened the notebook that held the Restfield sequence, flipped past the Packard Street readings, and stopped at the page where the civic contract branched through the school records office. Willow rotated her laptop toward him before he asked, the reclassification timestamps lined up beside his earlier measurements.

“There it is,” Ethan said. The amusement had gone out of his voice. “Same spine as the boundary working. They haven’t invented a new attack; they’ve invoked the next clause.”

Giles nodded. “This time the weapon is administrative rather than physical. Xander’s legal presence has been reclassified.”

Ethan cocked an eyebrow, the smile flickering. “I do love a man who respects tradition.”

Buffy rolled her eyes. “Of course the evil contract has fine print.”

Ethan shrugged. “It’s my brand.”

He asked to see the watch. Xander hesitated for a second, then unfastened the band and slid it across the table. Ethan picked it up, spun the dial with two fingers, and tilted it toward the window, eyes narrowed as if he could read the device’s intention in its reflection.

“It’s not broken,” he said, after a long pause. “It’s being refused.”

Buffy frowned. “Like, security software?”

“Closer to a bouncer at a very exclusive club,” Ethan said. “The working doesn’t jam or block. It recognizes the legal identity of Alexander Harris as a resident of Sunnydale—and then carves out an exception for any government-sanctioned asset, including this little beauty.” He held up the watch. “The field reads the device as ‘federal property’ or, worse, a foreign asset. In the current paperwork, you have no standing here. Your entire house could be on fire, and this would not call in the cavalry.”

Giles braced his elbows on the table. “How robust is the working?”

Ethan shrugged, but his eyes didn’t leave the watch. “Depends on the distribution of anchor points. I’d wager she learned from last time and didn’t keep all her eggs in one file cabinet.”

Willow, who had been taking notes, said, “It’s like a terms-of-service agreement that only applies to him.”

“Exactly that, yes, and considerably less funny,” Ethan said. “The only way to break it outright is to access every anchor. That’s a scavenger hunt even I wouldn’t attempt without hazard pay.” He put the watch down. “But there is a backdoor.”

Buffy crossed her arms. “There always is.”

Ethan opened the second notebook, revealing a sheet of white paper covered edge to edge in his precise, slanting script. “The working depends on the legal and magical congruence of identity. If we reinforce your presence—make your existence so thick, so redundant, that the exclusion clause can’t get purchase—it will exhaust itself trying to push you out. The working’s own logic will gum up the machinery.”

He pulled from his bag a stick of white chalk, a bottle of iron filings, and the sheet of paper. He set them neatly on the table. “We do a ritual. Not flashy. Just a reinforcement. But it has to be layered correctly.”

He glanced at Willow. “Same division as Restfield. You hold the precision work and the living connection; I’ll carry the clause and keep the structure from folding back on us.”

Willow studied the diagram, then shifted closer. “Clockwise again?”

“Clockwise until the third turn, then reverse,” Ethan said. “They changed the return path after Packard Street.”

Xander, dry: “I’ll just be over here, existing.”

Ethan turned the paper toward Willow. “First, chalk a boundary on the floor. Then, four sigils—one on each wall, clockwise, starting from the door.” He pointed at the diagram. “This pattern, exactly. Not an inch off.”

Willow studied the script, then nodded. “I’ll need a ruler and maybe a compass.”

Ethan reached into the bag and produced a plastic protractor and a battered tape measure. “Voila.”

He turned to Xander. “I’ll need three objects: the birth certificate, the enrollment card, and something you’ve carried for at least six months. Something with your print on it.”

Xander checked his pockets, then fished out a battered library card. It had once been blue, but now was mostly gray with the passage of time and too many trips through the laundry. “This work?”

Ethan looked at the card, then at Xander, and nodded. “That’ll do.”

Buffy looked from Ethan’s diagram to Giles’s legal pads. “Do you two ever solve anything without making it look like the world’s worst group project?”

“Not since Oxford,” Giles said. Ethan grinned without looking up.

Ethan grinned, then drew a slow line of chalk along the tile, humming tunelessly. “Give us an hour and a clear room,” he said. “And a fresh legal pad. This is going to get… complicated.”

Buffy nodded and started ushering people out, but Willow lingered, tracing her finger along the first sigil. “We can really do this?”

Ethan smiled, for real this time. “We didn’t get this far by guessing.”

As he sketched the circle, the air in the library changed—more charged, more alive. For the first time that day, Xander felt like maybe the system didn’t get to write the whole story.

Willow went to fetch the ruler. Xander stood by the chalk outline and waited for his existence to become, again, a matter of public record.

It was, he decided, a step up from non-existence.

Scene 7

The back office had become the latest in a chain of improvised workrooms. Stacks of legal pads and loose papers crowded the desk; Willow’s timestamps ran down one wall, Giles’s case notes covered another, and Ethan’s chalk marks connected both to the documents on the floor. A battered desktop computer took a full minute to boot and another three to load a web browser. The single high window threw a sickly rectangle of light across the counter-working they had built together.

Ethan knelt at the north edge of the figure, sleeves rolled, hair in his eyes. The looping shape was the fifth revision of the pattern they had begun at Restfield and corrected after Packard Street—part geometry, part flowing script, every turn keyed to a piece of evidence someone in the room had found. He set the birth certificate, enrollment card, and battered library card at its exact center. Giles checked the final clause from the doorway and gave one short nod.

Willow, shoes off and knees crossed under her, sat opposite Ethan. She flattened her palms on the documents at the point they had marked together, her face set in an expression that reminded Xander of the few times she’d tried meditation and actually succeeded. She was breathing through her nose, slow and steady.

Ethan uncapped the iron filings and tapped them at the first two points around the perimeter. Willow took the bottle from his hand without looking up and completed the sequence, adding the final pinch to the center exactly as they had rehearsed.

Buffy lingered by the outer door, one eye on the corridor and the other scanning for anyone who might need to be staked or dissuaded.

At Giles’s signal, Ethan began the clause, low and rhythmic, and Willow answered at each turn with Xander’s name and the matching record. The older words didn’t sound like Latin, Greek, or any language Xander had heard before. They were polysyllabic, the vowels long and dense, the consonants hard as a notary’s stamp. If language could have an aura, this one radiated bureaucracy—the cold efficiency of forms in triplicate, the grind of “policy” as a living force.

The chalk outline pulsed amber, as if each loop of the sigil caught and reflected the light. It crawled along the line, gaining speed, then arced around the points where the filings rested. Willow’s eyes snapped open. The birth certificate’s ink grew bolder, as if freshly signed; the blue of the school enrollment card deepened, the orphaned student number brightening for a moment before settling into a new, slightly different shade. On the library card, a faint grid of gold lines appeared, interlocked and perfect, then vanished.

Ethan’s voice slowed, then ceased. The air buzzed, not quite an electrical charge but not entirely metaphorical, either. He sat back, exhaled, and wiped a sleeve across his brow.

“That’s it,” Ethan said, voice hoarse. “The old bond is holding, and every record we recovered is reinforcing it. If the exclusion tries again, it will have to fight the whole chain, not one convenient omission.”

Willow kept her hands on the documents for another second, then withdrew them gently. She looked at Ethan, then at Giles, then at Xander. “Is it safe to go outside?” she asked.

Ethan nodded. “Safer, at least. The system may try to run another update, but it’ll find you too sticky to delete.”

Buffy re-entered, relaxed for the first time all day. “Nice glow, guys. Can you do that for my transcripts?”

Willow grinned. “Maybe after finals.”

Xander felt lighter than he had in days. “So, the watch?” He gestured toward the desk, where it sat, blank and useless.

Ethan picked it up, turned it in his palm, then held it to his own chest for a second. The screen flickered once, twice, then glowed a steady but faint blue. Not the error message, just a slow, gentle pulse.

Ethan set it back down. “Not perfect, but functional. You’ll have to recharge it every night, though, and try to keep a low profile with anything government-issued.”

Giles exhaled, tension easing. “The sequence held cleanly.”

Ethan offered a half-bow from the floor. “Weeks of being methodically irritating finally pay off. Wilkins’s current working is clever, but we know its habits now.”

Willow gathered up the cards, careful to preserve the new patterns. “So, this is going to work?”

Ethan shrugged. “It’ll hold. But keep a paper trail, and don’t assume the other side is resting.”

They left the back office, closing the door on the sigil and the scent of chalk and iron. In the main library, Buffy had already set out sodas and opened the pizza box.

Willow settled at her laptop, eyes bright. “I’ll finish scanning the documentation and add the new pattern to our backups.”

Giles looked at Xander, and, for a moment, the air between them was less about records and more about recognition.

They ate pizza, talked about nothing, and waited for the world to try again.

It always did.

Scene 8

It came in the late afternoon, when the sky outside was flat and gray and the only sound in the library was the gentle, regular tapping of Willow’s typing. Giles was in the middle of shelving a new run of periodicals—Scientific American, boxed by month and smelling faintly of glue. He didn’t notice the city clerk until she was standing at the desk, envelope in hand, eyes on the floor.

“Delivery for Mr. Giles,” she said, voice soft but crisp. She didn’t wait for a response, just set the envelope on the blotter and turned to leave. Her shoes made no sound.

Giles eyed the letterhead through the translucent window: “Office of the City Administrator, Sunnydale.” He slit the envelope with a bone folder, unfolded the document, and read it silently. The language was precise, the font smaller thanthe norm. He read it again, more slowly, then set the page on the table.

Buffy crossed the room and read over his shoulder. “That bad?”

Giles shook his head, a reluctant smile curling at the edge of his mouth. “It’s not a threat. It’s not even an accusation.” He turned the paper so Xander could see. “They’re placing the library under ‘administrative review.’ Until they verify the current enrollment of every student user.”

Xander read the paragraph twice, then skimmed the attachment: a single-spaced list of every student in the system. His own name was not there.

“They just cut me out,” he said. There was no heat in it, only the weird relief of a diagnosis finally delivered.

Willow’s typing stopped. “They’re not trying to erase you. They’re trying to make you work for every inch.”

Giles nodded. “It’s the nature of Wilkins’s machine. He doesn’t need to close every door, just enough that we spend all our time opening them again.” He placed a finger on the page, right at the signature: “R. Aldrich, Deputy City Administrator.” Below that, a copy line—“CC: Principal Snyder, Sunnydale High.” The chain of command had never been so neatly summarized.

Buffy read the letter a third time. “So what’s the move?”

Giles straightened. “We escalate within the system. File the appeals. Overwhelm the review process with documentation—Joyce’s, Willow’s, ours. We reinforce the magical protections, but we do not give Wilkins the satisfaction of a direct confrontation.” He looked at Xander. “He can make the world forget you for a day. But we can remind them, over and over, as many times as it takes.”

Ethan, who had been updating the counter-sigil in his notebook, added, “I’ll keep the magical record synchronized with Willow’s copies. Every time they revise the fiction, we answer with the same inconvenient facts.”

Xander smiled, then checked the watch on his wrist. The display was still dim, but it pulsed once—a healthy, regular heartbeat. He reset it, and the glow remained.

Willow was already drafting the response on her laptop. Giles started a letter addressed to “Patricia Hewes, Esq.”—the attorney whose card he had tucked in the back of the administrative law volume. Ethan reopened the anchor log beside Willow’s files, while Buffy propped her feet on the chair and sharpened a pencil, ready for whatever the next wave of paperwork would be.

Outside the library window, a city maintenance truck idled at the curb for a long, silent minute. The driver looked up at the building, expression unreadable, then rolled forward and out of sight.

In the stillness, the world pressed against the glass, but inside the library, the lines were drawn, the names were recorded, and the system—at least for today—had not quite won.

Xander leaned back, the pressure of erasure replaced by a steady hum: not disappearance, but persistence.

They went back to their work, prepared to fight the next form, the next letter, the next invisible line.

Some battles you win with memory.

Some with paperwork.

Some, only with each other.
