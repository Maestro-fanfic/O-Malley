Chapter 22

Xander's knuckles did not look like they had gone through five reinforced walls.

Buffy checked them anyway.

They stood in Giles's office before first period while the secure line connected to Colorado. Xander held the receiver between his shoulder and ear. Buffy turned his right hand over, inspected the unmarked palm, then repeated the process with his left.

"I told you I'm fine."

"You also told us four or five walls."

"Architecture is subjective."

"Skin isn't."

The Chinese cables had scorched his uniform and the electrical traps had blackened the concrete around his feet, but his body kept no mark of it—no cuts, bruises, or burns. His mind, however, kept everything. He'd slept for forty-three minutes, and each time he closed his eyes, the debriefing-room door locked again, the sound echoing until it became the repeating block, trapping him beside the same mailbox forever while children screamed somewhere he couldn't reach.

The school bell would ring in seven minutes.

He had a history quiz in second period.

The telephone clicked. "O'Neil."

"I'm here. Buffy too."

"Good. First, you're not being asked to apologize for leaving the facility."

Xander had prepared for anger. He had prepared for someone to tell him China deserved it. O'Neil offered neither, and that left him without the shield for either conversation.

"Okay."

"Second, wrecking the facility demonstrated why we need to work on what happens after somebody earns your anger. That's not punishment and it's not praise."

"Giles said procedure before anger."

"Giles has a point, even if he says it from behind a wall of tweed."

"He's right here."

Giles looked up from the doorway. "Good morning, Colonel."

"Morning, Giles. The United States owns its part. We handed Xander a location and proof of diplomatic authorization. We did not give him continuous overwatch, independent communications, a safe extraction option, or a person empowered to notice the host government had changed the mission."

"You couldn't have pulled me out," Xander said.

"Maybe not physically. We could have warned you that the departure corridor hadn't changed. We could have told you the convoy was taking you off the agreed route. We could have had aircraft, diplomatic contacts, and a secure landing site ready before you needed them. A guy who can fly still needs someone to notice he's missing."

Buffy released Xander's hands. "And next time somebody knows what happens if the rescue turns into a trap."

"There will be a next time," O'Neil said. "Not necessarily China. Somebody will try again."

Xander looked through the office window at students moving toward class. "So the lesson is never get angry."

"No," Buffy said.

O'Neil said it at almost the same time.

Buffy leaned against Giles's desk. "The anger was earned. That's what makes it useful to anybody trying to control you. They don't have to fake it. They just arrange for you to be completely justified while cameras are running and governments are watching."

"That is disturbingly close to the briefing I have in front of me," O'Neil said.

"I contain multitudes."

"Apparently."

Xander rubbed his eyes. "I don't want Washington deciding if I get to answer a call."

"Not on the table," O'Neil said. "You decide. The review is about what we owe you if you say yes, what you need to know before arrival, and what everyone does if the terms change."

"And if I ignore the plan?"

"Then we adapt. Voluntary doesn't mean unaccountable, but accountable doesn't mean owned. We will probably spend several thousand pages discovering the space between those words."

"Lots and lots of very official paperwork?"

"I knew you'd understand."

The warning bell rang.

Giles said, "Xander still requires sleep."

"He does," O'Neil agreed. "He also requires a review at sixteen hundred local. Secure line. Carter will have the receiver logs."

"I'll be here."

"And Xander?"

"Yeah?"

"Thirty-one people walked out because of you. That happened before any of this, and it still happened after. Don't let them rewrite it."

The line disconnected.

Xander remained still for a moment.

Buffy opened the office door. "Come on. You can have an international crisis after history."

"I didn't study."

"Excellent. A normal crisis."

They made it to class before Snyder found a reason to stop them.

Xander took the quiz with hands that looked completely untouched.

He knew the material. Giles had made him review it on the flight to Washington, and Willow had tested him again before the wildfire call. The first question asked for three causes of the French Revolution.

Xander read it six times.

Each time his mind supplied the sound of locking bars.

He wrote taxation, food scarcity, and inequality. Someone dropped a metal ruler behind him. The sharp crack on the tile was too close to an anchor firing into concrete. Xander flinched, losing four minutes as he stared at the page, his pen frozen in his hand.

Willow glanced over. Xander shook his head before she could ask, finished the remaining answers, and turned in the quiz at the bell.

Snyder waited outside the classroom.

"Mr. Harris."

"Principal Snyder. Always a comfort when authority lurks near an educational exit."

"You left school grounds without authorization during third period on Friday."

Brazil felt as distant as China and equally impossible to explain.

"Family emergency."

"Your family seems to treat international incidents as a hobby. Detention. Today."

"I have an appointment after school."

"Then tomorrow."

Xander accepted the slip. He could demolish a hardened military facility. He could not, however, make detention disappear without explaining to the world why a Sunnydale High student needed to.

Buffy caught up near the stairs and read the yellow paper.

"How's normal crisis?"

"Rescheduled for tomorrow. Very accommodating."

"You okay?"

He looked back at the classroom door. "No metal rulers in the rescue protocol."

She understood anyway. "Add them under lessons learned."

They went to chemistry.

Ambassador Wen returned to the White House carrying more information and less room to use it.

Leo began the meeting by reading the verified sequence into the record.

"At zero nine fourteen local time, the People's Republic requested humanitarian assistance. At ten oh two, Superman entered the authorized corridor. From ten nineteen until fourteen forty-seven, he operated under Chief Engineer Lin Wei's direction. Thirty-one living civilians were recovered. At fifteen twelve, Major Qiao directed him into a military convoy on the false representation that his departure corridor had changed. At fifteen twenty-eight, he entered Facility Seventeen. The capture systems were prepared before his arrival."

Wen did not interrupt.

"At fifteen thirty-one, Colonel Gao ordered him to submit to examination. Sedative gas, electrical restraint cables, mechanical anchors, and layered barriers had already been deployed. At fifteen thirty-four, Superman exited through the western side of the facility. No person was killed. Three suffered injuries requiring treatment. None were struck by him."

Leo closed the report.

"Does your government dispute any part of that sequence?"

"My government disputes the characterization of the facility's purpose."

Elizabeth Weir said, "Then it can provide the construction orders."

Wen looked at her. "Some records concern national security."

"You used humanitarian access to attempt custody of a foreign minor. Invoking national security doesn't change the fact that you tried to abduct him."

"Superman entered as an independent actor of uncertain legal status."

"Xander Harris entered as a sixteen-year-old American citizen invited for a rescue," Bartlet said. "You don't get to change which identity counts after the door locks."

Wen's composure thinned. "A Chinese military installation has been destroyed."

Fitzwallace folded his hands on the table. "If Superman had killed or seriously injured your personnel, this would be a different conversation. The fact that he did not matters. The destroyed gas delivery system, capture rig, restraints, armored vehicles, and blast doors do not make your planned detention disappear. They document it."

"You are asking China to accept destruction of sovereign property without consequence."

"No," Bartlet said. "We're refusing to discuss the broken lock as if nobody built the cage."

Wen opened his folder. Beijing had identified Facility Seventeen as a regional military-intelligence project operating under authority which remained under internal review. Colonel Gao had been relieved pending investigation. Major Qiao and Chief Lin were available for controlled interviews. Raw recordings would be preserved, though access remained disputed.

It was not an admission. It was no longer a denial.

Bartlet made no threat. He asked for the names of every official who approved the containment plan, construction, and use of humanitarian authorization. He required preservation of all records, access to rescue witnesses without military coaching, and written guarantees governing any future request.

"Superman's position should also be clear," Weir said. "He will not refuse future humanitarian assistance merely because the civilians are Chinese. He will not again rely on Chinese authorization as his only protection entering or leaving the country."

Wen read the sentence beneath the sentence. China had failed to frighten him away from its people. It had succeeded in making every future rescue an internationally supervised judgment on Chinese trust.

"Public release of allegations would complicate cooperation," he said.

"Public release of the rescue footage would complicate your alternative," Leo replied.

The footage already existed in too many hands. Children had emerged from the school beneath a red cape. Villagers knew which direction the convoy had taken afterward. An official story that began with attack would have to erase the rescue first.

China needed the scandal contained. So did Washington. Neither need made the event smaller.

Wen closed his folder. "I will convey your requirements."

"Convey the deadline too," Bartlet said.

"Forty-eight hours may not be sufficient."

"It was enough time to build a trap."

The meeting moved behind closed doors and stayed there.

Chegwidden gave Lieutenant Jordan O'Neil and Command Master Chief John James Urgayle the unclassified version first.

It lasted thirty seconds.

"A civilian support mission in Southern California," he said. "Small forward element, full reach-back from Coronado, unusual threat environment, strict limits on independent action."

Urgayle looked at the red-bordered folders on the table. "And the classified version?"

"Vampires are real. So are demons. A sixteen-year-old girl has spent two years providing the primary armed response to both. The President wants her supported, not recruited."

O'Neil held Chegwidden's gaze for a long beat, searching for the punchline. Finding none, she opened the folder. Neither she nor Urgayle wasted time on disbelief; they processed the impossible by turning it into procedure, reading the contact summaries and casualty estimates.

"Who wrote this consent language?" she asked.

"White House counsel."

"It says participation is voluntary. It doesn't say refusal cannot affect protection, medical access, or compensation."

"You're here because you noticed."

O'Neil looked up. "You label a young woman uniquely capable, drop her into a situation no one else can survive, and then tell her every choice is voluntary after you've removed all her other options. That's not a choice. It's a cage."

Chegwidden knew she had. Her own file contained a Senate program, a manufactured test case, and a career nearly reduced to proof that a political experiment had been attempted. It also contained the names of people who learned too late that Jordan O'Neil treated consent as an operational fact rather than a ceremonial signature.

"Fix the language," he said.

Urgayle tapped the field-authority paragraph. "Contact authority?"

"Summers determines Slayer priorities and supernatural field judgment. O'Neil commands Navy personnel."

"If our intelligence finds a threat before she does?"

"Develop the assessment. Offer the intelligence, personnel, equipment, transport, or proposed operation. State risks and basis. She decides."

"If she refuses?"

"We document, reassess, and remain available."

"If she's wrong?"

"Same answer."

"Mass casualties developing?"

"Clear, immediate, and independently reviewable. You may act to prevent them. That exception is not permission to rename uncertainty."

Urgayle nodded once. "Who reviews?"

"Me. Every use."

"Acceptable."

O'Neil turned another page. "No DRI personnel, facilities, records, or tasking."

"Correct."

"Does DRI know?"

"Only that it doesn't own the mission."

"That won't make them less interested."

"No. It makes our recordkeeping more important."

Chegwidden outlined the core of the mission. They would provide medical care that couldn't be used as leverage and secure transport she could trust. They'd be her logistics: supplies, replacement clothing, compensation. They'd handle intelligence, research, and cleanup. Most importantly, they'd provide personnel who understood that a horned civilian wasn't automatically a target.

"Not a SEAL team," O'Neil said.

"A SEAL team may be one tool. The mission is the infrastructure that knows when not to use it."

Urgayle looked at the personnel appendix. "Permission to cut this roster?"

"I expected you to."

"Half these people volunteered because they heard special operations and classified."

"Remove anyone who's here for the prestige. We need people who will do the work, not chase a commendation."

"That'll be more than half."

"Recruit better."

O'Neil closed her folder. "I want her refusal protected in every annex, including finance. No pay per patrol, no bonus per kill, no condition that she accept our equipment or personnel. Medical care cannot require samples unrelated to treatment. Intelligence cannot use threat collection as cover to surveil her friends."

"Put it in writing."

"And if an agency objects?"

"Bring it to me."

"If the White House objects?"

"Bring it to me louder."

Urgayle asked, "Does Summers know our names?"

"Not yet. No one deploys before she is briefed."

"Good."

Chegwidden regarded both officers. "I did not summon you because this is easy to explain. I summoned you because the government has already spent years accepting the work of a child while avoiding the duties which would make the arrangement visible. This command either corrects that failure or becomes its next layer of cover."

O'Neil said, "When do we start?"

Urgayle was already crossing names off the roster.

By fourteen hundred, a secure planning room at Coronado contained representatives from seven disciplines and no agreement about what to call themselves.

Coronado was not a symbolic choice. It put the command close enough to reach Sunnydale by road when aircraft would draw attention, close enough to move a trauma patient quickly, and inside a naval complex capable of supplying operators, communications, medicine, transport, and logistics without inventing each function from nothing. It also kept the flag headquarters outside a town whose police, mayor, and institutions could not yet be trusted.

The forward element would be small because Buffy had asked for support, not occupation. The command behind it would be large enough that small did not mean alone.

Chegwidden solved the problem by removing the proposed unit name from the board.

"You can have an identity when you have a functioning watch floor. Until then, you're a room full of section leads."

The operations commander looked wounded. The finance officer looked relieved.

Chegwidden drew three boxes.

BUFFY SUMMERS: SLAYER PRIORITIES.

O'NEIL: DEPLOYED NAVY PERSONNEL.

CHEGWIDDEN: RESOURCES, CONFLICT, MANDATE.

"These authorities overlap," he said. "They do not consume one another. Summers decides what Slayer work requires. Lieutenant O'Neil commands every person we send. I control the resources and settle disputes. Nobody converts logistical dependence into tactical authority over Summers."

The intelligence lead asked, "Are we permitted to collect inside Sunnydale?"

"On threats and patterns relevant to protection. Not on Summers or her associates as targets."

"Her associates include a vampire."

"Then your analysts will learn to write more precise collection requirements."

O'Neil placed a four-part offer form on the table. "Every proactive offer states the evidence, proposed support, known risks, and her right to decline. If she says no, the file does not quietly become an operation under a different authority."

"Unless mass casualties are imminent," the operations commander said.

"Clear and immediate," Chegwidden replied. "Every use reviewed by me. If you find yourself explaining afterward that casualties might eventually have become possible, you did not meet the exception."

The medical planner had three folders open. "We can establish treatment through a Navy physician under a protected patient number. No ordinary billing, no diagnosis in a shared insurance database, and no reporting outside the compartment except where immediate safety law requires it."

"Parental consent?" O'Neil asked.

"Emergency treatment is manageable. Continuing treatment is not. Her mother currently knows none of this. We can protect confidentiality, but we can't falsify the patient into a legal nonperson every time she needs care."

"What happens if a vampire follows her home?"

The planner paused. "That's operations."

"It's household medicine if her mother becomes the casualty. Does Joyce Summers know not to invite strangers inside? Does she know who to call if her daughter arrives unconscious? Can she consent to evacuation? Can she recognize coercion or a threat using Buffy as leverage?"

The intelligence lead said, "We can establish covert protection."

"Outside the house," O'Neil replied. "You cannot make an uninformed parent follow a plan she has never heard."

Chegwidden looked around the table. "Put the question to Summers. Do not answer it around her. State what we can protect temporarily and what we cannot."

The finance officer cleared his throat. "Hazard pay presents classification and employment problems."

"Solve them."

"We can establish a protected compensation trust under classified civil-defense authority. Initial payment can recognize prior service. Continuing disbursements would be periodic, not task-based. No enlistment, no federal employment classification, no payment per contact or operation."

"Usable when?"

"If counsel approves the authority, forty-eight hours."

"Twenty-four," Chegwidden said.

"Admiral, the account alone requires identity verification and a trustee arrangement for a minor."

"Then identify a lawful trustee who cannot redirect the funds or condition access. Bring Summers options. Do not spend three weeks perfecting a promise while she spends her own money replacing a coat torn on patrol."

Logistics began a list: ordinary clothing in Buffy's sizes, boots, stakes, crossbow bolts, first-aid material, evidence containers, secure lockers, portable lighting, radios, and a petty-cash mechanism which did not require an after-action report for every ruined shirt.

Communications proposed a twenty-four-hour duty number and a small encrypted handset. Transport identified one helicopter package, ground vehicles, and access to fixed-wing medical evacuation. Research requested municipal data feeds, archival support, and a path to Dr. Jackson for ancient-language and archaeological questions.

The Chinese report changed every movement plan.

No responder entered a host-controlled environment without continuous contact. No transport plan depended on the authority requesting assistance. Someone outside the operating area tracked location, expected movement, and changes to the agreed terms. Every extraction package named a person empowered to initiate it.

"Does that include Superman?" communications asked.

"Not under this command," Chegwidden said. "We coordinate if White House or SGC channels request it. We do not annex a global mission because the lesson is useful. Scope changes come through written authority."

He made each section state what it could not yet provide.

Medical had no cleared surgeon in Southern California. Intelligence lacked reliable classification for nonhuman contacts. Logistics could supply conventional equipment but had no validated source for magical weapons. Communications could not guarantee satellite coverage inside known distortions. Transport had no safe landing survey for Sunnydale.

The admissions covered half the board.

"Good," Chegwidden said.

The operations commander looked at him. "Good?"

"Honest gaps can be closed. Ceremonial readiness kills people."

At sixteen ten, O'Neil took the medical question to Sunnydale.

Buffy's coat had gained a six-inch tear under the left arm.

Her shoulder had gained a matching bruise, deep purple around a scrape she had cleaned in the school bathroom. She had completed a geometry worksheet during lunch, forgotten an English response paper in her locker, and slept through enough chemistry to make the teacher ask whether she was ill.

None of it qualified as an emergency.

That was how it kept happening.

Giles examined the shoulder in his office while Willow attempted to repair the coat with a sewing kit which had once belonged to the library's home-economics shelf.

"You should rest it," Giles said.

"I will. While patrolling. Very restful walking."

"The joint may be strained."

"Can still move it."

She proved this and winced only at the top of the motion.

She winced at the top of the motion. Xander looked up from the history book he hadn't absorbed. "That wince says doctor.

"That wince says ice pack. A doctor means forms, questions from Mom, and a cover story for getting hit by a tire iron in a cemetery."

Willow pulled the thread too hard. The coat seam puckered.

"Sorry."

"It's okay. Mom already thinks I ruin clothes recreationally."

Giles's secure telephone rang.

O'Neil introduced herself and asked to speak directly to Buffy. Giles put the call on the small conference speaker Carter had sent.

"Admiral Chegwidden has directed me to brief you before any personnel enter Sunnydale," O'Neil said. "The command is still forming. We have a medical question which affects immediate planning."

"Will it involve needles?"

"Not this one. Who protects your mother if a threat follows you home?"

Buffy stopped moving her shoulder.

O'Neil continued in the same level tone. "Who informs or evacuates her if you're incapacitated? Does she know not to invite an unknown person across the threshold? Can she identify a supernatural threat using you as leverage? If you require emergency treatment, who explains where you are and obtains consent?"

"We keep patrol away from my house."

"Can you guarantee an enemy does?"

Darla had crossed the Summers threshold because Joyce had invited her. Buffy remembered arriving home to find a vampire drinking coffee with her mother, safety breached by ordinary politeness and a rule Joyce had never been allowed to know.

She had always filed that memory under danger to herself.

Now she saw Joyce opening the door after Buffy failed to come home. Joyce answering a telephone call from something which knew Buffy's name. Joyce refusing an evacuation because armed strangers could not explain why. Joyce making medical decisions from a story built to hide the injury instead of treat it.

Her secrecy had not kept Joyce outside the danger.

It had left her blind inside it.

"We can provide discreet observation outside the home for a limited period," O'Neil said. "We can flag immediate threats and protect records. We cannot create an informed safety plan inside a house when the owner doesn't know the danger exists. I'm not telling you what decision to make. I'm telling you the gap."

Buffy looked at the ripped coat in Willow's hands.

"Thank you," she said. "Anything else?"

O'Neil described the command authorities, her right to decline, and the initial communication plan. Buffy asked three questions about surveillance and one about Angel. O'Neil answered each without evasion.

When the call ended, Buffy pulled her coat back on.

"Buffy," Giles began.

"You're coming with me."

"To patrol?"

"Home. I'm telling Mom. You explain vampires, Slayers, and the Council. All of it. You were the adult who knew. So you get to be the adult who explains it."

Giles went pale but reached for his coat.

Willow stood. "Do you want me?"

"Not tonight. She needs to hear me before this becomes a group intervention."

Buffy moved toward the doors.

Xander saw the next problem before Giles did.

He could see it already: Joyce, trying to process vampires and magic. Buffy could punch a wall and Giles could quote his books, but how would they prove this wasn't all just some story? A fantasy cooked up by adults to explain away a troubled girl they couldn't handle?

"She'll need somebody outside the Council," he said.

Buffy stopped.

"Not to tell her for you. Just to confirm it. Somebody she can't write off as part of Giles's secret club."

Giles did not object.

Buffy's face tightened as if the whole decision might collapse if anyone asked her to plan it.

Xander did not ask.

"I know where to get that," he said. "Go."

He was already moving toward Giles's office.

Buffy looked back once. "Xander."

"You do your part. I'll get backup for the impossible part."

She nodded.

Buffy and Giles left through the library doors. Xander changed identities and went through the roof.

Superman arrived over the White House lawn slowly, visibly, and exactly inside the approach lane the Secret Service had given him after his first visit.

That did not make the detail happy.

It made them organized.

Agents moved below him. A radio call reached the roof post. Xander hovered where instructed until an agent opened the cleared landing area, then descended with both hands visible and waited for identity confirmation.

"Unscheduled visit," the lead agent said.

"I know. I need to speak to the President. Personal and urgent, but nobody is under attack."

The distinction caused three additional radio calls. Xander surrendered the active costume receiver for a scan but not removal, repeated his recognition code, and followed the escort inside.

Mrs. Landingham met him before anyone decided which waiting room could safely contain an unexpected Superman.

She looked at his face once.

"You look like you're frightened for someone."

"Buffy. And her mom. Not attack-frightened. Conversation-frightened."

"Does the President know you're coming?"

"No."

"Of course not."

She turned to the agent. "The President has seven minutes before the economic briefing."

"Mrs. Landingham, we need to clear the contact."

"Then clear it while walking."

She did not ask Xander to explain Buffy's life in the corridor. She put him in the small room adjoining the Oval, gave him water, and went through the next door.

Bartlet appeared less than a minute later.

"Is Buffy in immediate danger?"

"No, sir. Neither is Joyce. Not a new danger."

"Then what happened?"

Xander told him about O'Neil's question. How did a support command protect a household when the parent did not know what could follow her daughter home? Buffy had understood the answer before anyone finished asking. She had ended the call, taken Giles, and gone straight home.

"She asked you to come here?" Bartlet asked.

"No."

"Does she know you're here?"

"She knows I said I'd get backup for the impossible part. She didn't stop to plan what that meant."

Bartlet's expression became careful. "Are you helping Buffy, or deciding what her family needs without her?"

The question hit exactly where Xander had known it might.

"I'm not asking you to reveal anything. Buffy's doing the telling. I'm not asking you to tell Joyce how to react, or when to forgive her, or whether Buffy should have told her sooner. I don't get to decide any of that."

"What are you asking?"

"Don't make Buffy prove herself too."

Bartlet waited.

"Joyce has to believe vampires are real. Then that Buffy has powers. Then that Slayer isn't a story Giles put in her head because she's scared or acting out or got in trouble at Hemery. Buffy tried to tell her parents before. They had her committed. If Joyce thinks this is Giles manipulating her, Giles can't prove Giles isn't manipulating her. He's part of the Council."

"And the Council is responsible for the system which left her unsupported."

"Yeah. He knows the facts and he should be there, but Joyce deserves somebody who doesn't need her to trust the Council first."

Bartlet moved toward his desk. The economic briefing had ceased to exist.

"What can we independently confirm?"

"That vampires and demons exist. That Buffy has strength and reflexes normal people don't. That she's fought them, saved people, and stopped things the government now knows were real. That you're building support because what she does is real. That nobody's recruiting her or taking her away."

"We cannot certify the theology."

"No. We can't prove every Council story about one girl in every generation, destiny, sacred calling, any of that. And we can't answer why Buffy lied. That's between them. But we can stop Joyce from having to choose between believing her daughter and believing the entire world she thought she knew."

Bartlet looked at him for a long moment.

"After four days as Superman," Xander said, "I got military doctors, satellites, and a panic button on my wrist. Buffy's been doing this for years, explaining away injuries and buying her own replacement clothes. The government finally admits she's not crazy, and now she has to prove it to her mom all by herself?

"No," Bartlet said.

"I thought maybe a secure call. After Buffy tells her. You confirm the facts, give Joyce a cleared number for questions, maybe send something official she can verify."

"What will she ask first?"

Xander thought of Joyce Summers at the kitchen counter, trying to build order around a daughter whose life kept refusing it.

"She'll want to know if Buffy's telling the truth. If the danger's real. If 'Slayer' is a real thing or just some story Giles made up. She'll ask why no adults helped, and if you're taking over now. And... she's going to ask how a mother doesn't know this about her own kid. You can't answer that one."

"No."

"Neither can Buffy. Not tonight."

Bartlet looked toward the photographs on the credenza. His daughters smiled out from several ages. Xander watched something in the set of Bartlet's jaw go quiet, though his shoulders never dropped.

"A telephone call can authenticate a fact," Bartlet said. "A document can establish authority. Neither can sit across from a mother and tell her the adults in charge knew her child was drowning and cashed the check anyway."

"Who can get there? The Air Force has people in California. Maybe the new Navy command, but Buffy hasn't met them yet. O'Neil could fly from Colorado if we call Hammond."

"I'm going."

Xander waited for the rest of the sentence.

It did not arrive.

"You mean you're calling."

"I mean I'm going to California."

"No."

Bartlet raised his eyebrows.

"Respectfully," Xander added. "Very respectfully. Presidentially respectfully. Still no."

"You came here asking for presidential confirmation."

"I was thinking of the voice kind. The kind that comes over a phone. From inside the building designed to keep you from getting shot."

"Buffy is already telling her."

"Then use the phone. Or a military plane. Tell the Secret Service. Tell Leo. Tell literally anyone whose job includes continuity of government."

"A military flight takes hours."

"The President disappearing out a window takes years off people's lives."

"I won't be disappearing. You'll know where I am."

"I am not the chain of command. I'm not even old enough to vote for the chain of command."

Bartlet took his coat from the stand.

"Sir, presidential consent does not make Superman authorized presidential transportation."

"A sitting President cannot abduct himself."

"Leo is going to have a competing legal theory."

"Leo frequently does."

"There are succession rules. Emergency communications. Nuclear command authority. Airspace. A protection detail that will use words about me which I am too young to hear."

"You listened in the briefings."

"This is not the moment to be proud of me."

Bartlet crossed to the window.

Xander moved in front of it. "At minimum, tell Leo."

"If I tell Leo, Leo tells the detail, the detail tells the Air Force, the Air Force develops a route, California closes roads, and Joyce Summers learns the truth at midnight after six federal agencies have held a scheduling conference."

"That is how not losing the President works."

"Mrs. Landingham," Bartlet called.

She appeared in the doorway.

"The President wants to leave through the window," Xander said.

Mrs. Landingham looked at Bartlet. "Will you be back for the residence call?"

"I expect so."

"Then take your heavier coat."

Xander stared at her.

"You too?"

"No, dear. I think this is a terrible idea. But he has already decided, and California is cold after dark."

Bartlet exchanged coats. On a sheet of presidential stationery he wrote: Leo, urgent family matter in California. With Superman. Back soon. J.

He placed it on the Resolute desk.

"That note will not materially improve anything," Xander said.

"It's informative."

"It's a confession."

Bartlet opened the Oval window.

Xander held a hand against the frame, listening with senses he barely understood. "Three seconds after the guard passes the west colonnade. That's our window. The alarm sensor has a pressure differential—I can shield it, but we have to be moving.

"Mr. President."

"Mr. Harris. A girl who has carried an adult failure for two years is already walking into her house. We can spend tonight obtaining permission to acknowledge it, or we can arrive while acknowledgment matters."

Xander had spent his whole life deferring to men who sounded that sure of themselves, and this man was also the President of the United States.

"Leo is going to kill me."

"He'll have to stand in line."

Rotary and fixed-wing support stayed on call. Every operation required independent departure planning, continuous contact, and an external watch officer empowered to react when terms changed.

"Sunnydale advance element," he said.

O'Neil stood beside a roster reduced from twenty-seven names to nine.

"Initial entry is four people. Me, Master Chief Urgayle, a communications specialist, and a medic. No tactical team until Buffy receives the offer and agrees there is a role. The others establish reach-back here."

Urgayle had chosen field personnel who could work around civilians, adolescents, and unknown nonhumans without treating ambiguity as permission to fire. Two qualified operators had been removed after referring to Sunnydale as a target environment. One physician had been removed after asking when Slayer tissue samples would become available.

"Evidence storage?" Chegwidden asked.

"Temporary secure lockers ready," logistics said. "Permanent site unresolved."

"Cleanup?"

"Protective gear and a contractor cover mechanism ready. No validated procedure for supernatural biological residue."

"Research?"

"Municipal and county requests drafted. Archives require local negotiation. No complete demon taxonomy exists, and sources conflict."

"Good."

The section leads had learned what he meant by then. Gaps stated plainly. No flags planted in uncertainty.

O'Neil asked, "When do we brief Buffy?"

"Today. Before one person enters her town under this mission."

"And the presidential incident?"

"Is not precedent for entry without notice. It is an example we will all work very hard not to repeat."

Chegwidden signed the first standing orders.

At the top, in language no later commander could mistake for a suggestion, the mission began with one verb.

Support.

Joyce did not sleep.

At two in the morning she knocked on Buffy's bedroom door and asked where the stakes were kept. Buffy showed her. At three, Joyce asked whether every invitation counted or only one given at a home. At four, she asked what sunlight did, whether crosses required belief, and why the windows did not have locks strong enough to stop anything Buffy had described.

At five, she asked whether Buffy wanted her there.

Buffy took longer to answer that one.

"I don't know yet," she said. "I want you to know. Those aren't the same thing."

Joyce accepted the answer because accepting it was the first useful thing she could do.

She called the gallery and said she would not be in. She called the school and reported Buffy ill without asking whether a night spent revealing vampires qualified. Then she stood in the kitchen for twenty minutes doing nothing she could afterward account for.

O'Neil called at seven thirty through the secure number left the night before. She explained that Buffy's hazard-pay account had been approved and funded, and that finance needed an adult fiduciary because Buffy was a minor.

Joyce's first response was anger. "You want to pay my daughter to fight monsters."

"No," O'Neil said. "Payment is not per fight. It is compensation for hazardous service she is already performing and has performed without support. It cannot be withheld because she refuses a mission, person, or piece of equipment."

"And you want me to control it."

"The fiduciary can protect the account but cannot redirect the money or condition Buffy's access. You would have legal duties, not ownership. Buffy can choose a cleared independent fiduciary instead."

Joyce looked across the table at her daughter.

The old instinct was to decide which option protected Buffy best, then announce it as a parental fact. After the night they had survived, Joyce heard the danger inside that instinct.

"It's her choice," she said.

Buffy looked up.

"Yes," O'Neil replied. "We'll explain both options at the briefing. The account is usable today either way."

After the call, Buffy touched the temporary card inside the delivery packet as if money could be more impossible than vampires.

"Hazard pay," Joyce said.

"Apparently."

Joyce had always understood 'hazard pay' as a warning. A reason to stop. For the first time, she heard it as a price. An invoice for services already rendered, submitted by the United States government for the piece of her daughter's life they now considered a line item.

Xander arrived at Giles's apartment after sunrise. The Secret Service had released him from questions shortly after midnight, O'Neil had conducted another rescue-protocol interview at one, and Snyder still expected him in school.

Ethan was awake at the small dining table, the old municipal map spread beneath the iron survey marker.

"You look dreadful," he said.

"Apparently abducting a consenting President is tiring."

"A sentence with admirable ambition."

"He says it wasn't abduction."

"Of course he does."

In Los Angeles, Rook began the identity working.

The room contained no photographs of Superman. The public identity was absence, and absence required no sympathetic anchor yet.

Alexander Harris required many.

She set his original school registration beneath a sheet of prepared vellum. His municipal residence record went to the north, birth registration to the east, attendance history to the west, and the 1841 territorial description beneath them all. A line of ink connected household to parcel, parcel to town, town to name.

Rook wrote slowly.

Alexander.

At Giles's table, Xander looked toward the window.

"Did you feel that?"

Ethan was already reaching for chalk.

Rook continued.

Lavelle.

The sensation closed around Xander without touching his skin. It was not cold this time. It felt like standing in a crowd and hearing a stranger speak his name from directly behind him.

He turned.

Nobody was there.

Harris.

The town noticed him.

Not Xander as Buffy knew him, Willow teased him, or Giles had taught him. Not the body wearing Superman's face. It noticed the person on school forms, utility records, maps, and disciplinary reports. Every small official agreement that he belonged here pulled in the same direction.

For one second, he felt more precisely located than he had ever been in his life.

Then the pressure vanished.

"What was that?"

Ethan drew a broken circle around Xander's chair, tested the air, and stood there a moment with his jaw tight.

"Your name has been placed under a rule."

Xander stood. "Where?"

"If I knew, I would have begun with that information."

"Can we track it?"

"Not yet. It came through relationships the city already accepts. Residence, record, perhaps blood. Destroying the object nearest you would be gratifying and useless."

"So what do we do?"

"Identify which facts the working has invoked. Your full name, certainly. Likely your address and the town boundary. We find places where those facts disagree, records that changed, jurisdictions that overlap. Rules built from identity are strongest when everyone accepts the same identity without question."

"Everybody does accept my name."

Ethan gave him a dry look. "You have spent the last week insisting that several billion people call you Superman, while several others absolutely must not. 'Uncomplicated' is not the term I would choose for your present relationship with names.

"Can I stop it by telling people?"

"Do not confuse identifying a tension with prescribing a cure. Disclosure may weaken the distinction, strengthen the public identity, or provide the caster a voluntary declaration she currently lacks. We investigate before you hand a professional sorcerer additional facts."

"And if we can't find the rule?"

"Then we learn what it permits before testing what it resists. Precisely as we practiced."

"What if I find the caster?"

"Then you will remember the caster has studied what happens when you become angry at a structure."

Xander looked down at the map. His street appeared as one thin line among hundreds.

At Wolfram & Hart, the ink dried.

Under ALEXANDER LAVELLE HARRIS, Rook connected the name to a lawful address, a school, a municipal parcel, and uninterrupted residence inside the jurisdiction.

Under SUPERMAN, she placed nothing.

No address.

No birth.

No accepted office.

No lawful claim of residence inside Sunnydale.

In Coronado, Chegwidden's standing orders built authority around the right to refuse. Communications, medicine, money, transport, research, and force were being connected so none could quietly become ownership.

In Los Angeles, Rook used the same tools of definition for the opposite purpose.

She drew the final line between resident and foreign power.

The working recognized Xander.

It did not recognize Superman.

In Sunnydale, Giles spread the revised threat map across the library table. It showed ports, airfields, power corridors, hospitals, and the places where panic would move faster than an army.

“We hold the ground response,” Giles said. “Not because Xander might arrive. Because civilians will be here whether he does or not.”

Willow layered the ward grid over the evacuation routes. Ethan marked the seams where hostile magic could turn a road, a shelter, or a crowd into an anchor. They built plans with Xander, without him, and for when the enemy learned the difference.
