Chapter 17

"One more time," Bartlet said, stopping his pacing to nudge a log in the fireplace with his shoe. An hour before the jet landed, and the air in the Oval Office still felt unsettled. "From the top."

Leo sighed from the couch. "He arrives. We meet. We lay out the options. We let him decide. That's the plan."

"That's not a plan, Leo. It's a prayer with a timetable."

"With respect, Mr. President," Fitzwallace said, "I don't believe there's a version of this where we control the outcome. He's sixteen, he's turned down easier asks than this before, and if he decides to walk out of that room having said no to all of it, no amount of preparation on our part changes that."

"I know that. I'd still like to not look like I'm improvising in front of Doctor Giles and Colonel O'Neill."

"You're the President, sir," Leo said, dry. "You could improvise the Gettysburg Address and half the room would think it was rehearsed."

There was a knock at the door, and Zoey leaned in without waiting to be invited. "Can I sit in?"

"On a classified national security discussion?" Bartlet asked, one eyebrow up.

"On the part where you all figure out how to talk to a teenager without sounding like a committee. Which, respectfully, is not your strong suit, any of you."

"She's not wrong," Fitzwallace said, and Bartlet shot him a look.

"Fine. But you sit quietly, and you don't editorialize in front of the Admiral."

"I make no promises." She dropped into the armchair by the window. "For what it's worth, he's not going to want to feel cornered. If you all sit there listing reasons he should say yes, he's going to dig in just to prove he can. I'd know."

"You'd know," Bartlet repeated, something rueful in it.

"Dad. I invented digging in just to prove I can. It's basically my legacy."

"God help us all," Leo muttered, and Bartlet, despite himself, laughed.

On television, the White House was a symbol. In person, it was just a building, smaller than he'd expected, and that was somehow worse. It made the power inside feel concentrated, pressed into the still flag and the unblinking windows. He felt Giles's hand on his shoulder, a grounding weight.

"You've faced down worse than a building," Giles said, his voice a low murmur.

"It's not the building," Xander said. "It's the expectations inside it."

"Xander." Giles waited until he looked at him. "You've already decided what you're going to say. Whatever happens in there, whatever they lay in front of you, that decision was made before you walked up this driveway."

"Yeah." He breathed out, long and slow. "Yeah, okay."

Giles had flown in that morning. The sight of him, solid and familiar in the echoing hallway, was the first thing all day that made the building feel survivable. The hug was instinctive, a grounding anchor against the pressure in his chest, and neither of them made it brief. When Giles finally pulled back, he kept a hand on Xander's shoulder, his expression doing the talking for him.

"How's Sunnydale? How's Buffy? Willow?"

"Managing. Willow sends her love and a very specific request that you not, in her words, do anything stupid in front of the President."

"No promises."

They were still catching each other up when a voice called out from further down the hall.

"You actually came."

Zoey was leaning against a doorframe like she owned the place, and for a second the whole day lifted clean off him.

"They kind of insisted," he said.

"I heard." She glanced at Giles, assessing, then back at Xander with something warmer. "You've got maybe five minutes before they need you. I made sure of it."

"You made sure of it?"

"I have some pull around here. It's almost like I know a guy who knows a guy." She grinned. "How are you not completely losing it right now?"

"I am completely losing it. I'm just doing it quietly."

"That's the trick," she said. "Nobody can tell the difference from the outside, if you do it right."

"You gonna be around after? Or is this a five-minutes-and-gone kind of visit?"

"I go to school two miles from here, Xander. I'm always around." She bumped his shoulder with hers. "Go do your thing. I'll find you after."

Leo appeared at the far end of the corridor and lifted one hand, a quiet signal that their five minutes were nearly gone. Zoey drew Xander toward the Oval Office doors, leaving Giles and O'Neill several steps behind.

"Doctor," O'Neill said quietly.

Giles heard the change in his voice. "Colonel?"

"When he talks about home," O'Neill began, his voice low, "he means that place? With his parents?"

"Yes. It's his legal residence." Giles's tone was cautious.

O'Neill was quiet for a long moment. "You ever been inside?"

The question was pointed enough that Giles stopped walking. "No. His parents have never been... particularly involved. Why?"

O'Neill's gaze was fixed on Xander. "Medical tests in New York. I saw old scars on his back. A belt buckle. Something that looked like the heel of a boot."

Giles stopped walking.

"You are certain?"

"I've seen enough of them to know. Carter didn't notice. Kid changed fast and kept his back turned."

"Why didn't you say something?"

"Because I had known him for less than a day, he could bench-press a truck, and Major Davis already had three agencies arguing about who was allowed to ask him questions. Pulling him into a child-abuse investigation before I knew whether he trusted any of us didn't look like help."

"And now?"

"Now he's about to turn down protected housing and go back to the same address."

Giles removed his glasses, then seemed to realize there was nothing on them to clean. "I knew his father drank. Xander has made comments over the years, jokes mostly. I should have..."

"Don't do that," O'Neill said, his voice firm. "You didn't know. But you know now. So what's the play?"

"He cannot return to that house," Giles said, the words like chips of ice. "He can stay with me. The logistics... we will manage the logistics. His parents may object, which will require some delicacy."

"Would they?"

"If money becomes involved, almost certainly."

O'Neill nodded once. "The federal offer puts a trust between them and any salary. Saying no means he doesn't get that protection."

"Then we provide another sort. Quietly. Xander is not to discover that choosing his own life means returning to a place where he is harmed."

"Talk to him before you make the arrangement around him," O'Neill said. "Whole point of today."

Giles put his glasses back on. "Yes. Quite."

"And call me if quiet doesn't work."

"What would you do?"

"Haven't decided yet." O'Neill looked toward Xander again. "Probably something loud and obnoxious. Possibly violent."

Giles understood the restraint beneath the answer. "I will speak to him when we return."

"Good."

Xander was still smiling when a brisk voice cut through the hallway.

"There you are." Mrs. Landingham approached carrying a small plate covered by a folded napkin. She looked past Xander first, taking in Giles, O'Neill, and the others. "The President can wait two minutes. Every one of you except Zoey looks as though you've been living on military coffee, and Zoey knows better than to admit what she lives on."

"I eat," Zoey said.

"Coffee is not a meal, dear. Neither is whatever you buy from that machine in the dormitory."

She uncovered the plate. Two oatmeal cookies rested beneath the napkin.

"Take one," she told Xander.

"I'm not really hungry."

"That was not the question."

He took one.

"Good. Now stand up straight." She set the plate on the table beside the doors and had his collar straightened before he'd registered that she'd moved. "You look like you're about to be sick, and the President does not need to spend his first impression of you wondering whether he ought to fetch a bucket."

"Yes, ma'am." His heels had already come together by the time his brain caught up.

"Better. Eat the cookie."

Xander obeyed. It was still warm.

"Mrs. Landingham," Leo called from inside the Oval Office, "are you delaying the meeting?"

"Yes," she called back. "You may tell the President I said so."

The door remained closed.

"He won't mind?" Xander asked.

"He will mind terribly. He'll survive."

She studied him for a moment, and the humor in her expression settled into something quieter.

"Every person in that room has spent several days reading about Superman," she said. "They have photographs, reports, estimates, and folders thick enough to stop a door. Most of them have not spent five minutes speaking to Xander Harris. Do not let the folders persuade you that they know you better than you know yourself."

"They're the President and the Chairman of the Joint Chiefs."

"Yes. Very impressive titles. They still put their shoes on one at a time."

Zoey leaned closer. "You should hear what she says when there aren't guests around."

"You should remember that I know your class schedule."

Zoey straightened.

Mrs. Landingham turned back to Xander. "They are going to talk about duty. They should. Duty matters. Service matters. But adults sometimes become so impressed by a young person's courage that they begin treating it as permission to spend that courage for him."

Something changed in Zoey's face. She looked down.

"I had two sons," Mrs. Landingham continued. "Andrew and Simon. They served in Vietnam, and I was proud of them every day of their lives. Andrew died in March. Simon made it home, and then died the following spring of something the doctors couldn't quite name, though I always thought I could. People said many fine things afterward about honor and sacrifice. Most of those things were true. None of them made my boys less dead."

Xander stopped holding the cookie halfway to his mouth.

"I'm sorry."

"So am I." Her voice remained steady. "I would never tell them that service wasn't worth doing. I would tell them that the choice had to remain theirs, because they were the ones who would pay for it. Whatever is said in that room, Mr. Harris, you are the one who has to live the life they are discussing. Listen carefully. Be respectful. Then answer the question they actually ask, not the one you think important men expect you to answer."

"What if the answer's no?"

"Then say no clearly enough that nobody mistakes it for uncertainty. President Bartlet argues when he respects someone. If he challenges you, it does not mean you have failed. It means he expects your answer to stand on its own feet."

"Did Zoey tell you what I was going to say?"

"Zoey did not need to. You have the expression of a young man who has made a difficult decision and is afraid adults will explain him out of it. I've seen it before."

She turned to Zoey, her manner changing without losing its weight. "And you. Behave yourself in there."

"I always behave myself."

"You have never once behaved yourself, dear, and we both know it, and your father knows it too." Mrs. Landingham smoothed Zoey's hair back, fondness softening her face. "Being silent for ten consecutive minutes will not cause you permanent injury."

"Has anyone tested that?"

"We'll consider today an experiment."

She retrieved the second cookie and put it into Zoey's hand, then looked at the group waiting behind them.

"Colonel. Doctor Giles. The President is ready. Captain Carter, Doctor Jackson, you'll find the Admiral inside. And a word of warning: any mud on that carpet becomes your problem before it becomes his.

"Yes, ma'am," O'Neill said.

Mrs. Landingham nodded, satisfied.

Xander looked at the door. It was polished wood, brass handle, nothing remarkable about it except what waited beyond. The cookie was warm in his hand, and the room no longer felt like a place where only other people understood the rules.

"Go on," Mrs. Landingham said, gentler now. "He's waiting."

She opened the door herself.

"Mr. Harris." President Bartlet came around the desk himself rather than waiting behind it, hand already out. "Thank you for coming."

"Uh. Thank you for having me. Sir. Mr. President." He shook the offered hand, aware of every eye in the room: Leo, Fitzwallace, O'Neill, Carter, Daniel, Giles settling into a chair near the back, Zoey slipping quietly into a seat by the window.

"Before we get to any of the business people are apparently very eager to discuss," Bartlet said, "I heard what happened in Georgetown." He glanced, briefly, at his daughter, something complicated passing between them. "You didn't have to do what you did. You could have let it happen and walked away and no one would have ever known you'd been there. Thank you."

"She's, uh." Xander's ears went hot. "Good person to have coffee with, sir. Wasn't really a decision, more just reflex."

"No," Bartlet said, something almost fond in it. "I don't imagine it was a decision at all."

Leo cleared his throat, gently steering the room. "Mr. President, if I may."

"Right." Bartlet gestured them all toward the seating area, and once everyone had settled, folded his hands. "I'll be straightforward with you, because I think you've earned that much. We asked you here because it's time to decide what happens next. Not what you're capable of. What you actually want."

Leo slid a folder across the polished table. "New York has a formal offer. Salary, housing, security detail, a new life as their official hero." Xander pictured it—a high-rise apartment, bodyguards instead of friends. The thought was so cold it almost made him laugh. "School?" he asked. "Tutors," Leo said, not unkindly. "It assumes full-time availability." "And my parents?" The question felt heavy in the quiet room. Leo glanced at his folder. "As a minor, their approval is an issue. Both New York and the federal proposals suggest a trust, but the legal ground is untested.

"So my parents get told I'm Superman."

"Not necessarily," Bartlet said. "They would have to know you were being offered some form of government work. We could protect the classified details."

"They'd want the money."

The words came out before Xander could stop them. Giles went still behind him. O'Neill's expression did not change at all, which somehow told Xander he had heard more in the answer than anyone else.

"The trust would prevent anyone from taking your salary," Leo said carefully.

"That wouldn't stop them from trying."

Bartlet closed the New York folder. "Then we count that as a problem with the offer, not a problem you have to solve for us."

Fitzwallace placed a second folder on the table. "The federal option avoids some of those complications. You would receive a security clearance, military training without enlistment, access to the best medical and scientific personnel available, and a direct role in preparing for the threat you warned us about."

"The invasion," Xander said. The word was not new; Ethan's letter had put it in the room days ago. What was new was that the government had a date, a threat assessment, and a plan that required his answer.

"March sixth. One hundred and eighteen days from today."

The date did not land as a revelation. It landed as a deadline: the point by which every argument about training, secrecy, and responsibility would have to become an operational decision.

"We do not know the enemy's order of battle," the Admiral continued. "We do not know where they will strike, what they will bring, or whether the changes already made have altered their plan. You are the only defensive asset on Earth we have reason to believe can engage their ships directly and survive. That makes you a valuable bonus to the response, not the response itself. The baseline plan must protect cities and contest the invasion without depending on you."

"Admiral," Giles said, warning in his voice.

"He deserves the truth."

"He deserves not to have the deaths of entire cities placed on his shoulders while you ask him to sign an employment agreement."

"They're already on his shoulders whether I say it politely or not."

O'Neill moved away from the wall. "No, they're on ours. All of ours. The kid can help. That doesn't make him the whole defense plan."

"At present, Colonel, he is the only part of the defense plan capable of reaching an attacking vessel without a launch vehicle."

"Then we need a better plan."

Fitzwallace looked ready to answer. Bartlet raised one hand and the argument stopped before it could take over the meeting.

"Colonel," Bartlet said, "tell him what the Stargate offer means without selling it."

O'Neill looked at Xander. "It means training. A lot of it. It means Carter keeps finding new and interesting ways to see what you can do, and I teach you what to do when being stronger than everybody else doesn't solve the problem. It means you see other planets. Some of them are beautiful. Some of them try to kill you before lunch."

"Jack," Daniel said.

"He said without selling it. It means orders, even if you're technically a civilian. Briefings you can't repeat to Buffy or Willow. Missions where we don't know what's waiting on the other side and sometimes don't know how we're getting home. It means people depending on you, and you depending on them, and neither side gets to disappear because they had a bad morning."

"Would I be on SG-1?"

"Not immediately. You're untrained and you're sixteen. I'd want you with us when you started going offworld, but not as a full team member until you knew the work and Hammond agreed you were ready."

"And school?"

"Correspondence courses, tutors, something like that. You could fly home when you were off duty. You wouldn't be living there most of the time."

Xander looked down at his hands. "So New York wants Superman full time, and the SGC wants Xander full time."

"That's one way of putting it," Leo said.

"Is it wrong?"

Nobody answered immediately.

"We've discussed a federal arrangement as well," Fitzwallace added. "Broader scope. Working directly with the Stargate program on a formal basis, offworld missions included, full military resources behind you."

"That one comes with school even less," O'Neill said, dry. "Just so you're picturing it accurately."

The Russians and the Chinese have also indicated interest," Leo continued, his tone matter-of-fact. "Nothing formal, but it's real. We wanted you to hear it from us first.

"Would I even be allowed to take one of those?"

"Allowed is a complicated word," Bartlet said. "You're not an American citizen as far as the public record is concerned. The public believes Superman is an extraterrestrial whose legal status nobody has settled. Privately, you are Alexander Harris, born in California, which makes you an American minor. If another country offered you employment in your public identity, we'd have several departments arguing over which fact matters more."

"And while the departments argued," Fitzwallace said, "Russia or China would gain access to everything we currently know about you. That would be a national security concern."

"So allowed means no."

"It means the United States would object," Bartlet said. "I'm not going to dress that up for you. It also means I would strongly advise against placing yourself under the protection of a government whose promises you have no reliable means of enforcing. But this meeting is supposed to give you choices, not conceal the ones we dislike."

"And DC Comics would like an ongoing relationship," Bartlet added, with the ghost of a smile. "Licensing, a movie, some very earnest young men who kept using the word synergy. Considerably lower stakes than the rest of it, but it's on the list."

"Do they own the name?"

"They own a fictional character who looks like you, dresses like you, and appears to have supplied your current abilities," Leo said. "Whether that gives them a claim against a real person is another question no court has ever answered. Their representatives have been cooperative. They want an agreement badly enough that cooperation is in their interest."

"If I don't agree?"

"Then we ask them not to sue Superman," Bartlet said. "I would enjoy listening to their attorneys explain that complaint to a judge, but I would prefer not to make you live through it."

"The costume itself creates complications," Leo added. "Merchandising, public appearances, use of the symbol. Sam is working through it with their people. There is probably a reasonable arrangement available that doesn't employ you or expose your identity. It does not have to be decided today."

Xander sat very still, aware of every single person in the room waiting on him. "That's... a lot."

"It is," Bartlet agreed. "Take whatever time you need. Nobody in this room is going anywhere."

"I don't need time," Xander said, and heard his own voice come out steadier than he felt. "I already know what I want."

The room went quiet in a way that felt different from before, more attentive, more braced.

"I don't want any of it," he said, the words coming out in a rush. "Not New York, not the federal job, none of it. I appreciate the offer, I really do. But I'm sixteen." He looked from Bartlet to Giles, trying to make them understand. "I have a junior year to finish. I have friends. If I let this become my whole life, I'm going to lose the part of me that's just... Xander. I want to help because I can, not because it's my job.

"Xander..." Fitzwallace started, real frustration in it. "Do you understand what you're turning down? The resources, the strategic..."

"I understand," Xander said, quieter now, but not backing down. "I'm not saying no to helping. I'm saying no to being owned by it. There's a difference, and I need you to actually hear that, because I'm not going to change my mind."

"Employment is not ownership," Fitzwallace said. "It is obligation, accountability, and support. If a city puts its resources behind you, it has a right to know when you will respond. If the SGC trains you and entrusts you with classified intelligence, it has a right to expect you to use that training when lives are at stake."

"I know. That's why I'm saying no before I take the support."

"You intend to train yourself?"

"No. I want to keep working with Carter. I want O'Neill to teach me. I want to know everything you know about the Goa'uld before March. I just don't want agreeing to learn from you to mean you decide where I live for the rest of high school."

"Training requires resources. People, facilities, classified equipment. You are asking the government to supply all of that while accepting no obligation in return."

Xander's face went red. "Then don't. If that's the price, I'll figure something else out."

"Percy," Bartlet said.

"No, Mr President. This is the point everyone is moving around because they like him. So do I, as far as I've come to know him. That does not answer the question. What happens when he decides a school dance matters more than an emergency deployment? What happens when two cities need him and he chooses the wrong one? Who reviews that decision? Who tells the families why the most powerful person on Earth was somewhere else?"

The room had gone painfully quiet.

"I don't know," Xander said.

Fitzwallace had expected resistance. The answer stopped him.

"I don't know how I'm supposed to choose," Xander continued. "I don't know how Superman in the comics chooses. Maybe the writers only give him one disaster at a time. But signing one of those folders doesn't fix it. It just means somebody else chooses for me, and what happens when they choose wrong? Who reviews them?"

"Congress," Leo said. "The courts. The electorate. Imperfectly, but there are systems."

"Systems that think I'm an alien and can't decide if I'm allowed to have a bank account."

"Also true," Leo admitted.

Xander looked toward Giles, then O'Neill. "In Sunnydale, when something happens, we help because we're there. Buffy doesn't get a salary. Giles barely gets thanked. Willow and me don't even get that most of the time. We still show up."

"Sunnydale is one town," Fitzwallace said. "You can cross the planet in less than an hour."

"That doesn't make every bad thing on the planet my fault."

Saying it seemed to cost him. Giles leaned forward, but Xander continued before he could speak.

"I've been thinking about it since the plane," he said, his voice dropping. "Every news report, every fire, every car crash. I keep picturing the faces, wondering if I could have gotten there in time. If I let every one of them become my responsibility, I'll never sleep again. I'll never be able to just sit in class or hang out with my friends without that clock ticking in my head. I can't live like that. Maybe that's selfish.

"It sounds human," Daniel said.

"He's not human," Fitzwallace replied. The words, spoken with strategic coldness, landed like stones in the quiet room.

O'Neill came away from the wall.

"He is sixteen years old," Giles said, each word precise. "Whatever happened to his body, whatever name the newspapers have given him, that has not changed."

"I was speaking strategically."

"He will hear it personally."

"I did," Xander said.

Fitzwallace looked at him. "Then I apologize. That was badly said."

"Okay."

"It does not remove the strategic problem."

"No," Xander agreed. "But making me your employee doesn't make me less sixteen either. It just gives me a uniform to be sixteen in."

Zoey covered a smile with one hand. Bartlet noticed and almost did the same.

"Suppose you go home," Bartlet said. "Monday morning, you return to school. At noon the White House learns a Goa'uld vessel has entered the solar system. What happens?"

"You call me."

"You don't have a telephone we can secure."

"Then we figure that part out."

"Suppose New York has another aircraft emergency while you're taking an exam."

"If I know about it, I leave the exam."

"A warehouse fire?"

"How bad?"

"You don't know."

Xander opened his mouth, then closed it.

"That's the job," Bartlet said. "Not lifting the heavy thing. Deciding which call deserves an answer when you cannot answer all of them. The federal proposal gives you people to make those decisions with you. That is its strongest argument, and I don't want you refusing without understanding it."

"I understand." Xander looked around the room. "But if I take the job because I'm scared to make the decisions, then I'm letting the job decide who I'm allowed to help. New York first, or America first, or whatever mission the SGC has. People outside that line still get hurt. There isn't an offer here that fixes what you're asking me."

Bartlet looked at him for a long moment, then nodded, the motion slow and deliberate. "No," he said softly. "There isn't.

"I want advice. I want people I can call when I don't know. I don't want somebody else owning the answer before the question happens."

"And March sixth?" Fitzwallace asked.

"I train for it. Every day if that's what it takes. I learn what O'Neill and Carter know. I help with the Enterprise if Jenny needs me. When the invasion comes, I fight. You don't need to pay me to make me care whether Earth gets invaded."

"No," O'Neill said. "We really don't."

Fitzwallace pressed his tongue against the inside of his cheek, held it there a moment, then leaned back like a man filing an objection for the record rather than expecting to win it.

"Good for you," Bartlet said, and there was nothing but genuine respect in it. "I mean that."

Leo was already nodding, pragmatic, three steps ahead. Giles wore an expression close to pride, unguarded in a way he almost never let himself be. Zoey, from the window seat, just met Xander's eyes and smiled, small and real.

"There's a practical problem, though," O'Neill said, stepping forward from the wall. "Whether you're on anybody's payroll or not, the world already knows your face. If your name and hometown get attached to it, if some reporter connects the dots one afternoon, your life as Xander Harris is over. No junior year, no best friends, no normal anything, ever again."

"I know. I don't know how to fix that."

I do." O'Neill straightened up from the wall. "Xander Harris stays off the books. His name, his life here—it all stays insulated. In exchange," he looked at Xander, "we operate on trust. When we have a genuine, world-ending emergency, we call. You answer. Not a contract. A favor bank.

"And how, precisely, do we contact a man with no listed address, no phone we can call, whose entire arrangement depends on nobody knowing who he is?" Fitzwallace asked, not unkindly, more like he actually wanted the mechanics worked out before he'd sign off on the idea at all.

"A pager," Carter said.

Fitzwallace looked at her. "A pager."

We give him a hardened receiver. It works on a dedicated, encrypted military band, one-way only. We send a coded burst via satellite, and it tells him what to do: call us, come in, or contact you. Anyone else who intercepts it just gets noise. No reply signal, no way to track it to him.

"Can you build it?"

I can modify existing field equipment in a few days. It won't be invisible to signals intelligence, but it won't be identifiable, either. Not unless they already know exactly who they're looking for.

"Who can activate it?" Fitzwallace asked.

"General Hammond and Leo," O'Neill said. "Two people. Both know what it costs if they use it for something that isn't real."

"That's two people who can summon Superman at will."

"No," O'Neill said. "That's two people who can ask him to call."

"And if I say no?" Xander asked. "If the sound goes off and I don't come?"

"Then you don't come," O'Neill said, simple. "That's the whole point of a favor bank over a contract. Nobody docks your pay. Nobody comes looking for you. We'd just know you had a reason."

Fitzwallace considered that a long moment, then nodded, once, grudging. "Get me a working prototype before he leaves for Sunnydale, Captain, and I'll withdraw my objection."

"Yes sir," Carter said, and Xander could tell from how still she'd gone that the engineering was already turning over in her head, the way it always did once a problem had actually caught her interest.

"How's a favor bank different from an employment contract, in the end?" Fitzwallace asked O'Neill anyway, once the mechanics were settled, an old argument he clearly wasn't quite ready to let go of.

"Because nobody's cutting him a check," O'Neill said. "Because he can say no, and mean it, and nothing happens to him for saying it. Try telling an employee they're allowed to say no sometime, Admiral, see how that conversation goes over."

"And if he says no every time we ask?"

"Then he says no every time we ask, and we live with it, because that's what trust actually costs." O'Neill shrugged. "I don't think he will. I've watched him say yes to plenty of things nobody made him say yes to. But that's his call, not ours, and it stays his call."

Bartlet considered that for a long moment, looked to Leo, who nodded once, decisively, and to Fitzwallace, who took visibly longer but got there, jaw working once more before he finally, grudgingly, inclined his head.

"Then that's the arrangement," Bartlet said. "Superman's identity is classified, effective today. Not a leak. Not an accident of circumstance. A decision, made by the people in this room, starting right now." He paused, wry. "Well, this is one of the things DC got right back in '78. Put the secret where everyone can see it."

"Sir?" Carter asked, missing the reference.

"The movie," Bartlet said, as if that explained everything.

"DC Comics themselves, we'll circle back on separately," Leo added. "That's not today's business, and frankly, given the identity situation, it's going to look different than it did an hour ago."

"Today's business is done." Bartlet stood, and the room stood with him. "Mr. Harris. Xander." He offered his hand again, and this time Xander shook it without his ears going hot. "Go finish your junior year."

Down the hall, in a bullpen that had been quietly bracing for news all morning, Leo delivered the verdict in about four sentences and watched it land five different ways at once.

"He said no," CJ repeated, already halfway to her feet, papers sliding off her lap unnoticed. "To all of it?"

"All of it."

"So how exactly am I supposed to explain to the press corps that Superman met the President of the United States and walked away with nothing? That's not a briefing, Leo, that's a minefield."

"You're not explaining anything. There was no formal offer accepted, there's no story beyond 'the President met with a private citizen,' and that's all anyone outside this building needs to know."

"That's not a strategy, Leo, that's a hope." But she was already pacing, already building something behind her eyes.

"The press knows he was in Antarctica," CJ continued. "They know military aircraft left New York, they know the President has been discussing him with foreign governments, and by tonight somebody will have seen an unusual Air Force arrival at Andrews. If I say he was merely a private citizen dropping by, Danny Concannon will ask what kind of private citizen crosses three continents between appointments."

Then we stonewall," Leo said. "No confirmation, no denial. Nothing.

"I don't have to confirm it. The question still exists. So does New York's offer, which the Mayor's office has been leaking around the edges since they wrote it. When Superman reappears in California instead of New York, every reporter in the country will ask whether he rejected the city, rejected us, or decided to work for somebody else."

Josh picked up one of the pages that had slipped from her lap. "We say he isn't working for any government."

"Then the follow-up is who he does answer to."

"Himself," Donna said.

CJ stopped pacing. "Morally, that's the right answer. Politically, it's terrifying. 'Answers to himself' makes people picture a god leveling a city because he got a parking ticket. That's the lead for every hostile editorial tomorrow.

"A man who can lift an aircraft answers to the federal government scares a different group of people," Sam said. "Probably more of them."

"Which is why I need a sentence that says independent without saying uncontrolled."

Toby looked away from the window. "Volunteer."

Everyone turned toward him.

"He's an independent volunteer," Toby said. "He cooperates with emergency services and public authorities when he chooses. He has accepted no salary, no office, and no government position. You're not saying he answers to nobody. You're saying nobody owns him."

"I can use volunteer," CJ said. "Volunteer sounds civic."

"It also happens to be true," Leo said.

"Those are my favorite kinds of answers. They hold up better on the second question."

Mandy frowned. "Except everyone knows his face. If he's going back to public school, how long before somebody recognizes him?"

The room quieted.

"That part does not leave this room," Leo said.

"I understand classified, Leo. I'm asking how classification stops a sixteen-year-old with a newspaper from looking at the photograph and looking across homeroom."

"It doesn't," Sam said. "The identity decision prevents official confirmation. It keeps federal records, military records, and investigative resources from doing the work for them. It doesn't make his face different."

"So rumors happen," Josh said. "A hundred kids in a hundred towns claim Superman sits behind them in algebra. Without confirmation, most of them stay rumors."

Donna shook her head. "Until someone gets a photograph."

"Or a yearbook," CJ said. "Or talks to a teacher."

Leo's expression hardened. "Then we protect the records we can protect, and we do not build a federal operation around intimidating high school students. The arrangement gives him a chance, not a guarantee."

"Does he understand that?" Mandy asked.

"Colonel O'Neill explained it in the room."

"Explaining risk and living with it are different things."

"Yes," Leo said. "They are."

CJ wrote volunteer at the top of a clean page and underlined it twice. Beneath that she wrote no confirmation of residence, citizenship, education, or family, then drew a line separating those facts from everything she could safely say.

"I can hold this for a day," she said. "Maybe two. After that I need photographs of him cooperating with someone who isn't wearing an American uniform. Fire department, rescue workers, something ordinary. If the only images the public sees are Superman beside Air Force officers and a starship, independent volunteer won't survive the pictures."

"We'll ask him," Leo said.

"Ask," CJ repeated. "Good. We're learning already."

Toby returned his attention to the window. "I had one shot at that kid, and I spent it listing off ways he could die. Nice to know it worked out fine without me in the room."

"Toby..."

"I'm not looking for sympathy. Just an observation."

"For what it's worth," Sam offered, looking up from a legal pad, "if his identity's staying classified, that actually simplifies the DC Comics conversation considerably. Fewer moving parts to protect, fewer people who need to know anything real."

"Small mercies," Josh said, and then, glancing sideways: "You met him, right? In New York?"

"Briefly," Donna said, not looking up from her own notes.

"And?"

"He seemed like a nice kid who'd rather be doing literally anything else than what was happening to him. Turns out he agreed with himself. Good for him."

"Huh." Josh looked at her a beat too long. "Smarter than the rest of us, deciding that at sixteen."

"Considerably," Donna said, and didn't quite hide her smile when Josh caught her eye.

"Are you two gonna do that thing where you pretend you're not doing a thing," CJ said, not looking up from her pad, "or should I just leave the room now."

"There's no thing," Josh said.

"There's a thing," Donna said, at the exact same time, and Josh looked personally betrayed.

Mandy stared, processing the implications. "So that's really it. We're sending the most powerful being on the planet back to tenth-grade geometry. That's the official plan.

"That's the whole plan," CJ said, already moving toward her office, heels clicking fast. "God help me, that's the whole plan, and somehow I have to make it sound like the sanest thing that's happened all week."

Xander found Zoey again on his way out, leaning against the same wall she'd found him against an hour ago, arms crossed the same way, like she'd just been waiting for him to loop back around.

"So," she said. "You told the President of the United States no."

"I told the President of the United States no." He blinked. "I did indeed do that thing."

I genuinely cannot tell if what you just did required nerve or a complete absence of self-preservation instinct, and I say that having witnessed some truly spectacular lapses in judgment in this building.

"I contain multitudes." He rubbed the back of his neck. "You're not gonna tell me I should've taken one of the deals?"

"God, no. I've spent my whole life being handled by people who mean well, and I wouldn't wish that on anybody who actually had a way out of it. You had a door. You used it."

"That obvious, huh."

"Takes one to know one." She glanced past him, to where an agent was already drifting closer. "I've gotta go. But hey." She pulled out the receipt, worn soft now at the creases. "You actually kept that."

"Course I did."

"Use it sometime. I mean it. I'm not going anywhere, and neither, apparently, are you."

"I will."

"You know what the weirdest part of today was?" she said, a little quieter. "Watching you turn down literally everything, and knowing exactly why you did it, better than anyone else in that room could."

"Yeah?"

"Yeah. Nobody in there's ever had to earn the right to be a normal person. I have. You have now too, I guess." She shrugged, like the observation cost her something to say out loud. "Anyway. Go be normal. I'll call you when I get bored of being handled."

"I'll answer."

The agent's hand found her elbow, gentle, familiar. She looked back once before she turned the corner, a small wave, and then she was gone.

He stood there a while in the quiet hallway, turning the whole day over, the White House and the refusal and the strange, mixed thing sitting in his chest that wasn't quite relief and wasn't quite grief either. O'Neill's group was already talking logistics somewhere behind him, low voices, Antarctica and jets and schedules. Giles was waiting by the door, patient as ever, already thinking, Xander guessed, about Sunnydale, about home.

He wasn't sure yet what he felt, not entirely. He just knew the decision was made, and it was his, entirely his, and for the first time since Halloween, that felt like enough all on its own.

✳

The communications workshop at Andrews had three clocks on the wall, two of which disagreed, and a workbench covered in enough dismantled electronics to make Xander wonder whether Carter had been given a room or had conquered one.

She had shed her jacket and rolled her sleeves to the elbows. An ordinary black sports watch lay open beneath a magnifying lamp beside the stripped casing of a military pager, a coil of wire finer than hair, and several circuit boards that looked too large to fit inside either one.

"You said a pager," Xander said.

"I was describing the function, not the housing." Carter picked up the watch and held it against his wrist. "A pager creates questions at school. This doesn't. Do you normally wear a watch?"

"I owned one until last Tuesday."

"What happened last Tuesday?"

"I found out normal watchbands aren't rated for going from zero to really fast before lunch."

Carter looked at the broken band he had brought from his satchel, then at him. "How fast?"

"Fast enough that I didn't find the watch afterward."

"That's not a unit."

"It's the unit I have."

She gave him a look that promised more testing later and returned to the workbench. "The receiver is simple. Making it small, rugged, and uninteresting is the harder part. This casing is sold in half the sporting-goods stores in the country. The band is layered ballistic weave around a flexible steel core. It should survive normal use, water immersion, and more acceleration than the original. I make no promises about your top speed until you let me measure it."

"And it still tells time?"

"It would be a conspicuous watch if it didn't."

"Good point."

Xander leaned closer. The face looked completely ordinary: digital time, date, two small buttons on either side, no antenna and no glowing military label. "Where do the messages show up?"

"They don't."

He looked at her.

Carter lifted a vibration motor hardly larger than a shirt button. "A display creates readable information. You only need three instructions, so the receiver gives you three patterns. Nobody looking over your shoulder learns anything."

"And nobody in algebra asks why my watch says REPORT TO SECRET MOUNTAIN."

"That too."

She connected the exposed motor to a testing lead and pressed a switch. It gave one long pulse.

"Call," she said. Another switch produced two short pulses followed by a long one. "Come to the SGC." The third produced three even pulses. "Contact the White House. One instance means respond when it's safely practical."

She keyed the first pattern again. One long pulse, a short pause, then the same pulse twice more.

"The complete pattern three times means the request is time-sensitive," Carter said. "Disengage from what you're doing and make contact immediately if you can do so safely."

"Same three messages. Two levels of hurry."

"Exactly."

Xander held the motor against the inside of his wrist while she ran them again in a different order.

"White House. Call. Mountain."

"Good."

"Can whoever sends it tell where I am?"

"No. Receiver only. No transmitter, no acknowledgment signal, no location hardware. The satellite sends the same encrypted burst across a broad footprint. Your receivers recognize their address and vibrate. They never answer."

"Receivers?"

Carter reached past the watch and picked up a second object, a flat black module about the size of two stacked quarters. A narrow clip ran across its back.

"You can't wear the same watch in both identities," she said. "Especially not in a high school. Someone with a sharp eye for accessories will eventually compare photographs. This module is keyed to the same address and clips inside the costume. It stays concealed. Wear the watch as yourself, carry this in the uniform."

Xander stared at the two devices. "You thought of that already."

"Colonel O'Neill used the phrase 'secret identity catastrophe.' I used more specific terminology."

"His is catchier."

"Mine was actionable."

She handed him the module. He clipped it inside his waistband and lowered his shirt over it. Nothing showed.

Carter keyed the first test again. One long vibration pressed against his side.

"Call," he said.

Two short pulses and a long one followed.

"SGC."

Then three even pulses.

"White House."

"The devices are independent," Carter said. "You can disable either one without disabling the other. If you're changing identities, you switch off the one you're leaving behind and activate the one you're carrying."

"Can I really turn them off?"

Carter paused for less than a second, but he saw the engineering part of her object before the rest of her caught up.

"Yes." She turned the watch over and pointed to a recessed switch beside the battery cover, then showed him the matching switch on the flat module. "You'll need a pen tip or paper clip, so you can't hit it by accident. Off means off. The device won't log a missed signal, and we won't know whether you received it."

"Fitzwallace is gonna love that."

"Admiral Fitzwallace approved a device that asks you to make contact. A tracking beacon would violate the arrangement he approved. So would an alert you couldn't disable."

Xander studied her face. "You already had that argument, didn't you?"

"Colonel O'Neill had most of it. I supplied diagrams."

"Lots and lots of very official diagrams?"

"Seven."

"Terrifying."

The door opened and O'Neill came through with a tray holding three styrofoam cups of coffee and a packet of crackers. He set one near Carter, surveyed the dismantled watch components, and nodded. "Making progress on the secret decoder ring?

"Watch," Xander corrected.

"Signal watch," O'Neill said. "Very traditional. Jimmy Olsen would be proud."

"Jimmy Olsen used his to call Superman."

"Right. So whatever you do, don't loan it to the school newspaper."

Carter ignored them both and fitted the vibration motor into the watch casing. "The patterns are alerts, not orders. Neither device can compel you to call, and neither can report whether you chose not to."

"I know."

"I want the design to make that clear."

That quieted him.

On the workbench, the parts didn't look like much yet: two ways to reach him anywhere, neither able to tell anyone where he was, a line to people who might need him that still left the decision entirely his.

"What if it's an emergency and I miss the pattern?" he asked.

"Then they send it again," Carter said. "If you still miss it, they handle the emergency without you. The system cannot be designed around the assumption that you'll always be available. That's how an option turns into a dependency."

O'Neill took a drink of coffee. "And how a favor bank turns into a duty roster."

Carter closed the watch casing and tightened four screws. She passed it to Xander, then reached for a small transmitter connected to the bench equipment.

"Put it on."

The reinforced band felt heavier than the old one but looked no different once he fastened it. The face displayed 4:17 and the wrong date. Carter keyed in a test burst.

Three steady pulses touched the inside of his wrist.

"White House," he said.

She sent another.

One long pulse.

"Call."

Two short, one long.

"SGC."

Carter disconnected the transmitter. "Prototype accepted?"

Xander turned his wrist beneath the light. It looked like something a kid might have bought at a mall. The second receiver had already vanished beneath his shirt. No seal, no visible serial number, no indication that two of the most secure facilities on Earth could reach either device through a military satellite.

"Yeah," he said. "They look normal."

"That's the idea."

He corrected the date and time, then tucked the watch beneath his sleeve. The flat receiver went into the small parts case Carter had prepared for the costume.

They were still requests. Now they were requests he could carry home without either identity giving away the other.

✳

The first joint planning packet arrived before Xander had finished testing the receiver. It was not an employment contract and it was not a request to pretend the invasion had become someone else’s problem. The baseline plan assumed Xander was unavailable: Carter’s technical work, O’Neill’s field doctrine, Navy logistics, White House continuity, allied coordination, and the Sunnydale team’s authority over the ground situation. His participation was listed separately as a possible advantage.

Xander read the page twice. “They actually wrote down who decides what.”

“That was the point of telling them,” O’Neill said. “If the information stays in one small group, the plan stays in one small group. If the plan fails, everybody discovers the failure at the same time.”

Carter tapped the communications section. “Your consent is explicit. You can decline a task. But if you accept one, the receiving teams will know what support you were promised, what boundaries apply, and who is responsible for the next decision.”

Xander looked at the watch, then at the flat receiver beside it. The devices were still small enough to hide. The response behind them was not.

“Okay,” he said. “That sounds less like being summoned and more like being included.”

“It is,” Carter said. “That is the change.”
