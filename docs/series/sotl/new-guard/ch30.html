The Guardian

Scene 1

The drill went through on the first pass, but not with the cheap thrill of a breaking bottle—more like a failed tooth extraction. The corer ground through four meters of ice, then stuttered and screamed as the bit found the seam of non-terrestrial alloy: a matte, black curve, too geometric for geology, too precise for anything that ever froze on this continent by accident. At -48 Celsius, nothing was supposed to smell, but the moment the breach flexed, a gout of metallic air shot outward and snapped the first two rows of floodlights with an audible pop.

O’Neill, first in the shaft, muttered, “That’s new,” but nobody had time to process it. Carter was already pressing forward, shoulder to shoulder with Teal’c, and Daniel was close enough behind that the ice fog from their breath mixed, white and gray. Jenny Calendar, not technically on the team but seconded by the President himself, tracked the sensor display over Carter’s arm, her own hands clenching and unclenching, never steady for more than half a second.

Carter’s primary sense was the pain behind her left ear—the way any proper stress migraine started—but today the world arrived in slices: the whiplash cold, the ozone tingle of the powered glyphs coming online, the wash of Data’s diagnostic echo as it rippled through her jury-rigged array and told her, in no uncertain terms, that this was the right place.

The shaft widened as they entered the vault. The wall was a patchwork of repeating triangles, each the size of a coffin lid, each edge inlaid with a strand of something that looked like fiber optic but glowed at the interface with a low, deliberate blue. The first row of glyphs to Carter’s left flickered twice, then steadied, their logic already resolving into a syntax that made her teeth ache—digital, yes, but written in a grammar that reminded her of nothing so much as Daniel’s index cards.

Daniel, for his part, had one of those index cards already out, along with a stub pencil and a flashlight that he didn’t bother pointing anywhere but at the wall. He started muttering, “Repeating pattern… not Sumerian… oh, that’s interesting,” and Carter wanted to hit him for it, but she let him be.

The breach wasn’t a doorway so much as an invitation to claustrophobia. The space just inside was big enough for two—maybe three—people to stand abreast, but every line of sight ended in mirrored dark. The floor was not a floor at all, just more of the matte alloy, less slick than the outside but with a thermal that sucked the heat right through the boots.

O’Neill, still at point, swept the P90’s taclight in a slow arc. “If anyone’s home, they know we’re here,” he said, tone pitched for ‘muttering to the room’ rather than ‘giving orders.’

Teal’c stepped in, back to the side, weapon cradled not so much at ready as in a kind of coiled patience. He moved with the discipline of a man for whom nothing about this was especially weird, just a Tuesday with a new dialect.

The pressure differential settled after a minute. With the floodlights dead, the vault’s own logic took over: every wall segment lit up in sequence, like a child’s toy or a runway for something that did not want to fly. Jenny, eyes glued to the diagnostic, said, “There’s a heartbeat. The sequence is pacing to Data’s output.” Her breath hung in the cold, but her voice was clean and flat, no vibrato.

Carter’s wristwatch ticked against her glove, a single short haptic pulse. The one-way link to Xander. She let the vibration ground her, then bent to check the array’s output: the vault was not just echoing the surface signal, it was mimicking it, shaping its own reply in a slightly distorted but unmistakably familiar pattern.

The team moved forward, careful as surgeons. Teal’c led with his own intuition—“This way,” he said, and was always right by two meters. O’Neill kept a hand to the wall, the other on the weapon, and Daniel scribbled as he walked, never once tripping over his own boots. Carter tracked every voltage spike, every thermal micro-shift, logging as fast as the array could buffer. Jenny drifted behind her, a ghostly second shadow.

At the third corridor bend, the vault opened up. They could see the breach itself, still steaming in the -48C air, rimmed in a ring of glacial frost that had been flash-melted and then instantly re-frozen. The wall opposite the breach was concave, a parabola with its focal point about six meters back into the structure. The sense of being watched was immediate and total—Carter could feel her skin’s electric field prickling, as if every cell was shouting for her to run.

It was Daniel who stopped first, voice barely above a whisper. “Something’s—”

The rest was lost as the guardian stepped into the corridor from nowhere at all.

There was no flicker, no cinematic fade-in. The figure was simply there: a column of blue-white logic, roughly humanoid, but scaled up—at least 2.5 meters from heel to helmet. Its ‘face’ was a cluster of overlapping geometric planes, and where its eyes should be there was a void, a cutout against the brightness that seemed to bend the light into itself.

The wings unfolded with a subsonic hum, the kind of noise that made bones vibrate but left the ears wanting. Two arcs, one from each shoulder, swept out and back, then settled at a forty-five degree angle behind the guardian’s frame. The wings were not solid, but a lattice of photonic grid and dark between—each segment micro-diffracting the vault’s light into a halo that danced across the wall.

For a second, nobody moved.

Carter’s sensor array registered a spike: positronic signature, nearly Starfleet grade, but with a delta she couldn’t explain. Her head spun with the analogies—“Like Data, but not Data. Like his language was taught to a different species and then spoken back with a new accent.”

The guardian’s voice arrived not from its mouth but from the air itself, as if the chamber had learned to speak. Each word was deliberate, no wasted bandwidth.

“This repository is sealed under the authority of those who came before. State your designation and the nature of your claim.”

O’Neill’s weapon did not move, but his stance shifted—alert, but no longer expecting a firefight. He said, “Jack O’Neill. United States Air Force. Our claim is that we dug two miles down, so—” He trailed off, because the guardian did not acknowledge the humor.

Instead, the guardian’s targeting reticle—visible, a soft blue line that bisected each member’s chest for exactly one heartbeat—paused on Carter’s sensor. Then on Jenny. Then again on Carter. Each time, the wing arcs behind it flexed, the resonance in the air tightening.

Carter risked a glance at Jenny. “It’s not just watching,” she said, voice low. “It’s reading the sensor logic. And maybe us.”

Jenny’s lips pressed flat. “If it’s reading, it can hear us.”

Daniel, unable to help himself, stepped forward, index card in hand. “The wall glyphs,” he said, “they’re in sequence. They’re not a warning, they’re a process log. It’s not telling us to stay out. It’s outlining a protocol for entry.”

Teal’c, who had not flinched since the guardian appeared, adjusted his grip and said, “This architecture is unfamiliar. But I have seen similar containment constructs among the oldest worlds of the System Lords. They are always traps, but sometimes also doors.”

O’Neill gave a tiny nod. “Then let’s not knock the wrong way.”

The air between them and the guardian shimmered, and for an instant Carter thought it was about to attack. Instead, it spoke again. “Designation registered. Protocol requires demonstration of comprehension. Advance, but do not breach the secondary interface. Failure to comply will be met with resistance calibrated to match origin.”

Daniel’s eyes widened, as if he’d just been handed the answer sheet to an exam he was born to fail. “It’s going to test us.”

O’Neill’s shoulders relaxed, a millimeter, but he said, “We’re very good at tests.”

Carter pulled up the diagnostic feed, watching as the wall glyphs shifted to a new pattern—faster, the pulses now in perfect sync with Data’s onboard sequence. She wondered if the android on Deck Seven was awake, if his logic core was listening.

She glanced at Jenny. The woman was staring at the guardian, eyes dark, face set. Her voice was low but certain. “It will want more than an ID.”

O’Neill said, “Let’s not give it a reason to ask for a retina scan.”

They stepped forward as a unit, the guardian’s gaze never leaving Carter’s sensor. At three meters, it stopped them with a lift of one wing. “Secondary interface engaged.”

The wall at the end of the parabola slid open. Not a door, but a reconfiguration—panels shifting until there was a hexagonal aperture, black inside, with a lattice of blue like an afterimage on the retina. The air temperature dropped, if possible, another ten degrees. Breath fogged instantly, the white plume sucked inward as if the vault was inhaling.

Daniel, clutching his notebook, said, “It’s a lock and a key. The protocol is the key.”

Jenny said, “Or the protocol is the lock. If you don’t get it right…”

Teal’c: “Then the door remains closed. Or we do not survive the attempt.”

Carter, parsing the diagnostic, saw a new readout: the positronic signature on the guardian had started to echo back her own sensor logic. It was not just reading. It was learning.

She keyed the array, toggling to broadcast. “Starfleet diagnostic output, continuous.” She was aware this might do nothing, or everything.

The guardian’s wings flexed. The blue reticle flickered to a double helix, then faded.

“Protocol accepted,” said the vault. “Proceed.”

O’Neill looked at Carter. “You’re up.”

Carter stepped forward, past the cold, into the hexagon, every cell in her body screaming retreat, but her mind unable to resist the symmetry, the logic, the knowledge waiting on the other side.

Jenny went after, and Daniel and Teal’c behind. O’Neill brought up the rear, P90 raised, eyes never quite leaving the guardian until they were through.

The vault’s inner chamber was a shock of light and structure, shelves of crystal like the nervous system of a planet, blue veins pulsing in time with the heartbeat of the thing on Deck Seven and the mind of the woman who carried his knowledge.

Behind them, the guardian folded its wings and waited.

Carter did not know if they had just succeeded, or if they had become the experiment.

It didn’t matter. She advanced, array raised, ready to see what knowledge looked like at the end of the universe.

Scene 2

The standoff stretched. The air inside the vault was so cold Carter’s eyelashes started to stick, but the sweat on the back of her neck trickled anyway, prickling under the layers of synth-fleece and SGC-issue Nomex. O’Neill’s boots squeaked on the crystalline floor, a tiny, disrespectful noise in the chamber’s logic-precise silence.

“Jack O’Neill,” he said, breaking protocol by introducing himself to a weaponized hologram with wings. “United States Air Force. And our claim is that we drilled through about two miles of ice to get here, so.” He let the sentence finish itself with a minimalist gesture, as if handing off the next move to the room.

The guardian didn’t so much as blink. Its targeting reticle moved again, slow as a heartbeat, parsing each member of the group in sequence. It took two passes—O’Neill, Daniel, Teal’c, Carter, and then, oddly, Jenny. It hesitated on Carter’s sensor array, as if recognizing the signature, then locked onto Jenny’s face with a prolonged pause that was not quite human, but close enough for Carter to read it as an algorithmic stutter.

Daniel, undeterred by the lack of small talk, had already started whispering to himself, notebook open and pencil ready. The glyphs lining the inner wall flashed in a slow, deliberate sequence, and he echoed the pulse as he read them: “Causal… test. Purity-of-intent. Hierarchy of claim… Oh, that’s not good,” he said, louder than he meant to.

The wings of the guardian twitched, tightening their arcs. The voice came again, just as flat as before: “You have partial comprehension of the notation. Partial comprehension is insufficient for access authorization.”

Daniel, to his credit, did not freeze. He said, “I can translate more if you let me interact with the panel,” his tone part plea, part challenge.

The guardian ignored the request, or filed it away for later. Carter, watching the array, saw the positronic signature spike again. She said, “The diagnostic pulse your system is tracking originates from a positronic matrix aboard the surface vessel. Lieutenant Commander Data, USS Enterprise.” She let that hang for a beat, then added, “He’s damaged. He can’t be here in person.”

The silence that followed was not random. Three full seconds—enough for Carter to check the array, confirm the pulse, and register the temperature drop as the vault’s systems spun up in response.

Then: “The signal is confirmed. The matrix is incomplete. Incompleteness does not invalidate origin.”

Jenny’s jaw set, and she took a half step forward, boots crunching on the ice. “He is not incomplete,” she said. “He is injured. There is a difference, and if your system is sophisticated enough to read his diagnostic pulses, it is sophisticated enough to understand that distinction.”

The guardian’s head swiveled, just a hair, to face Jenny. The reticle flickered, then split into two, overlaying her face and her chest, reading not just her surface but her interior. Carter wondered what it saw: the tangle of two lives, the bleed of memory and experience. Jenny’s hands were fists now, but her voice held steady.

Teal’c, still at Carter’s side, finally spoke. “I have encountered the architecture of many civilizations. This construction does not belong to the Goa’uld. It predates their reach.”

The guardian’s gaze slid from Jenny to Teal’c, the blue light pulsing softer now. “Observation is accurate. This repository is legacy. It is not intended for immediate use by transient species.”

O’Neill, weapon still trained, but voice a shade lighter, said, “Then who is it for?”

The guardian replied, “Those who pass the protocol.”

Daniel, who had been reading faster, now said, “The panel reads like a—” He broke off, then found the phrase. “It’s a test of logic and cooperation. It wants a demonstration.”

O’Neill: “A science fair?”

“No,” said Carter. “More like a Turing test. For civilization.”

Daniel said, “That’s… exactly right. It wants to see if we work together. Not just here, but as a society.”

The guardian’s wings flexed, and the vault’s lighting shifted, glyphs running faster along the walls. The targeting reticle on Carter’s sensor array vanished. The voice returned: “Partial comprehension is augmented by intent. Proceed to interface and state your collaborative purpose.”

Jenny exhaled, a long cloud of breath. “It wants us to admit why we’re here.”

O’Neill: “That’s easy. We’re here because we want to know what’s in the vault.”

Carter shook her head. “Not enough. It wants more than curiosity. It wants intent.”

Daniel: “If I’m reading this right, the protocol wants a unified statement. Something that represents all of us, not just the loudest voice.”

Teal’c nodded. “A test of will, not of strength.”

The interface was a crystal panel, waist-high, glowing in cold blue, etched with the same geometric glyphs as the walls. Carter stepped up, array in hand, and set it on the panel’s edge. Jenny joined her, and Daniel, with some reluctance, set his notebook next to the sensor. Teal’c stood at their left; O’Neill, covering the rear, did not lower his weapon but closed the gap.

The guardian said, “Collaborative intent required.”

Carter looked at the group. “If we don’t speak as one, we fail.”

Jenny’s voice was sure. “I’ll go first.” She keyed the panel, and a swirl of light traced her palm, then a pulse went up her arm. She flinched, but didn’t withdraw.

Daniel, next, put his hand to the glass. The panel flashed in sequence, the glyphs flickering, then stabilizing.

Teal’c followed, his touch steady. The panel took longer to react, then glowed with a soft gold before reverting to blue.

Carter placed her hand, the cold biting even through the glove. The sensor readout pulsed. She said, “Our intent is to preserve life. To protect against threats that would destroy it. To learn from those who came before.”

O’Neill, with a sidelong look at the others, shrugged and touched the panel. “And to make sure no one uses what’s in here to end the world, if that’s on the menu.”

The vault’s lighting shifted. The guardian’s wings folded to half span, and the targeting reticle disappeared entirely. The voice, for the first time, modulated in pitch, as if considering:

“Collaborative purpose registered. Protocol escalation: demonstrate synthesis of knowledge.”

Daniel said, “It’s going to ask us to solve a problem.”

Jenny nodded. “And probably one we can’t solve alone.”

Carter scanned the wall behind the panel. The glyphs changed to an array of mathematical logic, spatial diagrams, and something that looked like quantum field equations overlaid with what could only be medical schematics.

Daniel: “Those are—”

“Starfleet schematics,” Jenny breathed. “But not current. Older, but…” She tapped the glass. “It’s a diagnostic protocol. The same as Data’s. But in a new language.”

Carter’s hands flew across the sensor’s interface, capturing every pulse, cross-referencing with her own data. The logic made sense—a recursive pattern, but every time she found the loop, it skipped ahead, as if learning from her attempt.

Teal’c said, “It is adapting to your solution.”

Carter: “It’s testing us. If we can learn together, it will let us in.”

Jenny, teeth bared against the cold, said, “Let’s give it a reason.”

The four of them worked the panel. Daniel translating glyphs, Carter mapping the logic, Jenny tracing the medical patterns and calling out the parallels to Data’s own repair algorithms. Teal’c watched, then began pointing to symmetries in the glyphs that Daniel missed.

Every two minutes, the guardian’s voice would interject: “Progress measured. Continue.”

The collaborative effect was intoxicating. Each time Carter reached for a solution and missed, Daniel found the next step; when Jenny hit a wall, Teal’c pointed out the geometric motif that broke the pattern open. They weren’t just collaborating—they were building something new, an emergent logic that was neither SGC nor Starfleet, but both.

O’Neill watched, silent, but every time the group slowed, he’d say something that nudged them forward: “Try it upside-down,” or “What if the pattern’s supposed to be incomplete?”

After fourteen minutes, the panel flashed green. The diagnostic pulse matched the surface, Data’s own logic signature echoing perfectly.

The vault’s lighting went full-spectrum, for the first time, not blue but white. The guardian’s wings folded entirely, then faded from view.

“Protocol satisfied,” the vault said.

Daniel leaned back, blinking in the sudden light. “Did we just pass?”

Jenny, arms crossed over her chest, allowed herself a smile. “We did.”

Carter, exhausted but elated, said, “I want to see what we unlocked.”

The wall behind the panel slid open—a second, deeper chamber, lined with crystalline racks and, at the far end, a single sarcophagus-like pod.

Teal’c: “There is more. There is always more.”

O’Neill nodded at the pod. “Let’s see what’s in the box.”

They advanced as a group, the sense of mutual victory palpable, but under it all, Carter could feel the weight of what was coming next. Every answer in the universe came with a cost.

But at least, for now, the test was theirs to take.

Scene 3

The light in the inner vault was bright enough to trigger an afterimage in Carter’s right eye, but she didn’t blink as she set the sensor array down and started a direct patch to Xander’s receiver. The ice-hardened Wi-Fi relay in her pack was barely up to the task, but within ninety seconds she had the full telemetry streaming to the surface—and the haptic pulse on her wrist signaled a response almost before she could take a breath.

Three pulses: short, long, short. Xander’s agreed signal for “I have something.” Carter risked a glance at Jenny, who nodded, then at O’Neill, who didn’t stop covering their backs.

She toggled the mic to low power and said, “Consultant flags the guardian’s core process. It’s not doing threat assessment—it’s running a values-verification protocol. If I’m reading this right, it’s judging us by our choices, not our weaponry.”

O’Neill’s voice came back over the bone-mic: “Great. A personality test, in a freezer.”

Daniel’s lips twitched, but his focus stayed on the glyphs, which were now scrolling down the nearest wall in a sequence that Carter was starting to understand as a conditional flowchart—if, then, else, loop, repeat.

Daniel: “It’s not asking who we are. It’s asking what we are.”

The pod at the end of the chamber was not coffin-shaped after all. The closer they got, the more it resembled a sarcophagus repurposed for circuitry—pale crystal veins running through the sides, a logic board’s worth of etched lines on the lid. The hum in the chamber rose by half an octave as the team approached, the air alive with a frequency Carter’s array registered as somewhere between ‘slightly annoying’ and ‘inducing euphoria in fruit flies.’

The guardian did not follow them in. It waited at the door, wings retracted, its face now a mirror of the room’s light. Daniel kept glancing back, as if half-expecting the construct to teleport or phase in behind them.

At the panel on the pod, Jenny and Carter worked in tandem. Carter ran the SGC diagnostic, watching as the pod’s internal logic synced first to her system, then to the wristwatch relay, then—strangely, she noted—to the vault’s own wall displays. Jenny, hands steady now, traced the panel’s edges, occasionally tapping glyphs in a pattern that wasn’t random but which Carter recognized as a kind of musical logic—intervals, not language.

Daniel said, “There’s a second sequence on the wall. It’s… a log. Of past attempts.” He read, finger following the text. “Multiple prior engagements. Each time, the guardians closed the vault. They never made it past this point.” He looked at Carter, face pale but lit with adrenaline. “I think we’re the first to get this far.”

O’Neill muttered, “Yay, us.”

Jenny leaned in to Carter’s ear. “The interface is calling for a ‘unified statement of purpose.’ Not from one of us. From all of us. Like a chorus.”

Carter’s heart hammered. “All five?”

Jenny nodded, and the sense of gravity doubled. “If we get it wrong—”

Daniel interrupted, “The vault closes and we freeze to death. I know.”

Carter thought for a second. “But the values protocol—if it’s watching, we need to be honest.”

Teal’c, who had been silent, said, “Then we should speak what is true for each of us, and let the system judge the whole.”

Jenny’s voice, soft but absolute: “Agreed.”

The interface on the pod pulsed, as if impatient. Carter placed her hand on it and looked at O’Neill.

He said, “Fine. But I’m not singing.”

They lined up, each placing a hand on the crystal. The panel’s logic flickered, then merged all five prints into a single column of light.

Daniel started: “We are here to seek knowledge that can preserve life, not end it.”

Teal’c: “We are not of those who enslaved, but those who would free.”

Jenny: “We carry the memories and wounds of other worlds, and do not wish to repeat their losses.”

Carter, after a beat, said, “We want to understand. To build, not to destroy.”

O’Neill, deadpan, said, “And to keep the next set of idiots from screwing things up worse than we did.”

The logic stream on the wall turned from blue to a deep, shimmering gold.

The guardian’s voice sounded in the vault, not outside, but in the air between the team: “Unified statement accepted. Protocol escalation: demonstrate synthesis of knowledge.”

Jenny’s grip tightened, but she stayed steady. “It’s going to ask us to use what’s inside.”

Daniel’s notebook was already out, pencil flying. “The pod is a teaching device. The glyphs say… ‘seed memory, reset the tree, pass on what you learn.’ This isn’t a vault of weapons. It’s a boot disk.”

Carter was first to realize: “It’s expecting us to revive or emulate what’s inside. Maybe even combine it with what we brought.”

Teal’c: “A merger of wisdom.”

O’Neill: “If it’s another Data, I’m leaving you all in here.”

Jenny said, “It won’t be. It’ll be something new. Or… someone.”

The pod opened with a hiss and a flare of white light, like a film negative burning to transparency. Inside was not a person, but a crystalline lattice, pulsing with slow logic—data, raw and unformatted, waiting for a mind to give it shape.

Carter lifted the array and pointed it at the core. The sensor’s logic merged with the pod’s own, and the feed on her wrist pulsed again—this time in a pattern she recognized as a handshake, not just from Xander, but from the positronic signature that was Data’s. It was a perfect loop, a ready state.

Jenny’s eyes shone, and she placed both hands on the interface. “It’s safe for upload.”

Daniel looked at Carter. “Are we supposed to do this?”

She nodded. “That’s the point of the test.”

They did it together, Jenny and Carter running the arrays, Daniel reading the glyphs in time, Teal’c adjusting the interface to accept the energy. O’Neill kept his P90 up, just in case.

The vault’s temperature rose—barely, but enough to register. The pod glowed, then faded, then the walls of the vault itself began to rearrange, new logic overlays appearing in the glyphs as the system learned from their inputs.

Jenny closed her eyes, and for a moment, Carter saw the strain on her face—the collision of two lives, two memories, maybe more. But when she opened them, she said, “It worked. The vault accepts us.”

Daniel, with a scholar’s awe: “We’re being written into the log. As… inheritors.”

O’Neill, grinning now: “Does this mean we get a decoder ring?”

Carter laughed, the release of tension wild and total. She checked the sensor feed one more time, confirmed the handshake was holding, and said, “We did it.”

The guardian, still at the door, nodded once. The vault behind them began to open, shelves and racks of crystalline nodes—each one a bundle of knowledge, a slice of a lost world.

Jenny, trembling, looked at Carter. “We’re going to need a lot of notebooks.”

They stepped back, the pod sealed behind them, the vault now open to their return.

As they exited, the light followed, as if the chamber itself was seeing them off. The guardian’s voice, less a command now and more a benediction: “Assessment ongoing. Entry is provisional. The matrix on the surface will be consulted when it is able.”

Jenny’s hand closed around the sensor, hard enough to leave an imprint on the casing.

She did not look away from the door.

Carter, stepping into the corridor, found herself thinking of Data, alone on Deck Seven, and the hope that when this was over, he’d be there to see it too.

They walked out, one by one, the vault’s light pulsing at their backs.

The future, for once, felt like it might be worth the test.
