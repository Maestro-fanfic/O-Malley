Chapter 36 (22 of 24 in this book)

What You Become

Scene 1

The rooftop had never been his, exactly, but it was one of the few places in Sunnydale that hadn’t changed even when the rest of the town had gotten wise to weirdness. The tar felt like an old basketball court under him—yielding just enough to register the weight, still warm from the day’s sun, never quite letting go of the heat. Xander Harris lay on his back, knees up, elbows splayed, staring at the squared geometry of the night sky as it was diced into blocks by the high perimeter lamps and the rising heads of the HVAC units. The parking lot below was a geometry lesson, empty except for a janitor’s truck with the company logo applied at an angle, as if the decal had been installed by someone with a vendetta against symmetry.

He rolled the watch over in his palm: matte black, government-issue, the band still creased where Carter had cinched it to fit his wrist, as if the only way the device could be safe was if it had first passed through the hands of someone who believed in cause and effect. Carter’s cause: safety. Her effect: the world not falling apart for one more day.

He flicked the band, catching the familiar snap as it unlatched. It was a good sound. Not quite as satisfying as biting into a day-old Krispy Kreme—the proper, glazed kind, no filling, no nonsense—but a close second. The doughnut was already half gone, the bag collapsed in a way that exposed the remaining half-moon to the night air. The rooftop did not judge. Xander took a bite, letting the icing melt to paste between his tongue and the roof of his mouth.

He set the watch down beside the bag. It looked at home there, in an orphan’s row of things people left behind when they decided they were above it all. The donut, the watch. Obligation and breakfast, sitting in the same patch of gravel. He weighed them: the donut would disappear soon enough, but the watch would keep pulsing, a heartbeat of someone else’s idea of responsibility.

He looked at them, side by side, and wondered which one would get him through tomorrow.

The watch was a one-way line: the kind that never asked what he wanted, only if he would answer when called. He let it rest, dial facing up, and finished the donut. Each chew was a small act of defiance. The kind you could get away with, if no one else was around.

A breeze came up from the north, laced with the smell of fertilizer from the practice field and, faintly, the sharp ozone of the transformer box by the gym. There was a hum to the air—HVAC units, a distant car alarm on Revello Drive, the mutter of strip-mall neon blocks away. From here, the school was both fortress and island: above the world but never out of its reach.

The gym lights bled yellow through the frosted glass of the skylights, pooling on the roof in diluted rectangles. Xander shifted until one fell across his face, then closed his eyes and tried to remember the last time he felt sun-warm and safe at the same time. He gave up after five seconds, opened his eyes, and watched the shape of his own hand cross the light as he reached for the watch.

He buckled it on. The weight was familiar now; he’d worn heavier things in shop class, or in those summers when you learned to swing a hammer before you learned to swing a punch. The band caught a patch of sweat on his wrist, the cold sticking to skin for an instant before the warmth took over. He twisted the dial once, then again, just to make sure it still worked.

Down below, the janitor’s truck started up. The engine was a cough, then a grumble. The headlamps swept the base of the building, catching the fire escape in full relief. Xander swung his legs over the edge, boots scraping the pebbled ledge, and tossed the empty doughnut bag at the air. It caught a draft, hovered a moment, then landed next to the rusted metal of the ladder. He considered flying down—why not, when you could—but the idea felt cheap, like skipping chapters just to get to the end. So he climbed, step by step, hands on the railings, boots ringing the rungs, the world returning to its proper size with every foot of descent.

At the bottom, he didn’t look back up.

The watch on his wrist was quiet, inert, for now. In his chest, a similar mechanism waited for its own signal. On Revello Drive, the car alarm finally cut out. The parking lot was empty.

He walked home.

Scene 2

Giles’s apartment was finally quiet. The research books had been closed, Ethan had gone upstairs, and Giles was in the kitchen making enough noise with the kettle to announce that he was deliberately not listening. Xander sat beside the secure telephone with the post-it note from his wallet, its corner curled where it had stuck to an old receipt. Zoey’s Georgetown number was written beneath the White House routing phrase she had given him months ago.

He entered the routing code and asked the White House operator to connect him.

The secure line clicked through two exchanges, then rang. A student answered: “Georgetown, Copley Hall, room 412.”

He waited. The sound of footsteps, and then Zoey’s voice—warm, clipped, with that perpetual undertone of not-quite-daring you to argue with her. “Hello?”

The operator said, “Secure call for Zoey Bartlet from Xander Harris.”

A beat, then Zoey said, “You know most people call their friends without routing through the White House. Or is this a California thing?”

He could hear her smiling. “Put him through.”

It clicked. “Hey,” he said.

“Hey yourself. So what’s the emergency?” she asked. “Alien landing? Principal Snyder declare martial law? Or did you just remember that my meal plan comes with unlimited minutes for emotional support?”

“No emergency,” Xander said. “Just wanted to check in. You busy?”

She said, “I was working on a twelve-page response paper on the roots of European integration, but the urge to procrastinate has never been stronger.” A pause, then, “Tell me something good.”

He looked at the dark window above Giles’s desk, at the reflection of the lamp and the old municipal map still spread beneath it. “There was a cat stuck in a tree on First and North. I didn’t get it down. But I watched, and a little kid climbed up and got it himself, and then his mom grounded him for being reckless, which I think defeats the whole point of cats.”

Zoey snorted. “I bet you could have gotten it down without even breaking the branch.”

He shrugged. “Maybe. But it looked like he wanted to do it himself.”

She shifted on her end. He could hear the pop of a soda can and a muted “dammit” as it fizzed over. “So what’s really up?” she asked. “Because I know you, Xander, and you don’t call me unless you’ve been thinking about something for at least two hours.”

He made a show of being wounded. “That’s so unfair. I have layers. I have—”

She cut in: “—the ability to sulk like an Olympic event, yes. Now talk.”

He let a breath out. “Is the credentials thing tomorrow supposed to be a real deal, or just a formality? Like, are they actually putting me in a database, or is it just so they can say I’m not an ‘anomaly’ anymore?”

“Both,” Zoey said, instantly. “Welcome to American government. Everything is about filing you, and also about making you feel like it was your idea.”

He made a noise. “So I’m just the guy they want to tag and bag, but with cake at the end.”

“I hope there’s cake,” Zoey said. “But here’s the part you should pay attention to: my dad is good at making people think the show is for them, but the White House is always the real audience. Except with you, I think it’s actually both. He gets weird about favor banks—he talks about it in staff meetings, and then Leo tries to turn it into a flowchart, and my dad hates that. He wants to owe you, not employ you.”

Xander squinted, trying to picture this. “He wants to what?”

“He wants to owe you,” she repeated. “You did something, you didn’t ask for anything, and now you’re in his head. Not as an asset, but as someone he actually owes. It’s very old-school Catholic. Or maybe it’s just how he was raised.”

He ran his thumb along the reinforced cord of the secure handset. “And how do you know all this?”

“Because I have ears, Xander. And because my dad talks like he’s the only one in the room when he’s annoyed.” She softened a little. “Also, because I pay attention.”

He let himself smile. “You’re scary, you know that?”

“Only if you’re in charge of the dining hall,” she said. “Oh! Did I tell you about the lasagna incident?”

He shook his head, even though she couldn’t see it. “No. But now I have to know.”

“So there’s a senator’s aide, like, first week on campus, and he’s trying to be impressive at the table, right? He tells everyone how real lasagna is made with béchamel and not ricotta, and then he stands up to make a big speech, and the tray slips, and he dumps the entire serving—cheese, sauce, everything—down the back of the Dean’s suit. The Dean, who has a standing bet with the President about which of their kids can go longest without a headline in the student newspaper. Guess who lost?”

He laughed, and the sound surprised him. It felt real, not just a social noise.

“Did you ever have a food fight in your cafeteria?” she asked.

He thought about the messes at Sunnydale High, and the one time he tried to stand up to the football team, and how that ended with him wearing a week’s worth of Jell-O. “Once. I lost. Badly.”

Zoey’s tone shifted again. “So why the nerves, Xander? Is it the watch?”

He looked down at his wrist. “You ever wear something and feel like it was going to own you after a while? Like, not the shoes you break in, but the kind you buy because you think it’ll change you, and then it just reminds you every time you look at it?”

“Shoes, no,” Zoey said. “But once, my dad bought me this necklace. It was supposed to be a shield, or a charm, or whatever. All it did was catch on everything, and eventually I just stopped wearing it. When he noticed, he said, ‘I thought it would make you feel protected.’ I told him it made me feel like a kid who needed protection. Which is not the same thing.”

He spun the watch dial, slow. “If I throw this away, Carter would probably kill me.”

“Don’t throw it away. Upgrade it. Or draw something on it,” she said. “You’re allowed to change the things they give you, you know. The real power is when you make it yours.”

He chewed on that, then said, “You think the new set will be better?”

She said, “I think if they’re making both receivers two-way, it means they’re finally interested in what you have to say back. Which is the opposite of owning you.”

He held the phone, then said, “Will you be watching? For the ceremony?”

Zoey gave a warm little snort. “You think I’d miss it? I’ll have a notebook out and everything. If you do anything embarrassing, it’s going in the next call home.”

He nodded. “Thanks, Zoey.”

She lowered her voice, serious now. “Promise me you’ll call after. You can even route it through the White House again if you enjoy terrifying the duty operator.”

He said, “I promise.”

The line went quiet except for the kettle settling in the kitchen and the faint hum of the secure-line equipment beneath Giles’s desk.

He hung up and sat for a moment with the post-it in his hand, the ink beginning to blur where his thumb pressed too hard. Then he folded it twice, tucked it back into his wallet, and reached for his jacket.

The night was soft, the world a little less sharp-edged than before. He started walking home, the watch heavy on his wrist, but not as heavy as the promise he’d just made.

Scene 3

The headstones had been knocked down during a vampire nest cleanout three nights back, when the usual suspects—Buffy, Giles, and the new Navy guy with the tactical goatee—decided to take the direct approach and hit the crypt after dark. The vamps got dusted, the crypt got repainted in plasma, and a row of granite markers got torpedoed in the crossfire. Xander had watched from the tree line, not because he wasn’t invited, but because the last three times he’d tried to help, he’d caught the blunt end of someone else’s heroics and spent the night picking marble shards out of his hair.

The cemetery wasn’t quiet, exactly, but it was the kind of quiet you could work in. Xander gripped the edge of a fallen headstone with both hands and leaned his weight into it, rolling the rectangle upright until it thunked back onto its base. The engraved dates said “1922 – 1968,” but the name itself—“Fennimore”—was the only part still readable. He pressed the stone, tested its balance, then moved to the next one.

Buffy dropped onto the lid of a nearby crypt, knees drawn up, chin propped on one. “If you keep going, you’re going to alphabetize them.”

He shrugged. “I’m more of a Dewey Decimal guy. But I’ll settle for less corpse chaos.”

She watched him for a long beat. “So. This is your training now?”

He dusted his hands off, not looking at her. “It’s cardio. And less likely to get me an exorcism bill from the city.”

She slid down, boots hitting the grass with a thump, and circled around until she was in his line of sight. “You want to spar, or just talk in metaphors all day?”

He hesitated. Then: “If I say metaphors, do I still have to do the pushups?”

“No, but I’ll make fun of you for being emotionally avoidant,” she said. She squared off, but left her hands at her sides.

He took a ready stance. Not perfect, but better than a year ago. She didn’t wait for a signal—she just closed the distance and threw a three-count: jab, cross, knee. He blocked the first, ate the second, and stumbled back as her knee came up, stopping a centimeter short of his ribs.

She lowered her leg. “You’re distracted,” she said. “Which is not good. That’s how you lose the other kidney.”

He grinned, a real one. “I was just giving you the win. It’s your cemetery, after all.”

She let that ride. “You ever think about what comes after all this?” she asked, eyes narrowing. “Like, after high school, after Sunnydale, after the weird stops being weird?”

He tried to picture it, failed, and shrugged. “Maybe. If I live that long.”

She stepped back, then circled, making him turn to keep her in view. “There’s a difference between having power and using it,” she said. “That’s what Giles is always on about, but he never explains how you’re supposed to know which one you are.”

He waited. She was working up to something.

“You know what I figured out, running the briefings with Chegwidden?” she said. “The soldiers who freeze aren’t the ones who don’t know what to do. They’re the ones who haven’t decided who they are, when things go wrong. The rest just act.”

He snorted. “So what, I need a mantra? A secret identity?”

Buffy stopped, hands on hips. “No. I’m saying you need to stop letting everyone else write the narrative. You get to choose, Xander.”

He started to argue, but she cut him off with a look. “Who are you when things go wrong?”

He thought about it, about the last night on the roof, the phone call, the taste of metal and the heaviness of the watch.

He said, “The guy who shows up.”

She shook her head. “That’s not an answer. That’s a habit.”

She stepped in, quick, and threw a lazy elbow—telegraphed, easy to dodge. He stepped aside this time, not blocking, just letting the force slide past him. It was a different move, and she noticed.

She smiled, a little proud. “See? You’re learning.”

He looked at her, sweat starting to bead at his hairline, the grass at his feet pressed flat from his constant shifting. “Is this the part where you tell me to keep my head down and just survive high school?”

She shook her head. “Nope. I’m telling you that the ceremony tomorrow isn’t a leash, it’s a door. You walk through, or you hover outside and keep being weird. Your call.”

He stared at her, then down at his hands. The knuckles were already bruising from the granite, but he didn’t mind. He picked up the last headstone—a small one, “HARRIS” in block letters, no dates—and set it back on its marker.

Buffy watched him do it. “I never asked you to be part of the team,” she said, “but I’m glad you keep showing up anyway.”

He straightened the stone, brushed the dirt from its face, and nodded. “It’s what I do.”

She gave a crooked little smile, then jogged off, her feet barely leaving a trace on the grass.

Xander stood in the afternoon sun, the silence settling in again, and wondered which part of himself he’d left behind on the cemetery floor.

Scene 4

The bookshop was one of the old ones, the kind that sold more mass than margin: a narrow storefront, chipped paint, the scent of dry rot and accidental incense. Inside, it was books to the ceiling—some actual, some not. Giles had the back room on a retainer, cash only, and in exchange the owner turned a blind eye to the strange hours and the even stranger company.

The training space was all atmosphere: a battered worktable in the center, its surface pocked with burns and ink blots; a single metal chair, bent at the legs; floor-to-ceiling shelves of occult texts, most with titles in Latin or Greek, none with dust jackets. The only light was a naked bulb dangling from a cord, its pull chain long enough to trip on if you weren’t careful.

Ethan had the latest lesson laid out before Xander arrived: a small brass bowl filled with sand the color of old bone, the next exercise in the same sequence they had been working through for weeks. He sat with his legs crossed at the ankle, rolling a thin cigarette between two fingers. Without looking up, he flicked it into a second bowl already half-full of spent cigarettes and gestured for Xander to take his usual chair.

“Punctuality,” Ethan said, voice flat. “One of the few virtues Giles managed to cultivate. I thought you might rebel.”

Xander sat. The chair protested, a metallic squeal. “I’m trying to work on self-improvement.”

Ethan’s lips twitched at that. He reached out, index finger extended, and drew a perfect circle in the sand. “Look,” he said.

Xander looked. “Circle.”

“Unbroken,” Ethan clarified. He wiped it away and drew a second, identical except for a single line bisecting it at a shallow angle.

“Now,” Ethan said, “which is stronger?”

Xander stared. “The one with the line. It’s got a support.”

“Wrong,” Ethan said, not gloating, just correcting. “The line is a seam. That’s where you break it.” He pressed his thumb into the center of the bisected circle. The sand collapsed along the division, splitting clean. He did the same to the whole circle: the thumbprint deformed the shape, but it stayed one piece.

Xander nodded, absorbing. “So… seams are bad.”

Ethan shrugged. “Seams are inevitable. But they’re also predictable. That’s why every spell, every curse, every compulsion Giles ever taught you to resist, starts with an attack on the seam. On the place where you’re not one thing or the other.”

He wiped the sand flat, drew both circles again. “You’ve got two seams now, Xander. The one that runs down the middle of your name, and the one that runs down the middle of your new job.”

Xander tapped the edge of the bowl. “And if I just… don’t care about the seam?”

Ethan’s smile was dry. “Then you’re either a Buddha, or a corpse. Most people never get there.”

He paused, as if deciding whether to waste a breath on philosophy. “If you’re going to survive—really survive, not just keep getting up—you need to stop thinking of yourself as two things. There’s no Alexander Harris and Superman. There’s just you. And the world will push until you pick one, or until the seam splits.”

He waited, like he expected Xander to argue. When Xander didn’t, he nodded, as if that was the only correct answer.

Xander looked at the circles, at the line of collapse. “So, this is a magic thing? Or a life thing?”

Ethan snorted, then leaned back. “Magic, life, same difference. The universe doesn’t care about the category—just about the pressure.”

Xander stared at the sand for a long second, then drew his own finger through both circles, erasing them with a single, side-to-side sweep.

He said, “Show me the next exercise.”

Ethan reached for another bowl, this one shallower, and pulled it in front of him with care. “This is for mapping,” he said. “But the principle’s the same.”

He poured out a thin layer of blue powder, then traced an oval, not a circle, in the powder’s surface. “The shape can change,” he said. “But the integrity matters more.”

Xander watched the bowl, watched Ethan’s finger cut through the shape, watched the way the powder held its new edge even after the finger was gone.

Ethan nodded at him, serious now. “Try it.”

Xander reached out, hesitated, then drew a quick line through the blue, dividing it in half. The edge split, clean but obvious. He pressed his finger to the middle, just like before, and the two halves slid away from each other, no resistance.

Ethan watched, not smiling. “This is what the Council never teaches,” he said. “If you want to hold under pressure, you have to want to be whole.”

He leaned in, voice a little softer. “Otherwise you’ll spend your life patching seams, and the world will keep finding the weak spot. Every. Single. Time.”

Xander thought about it, then wiped the surface clean again, flattening the blue powder until it was almost perfect.

He looked at Ethan. “What if you don’t know what whole looks like?”

Ethan shrugged, but it wasn’t dismissive. “Nobody does. You fake it until you don’t have to anymore. The trick is, don’t fake it for the world—just for yourself.”

He rolled another cigarette, fingers quick and precise. “Let’s try the next one,” he said, voice sliding back to business.

Xander nodded, ready.

Scene 5

The conference room in the Sunnydale Naval Reserve facility was the kind of room that inspired lowercase letters—unremarkable, wood-paneled, lit by a bank of fluorescent tubes that made everything look a shade less trustworthy. There was a flag in the corner, a credenza along the far wall with a coffee pot and four mismatched mugs, and a long table with just enough veneer left to reflect the ceiling’s light back at you. Someone had thought to install a high-definition camera on the end of the table, angled to catch the whole room and whatever dignity could be salvaged by the arrangement of the chairs.

Xander took a seat. The metal frame creaked, old and Navy-issue. He tugged at his shirt collar, which had decided to shrink by half a size since his last growth spurt.

Captain Carter entered three minutes later, carrying a manila folder, two fountain pens, and what looked like a small jewelry case. She was in civilian clothes: a navy blue blazer, white shirt open at the throat, pants a touch too formal for “just passing through.” Xander noticed, and for the first time that day, he felt a little less like a specimen.

“Ready?” Carter asked. She set the folder on the table and sat one seat over, the better to avoid crowding him.

“About as ready as I get,” Xander said. He tried to sound cool, but the echo of the HVAC system made every syllable ring out.

Carter smiled, tight but genuine. “I know it’s weird. Just pretend you’re in the principal’s office, but the principal is the President.”

“That’s not weird at all,” he deadpanned.

She reached over and toggled the remote on the table. The far end of the room came alive: a wall-mounted screen flickered, then stabilized on a video call interface. The White House crest, a cheap digital watermark, hovered in the lower right corner.

Bartlet was already there, at his desk, a stack of blue folders at his left and a mug (presidential seal, possibly collector’s edition) at his right. Leo McGarry stood in the background, arms folded, gaze somewhere between protective and bored. Bartlet wore a suit that had been put on for the occasion and would be taken off the second the call ended.

“Alexander,” the President said, “thank you for making the time. We’ll keep this short.”

Xander nodded, then realized the camera probably didn’t pick up small gestures, so he added, “Yes, sir.”

Carter opened the folder, took out a single sheet of paper, and read: “Per Executive Order 13254, this is the credentials ceremony for Alexander L. Harris, Office of Independent Volunteer Liaison.” She slid a credential wallet across the table.

Bartlet said, “I’m supposed to say a bunch of things about the dignity of service and the importance of this office, but we both know you didn’t ask for it. In fact, you did the one thing nobody expects anymore, which is show up, do the job, and walk away.”

Leo grunted. Carter shot him a look, then softened it as she turned back to Xander.

The credential wallet was new, but inside was a laminated ID card with Xander’s own school photo—squinting, caught mid-blink, hair refusing to lie flat. There was also a single, embossed business card, the kind you’d expect from a high-end law firm: “OFFICE OF INDEPENDENT VOLUNTEER LIAISON — ALEXANDER L. HARRIS.” In the lower corner, the seal of the President.

There was also a small plaque, four by three, with the same title. Xander picked it up, turned it over, and found the back blank.

He said, “Is this for the desk I don’t have?”

Bartlet laughed. “You can tape it to your locker, for all I care. The point is, this isn’t a joke. We have people who get paid to do less.”

Leo said, “Much less.”

Carter pointed to the middle initial. “L,” she said. “It stands for whatever you want. The form had a middle initial slot, and you left it blank, so I filled it in.”

Bartlet said, “I assumed Legendary.” Leo, without missing a beat: “I assumed Liability.”

It was a better laugh than Xander expected. Carter’s smile grew a notch. “Take a look at the rest,” she said, pushing the jewelry case across the table. Inside was a matched pair: an ordinary-looking watch for Xander and a flat black module for the uniform. The watch used the same worn leather strap as the original, but now had two recessed controls on its face. The costume module carried matching controls along one edge, where he could find them through the fabric without exposing the device.

Carter picked up the watch, then the flat module. “The incoming patterns haven’t changed. Both devices still receive the same one-way alerts without answering or revealing your location. The upgrade is outbound and only activates when you choose it. Tap the reply control to acknowledge, decline, or request a secure voice channel. Hold the guarded control for an emergency burst; that calls in the cavalry and transmits your location until you cancel it. Same functions on both devices. Watch as Xander, flat module as Superman.”

Xander looked at the watch, then at Carter. “You trust me with this?”

Bartlet, still on screen, said, “The trust is already there. This is just the paperwork catching up.”

Carter nodded. “The old pair stays powered down as backup until these pass the field test. After that, I wipe them. I don’t want four devices sharing your address any longer than necessary.”

He took off the old watch and set it beside the original flat receiver Carter had opened for comparison. Then he buckled on the new watch and clipped the new costume module into the concealed pocket in his uniform satchel. He tested them the way Carter had taught him to change identities: watch on, module off; one reply pulse from his wrist. Watch off, module on; the same pulse through the satchel fabric. Then he returned to civilian mode. One channel, two form factors, never both answering at once.

Bartlet said, “Do you have anything you’d like to say for the record, Alexander?”

Xander looked at the camera. “I’m still finishing high school.”

Bartlet smiled, a little tired, a little proud. “Noted. For the record.”

Leo unfolded his arms, nodded once, and left the frame. Bartlet raised the mug, saluted with it, then the video link clicked off.

The silence that followed was both huge and completely ordinary.

Carter let it sit, then said, “You can hang out here as long as you want. I’ll be in the hallway if you need anything.”

He nodded, still turning the plaque in his hands. The edges were sharp, and the seal had been pressed deep enough to leave a mark if you pressed your thumb hard enough.

He sat there, watching the blue light on the camera fade, feeling the room slowly lose its ceremonial charge. He looked at the plaque, at his own name, at the “L,” and then, just for the hell of it, he tried to imagine what kind of office would ever need a volunteer like him.

He couldn’t, so he put the plaque back in the wallet and zipped it shut.

There was a tap on the door. Carter poked her head in. “Want a ride back to school?”

He grabbed the wallet, slipped it in his pocket, and followed her out.

Scene 6

Weatherly Park after midnight was an exercise in soft failure. The street lamps along the main walk were half-burnt and the grass was overgrown around the benches, each one scarred with the initials of people who’d never leave town. The fountain at the park’s center wasn’t much to look at: a waist-high ring of limestone, a rusted cherub at the middle with one wing snapped off and a plastic bag caught around the base. The water was still running, barely, and the surface was a scrim of algae broken by a constellation of pennies.

Xander stood at the edge, hands in his jacket, the credential wallet heavy in the inside pocket and the new watch bright on his wrist. He looked at the cherub, then up at the blank sky, then back at the fountain. The night air was cold and tasted of cut grass and ozone.

He pressed the reply control on the watch. It clicked cleanly beneath his thumb, sent a test acknowledgment, and returned one short pulse. Then he found the guarded emergency control by touch. That one was stiffer, recessed behind a raised edge so an accidental bump could not summon half the Navy.

He moved between the two controls until he could tell them apart without looking.

He walked a slow circle around the fountain. There were three empty Big Gulp cups in the basin, and a stick of wet chalk wedged behind the cherub’s foot. He picked it out, wiped it on his jeans, then drew a line along the edge of the limestone. Not a circle, but a lazy spiral. It disappeared as fast as he made it.

The park was empty. Not “nobody in sight” empty, but empty in the way that let you hear your own sneakers on the concrete, the dull slap and scuff as he walked. The watch glowed faint blue in the dark, the only steady thing for a block in any direction.

He reached in his back pocket and pulled out the crumpled Krispy Kreme bag. He’d carried it all day, more out of habit than hunger, the way he used to tuck his lunch in his jeans through middle school. He dropped it in the trash can by the fountain, half-expecting it to bounce out, but it didn’t.

He stood for another minute, nothing urgent, nothing looming. Just the broken cherub, the algae, the pennies. He tried to remember if he’d ever made a wish in this fountain, and decided he hadn’t.

He walked home. He didn’t fly.

The only sound was the echo of his shoes on the sidewalk, and, from somewhere two blocks over, the late bark of a dog on Revello Drive. The watch caught a slice of streetlight as he moved, marking each swing of his arm with a blink of blue.

It was enough.
