Chapter 36 (23 of 24 in this book)

March 5

Scene 1

The clock in SGC’s Cheyenne Mountain tactical room was one of the only visible reminders that time still existed outside the closed circuit of the operations floor. Carter didn’t look at it—she never did during a sim sequence—but Daniel had set his legal pad propped up just so, the clock’s reflection caught in the margin, maybe so he could pretend the world out there still rotated around a sun.

Carter had not left her seat in four hours. The smell of burnt coffee was so pervasive that it might as well have been a feature of the recycled air itself, not a consequence of O’Neill’s approach to brewing or his utter refusal to change the filter before a new pot. She knew every cold note of it by now, the same way she knew the way the stylus felt between her fingers after the third hour: sweat-slicked, but only at the thumb joint, never at the knuckle. Every time the grid flashed red, she bore down on the stylus as if it would help.

Iteration forty-three failed at the exact nanosecond she expected, the shield absorption curve peaking at 94.1 percent. The only surprise was the lack of surprise. Carter exhaled, and the stylus bent just a shade before she forced herself to relax.

“Copy the next run over,” she said, without looking up. “Increment by three thousandths. Repeat.”

Daniel didn’t move, but his terminal updated in lockstep with hers. “You know you just matched the last six iterations down to four decimals, right?” His voice was a little too chipper for someone who hadn’t eaten his sandwich, but Carter was used to it.

“Redundancy is the mother of non-catastrophic failure, Doctor Jackson,” she replied, mostly for effect.

O’Neill, arms crossed and spine against the far wall, didn’t even blink. He watched the screens as if trying to memorize the color of failure. Teal’c stood beside him, every inch as statuesque as the first time Carter had seen him on base, but with the addition of a slight crease between his brows—a tell only if you knew him.

The guardian’s telemetry from the Antarctic site was open in a split window on Carter’s secondary display. She’d parsed the raw binary herself: line after line of cooling, rational precision, a voice so level it bordered on contempt for inefficiency. The guardian did not explain, only reported. Four hours prior, its voice had split the bandwidth with a litany of concrete data: energy yields, shield harmonics, cycling rates down to the millisecond.

It was everything Carter had ever wanted from a black-box intelligence, and it made her want to throw her stylus at the wall every time the simulation failed to keep up.

She called up the phaser waveform schematic, zoomed in to the sixth digit, and made a note in the margin: “intra-cycle Q-switch lag.” The error bar was so small it almost made her laugh.

She didn’t laugh.

She adjusted the frequency: plus 0.003 terahertz.

“Iteration forty-four,” she called.

Daniel, flipping a page of transcript: “You’re looking for resonance overlap, not just power output?”

“I’m looking for anything that doesn’t end in us getting vaporized, Daniel,” she said.

The simulation ran in real time—no point in modeling delays, since Goa’uld never gave them time to breathe in the real thing. The shield model ramped up, the phaser array met it, and for half a second, the screen hovered yellow-orange before settling at amber.

Not red.

She stared at the readout. The stylus stopped moving.

O’Neill’s head came off the wall. “Hey, Sam,” he said, voice at parade rest. “That a good color, or did we just break the monitor?”

Carter let out a breath. “It’s a different color, sir.” She dragged the data into overlay, ran the next set with a rapid-fire sequence—this time, with a staged ramp at the front end, an artificial “pulse” Daniel had suggested two hours and three sandwiches ago.

Amber again. Three runs in a row.

“That’s not nothing,” Carter said, almost to herself.

O’Neill ambled over, hands now loose at his sides. He stopped a meter behind her, watching over her shoulder. “Well, as your superior officer, I’m obliged to suggest you keep poking at it until it goes blue.”

She didn’t look at him. “Blue is theoretical, sir. I’ll take not-dead.”

Teal’c stepped closer, his jaw unclenching by the millimeter. “Major Carter,” he said, “is the improvement sufficient to attempt field application?”

Carter looked at the array of numbers, let her eyes move through them, not as digits but as a kind of music—beats, pauses, the gaps where failure could slip through if you weren’t careful. “If we stack the output, synchronize to within half a millisecond, and compensate for the energy bleed at the emitter, yes. Theoretically, the shield will phase-slip.”

O’Neill: “In English?”

Carter, succinct: “It’ll poke a hole, but not for long. Maybe two seconds.”

Daniel set down his pen, finally. “That’s long enough, right? For a boarding action, or a bomb?”

Carter nodded. “Long enough.”

She saved the sequence, then immediately started building a second sim layer—this time, with the pulse tuned for five percent amplitude gain, just to see if it could be pushed any farther before catastrophic feedback.

O’Neill watched her go, then turned to Daniel, who was opening a fresh can of soda, the sandwich still untouched.

“Why do I feel like we’re going to need a bigger gun?” O’Neill said.

Daniel said, “Because the last time we needed a bigger gun, we built it out of duct tape and borrowed parts, and it worked exactly once before it almost killed us.”

Carter, not looking up, said, “Not this time.”

She keyed in the next run, fingers moving fast now, the stylus forgotten and the coffee going cold at her elbow. The grid pulsed, then held steady amber.

Behind her, O’Neill finally smiled. “That’s the color I like,” he said.

The room settled in, the tension in the recycled air shifting: not gone, but repurposed. There was momentum now.

The countermeasure was not a discovery. It was the latest answer to a timetable they had been living under for days: the Goa'uld arrival window opened tomorrow, and every failed run spent part of the margin they had left.

Carter felt it, too. The failures hadn’t stung any less, but the path forward had cracked open. She allowed herself one second to savor it, then built the next layer.

She could taste the solution—copper and caffeine, and something else, a pulse that said the world could change, given the right nudge.

She gave it.

Scene 2

The Situation Room after midnight was a different animal—less like a command center and more like the backstage of a half-cancelled dress rehearsal, everyone waiting for a cue that might never come. The overheads bleached color from the living, left only the grey. Bartlet sat at the head of the table with the posture of a man determined not to become a fixture, even as the clock and the weight of the binder threatened to flatten him into the furniture.

He’d never liked red pens. Too final, too public. But the staff insisted on it for markup—no ambiguity when it came time to change the words, change the world. He scrawled a wide bracket around the section titled “Option Bravo: National Evacuation Corridors,” the pen’s nib stuttering where the page met a coffee stain, and wrote “timeline?” in letters only Leo would recognize as urgent.

Leo McGarry stood in a position of deliberate casualness by the comms panel at the other end of the room. The triple bank of status feeds blinked in steady rotation: Cheyenne Mountain, Antarctic staging, Pacific Fleet. Each feed’s standby screen was a slightly different blue, as if the DOD procurement office had selected them solely for their capacity to irritate anyone who cared about color theory.

C.J. was two seats down from Bartlet, her binder open to a section with a yellow post-it emerging like a tabbed tongue. Her legal pad was a mess—black ink for notes, red for questions, blue for cross-references, a constellation of underlines that meant she was either bored or preparing to eat someone alive at the next press briefing.

Zoey sat at the wall, hands folded in her lap, the only person in the room who didn’t bother to pretend she was supposed to be anywhere else. She watched her father, not the feeds, her eyes clear and patient in a way that probably made him feel both proud and infuriated.

Bartlet finished the bracket, set the pen down, and picked up the coffee mug. It was cold, and he set it back in the same ring it had made the first time he’d tried and failed to drink from it.

Leo: “The data from the guardian came through clean. Carter’s team is working the analysis in real-time.”

Bartlet didn’t turn. “How long before she has something actionable?”

Leo’s thumb moved along the side of the folder, tapping a single page up, a single page down. “She said a few hours. That was three hours ago.”

C.J., eyes still down: “So, morning.”

Leo: “That’s the working theory.”

Bartlet squinted at the Pacific Fleet feed as if he could force it to cough up a secret, then turned to the binder, skipping forward three sections, then back. His thumb stopped on the page that said “Option Bravo,” and for a second he looked at the word “Bravo” as if it might blink back.

He set the binder aside, checked the mug again, and said, “This is a hell of a thing.”

C.J. stopped underlining. “Sir?”

He thumbed the binder. “I’ve got three million people on the Pacific coast who don’t even know they’re on a clock. I have to sign an order in the next twenty-four hours to put half of Oregon in their cars. And the only thing between ‘minor inconvenience’ and ‘catastrophe’ is a field test we haven’t run yet.”

Leo turned a page. And Harris? He is on the field roster now. The credentials are not a courtesy title; he gets the same threat picture as everyone else.

Bartlet’s finger rested on the evacuation map. “Then the strategic and operational tracks need to match before dawn. Allies get the same threat window, the public posture stays deliberate, and the evacuation trigger is agreed before anyone sees a ship. Cheyenne owns detection and engagement; the Navy owns movement and recovery; Sunnydale owns the ground. No one discovers the plan by accident.”

Leo said, “That’s why it’s Option Bravo, not Alpha.”

Bartlet: “There is no Alpha. I’ve read the book twice. You’ve got the only binder in the building that’s uglier than mine, and you know it.”

Leo’s mouth twitched. “It’s a prototype, sir.”

Bartlet turned a single page back, stared at it.

Zoey’s voice was soft but not hesitant. “Dad.”

Bartlet didn’t look up, but the pen in his hand stopped moving.

Zoey: “You marked that paragraph four times.”

He looked at the page. Red, red, red, red. “I’m aware.”

C.J., not even pretending to hide the smile: “Five, actually. The first one bled through from the page before.”

Leo coughed. It was not, strictly speaking, necessary.

Bartlet closed the binder, not gently, and stood. “I’m going to get some fresh air,” he said, then reconsidered the statement and instead walked to the window, which was several inches thick and presumably bomb-proof, but not openable.

He put his hands in his pockets and watched the darkness outside, the city’s lights diffused by the reinforced glass into a thousand pinpoints. He looked, in that moment, like a man who might actually vanish if someone let him.

At the table, C.J. scribbled another question on her pad, then stopped and tore off the page, folding it once and sliding it into the binder. She looked at Leo, who shrugged in a way that said: He’ll be fine. Or maybe: He’ll be fine if we’re lucky.

Nobody said anything for a while.

The red marks in the binder were still visible from three feet away. Bartlet’s silhouette, washed grey by the lights, did not move.

Tomorrow would come, or it wouldn’t, but for now, the Situation Room was the quietest place in the country.

Scene 3

By 0800 on March 5, the President’s senior staff had stopped discussing the Goa’uld arrival as a possibility. The question on the table was how to manage a known strategic event without turning the Pacific into a stampede. Bartlet sat at the head of the Situation Room table with Leo, C.J., State, Defense, and a secure line open to the British and Australian duty offices.

The working map showed three concentric decisions: what the allies were told, what the public could be told, and what moved if the threat crossed the outer-atmosphere threshold. The invasion window was tomorrow. Cheyenne had the detection picture; the Navy had the lift and recovery picture; Sunnydale had the ground problem. None of those lanes could afford to learn about the others at the last minute.

“We are not waiting for a dramatic photograph before we coordinate,” Bartlet said. “The partners get the threat window and the rules for attribution. The governors get the evacuation contingencies. The task force gets authority to act. If the ships never arrive, we will have spent a day looking prepared. If they do, we will have spent it being prepared.”

Leo slid the final page toward him. “And Harris?”

“Included,” Bartlet said. “He is a credentialed field asset with a defined role, not a secret we keep dressing up as a schoolboy. He gets the same brief as the people asking him to fly into it.”

C.J. marked the release schedule, State confirmed the allied calls, and Defense moved the evacuation language into its final review. By the time the room broke, the invasion had a strategic posture, a communications posture, and a clock everyone could see.

Scene 4

The operational brief began ten minutes later in Cheyenne Mountain, with Carter at the central display and O’Neill standing beside the only empty chair. It was not empty by accident. A secure video window showed Xander Harris in Giles’s library, still in a T-shirt, credential wallet open beside the borrowed laptop.

Carter brought up the known approach corridor, then the response tree: detection, confirmation, shield analysis, civilian protection, interception. “Tomorrow’s window is not a launch order,” she said. “It is the point at which we stop guessing. If the signatures confirm hostile intent, Xander takes the local relay and reports what he can see. The SGC handles the orbital picture. Navy support moves only on the agreed triggers.”

Xander leaned toward the camera. “And if I’m in the air?”

“You are still part of the net,” Carter said. “The watch and the suit module are one system with two form factors. You acknowledge on the device you are wearing, and you do not disappear into the costume just because it is easier for everyone else to pretend you are an unknown.”

O’Neill pointed to the handoff line. “No freelancing past the boundary. No heroic improvisation because nobody remembered to tell you the plan. You know the plan now.”

Xander looked from the display to Giles, then back to Carter. “Got it. Tomorrow is not when you decide whether I’m involved.”

“Correct,” Carter said. “Tomorrow is when we use the work we are doing today.”

Scene 5

The Sunnydale High football field held the heat from the day long after the rest of the town had cooled, the turf leaching it back up at dusk so the air hovered, not quite hot, not quite breathable. The field lights were off, and the only illumination was the strip of sky bleeding orange over the bleachers and the painted hashmarks. No audience, just the quiet shuffle of a practice not on any schedule.

Urgayle was a shadow with a strike shield, his silhouette squared off at the forty-yard line. He wore a faded Academy tee and shorts that were probably standard issue, but nothing about his stance invited even the idea of informality. He called the drill, “two, slip, three, pivot,” in a tone that made the numbers feel heavier than the shield.

Buffy moved before the words fully registered—faster than the echo, just like she’d been trained. The second strike was a palm heel, left, solid against the pad; the slip was a rotation, tight, hips leading; the third was a straight elbow that drove the shield back a foot, and the pivot brought her entire frame around, balanced but humming with residual velocity.

Urgayle absorbed it, resettled his footing, and called the sequence again. “Two. Slip. Three. Pivot.”

The shield was a comfort: it couldn’t break, and it didn’t judge the force. Only the man behind it measured the shape of each impact.

On the fourth rep, Buffy’s left heel snagged a divot in the turf. Not a full stumble, just a loss of kinetic transfer. She covered, but the pivot came in wide, and her center of gravity said so.

Urgayle: “Footwork.”

No inflection, no disappointment. Just observation.

Buffy nodded, pulled in her breath through her nose, and reset to start. The sweat had pooled at her collar and down the line of her spine, but it was a familiar pattern—nothing new to her, just the cost of the drill.

They ran it for another five sets, each one marginally better, the error shrinking to a phantom by the sixth run. Urgayle never offered a word of encouragement, nor a frown. His only language was repetition.

After a while, the sun was gone, and the field was an orange memory. Buffy’s arms trembled at the wrist, not from fatigue but from the charge of holding the form. She never dropped her guard.

Urgayle checked a stopwatch—old-school, not digital—and said, “Reset. Final sequence.”

Buffy exhaled through pursed lips, rolled her neck, and squared to the shield.

“Two,” Urgayle called.

Buffy drove it.

“Slip.”

She turned on the ball of her foot, clean.

“Three.”

The elbow landed, sharper than the pad deserved.

“Pivot.”

She landed it, perfect, no drift.

Urgayle lowered the shield, not immediately, but with a deliberate patience. He nodded once, a fractional thing.

“Again, tomorrow,” he said.

Buffy wiped her forehead with the back of her arm. “I’ll be here,” she said, and meant it.

He left her there, the turf cooling, the white lines underfoot still visible in the afterglow.

She stood on the field, let the sweat dry, and felt the engine in her heart settle back to idle. In the distance, the first of the field lights blinked awake, a mechanical flicker that would not have mattered to anyone except those who needed to see.

She did.

Scene 6

The back office of the Sunnydale High library had been converted from a records closet to a ritual lab by degrees: first a chalkboard propped against a filing cabinet, then stacks of textbooks for ballast, finally a strip of linoleum cleared of boxes, the floor pattern almost invisible under the mesh of chalked arcs and hastily erased misfires.

Willow and Giles crouched, knees parallel, elbows in, heads bent at identical angles as they mapped the next revision of the perimeter ward. The linoleum was cold even through their jeans, and it carried the dust of a hundred failed arrays. Willow held the folded graph paper in both hands, careful not to smudge it—she’d ruined enough in the last hour to know not to trust her own palms.

The outermost ring was packed with iron filings, each granule brushed into place by the edge of a laminated student ID card. The inner quadrants were a fractal of new sigils—sourced from a water-damaged Etruscan codex Giles had pulled from the discount bin at a university library sale, the text bled but the geometry weirdly intact. Willow read the numbers aloud: “Six point three two centimeters from axis, counterclockwise offset by one-seventeen degrees.”

Giles nodded, lips pursed, and used the tip of a mechanical pencil to nudge the chalk line left, then down. He said, “Now.”

Willow read the next value. Giles made the adjustment, then set the pencil aside.

As the line connected to the anchor point, the innermost sigil—three interlocked triangles, circled by a broken ring—emitted a low, teeth-harmonic hum. The nearest bookshelf rattled, enough to jostle the fake plant atop it.

Both of them froze.

Willow held her breath; Giles exhaled, slow.

The hum held, then faded to a stable sub-bass. Willow’s shoulders dropped. She wrote the value in Giles’s open notebook, careful block letters, then triple-checked the number.

Giles: “Again.”

They moved to the next quadrant, repeating the process with the care of bomb technicians or neurosurgeons. Chalk, nudge, hum. The work was less about the drama of magic and more about the nervous science that underpinned it—feedback loops, tolerance for error, the way iron filings bent but never fully broke when you handled them with respect.

The next pulse did not come from the Hellmouth. It came through the array from above, a pressure wave riding the ward’s edge. Willow’s parser marked it in a second color. Giles looked at the new line and said, “That is the approach they warned us about.”

The fluorescent lights overhead buzzed in sympathetic rhythm with the diagram, and for a moment, Willow wondered if the line between circuitry and sigil had always been so thin. She liked the idea.

They finished the next quadrant, recorded the value, and sat back on their heels, the joints in their knees protesting.

Giles’s voice was dry, but the approval was real: “Your margin is improving. Last week, it would have destabilized.”

Willow grinned. “It’s the graph paper. Or maybe the coffee.”

Giles said, “Don’t discount your own focus.”

They looked at the diagram, and for a second it seemed more art than ward—layers of intention, all converging on a single idea: keep the world intact, even if it meant getting chalk dust under every fingernail.

Giles pointed to the last quadrant. “Shall we?”

Willow rolled her neck, relishing the ache. “Let’s make it hum.”

They leaned in together, hands steady, eyes clear, the fluorescent lights shining just a little bit brighter.

Scene 7

Xander cut the east side loop with his left hand in his pocket, thumb brushing the knurled edge of the two-way watch until it left a mark on his skin. Restfield Cemetery was quiet tonight—too quiet, which in Sunnydale meant someone had scared off the usual undead loiterers. The shortcut behind the mall was lit only by the glare from the Chinese takeout and the spinning “OPEN” sign at the pawn shop, both of which seemed to survive every natural and supernatural disaster the town threw at them.

By the time he hit the Maple parking lot, the lamps overhead had clicked on, the sodium glow giving everything a color like old mustard and concrete dust. He walked with his head down, but not so far he couldn’t see over the hood of every car he passed.

Cordelia was waiting for him, just left of center, leaning against her trunk with one high-heeled boot propped on the bumper and a shopping bag over her wrist. She looked at her phone, then up at Xander with the expression of someone who expected better but was used to being disappointed.

“Harris. You look terrible.”

Xander made a face, then shrugged. “Thanks, Cordelia. I’ve been working on it.”

She studied him, not even pretending to hide the analysis. “No, I mean you look tired. Like, actually tired. Not ‘stayed up playing video games’ tired. More like ‘the world’s ending and you don’t have the energy to save it’ tired.”

He stopped two paces from her, hands out of his pockets now, fingers splaying to work out the nerves.

“I didn’t know there was a difference,” he said, and tried for a grin. It didn’t land.

What happens after tomorrow?

His jaw set, but this time he did not hide behind the silence. “They stopped treating me like a kid in a costume,” he said. “I’m on the plan. Tomorrow I fly when they tell me to fly.”

She pressed, voice even: ‘I’m not asking you to tell me the classified parts. I’m asking if you’re okay now that the secret identity is mostly paperwork.’

Xander glanced at the lamps, then back at her. The light painted her skin yellow, made her look more real than anyone else in the parking lot.

“Ask me on Saturday,” he said.

Cordelia held his gaze for a long second, then nodded. “Fine. Saturday.” She checked her phone again, scrolling with a practiced flick, but didn’t look away as he started walking.

As he passed, she called, “Just remember, Harris: whatever it is, you don’t get points for dying heroically.”

He kept going, but the words followed him.

At the far end of the lot, he stopped and looked back. Cordelia was still by her car, face lit by the screen, unmoving.

The town was waiting, holding its breath. So was he.

He pulled his jacket tighter and walked, the click of his shoes on the sidewalk the only sound until the lamps buzzed again, and the world slipped back to normal.

Scene 8

Beneath the ice, in the narrowest run of the Antarctic vault, the lights were tuned to a spectrum that hurt human eyes but was optimal for the array of sensors laced through the walls. The briefing chamber was two generations past “bunker”; the composite walls drank every word, so nothing echoed. The only decoration was the cluster of data screens, each angled with more intent than the room’s furniture.

The guardian’s voice filled the space, patient, crisp, and the opposite of human. “Goa’uld staff weapon, maximum output: one point four megajoules per pulse. Lethal radius, based on human thoracic target, 0.2 meters. Ha’tak shield emitter, primary cycle: 752.13 milliseconds, modulated by feedback loop every sixth cycle. Death glider ventral hull, structural tolerance exceeded at 13.2 gigapascals, localized to the secondary air intake.”

This was no longer a theoretical catalog. The SGC had an arrival window, Carter had a breach sequence, and the people in Sunnydale had been told to expect the first pressure wave before dawn. The only remaining question was how many ships would come through it.

Jenny sat at the secondary console, one hand resting on the medical tricorder, the other on the legal pad. She toggled the data overlay, merged the guardian’s output with the comparative anatomy file she’d started an hour ago. On the screen, the symbiote’s physiology glowed in cold blue, each node tagged with a cross-reference to Crusher’s memory.

She made a note: “Neural integration at cervical plexus. Immunological defense above baseline. Regeneration faster than Cardassian laceration model.”

The wall speakers ticked through another set of numbers.

“Median human resistance to staff weapon: negligible,” the guardian said, almost gently.

Jenny underlined “negligible.” She wondered, for a second, whether that was mercy or just a bias in the data.

O’Neill’s voice, patched in from Cheyenne, cracked through the speaker: “You’re telling me the only way to breach the shield is to hit during the cycle window, and that window’s under a second?”

The guardian: “Affirmative.”

O’Neill: “Is that window the same across all Ha’tak classes?”

The guardian: “Cycle interval is consistent within a twelve millisecond variance across all documented configurations. Exceptions exist for vessels modified after the last recorded survey.”

Jenny highlighted “exceptions,” then scrolled down to the next set of data, jaw clenched.

O’Neill, impatient: “And when was the last recorded survey?”

The guardian paused. Not long, but longer than any other pause in the session.

“Longer ago than is operationally relevant,” it said.

Daniel, hunched over his own terminal at the far end of the line, wrote the answer verbatim on his transcript, even though the system was logging every word. He underlined it, then circled the margin note: “Not omniscient—bounded.”

Jenny looked at her legal pad, then at the tricorder. She thumbed through the last three pages of notes, cross-checked against the flashes of memory that belonged less to her than to someone she’d never quite been. It didn’t matter; the logic was perfect. Crusher’s brain had always favored the straightest path to an answer, and now, so did Jenny’s.

She set the pad face-down on the console.

The guardian was still talking, listing comparative energy yields, shield phase slip tolerances, every piece of data the SGC could ever want and more than they could process in a week. It never seemed to tire, never needed a rest.

Jenny listened, and for the first time since the crash, let herself feel tired.

She leaned back, watched the scrolling lines of code, and let her eyes blur at the edges.

O’Neill broke in: “Anything we can use to improve the shield penetration protocol?”

The guardian: “Overlay the emission at 0.013 terahertz above baseline, stagger the pulse by one-half cycle. It will not breach the shield fully, but may create a transient interference zone.”

Jenny’s hand moved, automatic, taking the note even though she’d already known what the answer would be.

She set the pen down and stared at the wall, the numbers still crawling.

The data would outlive all of them, and the voice—flat, precise, and untiring—would keep talking as long as someone asked.

At the far end of the room, Daniel ran his hand through his hair, then started a new page.

Jenny closed her eyes for a second and saw the shield curves against the afterimage of the lights.

It was not comfort, but it was the closest she’d come in days.

On the screen, the final line in the sequence held: “Awaiting query.”

The chamber hummed, a machine waiting for its own question.
