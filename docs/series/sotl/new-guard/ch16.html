Chapter 16

Nothing on Earth had prepared Sam Carter for the sight of it.

She'd seen the satellite photos, studied Ferretti's reports until she could have recited them from memory, and none of it had done the scale justice. The saucer section rose out of the ice at a shallow angle, its whole port flank laid open in long, raking gouges where something had torn through the hull plating, half-buried in its own furrow of churned snow and earth that stretched back nearly a mile. Even wrecked, even silent, it dwarfed every hangar she'd ever stood inside.

"Would you look at that," O'Neill said beside her, and for once there wasn't a joke loaded up behind it.

"Sir," Carter said. "It's..."

Impossible," Daniel offered, his voice quiet with the kind of awe that came from seeing a myth made real.

The helicopter set them down a hundred yards off, rotor wash kicking up a fine spray of ice crystals, and by the time they'd crossed to the makeshift entrance, Ferretti, Warren, and Teal'c were already coming out to meet them.

"Colonel," Ferretti said, something in his face easing at the sight of them.

"Major." O'Neill looked up at the hull. "This isn't what I pictured when you said crash site."

"Wait till you see the other half, sir. This is the polite version."

"There's a reference for this, isn't there," O'Neill said, half to himself, squinting against the glare off the ice. "Ship this big, half-buried, scarred to hell. I feel like I've seen this movie."

"The Enterprise D," Warren said helpfully, practically vibrating with the effort of not saying it faster. "Veridian Three. Saucer crash, end of Generations. Same basic geometry, if you flip the damage side."

"That's the one." O'Neill grimaced at himself. "Kills me that I know that. Kills me more that you knew it faster."

"Xander," Carter said, spotting him coming around the hull in civilian clothes, hands in his pockets, looking somehow more at ease and thinner around the eyes both at once.

"Hey." He grinned. "Took you guys long enough."

"We came as fast as the Air Force could manage." Daniel pulled him into a quick, real hug. "You look..."

"Like I've been sleeping inside a wrecked spaceship? Yeah, I get that a lot lately." He stepped back. "C'mon. Someone you should meet properly this time, not just over a radio."

O'Neill caught his shoulder before he could turn away. "You also look like a kid who crossed an ocean without telling anybody where he was going."

"I knew where I was going. Mostly."

"That isn't the part of the sentence I objected to."

Xander's grin faded. "I heard about the ship and Jenny, and I could get here faster than anyone could give me permission."

"You could also have told us before you left."

"Would you have said yes?"

O'Neill's silence was answer enough, his gaze fixed on the broken saucer.

"Right," Xander said. "So I'd have come anyway, and we'd just both be mad."

"I'm managing a respectable amount of mad right now."

"He moved half the debris field before the weather turned," Ferretti said. "And pulled a collapsed support off one of my people. No injury, but we'd still be digging it out without him."

O'Neill closed his eyes briefly. "Of course he did."

"I helped," Xander offered.

"I heard." O'Neill squeezed his shoulder once before releasing it. "I'm glad you're okay. Next time, use a phone before you cross a hemisphere."

"Yes, sir."

"Don't call me sir when you're trying to make it impossible to stay annoyed at you."

"Got it, Colonel."

Daniel hid a smile behind one gloved hand.

She was waiting inside, in the black and grey of a uniform none of them had seen outside photographs, a data padd tucked under one arm like it was the most ordinary object in the world.

"Colonel O'Neill." Jenny held out a hand. "Jenny Calendar. Everyone's been calling me Commander Calendar. I suppose I should get used to it."

"Ma'am." O'Neill shook it, glanced at the hull again. "So. Enterprise, huh."

"That's the name on the hull, yes."

Funny thing," O'Neill said, and Daniel visibly braced. "Back home, every time they roll out a new prototype shuttle, I tell them to call it the Enterprise. For years, I've been making that joke. Never thought I'd live to see the punchline.

"I'll take that as a compliment," Jenny said, a real crack of amusement showing through her careful composure.

Daniel hadn't stopped looking at the hull. "Do you have any idea how old the design principles behind something like this actually are? The hull configuration alone, the way the saucer separates cleanly from the drive section, that's a design philosophy, not an accident."

"Later, Daniel," O'Neill said. "Let the man finish his tour. Or, uh. The ship."

"There's more of them than just this one," Jenny said, amused. "You don't have to worry about running out of things to ask, Doctor Jackson. I promise."

"Oh, I'm counting on it," Daniel said, already pulling a notebook from his jacket.

"You brought a notebook to Antarctica," Carter said.

"I bring a notebook everywhere. You know this about me."

As they walked, Ferretti gave them the brisk summary. The buried signal remained a persistent, unchanging mystery. The recovery was three days into staging scaffolding, a silent admission of the goal nobody dared say aloud: getting her flying again. His report on Data's condition was the most grim—unresolved, with no clear path forward. It was a project defined by unanswered questions.

"Feels less like a crash site every day," Ferretti said, gesturing at the scaffolding rising in tiers, workers barely visible against the scale of it. "More like a project. Warren's got a whole team drawing up structural assessments, seeing if she's even sound enough to attempt a lift."

"Good," O'Neill said, watching the scaffolding a moment, something in his shoulders settling. "Good," O'Neill said, watching the scaffolding. "I like a plan. It means we know which direction to work.

"Speaking of," Ferretti added, "you'll want to see what we've got set up in the mess before you settle in. Turns out feeding this many people three times a day required actual planning, who knew."

"Lead the way, Major," O'Neill said. "I've been living on jet food for two days, I could eat a horse."

"We might actually have horse, sir, given some of what's in the ration crates."

"I don't want to know."

"You gonna eat, or you gonna stand there?" Casey called up, from where he and Sands were wrestling a length of steel support into place along the scaffolding, the wind snatching at their voices.

"Figured I'd help first," Xander said, and before they could argue, he took the far end of the beam. Sands felt the weight vanish from his hands as if the steel had turned to air.

"Careful, that's—" Sands started, but the words died as he watched Xander swing the beam into position with one hand. "Okay. Yeah. That works too."

"You want it braced at the third strut or the fourth?" Xander asked.

Casey and Sands exchanged a look. "Fourth," Casey said. "How do you know what a strut is?"

"My uncle Rory's got a garage back home. I used to help out." Xander lined the beam up, held it steady while Sands ran the bolt through. "This is basically the same thing, just bigger. And colder. And attached to a spaceship."

"When you put it that way," Sands said, "it's not that different from my Tuesdays."

Still weird," Xander said. "Every single time.

"Man, I hope I'm still here in a year to ask," Sands said, tightening the last bolt. "Actually, scratch that, I really hope I'm not still here in a year. No offense to the ship."

"None taken, I assume, seeing as it can't hear you," Casey said.

"It's got sensors everywhere, who even knows."

"Now you've got him worried about offending a spaceship," Xander said, grinning.

"Good," Sands said. "Keeps him humble."

✳

"Deck Seven was sealed before you arrived," Ferretti said, leading them down a corridor that grew colder with every step. "That doesn't mean it stayed that way."

Up ahead, the first pressure door stood open by four inches, its duranium halves bowed outward. A knot of black, organic-looking cable had grown through the seam, clinging to the walls and ceiling in branching ridges. It wasn't like the ship's clean conduits; this was a second machine, built across the first without regard for what was already there.

"That was welded shut this morning," Ferretti said, his voice grim.

O'Neill stopped well short of the cable. "Closed how?"

"We cut everything crossing the door and welded a brace over it. The brace is on the other side now."

"You want to tell me what moved it?"

"No, sir. I'd really prefer somebody else tell both of us."

Jenny stood behind Warren with a tricorder aimed into the gap. Its screen pulsed in time with a faint green light somewhere deeper in the corridor.

"The Borg modifications are drawing from the emergency grid," she said. "Not much power, but enough. Every time we cut a connection, another route activates around it."

"Borg," Daniel said. "The cybernetic species Hammond mentioned."

They take," Jenny said. Her voice had gone carefully flat. "Technology, living beings—it doesn't matter. They don't build anything they can strip from someone else. They just absorb it, fold it in, and move on. Beverly watched it happen once. You don't forget." She paused. "This section went the same way before the Enterprise crossed over. Most of it should have been gutted after we retook the ship. Apparently someone missed a corner."

O'Neill glanced at her. "You sound like you've seen this before."

"I remember it." Her hand tightened around the tricorder. "That isn't quite the same thing, but it's close enough."

Something moved inside the gap. A thin cable lifted from the floor, turned blindly toward the opening, then attached itself to the edge of the damaged door. Smaller filaments spread from its tip and vanished into the metal.

Warren took one step backward. "That's new."

"Everybody agrees that new is bad, right?" O'Neill asked.

"In this context," Teal'c said, "I believe that is a reasonable conclusion."

Xander leaned to see past Ferretti. "I could go in. I'm invulnerable, I can fit through that gap, and—"

"No," O'Neill said, cutting him off. "I know exactly what you were going to say, and the answer is no. We're not testing your limits against that thing."

"Jack," Daniel said, "if the deck is pulling power from life support, someone may have to disconnect it from inside."

"Then we find another way."

"What about the eye-beam thing?" Xander asked. "If I can do that, I could burn through the cables from here."

"We don't know if you have that ability, or if you can control it," Carter said immediately. "This is not the place for a field test."

"And cutting the conduits has only made it adapt," Jenny added. "An energy weapon might just provoke it."

"I can't be cut," Xander said. "We tested that. Needles, knives, everything Carter had."

"We tested whether Earth metal can cut your skin. We did not test whether alien robot tubes can turn you into one of them." O'Neill faced him fully. "You don't volunteer for a threat because we haven't found the thing that hurts you yet. That's exactly why you don't volunteer. Understood?"

Xander looked back at the growth in the doorway. "Understood."

He did not sound pleased, but he stepped away.

"Commander," Carter said, studying the power readings over Jenny's shoulder, "can we isolate this deck at the distribution junctions instead of cutting individual conduits?"

"Normally. The Borg rewrote the distribution network when they took engineering. Some of those pathways shouldn't exist, and the computer still thinks they don't."

"Then we map the draw from outside. Shut down one branch at a time and watch what changes."

"Life support is sharing two of those branches."

"We move everyone out of the affected compartments first."

Jenny considered it, then nodded. "The saucer has enough independent atmospheric processors to keep the occupied decks running if we isolate the aft grid. Replicators will go down. Turbolifts too."

"People can eat rations and use stairs," Ferretti said.

"That's twenty-three decks of stairs," Warren pointed out.

"Then they'll be hungry enough to stop complaining by the time they arrive."

O'Neill pointed toward the open pressure door. "What happens when we shut off the power?"

"Best case, it goes dormant," Jenny said. "Worst case, it has its own power cells and decides the loss of the grid is damage it needs to repair."

"And the middle case?"

"It goes dormant until somebody turns the power back on and everyone assumes it is dead."

"I hate the middle case."

"So do I."

Carter crouched near the wall panel opposite the door, careful to keep her kit from touching the black material. "I can build an independent monitor. Separate battery, optical sensors, no physical connection to the ship. If anything in there moves after the grid goes down, we'll see it without giving it another route out."

"Do it," O'Neill said. "Ferretti, clear every compartment sharing those branches. Nobody touches black metal, loose cable, or anything that wasn't part of the ship when it left the factory."

"Shipyard," Warren corrected automatically.

O'Neill looked at him.

"Clearing the compartments now, sir."

It took two hours. Jenny and Carter worked from engineering schematics that disagreed with the physical readings in eleven different places. Sands and Casey went deck by deck, moving recovery personnel forward and leaving chemical lights at every stairwell once the turbolifts shut down. Daniel helped Warren trace the Borg modifications through logs salvaged from the main computer, learning enough terminology to ask questions Jenny did not have time to answer.

Xander carried portable heaters, medical supplies, and three days of ration crates into the forward compartments. It was work anyone could have done given enough time and enough people. With him, the evacuation finished before the wind outside rose enough to stop it.

At the final junction, Carter rested one hand above the cutoff control and looked toward Jenny.

"Ready?"

Jenny checked the tricorder, then the faces around her. "Do it."

Carter closed the circuit.

The corridor lights died in sequence, white to amber to black. The green pulse beyond the pressure door continued for another four seconds, each flash weaker than the one before it, then vanished.

On Carter's independent monitor, the cable at the door remained still.

Ten seconds passed. Then thirty.

"Power draw is gone," Carter said. "Nothing on infrared. No movement."

"Dormant or pretending?" O'Neill asked.

"No way to know yet."

The open door remained open. The black cable stayed wrapped through it, quiet now, no less alien for having stopped.

"Post a guard," O'Neill said. "Nobody enters. Nobody restores power to this section without Carter and Commander Calendar both present. We watch it for a week before anybody uses the word dead."

"Agreed," Jenny said.

As they turned away, a single filament detached itself from the edge of the pressure door. It retracted with a slow, deliberate purpose, coiling back into the main cable before going still again.

Carter's optical monitor recorded it.

None of them saw it happen.

"I wish Janet could see this," Carter said, meaning it more than she'd expected to when the words came out.

Jenny had walked her through two decks already, LCARS panels lighting under her touch like she'd done it every day of her life, which, Carter reminded herself, in the way that actually mattered, she had. They'd passed crew quarters stripped bare by the crash, a mess hall with tables still bolted at odd angles, corridors lit low amber by emergency power. They'd reached sickbay, quiet, most of the equipment righted since the first chaotic days, a room that felt less like a field station and more like somewhere people had actually expected to heal.

"She'd have questions I couldn't answer," Jenny said. "Doctor Crusher's memory only goes so far into her own equipment. I know how to use it, reflexively, the way you'd know a car you'd driven for thirty years. I couldn't build you a replicator from spare parts."

"Could we bring her here? If the transporters are functional?"

"They're not, not really. Emergency power only, and it's going to life support and the replicators, in that order of priority." Jenny shook her head, though something in her expression suggested she'd wondered the same thing herself. "Even if they were, I wouldn't want to explain to General Hammond why I beamed a civilian doctor onto an alien starship without authorization."

"Fair point." They shared a real laugh over it, the kind that came easier than Carter had expected from someone she'd known less than an hour.

"The pattern buffers still pass diagnostics," Jenny added, moving to one of the wall consoles. "At least, the computer says they do. The primary emitters are in the engineering section, the targeting sensors took damage, and I don't have enough reserve power to test the system with anything living."

"Anything living?"

"I transported an empty medical case three meters. It arrived as an empty medical case."

"That's encouraging."

"The computer also spent six minutes insisting the case was Ensign Kellerman, so I wouldn't call it ready."

Carter leaned closer to the diagnostic display. The interface placed a simplified human outline beside columns of data she could not read, then rearranged the columns when Jenny touched the panel. "This is tracking at the molecular level?"

"Subatomic. Matter is converted into an energy pattern, stored briefly, then reconstructed at the destination."

"You say that like it doesn't violate six different things I learned at the Academy."

"Beverly learned the practical version in medical school. I remember enough theory to know why it works and not enough to explain it without making a warp engineer cry."

"I know that feeling."

Jenny looked at her. "You do, don't you?"

"Most of my job involves explaining technology built by people several thousand years ahead of us to officers who need to know whether it can explode before they approve the next mission."

"Can it?"

"Usually."

"Then perhaps our jobs aren't that different."

They moved through the rest of sickbay more slowly. Carter stopped at a surgical arch capable of projecting internal scans without touching the patient, then at a pharmaceutical replicator Jenny said could produce medication from stored molecular patterns. Neither system was fully functional. Both were far enough beyond Earth medicine that Carter could imagine Janet refusing sleep for three days if someone let her near them.

"When the power situation is stable," Carter said, "we bring her properly. Through the door, not the transporter."

"I'd like that. I could use another doctor who understands that a medical system isn't a vending machine for miracles."

"Janet will like you."

"She hasn't met me."

"I have a good feeling."

"Can I ask you something?" Carter said, once the laugh had faded. "How do you decide what to trust? Doctor Crusher's memory, I mean, versus your own instinct."

"I don't, most of the time. It's not like consulting a second opinion. It's just... there. Both of us, at once, doing the same thing." Jenny considered it further. "The only time I notice the seam is when they'd disagree, and honestly, that happens less than you'd think."

It didn't last. Jenny led the way toward a set of covered containers tucked into an alcove clearly chosen for privacy, and something in her posture changed, shoulders drawing in.

"This is Data," she said quietly, drawing back the covering.

Carter had read Ferretti's description. It hadn't prepared her: the disassembled pieces laid out with a care that was almost ceremonial, head and limbs arranged like someone resting rather than wreckage. The exposed circuitry along the head caught the light in patterns too fine, too deliberate, gold filaments branching in structures that looked less engineered than grown.

"How long did it take you to decide how to arrange the pieces?" Carter asked, quiet.

"Longer than I'd like to admit."

She worked for the better part of an hour, pulling instruments from her kit, checking connections, tracing pathways with the tip of a probe, murmuring observations that Jenny answered when she could and admitted ignorance of when she couldn't. Once, twice, she asked Jenny to confirm something specific, positronic pathway density, a term Carter had never encountered before tonight, and each time Jenny answered without hesitation, some deep, reflexive knowledge surfacing the same way it had with the LCARS panels.

By the time she straightened up, her back aching, she already knew what she was going to say and hated that she had to say it.

"I can't fix this," she said. "Not here, not with what we have. Whatever built his positronic matrix is generations past anything the SGC has access to. I'm not going to pretend otherwise just to make this easier to hear."

"I know," Jenny said. Level, professional, giving nothing away, except her hands, folded a little too carefully in front of her.

"That's not the same as 'never,'" Carter added, gently. "It's just 'not by me.' The science is sound. Somewhere, there's an engineer who could look at this and understand it. I'm just not her. Not yet.

"I know that too." Jenny looked down at the arrangement a moment longer than she needed to. "Thank you for actually looking. Really looking. Most people would have told me what I already knew and left it there."

"I'm not most people," Carter said, and reached out, briefly, to rest a hand on her arm. Jenny didn't pull away.

"I don't like it," Teal'c said.

Daniel looked up from the printouts, sensor logs going back three days now, patient and unbroken. "You don't like not knowing, or you don't like what the readings are telling you?"

"Both. The second troubles me more, and has for some hours."

"Walk me through it again."

They had spent most of the evening stripping information away from the reading. Jenny had shown them how to exclude the Enterprise's own power systems, then the temporary generators outside, then every radio and radar source operating from McMurdo. Daniel had watched the shape beneath the ice become clearer as the noise disappeared.

It was larger than the first scan had suggested. Not a single chamber but a set of connected spaces arranged around a central vault, their edges too straight and their angles too consistent to be natural. Ice movement had distorted the uppermost section. Everything beneath it remained intact.

"The structure is at least twelve thousand years old," Daniel said, turning one of the geological surveys around. "Probably much older. That's only the minimum based on the ice above it. We can't date the structure itself without a material sample."

"Which you are not getting by drilling two miles through the world's most politically sensitive ice cube," O'Neill had told him before leaving them with the data.

Daniel had not agreed. He had merely stopped asking for the drill in O'Neill's hearing.

Teal'c changed the sensor scale again. The supposed life sign remained at the center of the structure, a single narrow line with no visible cycle and no change in strength.

"We scanned from different points along the hull," Daniel said. "It isn't an artifact caused by the ship's damage. The position triangulates within six meters every time."

"And yet the depth does not change," Teal'c said.

"Why should it?"

"Because our instruments moved. The ice between the Enterprise and the structure is not uniform. A true biological reading should distort differently as the angle changes. This signal does not."

Daniel leaned over the display. "You think it's being transmitted to us."

"I think what we are reading may not be the thing itself."

"A beacon?"

"Or a warning. Or an invitation."

"Those are three very different possibilities."

"That is why I do not like it."

Teal'c laid a finger against the sheet, tracing the flat, unbroken line of the readout. "In my years serving the Goa'uld, and since, I have encountered a great many signatures of life and of technology built to sustain or conceal it. This matches none of them. Not in shape. Not in depth. Not in the pattern of its energy signature."

"Maybe it's something new."

"Perhaps. But consider the reading itself, not merely what it might mean. A living signature fluctuates. Breath, heartbeat, the small, constant noise of a body doing its work."

"This one doesn't," Daniel finished, his eyes on the flat line.

"It has not changed in three days, not by a fraction. It is too clean. I do not believe it breathes, and that troubles me more than any enemy I could name to you."

Daniel sat back. "That's worse, isn't it. Not knowing what it is."

"Considerably."

O'Neill found them an hour later, stamping snow off his boots. "So. Do I want to know?"

"Something's down there," Daniel said. "Two miles under the ice. Not Goa'uld. Not anything either of us recognizes, and Teal'c's seen a lot more of this galaxy than either of us has."

"And Daniel would very much like permission to go dig it up," Teal'c added, dry.

"Yeah, no." O'Neill shook his head before Daniel had finished opening his mouth. "We've got a diplomatic situation that could go sideways if China so much as sneezes wrong, a ship we're already trying to keep out of the papers, and a robot with no engineers to fix him. We are not opening a second unknown at this site this week, Daniel, I don't care how interesting it is."

"Jack..." Daniel started.

"Flag it. Send it up the chain. Somebody smarter than all three of us combined can decide when it's safe to go poke at it. It's not going anywhere, Daniel. It's waited this long already. It can wait a little longer."

Teal'c said nothing to that, only looked back at the unchanging readout one more time, and did not entirely agree, though he kept the disagreement to himself.

While they chased ghosts in the ice, thousands of miles away in a Sunnydale kitchen that smelled of burnt toast, Willow was hunting for answers of her own.

"Slayers." Giles closed the book he'd been holding, frustrated. "Ethan mentioned, in his letter, that two Slayers had died fighting the invasion. Two, plural. I've been trying to work out how that's even possible."

"Two what?" Willow asked, not looking up from her own book.

"The note says two Slayers died fighting the invasion," Giles said, closing the book. "Two, plural. I have been trying to work out what that means for the Calling."

"Wait, isn't there only ever supposed to be one? Like, one dies, the next one gets called, but it's still just one at a time?"

"Precisely my confusion." Giles pinched the bridge of his nose. "Unless something happens, between now and then, that changes the fundamental mechanism of the Calling itself."

"Something happens to Buffy?" Willow's voice went thin.

"I don't know, Willow." He softened his tone, seeing the fear on her face. "That's what worries me. I don't mean to frighten you with speculation, only to be prepared for whatever this turns out to mean.

"You'll tell me? If you figure it out?"

"Of course." He hesitated. "Willow, I would ask that you not mention this to Buffy just yet. Not until I have something more solid than a stray comment from a man under considerable duress at the time he made it."

"I won't." She chewed her lip. "It's just. A lot of stuff is coming, isn't it. Not just the aliens. Like, other stuff too."

"I fear so," Giles admitted, and went back to his book.

The call came three days later, an edge in the operator's voice that had everyone bracing before they knew what it was.

"Colonel O'Neill," Hammond's voice said, "I need you and Mr. Harris both. Today, if possible."

"That bad, sir?"

"Nothing's on fire, Colonel. But the White House has requested Mr. Harris's presence, and given recent events, I'm not inclined to make them wait."

"Recent events," O'Neill repeated, slow, and looked at Xander, who had gone very still.

"You want to explain the incident in Georgetown, Colonel, or should I?"

"The what now," O'Neill said, and every head in the room turned toward Xander at once.

"I can explain," Xander said, wincing hard.

"You went to Washington. Alone. Without telling anyone." Carter's voice climbed half an octave.

"I just wanted a day off! And it turned into a whole thing. There was a mugger, and Secret Service showed up out of nowhere, and it turns out the girl I was talking to was..."

The President's daughter," Hammond finished, his voice dry as dust. There was a weighted pause on the line. "Yes, Colonel. I have the full, several-page report on my desk. It includes photographs."

O'Neill looked at Xander for a long moment, exasperated and unmistakably fighting down a grin. "You couldn't have led with that? Any point in the last three days?"

"It didn't come up! There wasn't a good moment!"

"Regardless," Hammond said, "the President would like to meet you properly, Mr. Harris. Not a summons. An invitation, and I'd suggest you take it as one. Jet's coming for you and the Colonel's team, ETA within the hour. Doctor Giles will be brought in separately from Sunnydale to meet you there."

"Yes sir," O'Neill said, still looking at Xander, still fighting the grin. "We'll be ready."

The line went dead. Nobody said anything for a moment.

"So," Daniel said finally, a slow smile spreading. "The President's daughter."

"It's not, we were just talking!"

"Uh-huh," O'Neill said, grinning outright now, no longer bothering to hide it. "Kid meets Superman, saves the day, doesn't tell anybody there was a girl involved. Real smooth, Harris. Real smooth."

"I hate all of you," Xander muttered, ears going red, and went to go pack, while the rest of them tried, not very hard, not to laugh at his back the whole way out.
