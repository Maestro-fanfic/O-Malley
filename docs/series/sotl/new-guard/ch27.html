Cold Case

Scene 1

The soundscape of Cheyenne Mountain ops on a weekday was a war between HVAC fans, low-level chatter from the watch floor, and the steady, digital tick of a hundred live feeds all competing for Carter’s concentration. The Antarctic site’s telemetry had its own set of monitors—three at her station, all running at 200% refresh, the leftmost split-screened for thermal, seismic, and acoustic overlays; the center dominated by the raw data feed from the sensor cluster they’d jerry-rigged to the crash site; the right, a dedicated loop of Daniel’s “contextual artifacts” database, because he’d refused to let the server rest even for low-priority hours.

It was the third shift of her week and Carter had just finished a four-hour run modeling the boundary conditions for the structural recovery on Deck 12, which was still ten meters below snow and stubbornly refusing to admit defeat. She was two paragraphs into an after-action for Hammond when the anomaly popped.

The sensor alert was polite—just a double chirp in the left earbud and a yellow bar on the main monitor. Carter squinted at the timecode, then the amplitude. The resonance wasn’t noise, wasn’t even remotely like the usual run of seismic drift or the jitter from the nearby micro-quake zone. It was a tight, repeating set of spikes, harmonics too clean for natural formation, each one doubling in at intervals of exactly 1.21 seconds, and the echo through the sensor net was so pure she could almost whistle the fundamental.

She let it run for another forty seconds, eyes flicking between the trace and the grid overlay. The frequency vector didn’t line up with any signature in the library—no match to known drilling operations, not the shatter profile for the Enterprise’s hull, not even in the family tree of Starfleet-standard alloy fatigue. The signature was new. And deep. And so regular it could have been printed.

She flagged it and toggled the “peer” notification to Daniel’s corner of the ops ring. She did not bother with the written message; he’d already told her he preferred to “read the waveform first, ask questions later.”

He materialized at her elbow, a mug of government-issue coffee in one hand and his tie already loosened at the throat, looking more like a grad student at the tail end of a weeklong conference than an SG team lead on a classified shift.

“Please tell me that’s a new data set and not just a calibration spike?” he asked, leaning over her shoulder in that Daniel way that was equal parts respectful and incapable of boundaries.

Carter highlighted the cluster. “It started four minutes ago. Too deep for the surface ice, too sharp for a mudflow event. And here—” she brought up the lateral cross-section, the anomaly blooming like a tiny starburst inside the substrate, “—look at the distribution. It’s not random. It’s a lattice. Or something close to one.”

Daniel angled the mug to avoid splashing her workstation and squinted. “That’s… that’s not a typical resonance. It’s got a sort of—what’s the word?—crystal habit? No, wait, that’s geology. But this is… mechanical. Or engineered.”

She pulled up the raw return, cycling the color gradient from cold blue to a livid orange just for effect. “It’s four kilometers down, but it’s throwing off a signal like a tuning fork. The energy budget doesn’t make sense unless something is feeding it, or the structure is huge.”

Daniel’s eyes darted between the readout and his own memory palace. “Subglacial lakes are possible, but there’d be a thermal signature, and the lateral spread would be more diffuse. This is almost… planar? Like a plate?”

She already had the overlay queued. “I ran it against the last two weeks of seismic. The node is static. No migration, no fade. It’s either been inert for a very long time, or it just woke up.”

He set the mug down on the console, almost reverently. “That’s not good.”

Carter allowed herself a thin smile. “I’m not betting on ‘good’ until we get eyes on it.”

The third member of the ops ring, standing just inside Carter’s peripheral vision, was Teal’c. He wore the usual SGC-issue wool and stood with his arms folded, jaw set in a way that suggested he’d been watching both the screen and their conversation for several minutes. Teal’c did not glance away from the display.

On the third monitor, she’d left up a topographic render of the anomaly, a wireframe cross-section. Teal’c stepped forward, not breaking stride, and leaned in to tap the upper right corner of the shape. The gesture was precise; the kind of move that said he knew exactly what he wanted to see and didn’t need to ask.

Carter zoomed in. The corner came into focus, the lines resolving into an angular geometry that was all right angles and hard planes, nothing like the slouched, rounded forms of buried rock.

Teal’c’s voice was quiet but authoritative. “This structure is not natural.”

Daniel made a noise somewhere between a cough and a laugh. “That’s what we were just—”

Teal’c: “It is not of this world, either.”

Daniel frowned, then tried to correct himself. “Not human, you mean.”

“I mean,” said Teal’c, “it is not of this world.”

Carter hesitated, then ran a comparison against the offworld artifact database—Daniel’s favorite toy, which she kept as a read-only drive to avoid accidentally tripping an international incident. The match algorithm hummed for a few seconds before returning “no close analog,” which was both expected and deeply unsettling.

Daniel shook his head. “If it’s not Starfleet, and it’s not the ship, and it’s not geological—”

Teal’c didn’t blink. “There are worlds where such structures are common. But the design is not Goa’uld. Not Ancient. It is… familiar, but not the same.”

Carter watched him for a second, then said, “Do you recognize something?”

Teal’c looked at the screen again, his focus total. “I am uncertain. I require time.”

She nodded, and began packaging the data for escalation. SGC protocol required a double-signoff for anomalous finds, and Hammond wasn’t due in for another hour, but she could mark the file for immediate review. She dumped the sensor suite’s full packet, flagged it with PRIORITY—ANOMALOUS SUBSURFACE STRUCTURE, and printed the report to the secure line.

She picked up the phone, dialed the direct to Hammond’s office, and, when it rolled to voicemail, switched lines to the White House situation room. “This is Major Carter, SGC operations. I have a Priority escalation, flagged for Doctor Weir and immediate circle.”

The voice on the other end recognized her, and within seconds she had a confirmation: Weir was on the schedule, open slot in seventeen minutes. Carter finished prepping the file, snapped the backup drive from the console, and handed it to Daniel, who took it without spilling his coffee.

Teal’c stepped back, eyes still locked on the now-magnified angular corner. “Major Carter,” he said, quietly. “If the structure is what I suspect, we must be prepared for… unexpected behavior.”

Carter glanced up. “Define unexpected.”

But Teal’c only shook his head, lips pressed in a line, and said, “It is best not to define until we are certain.”

Daniel tried to lighten the air, as usual. “Maybe it’s a good surprise.”

Carter gave him a look, then grinned. “On this program, ‘good surprise’ is an oxymoron.”

She watched as the printer spit out the final page, the anomaly’s cross-section etched in false color, its corners too sharp for comfort.

She squared the report, clipped it tight, and headed for the briefing room.

In her wake, the ops floor hummed on, business as usual, but the sense of something new—something not in any protocol—hung in the air like the first static charge before a lightning storm.

Scene 2

There was a particular climate to the White House Situation Room at quarter-past seven: the predawn gray seeping through the frosted windows, the aftertaste of strong coffee and diplomatic anxiety, and the faint, tart ozone of freshly unsealed classified envelopes. Weir stood at the head of the briefing table, her copy of the SGC sensor packet splayed in front of her, the logo on the top sheet still wet enough to transfer to her palm if she pressed down.

President Bartlet sat directly across from her, his tie already loosened, the deep furrow between his brows a familiar sight to anyone who’d spent a year in the West Wing. To his right, Leo McGarry was in reading-glasses mode, skimming the summary sheet with the thumb-and-forefinger intensity of a man who could recite the Antarctic Treaty verbatim but refused to trust anyone else’s translation. Josh Lyman anchored the end, two legal pads to his left and a government-issue pen uncapped and ready, his gaze flicking between the paperwork and Weir’s face as if searching for the flaw in a closing argument.

Weir led with the facts. “This packet contains the most recent sensor data from the recovery site at McMurdo. The anomaly is located approximately 4.1 kilometers beneath the Ross Ice Shelf, just east of the main crash furrow. It is a geometrically regular mass, angular, with a resonance profile inconsistent with either natural formation or any known terrestrial alloy. It predates the Enterprise’s arrival by several thousand years, according to sediment displacement and stratigraphy. The signal is persistent and does not match anything in the SGC or NORAD libraries.”

She paused to let them digest. Bartlet raised a hand, the one with the presidential signet, and rotated it in a small circle. “Doctor Weir, is there any indication that this… artifact, or whatever it is, has anything to do with our new neighbor?”

Weir did not blink. “We don’t know, sir. It is outside the hull perimeter. There is no clear connection, except for proximity and the fact that the signal only spiked after the Enterprise’s arrival.”

Leo asked, “Is there any evidence the ship’s crash activated it? Triggered something that wasn’t active before?”

Weir considered. “It’s possible, but the timeline is ambiguous. We have only three months of solid sensor coverage prior to the event, and the anomaly’s activity may have been below our detection threshold. The most significant change is the pattern: it is now repeating, almost like a coded handshake. That started two days after the saucer section stabilized.”

Josh’s pen did a little jig on the pad. “And we’re sure the Russians or the Chinese don’t have a remote drone under there, lighting this up just to see what we’ll do?”

Weir said, “We’re sure, unless someone’s figured out a way to tunnel four kilometers through Antarctic bedrock without leaving any evidence on the seismic net. The anomaly’s mass is at least thirty tons, possibly much more, and it’s cold enough to suggest it’s been inert for millennia.”

Josh grunted. “So we have a pre-existing alien… something, sitting right under the most sensitive site on Earth, and we only found out because a different spaceship crash-landed on top of it.”

“Yes,” said Weir.

Bartlet looked up, eyes sharp. “I assume the real issue is what happens when we try to dig it out.”

Weir nodded. “Legally, sir, we’re in a box. The Antarctic Treaty’s Environmental Protocol requires a separate excavation framework for anything not covered by the current salvage operation. If we try to lump this in with the Enterprise recovery, the Chinese and Russians will protest. They have observer teams in Christchurch already, and the PRC has stated—in writing—they expect to be involved in any new discoveries.”

Leo closed the folder and tapped the edge. “So we either get ahead of this and file for a separate mandate, or we risk a diplomatic incident.”

“Correct,” Weir said. “If we move now, we can set the framework ourselves. If we wait, we cede the process to the Treaty parties. Either way, it’s going to be a race.”

Josh asked, “How long to draft a charter?”

Weir said, “If I have full support from the Legal team, six hours. If I need to negotiate language with the State Department, twelve. The Chinese will file an injunction within twenty-four, so the window is tight.”

Leo looked to Bartlet. Bartlet leaned back and, for a moment, seemed to weigh the merits of inertia versus acceleration. Then he tapped a finger on the folder, a crisp snap. “Draft the excavation charter. Attach a science-first preamble—make it clear we’re not sending a tank division, we’re sending geologists and archaeologists. Include observer language that’s generous, but doesn’t give up our point of control.”

Josh asked, “And if we get a protest?”

“We’ll get a protest,” Bartlet said. “But we’ll have set the terms. I’d rather go to the mat with the Treaty parties than wait and have this turn into an open auction.”

Weir closed her copy of the sensor packet. “I’ll have the framework on your desk by end of day.”

Bartlet nodded, then said, “And, Elizabeth? If it turns out this thing is the front door to a Stargate or a wormhole or a second spaceship, I want your word that you’ll tell me before you tell the press.”

Weir’s eyes glinted. “Sir, if it’s a front door, I will tell you before I even turn the knob.”

Leo stifled a smile. Josh said, “That’s a hell of a quote for the briefing book.”

Weir already had her packet in hand, the notes she’d made in the margin now highlighted in yellow. She didn’t wait for the formal dismissal; she nodded once, and was out the door before the silence settled.

In the room, Bartlet watched the closed door for a long beat.

“Leo, how many people do you trust to dig up an alien artifact without breaking the world in half?”

“Today? Just her,” said Leo.

Josh twirled the pen, looked at his pad, and said, “I bet the Chinese have their own Weir.”

Leo: “I hope she’s half as competent.”

Bartlet sat back, gaze drifting to the anomaly’s angular corner, now etched in a glossy printout in front of him.

“Some days,” he said, “I wish the future would just take a number and wait its turn.”

Nobody argued with that.

Scene 3

Scene 4

The White House did not wait for the drill to reach the buried structure. Before the first rig turned, State opened the Antarctic Treaty channel and put the proposed excavation framework in front of every party with a standing claim or observer right.

China and Russia asked whether the discovery was connected to the Enterprise recovery. Australia and France asked for the scope of the work and the safety exclusions. The United Kingdom wanted a technical representative on the next call. Nobody was being asked to approve an American fait accompli; everyone was being given notice before the ground moved.

Bartlet’s instruction was simple: disclose the limited drilling plan, offer observer access, reserve operational control for the site team, and make clear that the formal mandate was still being negotiated. The legal language went out with the science packet, not after the first alarm made delay impossible.

By the time Weir finished the last call, the consultations were underway. None of the Treaty parties had surrendered a claim, but none could credibly say they had been excluded. Carter’s rigs had a narrow window, and the diplomacy had started before the machinery did.

The wind in Antarctica didn’t just howl; it rewrote the rules of pressure, trading decibels for pure, molecular spite. Even through the triple-insulated shell of the staging tent, Carter could feel the flex of the gusts as she keyed in the diagnostic for the third time, fingers so cold she doubted she’d ever get the sensation back. The console bolted to the scaffolding railing had a backup heater, but even at maximum it just kept the frost from forming on the screen. The ambient read -31 Celsius and falling.

They’d set up the four-rig array in less than twelve hours. The engineering crew—half SGC, half civilian, all certified maniacs—had worked through three shifts without sleep, their work punctuated by the staccato of mallets chipping ice from hydraulic lines and the occasional bark of a tech as another cable went brittle and snapped under the wind load. The rigs stood in a staggered line, each one perched on a tier of bolted-together scaffolding that could, in theory, support a tank battalion. At the center: Carter and her portable, the nerve center for the whole show.

O’Neill had claimed a spot behind her, arms crossed, balaclava drawn so tight only his eyes showed. He checked his watch every few minutes, then said things like, “You still in there, Carter?” or “Let me know if I need to send in a search and rescue.” He’d made it clear to the team that if anyone had to go on a rescue, they’d better bring snacks.

The drilling had been clean—almost too clean, if Carter was being honest. Each rig advanced at exactly the projected rate, the pressure on the bit never rising above green, the thermal drift so steady she began to suspect the computer was padding the data. At 818 meters, she saw the first anomaly: a tiny, repeating spike in the accelerometer, interval fourteen meters, each one sharper than the last. Not the signature of sediment; not the work of glacial scouring, which should have been a messy, low-pass filter of vibrations. This was deliberate. Engineered.

She flagged the event and paged Daniel, who’d been pacing the perimeter with a thermal mug and what looked like a third-hand notebook. He appeared instantly at her elbow.

“Let me see,” he said, breath frosting in the air.

She slid the screen so he could read. “Every fourteen meters, a spike. Dead regular.”

Daniel squinted. “Layered composite, maybe? That’s… it doesn’t fit the model.”

Carter pulled up the seismic overlay. “The echo shows lateral continuity. It’s not a vein or a fossil bed. It’s—”

“Stratified,” Daniel said. “As in, someone put it there on purpose.”

From behind, O’Neill: “I hope you’re talking about a thousand-year-old beer cellar. Because if it’s another Stargate, I’m not cleaning up the paperwork.”

Carter half-smiled but kept her focus on the numbers. At 871 meters, the resonance doubled in amplitude. The core sample from Rig One was dead ice, but the camera on Rig Three’s probe showed a faint gray line on the down-angle. She logged it, checked the depth, and called to the tech at Rig Three: “Can you focus the camera at 882 meters? There’s a discontinuity in the substrate.”

The tech—wrapped in three layers and cursing in a dialect Carter didn’t recognize—tapped his comm. “Copy, Major. Rotating to south azimuth.”

The image swung around, the lens rim fogging at the edges. The seam was obvious: a dark, almost metallic plane, parallel to the ice but too smooth to be natural. The bit chewed through the last six centimeters and stopped, as if it had hit air.

“Pressure’s dropping,” the tech said, voice suddenly sharp.

Carter checked the gauge. “Negative flow. We’re venting into a cavity.”

O’Neill uncrossed his arms. “You said ‘cavity.’ I don’t like cavities. Cavities mean something is waiting inside.”

Daniel said, “There’s no biological signature. No methane spike. If there’s life, it’s not like anything we’ve seen before.”

Carter: “Or it’s dormant.”

The tech toggled the camera light. A halogen pulse lit up the void, and for a split second the feed went white with snow, then resolved: the drill bit hung in open air, and below it, flat and perfect as a mirror, was a sheet of what looked like brushed titanium, crisscrossed with veins of pale blue light, pulsing in perfect time with the frequency she’d logged earlier.

Carter let out a low whistle.

O’Neill, less impressed, said, “It looks like the inside of a high-end laptop. Only angrier.”

Daniel angled closer. “That’s not just a power conduit. It’s… it’s a circuit. Maybe a signal repeater.”

O’Neill: “Please tell me it doesn’t have Wi-Fi.”

Carter ignored the banter, snapped a still from the feed, and sent it up the chain to the science desk at SGC. She toggled her comm to the upper platform: “Teal’c, do you have visual?”

Teal’c’s reply came through with the calm precision of someone who’d never met a winter he couldn’t out-stare. “I do, Major Carter.”

“Thoughts?” she prompted.

There was a long silence, just the hiss of wind and the distant, rhythmic clank of the other rigs. Then: “This technology is not of the Goa’uld. Nor of the Ancients. It is… similar to the constructs found on P3X-984. But not identical.”

Carter’s memory supplied the reference: an outpost, long-dead, built by a species they still hadn’t catalogued.

Daniel wrote something in his notebook, then looked up. “Teal’c, can you read the inscription on the panel? There—lower right quadrant, just above the seam.”

Teal’c did not hesitate. “It is not a language I recognize. But the symmetry is deliberate.”

O’Neill said, “If you want symmetry, I can show you my old report cards. Straight C’s, all the way down.”

Carter keyed in the coordinates for the platform’s external sensor, sending the feed to Teal’c’s tablet. She watched as he panned in on the corner, the angular junction she’d noticed in the original data set. It was even more striking up close: a perfect, repeating motif, the angles too crisp to be anything but manufactured.

Teal’c’s voice, softer: “I have seen this motif before. On a device used for containment.”

Daniel scribbled furiously. “Containment of what?”

Teal’c: “Energy. Or perhaps something alive. The context was unclear.”

O’Neill, sotto voce: “I’m going to start voting ‘none of the above’ on all away missions, from here on out.”

Carter reran the frequency sweep. “If it’s a containment vessel, it’s still intact. The structure is stable. No signs of breach or decay.”

The tech at Rig Three—now openly sweating despite the cold—called up. “Should I retract the bit, or…?”

Carter hesitated, then: “Pause all mechanical movement. Hold at current depth.”

She sent the order to the other three rigs, locking them in place. The platform creaked in protest, a low groan that vibrated through the soles of her boots. For a moment, all was still except for the wind and the soft whir of the portable’s fan.

Daniel’s voice was uncharacteristically hushed. “If this is a containment structure, and we just drilled into it—”

Teal’c: “We should proceed with caution.”

O’Neill: “Carter, remind me again why the Air Force sends us instead of robots?”

Carter smiled, just a flicker, then said, “Because robots can’t improvise.”

She brought the camera back up, increased the resolution, and toggled through the spectrum. The blue veins pulsed, steady, now accompanied by a faint afterglow in the UV that she hadn’t noticed before. The energy signature was climbing, slow but undeniable.

Carter turned to O’Neill. “Sir, we need to inform command. And we need a protocol for first contact, in case whatever’s down there decides to say hello.”

O’Neill nodded, eyes still glued to the monitor. “Make the call, Carter. And maybe—” his gaze flicked to the feed, to the sharp corner and the logic of the pattern—“maybe tell them to read the manual before they poke it again.”

Carter grinned. “I’ll put it in the report.”

As she prepped the transmission, she caught Teal’c watching her, the angle of his head just slightly off—thoughtful, not worried, but not casual either. He nodded once, as if to say, It is your turn, Major Carter.

She keyed in the address for SGC, attached the priority tag, and in the body of the message wrote: “Contact with unknown structure. Possible containment. Recommend immediate escalation.”

She sent it.

As the wind battered the platform and the blue veins below flickered to a more insistent tempo, Carter wondered if maybe, just once, the surprise would be a good one.

But even she had to admit: the odds were not in their favor.

Daniel caught her glance and raised his mug, as if toasting the unknown. O’Neill rolled his eyes but didn’t disagree.

They stood together, three points on a frozen scaffold, staring at the future through a borrowed lens.

The veins pulsed, waiting for the world to catch up.
