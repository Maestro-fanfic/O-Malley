Chapter 15

The alley behind the dry cleaner's smelled like damp cardboard. He shucked the suit, folding it into the satchel, and pulled on jeans and a jacket that were reassuringly normal. Then he just walked, melting into a city that had no reason to know his face. He passed students with coffee, a couple arguing, a man failing to interest his dog in a tennis ball. Nobody looked at him twice. He circled the block again, just to be sure. The anonymity felt like a forgotten luxury. From a distance, he saw a flash of white columns—the White House—and deliberately turned away. That was for later. This walk was for him.

He found a bench along the canal and sat down.

"You're going to get pigeon-mobbed if you sit that still," a voice said, and he looked up to find a girl about his own age dropping onto the other end of the bench, coffee in one hand, a canvas bag of books sliding half off her shoulder before she caught it.

"I'll take my chances."

"Brave man." She popped the lid off her coffee, blew on it, didn't drink it. "First time down here?"

"That obvious?"

"You've got the look. Everybody who's actually from here walks like they're late for something, even on a Saturday, even when they've got nowhere to be. You're walking like someone who's never seen a canal before. Either that or you're just happy not to be heading somewhere specific."

He laughed, surprised out of him. "Yeah. Kind of."

"Zoey," she said, not quite offering a hand, just the name.

"Xander."

If the name meant anything to her, she didn't show it. He watched for the tell anyway, out of habit. It didn't come.

She glanced back along the towpath, not nervous exactly, but checking. "If three men in dark suits come through here looking like somebody misplaced a nuclear weapon, you haven't seen me."

"That happen a lot?"

"More than you'd think."

"Are they cops?"

"Not unless something's gone very wrong. They're people whose job is knowing where I am. I decided they could practice not knowing for an hour."

Xander looked along the canal. Nobody seemed to be running toward them. "Is that safe?"

"Probably."

"That's not a yes."

"It's the best answer anybody gets about anything lately." She took another careful drink from the cup. "I know how this sounds. They're good people. I just wanted to walk from one building to another without somebody checking the route first."

"Okay," he said. "I can understand that."

She studied him over the rim of the cup. "You said that like you actually can."

"Maybe I have people too. Recent development."

"And you got away from yours?"

"They were on a different continent when I left, so I had a head start."

That earned him a real laugh. "Now I want to know what kind of people you have."

"The kind with uniforms and clipboards."

"Mine have earpieces and no sense of humor."

"Mine has a sense of humor. It's just not always a good one."

She looked behind them again, then checked her watch. "Forty minutes, give or take. You want the tour you were pretending not to need?"

"I wasn't pretending."

"You walked past the same bridge twice before you picked this bench."

"You saw that?"

"I was in the coffee shop. The second time, I thought maybe you were following somebody. The third time, I decided you were lost."

He pushed himself up from the bench. "Fine. But if the tour's bad I'm telling the dark suits where you are."

"That seems fair."

"So what's the story, Xander," she said. "Skipping school? Running from something?"

"Neither. Both. It's complicated."

"Everything interesting is." She drank the coffee, made a face at the temperature, drank more anyway. "You go to school around here?"

"California. You?"

"Georgetown." First year. Watching his face for a reaction, and when it didn't come, something in her shoulders eased. "You're the first person in about a week who hasn't asked me a follow-up question about that."

"What's the usual follow-up?"

"You'll see." She waved it off. "Where in California?"

"Small town. You wouldn't have heard of it."

"Try me. I collect small towns nobody's heard of. It's a weird hobby, I know."

"Sunnydale."

"Huh. You're right, never heard of it." She tilted her head. "What's it like?"

"Weird," he said honestly. "Really weird, actually, in ways I can't totally explain. Good weird and bad weird both. High crime rate for a town that size. Great mall, though."

"Every town has a great mall. That's not a selling point."

"Fine, mediocre mall. Weird town. Really good friends, though. That part's not mediocre at all."

The tour began with her informing him that Georgetown was not actually designed for people who wanted to know where they were going. She took him along the canal first, pointing out the old locks and the narrow brick buildings crowded against newer storefronts. She knew which alleys went through and which ended behind kitchens, which coffee shop burned everything after noon, and which bakery sold the scones she'd been defending before he arrived.

"This is the famous scone?" he asked when she handed him half of one outside the shop.

"The only decent one in the district. Respect the institution."

He took a bite. It was dry, faintly sweet, and covered his jacket in crumbs. "Sunnydale has better doughnuts."

"That's not the same category."

"It is if you're hungry."

"You Californians have no standards."

"We have standards. They're just more relaxed."

They walked down toward the waterfront while she made him identify buildings. He got the Kennedy Center right because he'd passed it on the way in and the Watergate wrong because he'd expected something with a gate. Zoey told him that was a common disappointment and refused to say whether she'd made the same mistake when she was younger.

"So you really came all the way from California to wander around Georgetown?" she asked.

"I wanted to see Washington."

"The museums are that direction. So are all the things on postcards."

"I know."

"And you're walking away from them."

"The people I came here to avoid are mostly over there."

"Uniforms and clipboards."

"And suits. Lots of suits. Everybody has an offer or a plan or a form they need me to sign even though half of them can't agree whether I'm old enough to sign anything."

"You're not here with your parents?"

The question caught him hard enough that he nearly stopped walking. "No."

She waited for more, then let it go when he didn't give her any. "Mine would send people after me if I crossed the street without telling somebody."

"I'm starting to get that."

"It isn't always bad. Mostly it's people trying to keep me safe. There's a difference between a security blanket and a gilded cage, and some days it's hard to tell which one you're in."

"Yeah," he said. "That I understand."

They reached the water and leaned against the railing, watching a rowing crew cut past below them. The city looked different from there, lower and less official, the monuments out of sight and the traffic far enough away to become one steady noise.

"What are they offering?" Zoey asked.

"Jobs, mostly."

"At sixteen?"

"Seventeen," he said automatically, then remembered he wasn't, not yet. "Almost. And yeah. Jobs I'm not qualified for, jobs nobody's ever been qualified for, jobs that don't exist yet but apparently still come with lawyers."

"What do you want?"

He looked out at the water. Carter, O'Neill, Giles... they had all given him choices, good ones even. But they were all choices about what kind of hero to be, inside a life that was no longer his own.

"I want to go home," he said. The answer surprised him by being there. "I think. Go back to school, help my friends, finish the year. Still do the other stuff when people need me. I just don't want the other stuff to be the only thing I'm allowed to be."

"Then tell them that."

"They're the United States government."

"They still speak English. Most days."

"I don't think you get how much pressure we're talking about."

She gave him a look he couldn't read. "You might be surprised."

They moved on before he could ask. She showed him the university from a distance, explaining which buildings were worth entering and which ones existed mostly to make new students get lost. He told her about Sunnydale High's genuinely embarrassing mascot and the library where he spent most of his time, leaving out the weapons cage and the books bound in things nobody should use to bind books. She told him about a professor who treated a ten-page assignment like a moral test and a roommate who could sleep through construction but not through someone opening a candy wrapper.

He found himself talking more than he'd meant to, more than he'd talked to anyone who wasn't Giles or Buffy or Willow in longer than he wanted to think about. She didn't ask him to prove anything. Nobody here was writing anything down.

By the time they worked back toward the canal, her coffee was gone and the bag of books had moved from one shoulder to the other three times. The bench was still empty. They sat again, a little closer than before without either of them seeming to decide it.

"Can I ask you something," she said eventually, turning the cup in her hands, not quite looking at him, "and you can tell me it's none of my business."

"Sure."

"Everyone in this city's talking about the same thing right now. Superman, the wreck down south, all of it." She glanced at him. "You've got a face people would recognize, if they were paying attention. I've been paying a lot of attention lately. Are you going to make me ask?"

He went very still. "I, uh.

"You don't have to."

"No, I..." He looked at her properly, and something about how she was watching him, unhurried, not scared, made the decision for him. "Yeah. It's me."

"Okay," she said, like he'd told her the time.

"That's it? Okay?"

"What did you want, applause?"

"I don't know. Something. Nobody's ever just... okay'd it before."

"I'm not big on applause." Small smile. "For what it's worth, I'd have believed you either way. Wrong face for lying."

"It's easier to be honest with someone who doesn't already have a file on you. Everyone I've met this week... they decided what Superman should want before I even got in the room. You're the first person who's actually asked me."

"I get that," she said, and something about how plainly she said it told him it had nothing to do with him at all.

"How'd you clock it, anyway? People back home barely notice, half the time, and they've known me for years."

"Practice," she said. "I read a room fast. Comes with the territory."

"What territory?"

"We'll get there." She wasn't quite smiling, but close.

"So what about you," he said. "Turnabout. Who needs three men in dark suits just to get from one building to another?"

She hesitated, then smiled. "You really haven't figured it out."

"Should I have?"

"Probably not. It's just been a while since somebody didn't."

"So this territory of yours," he said. "Should I be worried?"

"Depends how you feel about the Secret Service."

He blinked. "Wait."

"Yeah."

"Wait, hold on, are you..."

"I'm not finishing that sentence for you." She was grinning now, unmistakably enjoying herself. "You can say it."

"You're the President's daughter."

"There it is." She toasted him with the coffee cup. "See, this is why I don't lead with it. People get weird."

"I'm not being weird."

"You're being a little weird."

"I'm allowed to be a little weird! You just told me you're basically American royalty and I'm sitting here in a jacket that still smells like a dry cleaner's."

"For what it's worth," she said, "you handled it better than most people do. Most people either apologize a lot or start talking really formally, like I'm going to report them to Congress for saying something wrong."

"I don't really have room to judge anyone else's weird reaction today. Given, y'know." He gestured vaguely at himself.

"Given that you can apparently bench-press a tank and I have a Secret Service detail, and we both just sat down like this was a normal Tuesday."

"When you put it like that it sounds like the start of a bad sitcom."

"Little bit," she agreed.

He never saw the guy coming. One second her bag was on the bench between them, the next it was gone off her shoulder and a man in a gray hoodie was three strides past at a dead sprint, and Xander's arm was already moving before the rest of him caught up.

His hand closed around the guy's wrist as he passed. That was all. He didn't grab hard, didn't think about grabbing hard, and the man stopped as abruptly as a car hitting a wall, his own momentum spinning him half around and dropping him flat with a dense, wet thud, all the air going out of him at once.

"Oh," Xander said, staring at his own hand and not quite sure what he expected to see there.

"Hey!" Zoey was already up, snatching her bag off the ground. "That's... thank you, that's actually mine..."

The man on the ground was groaning, cradling a wrist Xander was fairly sure wasn't broken, just extremely surprised. Then three men in dark suits came out of nowhere at a dead run, hands already moving toward their jackets.

"Ma'am, are you hurt..." the lead one started, before his eyes cut to Xander and went hard, careful, weighing him. "Sir, step away from her."

"He just stopped a guy from mugging me," Zoey said, stepping half in front of him without seeming to notice she'd done it. "He's not the problem."

"We've been looking for you for forty minutes." The agent's hand hadn't come off his jacket. "You are not supposed to..."

"I know what I'm not supposed to be doing, Agent Cross."

"This man overpowered an assailant with one hand," another agent said, low, watching Xander the whole time. "I need to know exactly who he is before anyone in this park relaxes, ma'am, and I mean that."

Xander held both hands up, slow, the same gesture he'd learned on a runway in New York what felt like a lifetime ago. "I promise, I'm really not..."

"Wait." The second agent's eyes had gone to his face properly now, and something shifted, recognition landing like a physical weight. "Sir. Are you... you're the one from New York."

"Yeah," Xander said, wincing. "That's, uh. Also me."

Superman," the third agent said, his voice flat with disbelief as he stared from Zoey to Xander. "On a park bench." He shook his head slowly. "Just... on a park bench.

"In fairness," Zoey said, "neither of us knew that about the other one until about ten minutes ago."

"That does not actually make this better, ma'am."

The tension changed shape rather than vanishing, three men recalibrating in real time what exactly they'd walked into, radios crackling low with half-finished sentences, one of them already circling the still-groaning mugger with visible relief at having a simpler problem to manage. Zoey looked between all of them with an expression that was somewhere between mortified and, unmistakably, delighted.

"Well," she said. "This is going to be a fun report to write up."

They gave her a minute. Agents a few feet off, radios crackling, the mugger being walked to his feet by a fourth agent who'd materialized from nowhere.

"So," Zoey said. "Neither of us picked this."

"No."

"And there's about to be a whole lot of people deciding things about both of us for the rest of the day."

"Story of my life since Halloween," he said, and she laughed, short and real.

"Can I tell you something kind of embarrassing?"

"Sure."

"When my dad first told us about you—before it went public—my first thought wasn't about national security. It was a completely selfish, 'Of course it's New York.' It felt like the universe was rubbing it in that some places get superheroes and we just get motorcades."

"That's fair. New York does kind of hog everything."

"I'm serious! And now I've met you before basically anyone else my age has, and I still can't tell anyone, which is somehow worse."

"Welcome to my life. I've saved a plane full of people and I still can't tell my own parents what actually happened to me on Halloween."

"That's rough."

"Yeah." He shrugged. "You get used to the not-telling part. Doesn't make it not weird."

"Are they going to notice when you go home?"

"That I can fly?"

"That everything changed."

He watched Agent Cross speak into his wrist, eyes moving over the towpath and back to Zoey every few seconds. "I don't know. My dad notices if the electricity bill is too high and my mom notices if a dish gets broken. Anything else... they're not big on it unless they need somebody to blame."

"I'm sorry."

"Don't be. You didn't do it." He nudged a loose piece of gravel with the toe of his sneaker and sent it farther than he'd meant to. "Giles notices. My friends notice. That's enough most of the time."

"Giles is clipboard guy?"

"Glasses guy. Clipboard is Carter. Uniform and bad jokes is O'Neill."

"You have categories."

"It helps."

Zoey looked toward the street, where a dark car had pulled up beside the curb. "They're going to call my father before I get through that door."

"The President."

"Yeah. Him too."

"Is he going to be mad?"

"First scared, then mad, then very calm, which is worse. After that he'll ask twelve questions that are secretly one question and pretend not to know he's doing it."

"What's the one question?"

"Whether I'm okay."

"You are, right?"

She looked at him, surprised, then nodded. "I am. Are you?"

He almost gave her the easy answer. Something about being invulnerable, because that was what everybody meant when they asked now. Instead he thought about the stacks of folders on the government conference table, each one a different plan for his life. He thought of Antarctica bright beneath him, of Giles flying home alone, and the answer he'd found at the railing.

"I think I will be," he said. "If I can convince everybody that helping people doesn't mean they get to own the rest of me."

"Say exactly that when they put you in the room."

"You make it sound easy."

"It won't be. Say it anyway."

Agent Cross finished on the radio and looked toward them. Whatever small grace period he'd allowed was almost gone.

"Here." She dug a pen out of her bag and wrote a number on the inside of a coffee-shop receipt, quick, before anyone could tell her not to, ink smudging where her hand moved too fast. "That's the switchboard. Ask for me by name, they'll figure out who you mean eventually. Might take a minute, they get a lot of strange calls."

He folded the receipt carefully, which was ridiculous given what his hands could do to steel. "I might take you up on that."

"You'd better." She glanced toward the agents. "For what it's worth, I don't actually get an hour like this very often. The one where nobody already knows the whole story before they meet me."

"Yeah," he said. "Me neither."

"We should probably compare notes sometime. On being handled. I feel like between the two of us we could write a pretty good book."

"I'd read that book."

"I'd write that book. Somebody's gotta warn the next kid this happens to."

"I'd like that," he said, and meant it more than he'd expected to when he said it.

An agent's hand landed at her elbow, gentle but final. The girl who'd been arguing with him about pigeons twenty minutes ago was gone, replaced by someone the agents were already talking to in a different register entirely, careful and formal, already moving her toward a car he hadn't noticed idling at the curb the whole time.

She looked back once before she got in. He lifted a hand.

He stood there after the car pulled away, the receipt a small, warm square in his fist. The mugger was gone, escorted off by an agent. The ordinary Saturday afternoon settled back over the towpath like a curtain closing on a strange play. After a moment, he walked back to the alley behind the dry cleaner's and launched himself, silent and alone, into the sky.

The report reached Leo's desk forty minutes later, three pages, unusually thorough for an incident report, the kind of thorough that meant whoever wrote it up knew exactly how much trouble they were in if they left anything out.

"You're going to want to sit down for this one," his secretary said, setting it in front of him.

"I'm already sitting down."

"Sit down more."

He read the first page standing anyway, then sat down properly for the second, and by the third he'd picked up the phone without quite deciding to.

"Get me the President," he said. "No, it can wait until he's off the phone with the Ways and Means people. Just, when he's off. Tell him it's about the daughter thing." He hung up, read the report a second time, and rubbed both hands down his face slowly. "A mugger," he said, to the empty office. "Of course it was a mugger."

Bartlet found him twenty minutes later, report still open on the desk.

"Leo. You look like a man who's had a day."

"Zoey slipped her detail again."

"That's not new information, Leo, that's a Tuesday."

"She slipped her detail again."

"I have a calendar for that, Leo. It's not a national crisis."

"She was in Georgetown. There was an incident. A mugging."

Bartlet's posture changed instantly. "Is she..."

"She's fine. Because Superman happened to be on the park bench next to her and stopped it."

Bartlet just stared at him. "Run that by me again. Slowly."

"He was there. They talked for an hour. Apparently neither of them knew who the other was." Leo pushed the report across the desk. "Cross has pictures."

Bartlet read in silence for a long moment, something moving behind his eyes that wasn't quite worry and wasn't quite something else. "Is she hurt?"

"Not a scratch. Neither of them are, near as I can tell, aside from the mugger, who Agent Cross describes as, and I'm quoting here, 'considerably more surprised than injured.'"

"And Mr. Harris knows who she is now."

"He does. She told him. Apparently only after he told her who he was."

"He volunteered that?"

"According to Cross, they'd been talking for close to an hour. Zoey recognized him before he recognized her."

"She would," Bartlet said. "She's seen every photograph we've been given and half the ones on television. Where is she now?"

"Outside. I thought you might want to see her before she went back to campus."

"You thought correctly."

Leo opened the door. Zoey came in first, still carrying the canvas bag from the report, Agent Cross behind her with the particular expression of a man prepared to accept blame for an event he had not caused and had done everything possible to end safely.

Bartlet was across the office in three long strides, his hands landing on her shoulders, checking for damage before pulling her into a hug that looked tight enough to bruise. She made a small, surprised sound, then her arms went around his waist, holding on.

"I'm okay," she said.

"I can see that."

"Then you can loosen up before you crack a rib."

He did, though not by much. "You were mugged."

"Someone took my bag. He had it for maybe three seconds."

"That's generally how mugging works."

"And then Xander stopped him."

"Xander," Bartlet repeated.

"That's his name."

"I know his name, Zoey. What I didn't expect was for you to be on a first-name basis with him before I'd even managed to get him in a room."

"Neither was I."

Bartlet released her and turned to Cross. "What happened to her detail?"

"She used a service exit in the language building right at the shift change, sir," Cross said. "She knows our rotation. By the time we confirmed she wasn't on her primary or secondary route back to the dorm, she'd been off-campus for ten minutes."

"So you lost her."

"Dad," Zoey said.

"I am speaking to the agent responsible for protecting you."

"I'm the one who decided to make that difficult. He found me."

"After you were attacked."

"After somebody grabbed my bag."

"That distinction matters a great deal less to me than it does to you."

Agent Cross remained still through the exchange. "The failure was mine, Mr President. I'll submit to whatever review is required."

"No," Zoey said. "It wasn't. I planned it because I know their procedures and because I've been watching them since I was twelve. If you fire him, I'll know the next person's procedures too."

"That is not the reassuring argument you believe it is."

"It isn't supposed to be reassuring. It's supposed to be true."

Leo moved around the desk and quietly closed the open report. The argument was already escalating beyond the facts on the page.

"Agent Cross," he said, "give us a few minutes."

Cross looked to Bartlet, received a nod, and left the office.

"I wanted one hour," Zoey said when the door had closed. "I know it was stupid. I knew it while I was doing it. But I wanted to walk somewhere without a car in front of me and another one behind me."

"You could have been hurt."

"I could be hurt walking down a hallway with six agents around me. You know that."

"The purpose of the six agents is to make it less likely."

"And most of the time I let them. Today I didn't. I'm sorry I scared you. I'm not sorry I wanted the hour."

Bartlet looked ready to answer, then stopped. Some of the anger left his face, not all of it.

"What was his state of mind?" Leo asked, cutting through the family tension. "Mr. Harris. Why was he in Georgetown?"

Zoey shifted her bag higher on her shoulder. "He was lost."

"In Georgetown?" Bartlet asked.

"A little bit in Georgetown. Mostly in everything else."

"He flew from Antarctica to Washington because he was lost?"

"He came to see the city. He said everybody involved with him has an offer, a plan, or paperwork. He wanted an afternoon where nobody was testing him or negotiating over him."

Leo and Bartlet exchanged a look.

"What else did he say?" Leo asked.

"That he wants to go home."

"Sunnydale," Bartlet said.

"He didn't tell me where home was until I asked about California. He wasn't giving away secrets. He just said he wants to finish school and help his friends. He still wants to help other people too, but he doesn't want that to be a job that takes the rest of his life away from him."

"Those were his words?" Leo asked.

"Close enough."

"He already agreed in principle to work with us," Bartlet said. "General Hammond's people were confident he was on board."

"Did they offer him a choice, or just a selection of ways to say yes? There's a difference, Dad."

"Colonel O'Neill is not a general."

"That's the part you're correcting?"

Bartlet rubbed a hand across his forehead. "No. It isn't."

"He told me helping people doesn't mean everybody else gets to own the rest of him," Zoey said. "I told him to say that when you put him in the room."

"You told Superman how to negotiate with the President of the United States."

"I didn't know the President was planning to negotiate with him."

"You had to have guessed."

"I guessed you were planning something. You've been carrying his picture around for days and asking me questions about Superman comics during breakfast."

Leo failed to hide a smile.

"There will be consequences for slipping your detail," Bartlet said.

"I know."

"Real ones."

"I know."

"And you're going to apologize to Agent Cross."

"Already did. Twice."

Bartlet nodded toward the door. "Go with him. I'll call you tonight."

Zoey turned to leave, then looked back. "Dad?"

"Yes."

"Ask him what he wants before you tell him what you need."

Then she was gone, the door closing behind her.

Bartlet remained where he was for a long moment. "You know, Leo, I've spent the better part of a week trying to manufacture a reason to get that young man through my door, and it turns out all I needed was my daughter sitting on a park bench in Georgetown."

"That's one interpretation."

"It's the interpretation that doesn't require me to spend the next hour imagining what could have happened if he hadn't been there." Bartlet picked up the phone on Leo's desk, then stopped with his hand around the receiver. "She was right."

"About which part?"

"We have been discussing what to do with him as though choosing from our offers is the same thing as choosing."

"We need his help."

"I know. That doesn't make her wrong." He lifted the receiver. "Get General Hammond. Mr. Harris has an answer for us—let's make sure he gives it to the right people."

The call reached Giles’s apartment before breakfast. Willow took it on the secure line, listened without interrupting, and then looked toward Buffy with the expression she usually reserved for a spell that had just acquired a second, worse purpose.

“The White House has the packet,” Willow said. “The invasion warning. The photographs. The date. All of it.”

Buffy’s hand closed around the edge of the table. “They know about Xander?”

“They know what he is doing. They know what the warning says. His name is still restricted.” Giles took the receiver from her and read the short confirmation twice. “The hand-off is complete.”

No one celebrated. The secrecy had protected Xander when the danger was people trying to own a child they did not understand. Now the danger included a fleet, orbital weapons, and governments making decisions without the information the Scoobies had been carrying alone.

“So what changes?” Buffy asked.

“Everything that requires resources,” Giles said. “Nothing that requires surrender. We tell the truth to the people who need it, and we keep deciding what Xander is willing to do.”

Willow opened a fresh notebook. At the top of the page she wrote: THREAT RESPONSE, not SECRET. Beneath it she began listing what the government would need from them, and what they would need from the government in return.
