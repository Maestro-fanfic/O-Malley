The Eve

Scene 1

The first hard confirmation came at 23:06, local, after a day of simulations, ward readings, and arguments over how much of the operational picture Xander Harris was entitled to see. SGC Cheyenne’s sensor array mapped a deviation at the edge of Saturn’s gravity well: an energy spike, mag-transit delta, amplitude so tight it was probably a pingback from the outermost ring.

“Sir,” the tech said, voice catching in a molar-grit yawn. “We’ve got a Thing on Scope Three.”

The watch supervisor, a woman with a scalp tattoo half covered by her regulation bun, didn’t look up. “You been eating in here again, Martinez?”

“No, ma’am.” The tech’s tone shifted from bored to not, a microchange. “It’s not ours. It’s not one of theirs, either. Doesn’t match any of the catalogued debris.”

Now she turned, eyes narrowing. “Send the feed to Station Two.”

He flicked it over. The second-level monitor bank, staffed by an off-duty pilot who’d once flown F-15s out of Lakenheath and now specialized in Not Dying at His Desk, picked up the thread. “Not a natural object,” the new voice confirmed. “Resonance on the back end is… what the hell? Goa’uld signature, but folded. Like a damped cloak.”

This, at last, got her attention. She pulled the mic down, toggled it to general. “Bridge to TOC. All officers to Ops floor. I repeat, all officers—”

The transmission cut out, but the message flew. In less than a minute, the room was full: O’Neill and Carter, fresh from an underground sim cycle; Daniel Jackson, clutching a notepad and a protein bar like the world’s most exhausted grad student; Teal’c, utterly silent but radiating readiness from the edge of the console bank. Within five minutes, the lights dimmed a shade, the wall displays reconfigured, and every member of the overnight command staff was arrayed in a loose horseshoe around the primary display.

O’Neill took in the readings with a fast, practiced glance, then pointed at the anomaly’s plotted course. “That coming straight at us, or am I imagining the worst?”

Carter, hands moving across the controls as if she’d never been away, punched up a telemetry overlay. “Course vector has a divergence at one-point-six degrees, but that could be drift. If it stays on this heading, it’ll intersect Mars orbit in ten hours, then Earth in twelve.”

Daniel, still chewing, said, “Could it be a probe? Or is that wishful thinking?”

Carter didn’t look up. “Power output suggests a relay craft, not an automated beacon. There’s a distinct dropoff after every pulse—could be a transmission cycle, or a shield modulation.”

Teal’c said, “The Goa’uld do not waste resources on probes. If they wish to observe, they will do so from a position of strength.”

A new light flashed on the secondary board. The supervisor toggled it on. “We’ve got a handshake on the military satellite net. Priority channel.”

O’Neill grunted. “That’s going to be the White House.”

“Patch it through,” said Carter.

The main wall display cleared, replaced by a logo that said EXECUTIVE OFFICE OF THE PRESIDENT. The Situation Room appeared next, its own blue wash making it indistinguishable from a crime scene at two a.m. Bartlet, hair askew but eyes alive, looked up from the table.

“Colonel O’Neill,” he said. “How many signatures?”

“One, sir. But it’s moving fast.”

Bartlet made a note, then handed the pad off-screen. “We’ve got chatter from our Euro partners and a noise report from Arecibo. They’ll know something’s up in the next fifteen minutes.”

O’Neill: “Do you want us to engage if it enters local space?”

Bartlet’s face flattened. “I want you to hold. We’re running the protocol with Chegwidden now.”

Carter, voice precise: “It’ll be in our outer atmosphere in less than a day. If it’s a Goa’uld strike, we’re not prepared for orbital assault.”

Bartlet nodded, once. “That’s why we’re running the protocol.”

The screen faded. The room went gray-blue again. Carter stood, then started a sequence on her wrist computer. O’Neill watched her.

“You know it’s going to hit the relay, right?” he said, not quite a question.

She nodded. “If the signals are in open space, the priority ping goes out. That was the new protocol.”

O’Neill: “I just hope he’s ready.”

#

In a small California kitchen, lit only by the half-functioning under-cabinet bulb and the afterglow of the living room TV, Xander Harris sat hunched over a stack of textbooks and a bowl of Fruity Pebbles gone to paste. The watch on his wrist vibrated—not a buzz, not a beep, but a pulse, cool and insistent, the way Carter had promised it would if the world decided to end at a really inconvenient hour.

He froze, spoon halfway to mouth. The watch vibrated again, but not like before. This time it had a rhythm: two quick pulses, then a pause, then one more—exactly as Carter had demoed in the practice run.

He looked down. The display had gone from its usual dead-black to a color he’d never seen: a pale, electric blue. It was the color of the world’s worst emergency.

He set the spoon down, wiped his hands on the knees of his jeans, and watched as the next signal came in: two more pulses, then a steady hum. He didn’t wait for the third sequence. He pushed back from the table, the chair legs catching on the vinyl floor with a noise that would have woken the house if anyone else cared.

He pulled the satchel off the back of the chair and walked it to the door, not running, just moving as if every step was a decision. The suit inside was folded the way Carter had told him—“So it doesn’t chafe”—and he unzipped the bag, laying the fabric across his forearm like a restaurant napkin.

He hesitated at the edge of the kitchen, the world’s dumbest version of Superman. There was the matter of the half-eaten cereal, the TV still running Wheel of Fortune, the dead porch light, the pile of unsorted mail. It was all so perfectly ordinary, and he felt, for the briefest moment, like an actor in a version of his own life he had forgotten how to enjoy.

He shrugged out of his T-shirt and shimmied into the suit. Before closing it, he switched off the civilian watch and tucked it into the satchel with his clothes. Then he clipped the flat transceiver into the concealed mount below his collarbone and switched it on. One short vibration confirmed the handoff: same channel, different identity, only one device answering. The fabric caught the overhead light and refracted it in a way that made the color look less comic book and more real.

He pulled the mask over his head, tucked the satchel back under his arm, and paused at the back door. The paint was flaking—every year it lost another layer—and the knob had a trick to it, a double-push so the latch wouldn’t stick. He did it, muscle memory, then stepped onto the concrete step.

The sky was not empty. There was the faintest streak of green across the south horizon, and the air was warm, unseasonably, for March. He flexed his fingers, once, twice, and then, with the same motion as a kid kicking off a curb, he left the step and drifted into the air, silent as the end of a dream.

#

On the SGC floor, the anomaly’s course was locked in. The blue line on the screen cut a perfect arc for Earth’s upper atmosphere.

Above the Harris back step, the flat transceiver beneath the uniform pressed three vibrations against his collarbone, then settled into a steady link.

Xander did not look back at the house.

He flew.

Scene 2

At 17:41 on a Thursday, Giles’s living room had been demoted to the status of “war room” for the duration of the emergency. The coffee table—a survivor of three teenage girls and a summer’s worth of pop culture marathons—now sagged under the weight of open books, a web of highlighter scars crossing ancient parchment and spiral notebook alike. The hand-drawn map of Sunnydale’s current ward perimeter had lost the fight with entropy: the edges curled, the overlay transparency crinkled, red marker bleeding into the ghost of last week’s spilled tea.

Willow hunched over her laptop at one end of the table, knees tucked under her, one socked foot bouncing in a nervous staccato against the leg. The laptop’s screen reflected in her glasses: columns of live data, the output from her homebrew ward-resonance parser, mapped in false-color against the city grid. Every fifteen seconds, she called a number, the cadence as steady as a metronome: “Six point two. Holding. Six point two, Giles.”

Giles, in shirtsleeves and vest, circled the reading on the map with a stub of grease pencil, lips pursed so tight the line almost vanished. “That’s six in the last hour,” he said, dry as chalk. “More than in the last week combined.”

Ethan stood near the fireplace, glass of something amber balanced in his hand, gaze fixed not on the data but on the space above Willow’s head, as if he could see the pattern they had been tracking in the air. “Reminds me of Prague, ‘93,” he said. “Same pressure-and-response structure we’ve been watching here. Something’s tuning the Hellmouth to see what it will do.”

Jenny Calendar, hair pulled back with a pencil, laid out the field med kit on the dining room table with a surgeon’s focus. The click and slide of scalpels, sterile wraps, and vials of saline was steady and unhurried, every movement precise. She checked the kit against a list she’d written in block capitals, then reset everything in the same order, the way a person does when the routine is the only comfort left.

Buffy came down the stairs two at a time, hair twisted up in a knot, patrol jacket already zipped, the business end of a stake sticking out of each of the three interior pockets. “Did I miss a shift in the apocalypse schedule, or are we just having a pop quiz?” she asked, eyes flicking from Willow to Giles to the back of Ethan’s head.

Willow: “Not a quiz. More like a stress test.” She tapped the screen, making the columns dance. “Every ten minutes, it pushes a little harder. I think it’s learning.”

Ethan sipped his drink, then set it on the mantel. “It’ll escalate again, right before midnight. That’s how these things go.”

Giles, not looking up: “It will escalate again when it pleases, and not before. Magic has no clock. We’re simply the timers.”

Jenny’s voice was calm, but the words landed with clinical gravity: “If the Hellmouth breaches, we’ll be the ones handling the casualties. Giles, is the medical station at the library still equipped?”

Giles: “Yes. And the secondary cache is in the teacher’s lounge, though I recommend you keep to the old corridors. The Navy detail can be… territorial.”

Buffy, adjusting her jacket, looked at the ceiling. “What about the rest of the team?”

Willow: “Oz is on site at the east gate. Cordelia’s monitoring the comms from her house—she said she didn’t want to wear the earpiece because it ‘ruined her aura,’ but she’s online.” Willow managed a twitch of a smile. “Xander’s MIA. Last signal had him at home, but he pinged the network five minutes ago. He’s moving, just not here yet.”

Buffy, a little softer: “He’ll be here.”

Ethan, moving off the fireplace, hovered behind Giles and peered at the map. “You ever see a distribution like this? It’s not random. Every spike is equidistant from the central node.”

Giles, after a beat: “I have, actually. It’s a classic pressure test. You find the cracks, you widen them, and then—”

Willow: “And then it all goes at once.”

Jenny: “Is the perimeter stable enough to hold?”

Giles: “It’s as stable as we can make it, given the town’s geology. The new circle of iron and salt has held for three consecutive nights.”

Ethan: “Unless they’re just playing with us. Which they are.”

Buffy: “Giles, are you calling the Navy in, or is this a ‘let’s see who survives the first round’ thing?”

Giles, dry: “Chegwidden’s task force is already on alert. But their first response will be isolation and observation. We’re on our own until we lose control, at which point the cavalry arrives with cameras rolling.”

Jenny, snapping the med kit shut: “Then let’s not lose control.”

Willow, staring at the screen, didn’t look up. “The next spike is coming. You can see it in the ramp pattern.”

Buffy: “Then let’s get ahead of it.”

Giles nodded, rolled up the map, and tucked it under his arm. He picked up the thickest of the old books, slipped it into a battered satchel, and paused at the threshold. “Everyone ready?”

Ethan drained his glass, set it upside down on the mantel, and said, “Always.”

Jenny threw the med kit over her shoulder, checked her pockets for gloves, and nodded once.

Willow snapped her laptop closed, tucked it under her arm, and rose in a single, smooth motion.

Buffy, already at the door, held it open. The group filed out, a not-quite-family with the rhythm of a pit crew or a very dysfunctional SWAT team.

At the edge of the walk, the sigils they’d chalked into the concrete at dawn glowed faint amber, a warm hush under the sodium streetlight. The glow pulsed in time with Willow’s last called number: “Six point four. Still holding.”

They stepped over the line, out into the air, into whatever came next.

Scene 3

Below the Antarctic, the corridor was colder than outer space and twice as quiet. The material—Carter still refused to call it “metal,” since nothing on the periodic table behaved this way—caught the helmet lights and ran them the full length of the tunnel, so every step produced a ripple of white along the wall, like walking inside a frozen optic cable.

Carter was point, the relay tucked under her arm and a patch cable running straight from the data port to the sleeve of her suit. Behind her, O’Neill, Teal’c, and the two support techs fanned out at regular intervals, their footsteps crisp, deliberate. The further they descended, the more the tunnel narrowed, until the four of them were single file, breath clouding out in little puffs that vanished before hitting the wall.

The positronic interface had been dead quiet for the first seventy meters, then burst to life, the signal so strong that the nearest field generator shuddered with the force of it. “There,” Carter said, out loud but mostly to herself. “The relay node is active.”

O’Neill, behind her: “Can it hear us?”

Carter toggled her mic. “No, but it’s listening. Every watt of power in the suit’s backup array is being drawn to the interface. It wants to talk.”

Teal’c, even and low: “Then let us hear what it has to say.”

She jacked the connector to the panel and watched as the readout on her arm filled with line after line of compressed data. It looked like log output—block after block of digital time stamps, each referencing a sub-event in the core. But the resolution was staggering. “It’s sending a live update,” she said. “Every time the Goa’uld signature spikes, the system logs and relays it.”

O’Neill: “Is it just a recorder? Or can it do something about the problem?”

She ran a quick checksum, overlaying the last hour’s worth of pulses. “It’s not reacting yet. But the volume of data means something is about to tip.”

At the bottom of the tunnel, the walls flared outward, forming a capsule-shaped chamber around a single chair, the sort of thing you’d expect in a control room or an execution chamber, depending on your mood. The chair was fused with the wall, one piece, but there was a socket at its side that matched the relay’s input port.

Carter slotted the relay into the port and waited.

Nothing.

She tapped the activation sequence—three quick clicks, then a hold.

The world flickered, just once. Then the chamber hummed, the sound low, a bass note that vibrated her bones and made O’Neill glance up, as if expecting a ceiling collapse.

The interface spoke—not words, just a solid wall of output.

Carter patched the stream to the wrist display. The first output block was dense, schematic, layered with visual overlays. She slowed it, frame by frame, trying to parse the intent. “This isn’t a weapon,” she said. “It’s a map. Or… coordinates.”

O’Neill, arms folded, “Bottom line it, Carter.”

She pulled up the full output and rotated it. There, at the heart of the schematic: a shape she knew from every star chart the SGC had ever recovered, rendered here in a geometry more perfect than any artist’s impression.

“It’s a relay,” she said. “This whole vault, the crash site, the chair—none of it is an endpoint. It’s a jump point. The Ancients—or whoever left this—meant for the data to be transmitted, not stored.”

O’Neill’s jaw set. “Transmitted to where?”

She moved through the data, zoomed in. “There’s a second set of coordinates, buried in the signature. Not in this galaxy.”

Teal’c, without inflection: “The Ancients anticipated this. They left a warning, and a message for anyone who found it.”

Carter nodded, but she was already running the overlay, plotting the outgoing signal against the SGC’s best models of hyperspace communication. “It’s timed to the arrival of the Goa’uld. The instant their ships enter the solar system, the vault transmits.”

O’Neill looked up at the capsule ceiling. “How long until that happens?”

Carter checked the last output block. “They’ll be in visual range in about four hours.”

O’Neill: “Plenty of time to get cold and die. Let’s pack it up.”

She slotted the relay out, three clicks, and wiped the buffer to three separate drives. The hum from the walls went soft, then cut out entirely, leaving them with only the crunch of boots on tunnel floor.

They started back up the corridor, faster this time. The cold felt sharper, less like an inconvenience and more like a reminder.

Behind them, the interface sat dark, the last pulse of the outgoing message echoing in the walls long after they were gone.

Scene 4

The edge of the sky was a lie, but it was the best kind: a place where you could float for hours with nothing but ozone and the memory of wind, where the horizon was a secret handshake between blue and black, and the only gravity was what you chose to carry with you.

Xander hovered at eighty thousand feet, give or take. Below him, the Pacific curved in a lazy exhale, silvered by moonlight, the edges blurred by the night. Above, the stars were different than on any poster, any science show he had ever seen: too sharp, too close, as if someone had scrawled them in with a diamond-tipped blade. The flat transceiver beneath the suit was still warm from the launch, its clip secure against his ribs and its controls reachable through the fabric.

He held there, not moving, not shivering, even though the suit wasn’t meant for this kind of cold. The mask kept his breath from freezing, but the air bit his lungs in a way that said “You’re not supposed to be here.” He ignored it.

The world was so big from up here that even his best jokes seemed to flatten, their punchlines lost in the static of the wind. He tried a few out loud, anyway, just to hear how they sounded.

The first time, nothing answered.

He turned his eyes up, dialing the focus the way Carter had shown him: long-scan, then tight, then back to infrared, then ultraviolet. The trick, she’d said, was in the light shift. Goa’uld ships didn’t just travel fast—they bent the spectrum around them, left a signature like a fingerprint in the gap between visible and possible.

He swept left to right, slow, then again. He counted stars—one, two, three, four thousand—and waited.

On the third pass, something changed. It wasn’t a sound, wasn’t a flash. Just a tickle at the edge of sight, a pinprick that moved wrong. Not fast, not jittery—just too precise. It bled into focus, blue-shifting for a millisecond, then red, then gone. And then, behind it, two more.

He felt the first flicker of fear, the good kind, the kind that made you want to laugh and throw up at the same time. “There you are,” he whispered, not sure if it was for himself or the ships.

The ha’tak came in from the shadow of the planet, their hulls catching the last knife-edge of sunlight and glowing like angry jewels. Not perfect formation—one, then a lag, then two together—but close enough that you’d have to be blind not to see they were moving with intent.

Xander’s jaw tensed. He didn’t ball his fists, didn’t clench his teeth. Just… set himself, the way you do before a punch you know you can’t dodge.

He counted the seconds until the first ship hit the upper atmosphere, then the next. The concealed transceiver vibrated again, a single pulse, like it wanted to remind him of the math.

He held there, hovering, the whole world stretched out beneath his feet and the universe waiting above. The points of light grew, three little blades coming straight for the blue.

He waited.

He was the only thing in the sky that didn’t move.

And, for a moment, the world felt quiet enough to hold.
