The New Normal

Scene 1

The library had always smelled of dust and ambition, but today it was competing with packing foam and the antiseptic tingle of fresh plastic. Willow had the first crate open before Petty Officer Tran finished the sign-in paperwork, and she was already wrist-deep in a sandwich of bubble wrap and brown paper sleeves. Her hair flared in static every time she shifted a volume, leaving ghostly red arcs floating above the pile.

Tran, in civvies, stood a full step inside the door and failed to blend with the furniture. The SGC badge on his lanyard winked like a nervous tic. He clutched his clipboard the way some people might clutch a life vest on a leaky boat, his eyes tracking Willow’s every move with the apprehension of a man briefed on what could happen if you mishandled certain grades of artifact.

Giles prowled between crate and desk, lifting each book with a reverence reserved for rare editions and high explosives. He would rotate the volume, squint at the spine, and either set it on the “approved” stack or make a new stack entirely, labeling each with a yellow Post-it on which he inscribed in tiny capitals: “RESTRICTED — CONSULT FIRST.” Willow noticed he was being more granular than usual; one stack was already three thick, but she saw him hesitate twice before setting another book down.

Willow reeled off questions in a chain-gun burst. “Petty Officer, is this the entire shipment or did you stage a partial? Because the manifest lists a Sumerian lexicon that’s not in this crate and I’m also supposed to sign for two units of sol-gel but the vials are missing serials. Did you cross-load those to medical or—”

Tran interrupted by checking a box on the clipboard, not looking up. “Second crate is still in the vehicle. Also, the lexicon was flagged at dispatch for additional decontamination. ETA, thirty minutes.” His voice was monotone but not unkind, and when Willow threw him a half-glare, he added, “Protocol, ma’am. Not my idea.”

Giles cut across Willow’s third query, which involved the volatility of pre-packaged incense near unshielded iron, with a level “Willow,” that had enough weight to make even Tran glance up. Giles eyed the vial she was brandishing up to the ceiling light, and she set it down with exaggerated care, as if putting a baby bird back in a nest.

At the edge of the table, a rolled bundle of felt unraveled, revealing a portable ward array: six slender rods bound with copper wire, each tip dusted in an iridescent powder. Giles plucked it up, traced the line of the rods with his thumb, and turned it so the copper didn’t touch the metal table rim. He made a note in his pocket diary, cross-referenced it against a page of names, and set it between the two stacks, a Switzerland of magical armaments.

Willow cross-checked the SGC manifest against her own pre-cataloged list, which ran two legal pads in dense blue ink. She asked, “Chain of custody on the reagents? Some of these are time-sensitive, especially the aqueous stuff.” Her tone was sharp, but Tran seemed used to it.

“Maintained at four degrees until transfer. Ambient temperature at handoff is logged on the form.” He offered her a carbon copy. Willow took it, scanned, and added it to her notebook.

There was an undertone to the activity, the rhythm of people who had done this before but still hadn’t decided if they liked it. The crate made a thunk on the floor every time someone set it down, echoing off the institutional ceiling tiles. Tran’s rubber-soled shoes squeaked on the linoleum, a small anxious noise in the grand clockwork of library silence.

Giles moved to the whiteboard near the card catalog, where two columns had appeared in his upright script: “Watcher Archive Integration” and “SGC Supplement — Verify Provenance.” He drew a line between three titles in the first column and made a circle around the phrase “Cross-verify all translations.”

Xander sat on the table at the far end, one foot resting on a battered chair, a doughnut in one hand and his gaze drifting between the activity and the paper napkin on his knee. He looked like someone who’d shown up to a group project because he promised to bring snacks, and then remembered he had algebra to finish. The backpack at his feet was zipped to the teeth, but the mesh pocket bulged with the fresh first-aid kit he’d picked up from Chegwidden’s support element that morning. He considered it a security blanket, even if he was usually the last person to need one.

He watched the cataloging show for a while, then nodded at the “restricted” pile. “That one’s restricted? It’s got a bunny on the cover.”

Giles didn’t look up. “It is not a bunny.”

Xander tilted his head. “I dunno, Giles, those are some pretty big ears.”

Willow, not missing a beat, said, “If it’s the Eisenstadt edition, it’s a German translation of a 16th-century bestiary with deliberate obfuscation on the cover. I think the ‘bunny’ is a demon in disguise.”

Tran, under his breath: “Would explain a lot about my sister’s pet.”

Giles finally looked up, his glasses perched precariously on the bridge of his nose. “Thank you, Willow. Petty Officer, if the Sumerian lexicon is in the next crate, we will refrain from opening any of these until its arrival.” He gave Tran a nod, the sort reserved for visiting dignitaries or competent but unproven subordinates. “You are welcome to retrieve it. And thank you for your diligence.”

Tran looked relieved, set the clipboard on the table, and excused himself with the kind of deference you normally see at a very polite hostage exchange.

The library was quieter after he left, the only motion the slow settling of Willow’s hair as she stacked the last book. Giles made a final pass through the crate, running his palm along the inside wall, then closed it up. Willow checked the clock and said, “If they keep this up, we’ll need to expand into the AV closet.”

Giles, absently: “I believe that’s Principal Snyder’s current office, so perhaps we should avoid giving him a reason to audit our space.”

Xander finished his doughnut, dusted the sugar from his hands, and said, “You want me to move these to the back room?” He gestured at the approved stack.

Willow looked at the pile, then at Giles. “Should we log them before they move?”

Giles said, “Just make a note of the current location and date. We’ll do a formal intake after the Sumerian arrives.”

Willow wrote the details on a Post-it, in red marker, and stuck it to the top volume. Xander gathered the stack under one arm, then reached for a second doughnut. He caught Willow’s eye and offered her the last one, but she waved him off, already scribbling a reminder in her legal pad.

As he walked the stack to the back office, he said, “I still say it’s a bunny.”

Willow called after him: “Then you carry it, Xander.”

He made a show of holding the book at arm’s length, as if it might bite.

Giles re-centered himself at the table, taking a moment to align the yellow Post-its into a neat, horizontal row. He glanced at Willow, whose nose was now buried in the carbon copy from Tran, and asked, “Do you ever tire of being correct, Willow?”

She grinned, not looking up. “I find it soothing.”

Giles smiled, then took a breath, as if settling the atmosphere with a dose of order.

Xander returned, dusted off his hands, and said, “So what’s the plan after the great bunny conspiracy?”

Willow ticked through the inventory list. “I have to cross-check every author field against the Council’s blacklist. There are at least four duplicate titles, which means either the SGC is hedging bets or they’re not sure what overlaps with our collection. After that, I’ll catalog the ward arrays and check the chemistry kits for expiration.”

Xander nodded, like he’d expected that. “Anything fun in the new batch?”

Willow’s lips quirked. “The bestiary’s second section is supposed to have a classified diagram of a Slavic werewolf. The last copy that surfaced in Prague caught fire when someone tried to digitize it.”

Giles, with a dry edge: “The SGC would do well to read that footnote before their next scanning project.”

Willow and Xander exchanged a look, both thinking of the time the library’s microfiche reader had caught fire during a research session on Lithuanian spirits. It had not been their best day.

The clock ticked past the hour. Willow started logging the day’s accessions, Giles reshelved the empty crate with unnecessary care, and Xander took his seat again, back where he started.

Willow looked at her list, then at Giles. “You think they’ll send us anything actually useful next time?”

Giles did not look up. “I suspect the definition of ‘useful’ is a moving target.”

Willow grinned, made a note in her legal pad, and started a fresh page.

Xander finished the last doughnut, considered the crumbs on his lap, and thought, not for the first time, that this was a better kind of weird than he’d ever hoped for.

The silence, when it settled, was not empty but full—of books, and purpose, and the ongoing argument about bunnies.

Scene 2

Sunnydale’s industrial strip could have been anywhere in California after midnight: the cracked blacktop, chain-link fence bowed out where delivery trucks had years ago clipped its spine, the cold-storage plant’s wall looming in slabs of painted concrete and the chemical whiff of freezer burn baked into its bones. Tonight the only motion was a pair of sodium-vapor lamps burning holes in the dusk and the wind agitating a dozen plastic wrappers across the lot.

Urgayle was first on the scene, the kind of early that was never quite out of style in her line of work. The folding table was up in thirty seconds, the orange cones faster, and the protocol card centered on the table’s edge, its plastic corners squared to the battered wood. The card was heavy with signatures: Chegwidden, O’Neil, Urgayle herself, and one line at the bottom in ballpoint blue, the only part that said “Buffy” and nothing else.

She swept the perimeter, every movement a quiet claim of space, then set her hands flat on the table and waited.

Buffy arrived exactly three minutes late, which was right on time for a meet where she wasn’t sure if she was the subject, the trainer, or the test. She clocked the cones, the table, the waiting, then zeroed in on the protocol card before even glancing at Urgayle.

She lifted the card, read the line referencing “no command override / evac only on request,” and set it back down. Her mouth didn’t smile, but her shoulders moved down a notch.

“Let’s get this out of the way,” she said. “You don’t call the play. You don’t pull me out unless I ask, or I’m unconscious. If I say clear the area, you clear it.”

Urgayle nodded. “Understood. My job is to make sure you don’t have to protect me while you work. That’s the whole job.”

Buffy looked at the table, then at Urgayle, as if trying to locate the trick. She didn’t find one. “You want to see the run?”

“Always good to start with a baseline,” Urgayle said.

Buffy gestured at the lot. “All yours.”

Urgayle moved through the course with mechanical precision, her path slicing the gaps between shipping containers, hugging corners, sometimes pausing at the mouth of an alley or ducking behind a dumpster. She used her flashlight once, low and quick, then made the last thirty meters in a sprint and reappeared beside the table as if nothing had happened.

Buffy watched with arms folded, feet planted wide. “Standard pattern. Textbook.”

Urgayle said, “Want to show me yours?”

Buffy walked to the starting cone and waited for Urgayle’s nod. Then she ran the course, but her route was nothing like the one before: she went high on the pallets, skipped two entire aisles, and appeared from the blind side of the shipping container not just faster but at an angle that Urgayle’s own search had never covered. She loped back, a tiny half-smile on her lips.

Urgayle wrote something in a notebook. “You see the world differently.”

Buffy shrugged. “I see predators.”

“Noted,” Urgayle said, and did not appear bothered by the data point.

Next, Buffy set up the scenario: Urgayle would play “civilian pinned behind the container, unable to self-rescue.” Buffy’s job was to clear the simulated nest using a set of wooden training stakes, then extract Urgayle in the fastest window that didn’t compromise either party.

They ran the drill three times. Urgayle started the timer on her watch each time, logging the moment Buffy moved and when the “casualty” was clear. On the third run, Urgayle called out, “Back is exposed at entry point two.”

Buffy, without turning, said, “Not unless you’re moving. I hear you shift your weight. That’s the tell.”

Urgayle closed the notebook. “No point of approach is safe, then?”

“For them? No. Not in my town.” Buffy dusted her hands and leaned back against the table.

“Let’s talk use-of-force,” Urgayle said.

Buffy was ready. “If it looks human, I don’t kill it. If it’s a vampire, I do. If it’s not clear, I stall until it is.”

“Extraction rule?” Urgayle asked.

“My call. Or if I’m down and not moving on my own power in ten seconds, you pull me.”

Urgayle said, “Medic response window is forty seconds from my call to hands-on. That’s if we’re staged within a quarter mile.”

“Don’t let anyone bleed out,” Buffy said. “Otherwise, stand back and watch the show.”

Urgayle reached into her jacket, produced a compact first-aid kit, and set it on the table. “This is yours, then. You know how to use it?”

Buffy took it, gave it a once-over, and clipped it to her belt. “Every other week, someone in my group ends up bleeding. Not always the same person.”

Urgayle nodded, as if that was more data for the model.

They reset the cones and ran a final dry run, but it was more rhythm than test. Both knew how it would go.

After, while packing up the cones, Urgayle said, unprompted, “Had a team in Bosnia, ten years ago. Training exercise. Rules were strict. One guy improvised, went off script, saved the objective. During the debrief, command said, ‘Right outcome, wrong method.’ Spent years wondering if that was true.”

Buffy tossed a cone from hand to hand, thinking. “In my experience, the outcome is usually the only thing that matters when you’re standing over a body.”

Urgayle smiled, small and quick, and loaded the cones into the trunk.

They didn’t shake hands. Urgayle gave a nod, precise, and Buffy matched it. The sodium lights buzzed overhead, the wind rattled the chain-link, and the lot returned to neutral.

The next fight would be the real thing, but both knew the rules. For now, that was enough.

Scene 3

The cannery wasn’t haunted, it was just dying. The only ghosts in the peeling letters above the loading dock were the memories of twenty years of frozen fish and underpaid graveyard shifts, now replaced by broken windows and the chemical reek of ammonia still baked into the grout. Tonight, the lot was dark except for the hush of tires on wet asphalt and the faint blue blink from the comms van parked a hundred feet up the block.

Inside the van, Reyes had her eyes on the drone feed. It traced a sickle of infrared over the cannery’s roof, the image flickering as the microcopter dodged a tangle of utility lines. She double-tapped her earpiece: “Buffy, confirm—visual on four heat signatures. Repeat, four. Two basement, two upper, no movement on ground floor.”

Buffy’s voice came back level. “Copy four. Entry in ninety.”

Urgayle was already moving, keeping Xander at her left, Buffy five paces ahead. Xander watched Buffy, mostly, her stride shifting with each new surface, her attention split between the earpiece and the bat-shaped shadow of her stake bouncing on the wall. He had his own stake, a new one from the supply kit, and the handle still had sharp edges where the lathe hadn’t sanded it smooth.

At the side door, Urgayle picked the lock in four seconds flat, and they went in single-file, shoes silent on the unfinished concrete. They passed through the main hall, shelves still lined with the detritus of the pre-bankruptcy inventory—boxes, sacks, a stack of “for here or to go” sandwich wrappers that looked like an archaeological dig of failed branding.

At the bottom of the stairs, Buffy paused, then held up two fingers. The earpiece crackled with Willow’s voice, tinny but precise: “Two hostages, both alive. Boy’s been bit, bleeding a lot. You’ll see him on your left when you enter. Girl’s older, also zip-tied, seems less injured.”

Buffy said, “Thanks, Will.” Then she was down the last two steps and through the door, Urgayle and Xander close behind.

The basement ceiling was low enough that even Xander had to duck. It smelled of wet stone and old mold. A bare bulb hung from a bent conduit in the center of the room, casting hard shadows.

The vamps were exactly where the drone had shown them: one perched on an upturned crate near the boy, another leaning against the back wall, arms crossed. The hostages were zip-tied to a support column, the boy slumped forward, blood oozing slow and black from his neck. The woman stared at the floor, jaw set, every muscle in her arms standing out in white relief against her skin.

Buffy took the first two in under eight seconds. She stepped past the crate and drove the stake up through the first vamp’s ribs, twisting as she pulled back. The second tried to move, but Buffy caught him with an elbow in the jaw, then a stake through the chest as the dust from the first hit the floor.

Xander handled the third. He’d seen the motion in the corner of his eye: a blur as the vampire came out of the utility room, jaws open, eyes wide. Xander pivoted, caught the vamp by the collar, and slammed him against the cinderblock so hard that the concrete cracked. He drove the stake home, easy as breathing.

The fourth tried for the stairs. Urgayle was waiting at the top, her feet planted, hands low. The vampire came up fast, arms spread, but Urgayle just sidestepped and thrust a stake into his chest with one clean motion. The vamp went slack, then dust.

Reyes called out on the radio, “Basement secure, four down. Med on approach.”

Buffy went to the hostages, flicked a knife from her belt, and cut the zip ties. The boy was still conscious but shivering, eyes locked on the growing pool of blood under his chin. The woman muttered, “Thank you,” then tried to stand, but her legs folded.

Xander caught her, guided her down to sit, then pressed the gauze from his own kit to the boy’s wound. The blood soaked through instantly. He kept pressure on it, hand steady, and waited for the medic.

Cho arrived in forty-one seconds, per Urgayle’s mental clock. She knelt, gloves already on, and swapped Xander’s gauze for a pressure bandage in a single practiced motion. The boy gasped, but Cho kept him upright, murmuring something low and soft.

Willow’s voice, softer now, filled the earpiece: “Nest history says two prior sightings, both logged as ‘unconfirmed’ on the record. We didn’t escalate.”

Buffy, still kneeling by the hostages, said, “Unconfirmed.”

Willow: “There are forty-seven like that in the last eight months. I made a list.”

Nobody said anything for a while, just the hiss of the first-aid wrap and the slow, hard breathing of the woman as the shock wore off.

Urgayle logged the event on her clipboard: “Clean-Up #4,” not #3. The first solo assist had happened two nights earlier, no Scooby presence, just Navy and Cho and a mostly-dusted nest that never made it into the regular files.

They left the basement in silence, all motion focused on getting the living outside.

On the way back to the van, Xander rode shotgun in Urgayle’s vehicle. He opened the new first-aid kit and ran his thumb over each tool, each bandage, as if learning the contents by touch. His left wrist itched, so he pulled back the sleeve and looked at the watch Carter had given him. It buzzed once, then again, a new pattern—short, long, short. He tapped the face, saw coordinates and a single tag: CIV-ASSIST.

Not a favor, not a casual call. A request. It sat on the display, insistent and official.

Urgayle noticed, but kept her eyes on the road.

Xander closed the kit, stowed it in the glove box, and watched the streetlights roll by. Sunnydale at night was quieter than he remembered, but it was the kind of quiet you got after the noise had given up.

The watch buzzed again, and this time he left his sleeve up.

Some problems didn’t get easier, but you got better at reading the manual.

He leaned back, closed his eyes, and let the next call wait until they reached the edge of town.
