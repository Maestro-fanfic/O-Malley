The Sorcerer's Apprentice

Scene 1

Scene 1

The library’s after-hours hush was punctuated by the tick of Giles’s wind-up desk clock and the click of Ethan Rayne’s battered occult meter, whose needle vibrated in the red every time someone spoke above a certain threshold. Ethan had the thing laid out like it was a remote detonator for a science fair, brass case dented and inscribed with more than one set of initials. He rolled the dial with his thumb, squinted, and lined up the face against the edge of Giles’s blotter, as if triangulating the existential location of the meeting itself.

Willow sat cross-legged under the window, the screen of her laptop etching highlights into her face. She typed with both hands but her gaze flicked constantly between the display and Ethan’s movements, as if expecting him to sprout a second, more dangerous sensor and make off with the readings. Her hair was back in a ponytail, but two strands kept escaping to shadow her jaw. The legal pad next to her foot was half-filled with dates and packet numbers, each in a different color of gel pen.

Giles anchored the desk, three monographs splayed open and a yellow legal pad beside them, the left margin heavy with underlines. He kept shifting his gaze between the hand-drawn map Ethan had produced and the printed city plat Willow had summoned from the archive. Whenever Ethan made a mark on the map, Giles double-checked it against the plat, his pencil tapping a staccato on the edge of the desk. Every third or fourth tap, he’d stop, close his eyes, and rub at the bridge of his nose, glasses in hand. It was a negotiation: his old way of seeing the world versus the new math of it.

Ethan’s finger hovered over the map. “Here,” he said, voice calibrated for maximum clarity. “Packard Street. Cluster of five, possibly six, readings. Highest spike at the old cold storage warehouse, but the pattern radiates down the block.” He looked up at Giles. “This is deliberate. Not random.”

Giles squinted, then flipped a page in his notebook. “You’re certain it’s not demonic?”

Ethan’s lip curled. “Not even close. Demonic work is bombast. This is… institutional. Neat. Like someone doing lines of code, but in blood and ammonia.”

Willow, barely glancing up, said, “The monitoring array on the perimeter flagged a matching spike five hours before the last anchor break. And then again just before the incident at City Hall.” She turned the laptop so Ethan could see. “Look at the frequency hop. It’s almost perfectly in phase with your readings.”

He frowned, leaning in. “They’re running the same diagnostic as you. Maybe better.”

Giles absorbed that, mouth tight. “Which means they’ve been watching us longer than we thought.”

“Long enough to pattern-match our response times,” Willow said. “The working isn’t just a barrier. It’s an adaptive profile. Every time we hit it, it learns.”

Ethan cocked his head at Giles, almost smug. “Told you they’d go machine-learning before the Council did.”

Xander, leaning against the door with his hands in the pockets of a flannel overshirt, said, “So, wait. We’re being stalked by evil grad students with an algorithm?”

Buffy, perched on the radiator with her jacket zipped and boots already laced, tapped the toe of her boot on the floor. “What’s the move? We sit here and get studied, or we break their little science project?”

Willow ran her finger along the screen, then tapped a blinking line of text. “It’s not just collection. They’re feeding back into the local environment. The boundary work last night was tuned to repel only after a certain physical threshold. Someone’s modeling how hard we hit, and then reinforcing.”

Ethan closed the meter, snapping it shut with a click. “It’s too clean to be self-taught. There’s a framework here. I’d bet cash on a Wolfram and Hart instructional subroutine. They license these to hedge fund magicians and city managers in the know. Only this time, someone’s optimized for combat testing.”

Giles winced, the phrase leaving a visible mark. “So it’s a curriculum.”

“A beta test,” Ethan said, with a touch of respect.

Xander straightened, rolling his shoulder against the door frame. “So they’ve been doing homework on us.”

Willow nodded. “Final exam’s coming up, and I don’t think we’re the ones grading.”

Buffy stood, zipped her jacket the last half-inch, and looked at the address on the map. “Then let’s go see what they’ve learned.”

Ethan rolled up the map, tucked the sensor into his jacket, and grinned. “Always did love fieldwork.”

Giles closed the books in sequence, made a notation on the legal pad, and slid his glasses into place. He looked at Willow, then at Xander, and then at Buffy, as if cataloguing the present for insurance. “Be careful. The first move will be data gathering. The second will be more direct.”

Buffy smiled, teeth flat and even. “If they want direct, I can give them a tutorial.”

The four of them walked out, Ethan leading with the sensor half-concealed, Willow with her laptop clamped to her chest, Giles trailing with a hand in his pocket and the other clutching the legal pad. Xander closed the door behind them, then hesitated, looking at the silent clock on the wall.

He said, just loud enough for Giles to hear, “It’s always about the paperwork.”

Giles smiled without turning. “Welcome to the modern occult.”

The night outside was cold and bright, a sodium haze over the empty streets. Packard Street waited, and so did whatever was learning from them.

Buffy set the pace.

Scene 2

Scene 2

The trek to Packard Street was conducted at a brisk, mutual-dare pace. There were no cars. The street was flanked by dead lots and decomposing strip malls, each store window opaqued by layers of sun-bleached posters or tinfoil insulation. Two blocks in, the sodium lamp Willow had clocked from the main drag revealed itself to be a total liar: up close, its glow was patchy, rippling with the faint pulse of a busted ballast. Anything more than twenty feet away dissolved into a matte, uniform gray.

They cut through a lot full of decaying vending machines, then skirted a dented cyclone fence. Ethan stopped at the corner, sensor out, and gave a sharp “hold.” He flicked the meter on, and the needle leapt, hovered, then fell back by half, jittering as if the air were full of hidden fish. Willow, breathing fast but steady, opened her laptop and watched as the packet log started feeding her fresh, local noise.

Buffy took point, eyes on the warehouse, posture loose but predatory. Xander followed two paces behind, hands in the open, every line of his body broadcasting “not here for trouble, but ready to eat it if served.” Giles stayed well behind, tracking the perimeter in a slow, orbiting path, legal pad in hand and pencil ready, as if he could will the laws of magic to comply with Roberts Rules.

The warehouse itself was a one-story sheet-metal fossil, with its roll-up door secured by a chain so old it looked more likely to powder than to break. The side entrance was a punched-out fire door, barely hanging on its frame. There was a clean oval in the dust where the knob had been used recently, and a boot heel print, not even twelve hours old, pointed in. The ground outside was scuffed clean of debris in a three-foot path leading up to the entrance.

Ethan motioned everyone to keep tight and led the entry. He rolled his feet as if sneaking up on a sleeping bear. Inside, the air was bitter-cold and flavored with the metallic tang of ammonia. The warehouse had been gutted: no pallets, no crates, just an empty floor scored with forklift tire tracks and the marks of past, failed tenants. The only real feature was the network of old refrigeration tracks bolted to the ceiling, now home to the occasional seagull nest or industrial-sized cobweb.

But the walls—every wall—had been stenciled with sigils. Some were jagged, fresh, and black as printer toner; others had been overlaid in blue spray paint, and a third layer, darker and spattered, suggested something organic. The effect was less satanic than it was code: lines and circles, recursive logic, diagrams meant for another kind of mind.

Ethan’s meter beeped. “Peak is dead ahead, twenty meters.” He pointed at the center of the warehouse floor, where concentric rings of runic script had been drawn in what looked like a mixture of table salt and filings shaved from actual iron. The rings weren’t perfectly round, but close. The innermost circle contained a single figure, his back to the entrance, hunched forward in the classic pose of “working at something.”

Xander’s first instinct was “another Ethan” but the size was off, too slight and too fast—hands moving in short, jerky cuts, drawing on the air in front of him as if there was glass between him and the rest of the world.

Willow, whispering: “He’s running the script. In real time.”

Buffy: “How old is this one?”

Xander: “My age. Maybe younger.”

Ethan crouched, extended his arm to block the others, and mouthed, Don’t rush him.

But it was too late for stealth. The kid—he was a kid, Xander saw now, not even driving age—spun on his heel, both hands up, palms wide. The look on his face wasn’t rage; it was the pure, glassy shock of a deer right before the hood ornament. For an instant, the lines of blue-white light that had been arcing between his fingers snapped into a single, trembling vector, aimed dead at the group.

Xander said, “Wait, we’re—” but the kid’s hands jerked in a gesture neither practiced nor safe, and a bolt of fire the width of a flashlight blasted straight at Xander’s chest.

The impact was all heat and static, a punch of light followed by the smell of burning cotton. Xander rocked back, the top button of his flannel vaporizing, but the rest of the blast just rolled over his torso, dissipating in a haze of ozone. The kid gawked, visibly stunned that Xander hadn’t been incinerated.

Buffy advanced, silent and very fast, fanning out to the left flank. Ethan dropped low, muttering in a language Xander had never heard and probably didn’t want to. Willow set her laptop on the nearest concrete ledge and typed, one hand tracing the incoming data even as she tracked the fight.

The kid tried again, this time hurling two wild arcs in succession: one went high, clipping a refrigeration track and dumping rust and corroded dust down on the field, the other smashed into the far wall, scoring a line of melted cinder block and blowing a cloud of concrete dust out into the chain-link yard behind. The noise was astronomical, but the blast still wasn’t controlled. This was no seasoned villain. It was a kid running on desperate adrenaline and the ghost of a bad lesson.

Buffy zigged, drawing attention, while Xander walked forward, hands open and palms empty, voice as calm as he could muster. “Hey. You don’t have to—”

The kid backpedaled, smearing his heels through the inner circle, then flicked his left wrist in a weird staccato. There was a sharp, sour tang of burning metal, and suddenly a glyph on the ground at the far exit started to glow orange. A trap, Xander realized—a pressure glyph, probably designed to set the whole warehouse ablaze or collapse the salt circle.

But the kid’s hands were shaking so badly that he failed to lock the pattern, and the glyph fizzled, a weak strobe that faded back to gray. Xander closed the distance in three slow steps, got both hands on the kid’s wrists, and locked them in a grip that was gentle but utterly final. There was no snap, no crunch, no cry. Just resistance, then nothing, then a slow, wilting collapse.

The kid slumped, knees hitting the floor, and let out a thin, wet sound—not a wail, not a sob, just the spent exhale of a balloon that knows it’s not getting reinflated. Xander knelt, released one wrist, and kept the other in a loose hold just in case.

Buffy held at perimeter, watching for movement. Willow, seeing it was over, paused the script and exhaled. Ethan, crouched over the sigil at their feet, began reading it in a low, even voice, translating the structure aloud.

“Wolfram & Hart’s own grammar. They build these for compliance—feed in the energy, enforce the pattern. He didn’t write this. He was given it. The rest is just brute force.”

Xander loosened his grip, but didn’t let go entirely. “Hey,” he said, voice level. “Nobody’s going to hurt you. But you need to stop now. Okay?”

The kid looked up, face ghost-white, lips trembling. There was a raw burn along his left forearm, where the latest blast had scorched through the sleeve. Under the grime, the features were plain, familiar, a face that could have belonged to any kid at Sunnydale High.

Ethan, still reading, added: “They pushed him to burnout. That’s the point. These setups don’t expect the operator to survive.”

Buffy, inching closer, saw the way the kid’s hoodie hung off his shoulder, the frayed seam mended with dental floss. She holstered her stake. “He’s not the enemy.”

Willow said, “He’s a vector. A test case.”

The kid’s head dropped again. This time the tears came, but it wasn’t loud or performative—just the steady leak of someone who’d run out of defenses.

Xander shifted his grip, let the other wrist go, and waited.

After a long minute, the kid whispered, “Are you going to kill me?”

Xander shook his head. “Not tonight. Not ever.”

Buffy walked over, crouched so she was eye-to-eye. “What’s your name?”

The kid wiped his nose, not looking up. “Ben.”

Ethan said, “He doesn’t know who sent him, just where to be and what to do. They recruited him on a message board, probably. Promised him power, then dropped the real work on top of him.”

Willow said, “That’s how they scale. They’re not building armies; they’re running a lab.”

Giles, who had drifted closer during the aftermath, glanced at Ethan. “Is there a reversal, or a safe deactivation?”

Ethan frowned. “Not really. But if you short the sigil here—” he pointed at a break in the salt ring—“it will close the loop without a blast.”

Willow, quick, said: “If we don’t, does it go off on a timer?”

Ethan: “No timer. But if someone steps in the circle, or tries to leave, it’ll detonate.”

Buffy gave Xander a look. He got the message, then asked the kid, “Can you stand up?”

Ben tried, knees knocking, but managed it with Xander’s help. Buffy held out her hand, palm up. “May I?”

Ben hesitated, then nodded. She took his hand, led him out of the circle, and waited for Ethan to do his thing. Ethan grabbed a handful of salt from the perimeter, flicked it into the break in the line, and said a sharp word. There was a flash, not much brighter than a camera strobe, and the ring lost all charge.

Willow, already at her laptop, began cataloguing the sigil pattern, uploading the log in real time. “We need to get a copy to Carter. She’ll want to see what this looks like from the outside.”

Ben, swaying, said, “Can I go?”

Xander said, “Not yet. But soon. Do you need a doctor?”

Ben shook his head, but the shake was too small to be convincing.

Buffy said, “We’ll take you somewhere safe. After that, you can choose.”

Ben nodded, the relief visible in every muscle.

Giles made a note, tore the page from his pad, and handed it to Ethan. “Evidence for the record. Document everything. We’ll need it.”

Ethan accepted the page with a nod, then looked at Ben. “You have a future, if you want one. That’s more than most of the people who ran this playbook.”

Ben’s voice was thin, but audible. “I didn’t want to hurt anyone.”

Buffy, with the faintest of smiles, said, “That’s a good start.”

They walked Ben out together, Giles holding the door, Willow taking the rear with her laptop still running diagnostics. Ethan paused on the threshold, sensor in one hand, and glanced back at the wall of sigils, the paint already flaking at the edges. “They’re watching us,” he said, quiet.

Xander, almost cheerful, replied, “Let ‘em.”

The night outside was unchanged: cold, silent, with the flicker of the sodium lamp struggling to keep the dark at bay. But the world inside the group had moved a little. Maybe enough.

They walked Ben home.

Scene 3

Scene 3

Xander thumbed the signal on his wristwatch—three short, two long, just like Carter’s training. He’d never actually used it outside of drills. The first time, she’d made him test it by sending a message from under the metal stairs at school, just to see if it would bounce off the Army’s own bandwidth. It had. Now, it pulsed green at the edge, and a voice in his head started counting seconds from the moment he made contact.

Ten minutes and fifty-four seconds later, Chegwidden’s team rolled up in a black SUV so nondescript it made the nearby shopping carts look interesting by comparison. Two men in civilian parkas set up an impromptu perimeter, one at each end of the warehouse block; a third, wearing the medical patch, hoisted a hard case and double-timed it to the side entrance. Chegwidden himself came last, walking with the even, practiced stride of someone who’d spent half his life in places where you measured risk by the inch.

Inside the warehouse, Ben sat on the floor against the cinder block wall. The medic squatted in front of him, speaking quietly, the movements careful and nonthreatening. Ben’s legs were pulled up tight, but his face had gone from terror to pure exhaustion. He didn’t resist the blood pressure cuff or the pulse oximeter, didn’t even flinch when the alcohol swab touched his arm.

Chegwidden did a sweep of the sigil room, noting the scorch on the floor, the blown-out patch on the far wall, and Xander’s blackened shirt collar. He registered these things the way other people count the number of chairs at a meeting—inputs for a later summary, not a cause for drama. Then he walked over to Xander, took his measure, and said, “Threat neutralized?”

Xander didn’t hesitate. “He’s not a threat. He’s a product.”

Chegwidden’s face didn’t twitch, but his voice lost any hint of performative courtesy. “Standing protocol is neutralize and contain. Hostile practitioner in the field—same as a bomb with legs. You know the rationale.”

Xander looked at Ben, who was now allowing the medic to wrap a silver foil blanket around his shoulders. “You can have the rationale. But he’s not a biohazard. He’s a target because someone taught him to be.”

Chegwidden stepped two paces away, motioned for Xander to follow, and dropped his voice to the floor. “You are not wrong. But you are not entirely right.” He glanced over his shoulder. “You don’t get to rewrite the playbook every time you meet someone who looks like you used to.”

Xander felt the hit, but didn’t look away. “I’m not asking for special treatment. I’m telling you that if this goes down like the others, and you red-bag him, it’s going to go on a loop. For years.”

Chegwidden waited, the way a man might wait for a child to finish screaming before starting CPR. “We don’t want it on a loop,” he said. “That’s the whole reason we’re here.” He lifted his chin at Ben. “That one’s different. Is that what you’re saying?”

Ethan, who had spent the last five minutes pretending to photograph the wall sigils with his phone, chimed in without looking up. “He’s a weaponized vector, Admiral. Not a willful actor. The real question is whether he was supposed to go off, or just be seen.”

Chegwidden considered that, then turned to the medic and said, “Get him stabilized. Secure transport to Coronado, Code Three. No sedation unless medically required. Full chain of custody on the handoff.”

He didn’t rescind the rules. He just didn’t need to. The medic stood, gave Chegwidden a silent nod, and helped Ben up, hand under his elbow. Ben walked out under his own power, eyes on the ground, blanket pulled tight. The other two support staff had the site taped in orange cord by then, nothing left but the residue of weird and the stench of salt in the air.

Chegwidden waited until the van doors closed behind Ben, then turned back to Xander. “You do realize,” he said, “the other side wanted to see how you’d handle it.”

Xander shrugged. “Didn’t expect to be graded tonight.”

Ethan finally lowered the phone. “If it helps, you scored high marks for empathy. I’d call it a pass/fail, but these people don’t believe in binary outcomes.”

Chegwidden glanced at the wall, then at Ethan. “You get anything useful?”

Ethan scrolled his photo roll, showed the screen. “Working was Wolfram & Hart. Training-level stuff, but with high-voltage channeling. Someone wanted to know how much power a kid could pull before the system locked up or melted down. They ran a local; next time, maybe they’ll try remote.”

Chegwidden’s face did not move. “What do you recommend?”

Ethan gave a smile that was as thin as the air in the freezer. “Keep the incident report loose. Let them think you’re still following their script. Meanwhile, tighten the net on any new hires in the local area. And if you see another signature like this, consider running a counter-campaign. Make them flinch first.”

Chegwidden nodded. “Noted.”

The three of them stood there, triangle of stillness, for an awkward second that felt longer than it was. Then Chegwidden said, “You’ll want to get that burn checked out, Mr. Harris.”

Xander looked down at the scorched shirt, flexed his shoulder, and said, “I’ll live.”

Chegwidden almost smiled. “That’s the plan. Walk me out?”

They did, Chegwidden in front, Xander and Ethan behind, the two support staff already pulling down the cordon and logging the last details on a legal pad. Outside, the cold was sharper, the wind blowing in from the dead lots with an edge that sliced through every layer.

At the car, Chegwidden said, “We’ll follow up in forty-eight hours. If there are repercussions from tonight, you’ll get a direct call.” He looked at Ethan. “You, I assume, will be monitoring.”

Ethan nodded, slid his phone away, and said, “I always do.”

Chegwidden got in the car, closed the door, and left the window down just long enough to say, “Thank you for your discretion.”

The SUV pulled away, the sodium light briefly painting the side in pale fire before it turned the corner and vanished.

Ethan stood beside Xander for a moment, watching the empty street. “You think they’re done?” he asked.

Xander shook his head. “No one ever is.”

Ethan nodded, satisfied. “That’s why you get to keep trying.”

They walked back toward Main, hands in pockets, not saying much, but matching strides. The night was quiet, the evidence all bagged and logged, but the feeling in the air was different: something had learned, and something else had survived. For the moment, that would have to be enough.

At the crosswalk, Xander stopped, looked at the bandage on his arm, and said, “Thanks for the backup, Ethan.”

Ethan grinned. “Thank yourself, kid. I just read the lines.”

They crossed the street, two shadows in the long, orange light, headed for the future one case at a time.
