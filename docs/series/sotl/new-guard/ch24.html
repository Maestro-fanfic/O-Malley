Chapter 24 (11 of 24 in this book)

"Jurisdiction"

Scene 1

Xander’s watch vibrated in two sharp, precisely timed pulses. He didn’t look at it right away. He kept his eyes on the sidewalk, on the exact place where the town’s working had drawn its invisible, immovable line. It felt the way an oncoming front feels on the skin, pressure dropping out of nowhere, just enough to make you swallow for no reason. If he reached out, he could almost believe his hand would meet resistance—dense air or static or the thick skin of the world pulled over Sunnydale to keep him in, or keep something else out.

He waited for the third pulse. None came. The alert pattern was only for noncritical, civilian-assist traffic, the kind the Navy had gotten good at farming out as training missions for Xander Harris, Heroic Minor, but still too urgent to ignore. He thumbed the watch face, checked the icon. Nothing from the home team, no direct line from Giles or Willow, just the generic emergency overlay blinking quietly against the digital numbers.

On the street behind him, a woman jogged past with headphones, not even glancing at the kid in jeans, sneakers, and a military windbreaker he’d found at a thrift store. If he called out, she would look at him like he was invisible, the way everyone in town had started looking at him after the first three headlines. The way even his own mother looked at him when he brought groceries in, making sure to keep a “Xander did not rob a liquor store” joke on the tip of his tongue so she’d laugh and go back to pretending everything was fine.

He pressed the side button, short-short, as O'Neil had shown him. It sent a clean digital refusal: unable to respond, not declining future requests, just not now. The watch didn’t buzz to confirm. It went silent. On the sidewalk, nothing happened. Xander waited for the shame to arrive, but it didn’t—not at first. Not until he imagined the call routing through whatever disaster was happening, firefighters waiting, a crew short one pair of hands, and the inescapable knowledge that if he jogged over the town limit and put on the suit, he could be there in half an hour, hour tops, problem solved, nobody else even needed to know.

But crossing the line meant taking the risk: the split, the sidereal hangover, the days of feeling like a tourist in his own body while the rest of him drifted outside the boundary, refusing to merge.

The mission would survive. Someone else would make up the time, or the loss would be entered in the log as Acceptable, per standing order.

He took a step away from the boundary, feeling the pressure ease off the back of his neck. The watch face stayed blank.

*

At Coronado, the watch floor was in the first lull of the day. Two analysts traded passwords under their breath, and the comms specialist, a pale-skinned petty officer who looked like she’d been recruited directly from a hacker sleepaway camp, was juggling three secure lines and a high-caffeine beverage in a battered travel mug. Lieutenant O‘Neil stood by the whiteboard, logging the last four anomalies and circling two with a red marker.

The duty officer at the comms desk nodded once and said, “Asset Harris unavailable, signal received.”

O’Neil didn’t look up. “Reassign.”

“Next up is SG team two, Mountain contact.”

She made a note. “If the incident is still active, send over the observer logs. Give them the heads up on why we’re not deploying the usual.”

The petty officer replied with a precise “Aye, ma’am,” then returned to typing. The SG contact in Colorado took the briefing without comment, and the mission re-tasked itself in under thirty seconds. Nobody asked why the asset was out. Nobody speculated, gossiped, or even used a euphemism. The protocol was the protocol. If the world didn’t allow for assets who needed a day off, it wasn’t a world O’Neil intended to live in, much less enforce.

She underlined the incident in red, wrote “COMPLETE” next to it, and erased the action item from the board.

*

Wildfire containment in the Angeles National Forest burned through an extra forty minutes and two EMTs got dehydrated before the evening relief team came in. The Navy’s after-action report flagged the delay, but not the reason. The local hospital prepped for overrun and only had to divert a single case, a minor laceration, to a neighboring facility. Nobody was hurt. Nobody died. The system held.

*

At the boundary, Xander stood very still. The sidewalk was empty, the watch face dark, the town itself almost too quiet, as if waiting for him to dare it.

He let his jaw unclench. One degree. Two.

He looked at the line, then looked away.

He walked home, and for the first time in weeks, he didn’t check the watch again until he was through his own front door.

Scene 2

Chegwidden’s exit left a vacuum in the kitchen, which Joyce attempted to fill with the scent of coffee she didn’t intend to drink. She rinsed the carafe twice, ground the beans by hand, and measured each spoonful like an oath, as if a perfectly calibrated cup might keep the next two hours on course.

Buffy perched on one side of the table, hands folded on the laminate, her eyes already skimming the draft protocol. She read in hard, vertical lines, pausing only to circle a clause or underline a word that failed to meet her personal standard of bullshit detection. O’Neil sat across from her, posture military but hands relaxed. Urgayle had positioned herself in the far corner, back to the wall and a notebook open, but her eyes drifted from the page to the windows, to the exits, to every break in the perimeter.

Joyce poured the first mug and set it near Buffy, careful not to spill, then hovered with a second until O’Neil said, “Thank you, ma’am, but I’m on clock.” Urgayle shook her head once, declining by absence rather than voice.

After the third pass with the mug, Joyce retreated, carrying her own upstairs. She paused at the landing, listened to the absence of voices, and let the adults handle themselves.

Buffy’s first salvo came before O’Neil finished opening her own copy. “Page two, hostile entity definition. Your language says ‘any non-human entity demonstrating aggressive behavior toward civilians.’” She tapped her finger on the margin. “That covers about three-quarters of the town, and all of the lunch crowd at Hot Dog on a Stick. It needs to be narrower.”

O’Neil tilted her head, the way you do when you know the other side has a point but you want to make them say it. “Operationally, we’re directed to intervene before civilian harm. That’s not a lot of room.”

Buffy didn’t blink. “Then the designation is mine. If I say it’s not a threat, your people stand down. If I say it is, you have my explicit authorization. But you don’t get to shoot first and explain later just because the target has scales or an unfortunate dental plan.”

Urgayle marked a check in his notebook.

O’Neil considered. “Add: ‘Active threat as determined by local lead or designated field commander.’ That gives you the call.”

Buffy grinned, but not like she was happy. “Field commander is me, unless I’m dead or stuck in a boundary working.”

O’Neil wrote it in, longhand. “Noted.”

The next section was all infrastructure: medical, transport, chain of custody on anything weird enough that the coroner or the EPA would run screaming. Buffy skimmed, made a show of not caring, but signed off on the part where every patrol wound meant a Navy medic on call, and all incident reporting would be under an alias unless she opted in.

Urgayle pointed with her pen: “You left out the ninety-second rule.”

Buffy frowned. “What’s that?”

“If any of our people enter your designated patrol zone without prior clearance, they check in every ninety seconds. No more shadowing you, no more unannounced backup. If we miss a check-in, we fall back, and you get the call.”

Buffy glanced at O’Neil, then at Urgayle. “You’ve done this before.”

Urgayle shrugged, an understated motion. “Never with a minor in charge. But more often than anyone wants to write down.”

Buffy marked a plus sign in the margin.

Third fight: jurisdiction.

Buffy said, “If a supernatural incident overlaps with a civilian crime scene, your people do not talk to Sunnydale PD.”

O’Neil waited for elaboration.

Buffy gave it. “Not because I don’t trust you. Because I don’t trust them. They’re not equipped. Half the time they’re in the pocket of the Mayor, and the other half, they’re in the pocket of whoever last bought them a round at Willy’s. You keep your support crew in the background, and I’ll handle Snyder and the rest.”

O’Neil nodded, no argument, just a dry, “Confirmed.”

The rest was routine, as far as multi-jurisdictional supernatural operations were ever routine: “Medical extraction for you and your associates after every engagement, no questions asked. Equipment resupply through a dead-drop at the storage unit on Maple. Weekly intelligence summary of any signals traffic that mentions Sunnydale, even if it’s just tabloid stuff or satellite noise. Standing offer of forensic analysis for any artifact Giles can’t identify with his own team.”

Buffy, surprised, said, “That’s actually useful.”

O’Neil made no show of pride. “If the goal is support, it has to be support.”

They reviewed the final clause together: “If I want the base to leave, the base leaves.”

Buffy asked, “Has anyone ever taken you up on that?”

O’Neil paused. “Once. It ended poorly, but not because we stayed out.”

Buffy didn’t ask for details. She flipped to the signature line, considered, then asked, “What happens if I change my mind?”

“You call. We meet again.”

Buffy took the pen from the counter, the red one, and signed her name in three quick strokes. “If you violate, I get to rescind unilaterally.”

“That’s the contract,” O’Neil said.

Urgayle countersigned for the Navy, folded the copy in half, and tucked it into her pocket. “We’ll be at the storage unit Thursday at six. If you need it sooner, call.”

Buffy said, “Will you bring donuts, or is that the support unit’s job?”

Urgayle, almost smiling, said, “We’ll bring something better.”

The meeting ended without a handshake. O’Neil stood, smoothed her uniform, and said, “If it helps, I never got a protocol signed on the first try. Not even in D.C.”

Buffy said, “That’s because you never did it with a teenager who grew up on custody battles and TV law.”

O’Neil gave her a look of genuine respect. “See you Thursday, Buffy.”

They let themselves out.

Buffy sat back, exhaled, and stared at her name in red.

Joyce, waiting at the top of the stairs, didn’t ask how it went. She brought down an extra mug and made sure it was warm.

Scene 3

Xander perched on the corner of the Sunnydale High roof, knees pulled tight and heels braced on the corrugated lip of the HVAC housing. Below him, three stories of unremarkable brick dropped to a courtyard overgrown with half-dead lawn and the faded paint lines of a running track nobody had bothered to resurface since the Reagan administration. His satchel rested on the steel beside him, the Superman suit folded inside a textbook sleeve labeled WORLD HISTORY: MODERN.

He keyed the secure line, watched the LED blink green, and waited for the connection.

Zoey picked up on the second ring, already mid-thought. “—which is ridiculous because if you can’t trust a Georgetown ethics seminar to go off the rails, what can you trust, right? Tell me you have something better.”

He smiled. “You have no idea how much better. I’m on a high school roof in California, and I just watched a squirrel walk off with someone’s Cheetos. Like, the whole bag. One mouthful.”

“Sunnydale wildlife is advanced,” Zoey said. “Is this the good line?”

“Direct to the secure,” Xander said. “No party line, no Mrs. Landingham.”

A pause, then, “Is she still calling you Superman?”

“Not in public. But I’m pretty sure she calls me the ‘asset’ when she thinks I can’t hear. It’s dehumanizing, but in an endearing, pastry-based way.”

Zoey laughed, a real laugh, and the tension of the last two days unwound in the space between them. “So what’s up? Is this a just-because call or an imminent-disaster call?”

He leaned back and watched a cloud slide over the sun. “A little of both. I got an alert this morning, boundary-side. Something in the Angeles. I didn’t go.”

He waited for the punchline, or the letdown, or the disappointment. Zoey just said, “Good.”

He blinked. “Not even going to ask why?”

“Don’t need to. You told me if you ever did it, you’d be the last to believe it. So, I believe you.”

Xander found himself gripping the edge of the roof. “Any fallout on your end?”

Zoey was quiet for a moment. “I just… I saw the news. Angeles National Forest, the wildfire. They said nobody died, but the containment took longer than expected. I wondered if that was the thing you didn’t go to.”

“That was it. Me not showing up.” He closed his eyes.

“Then I’m glad you didn’t,” she said, and her voice had that particular steadiness that meant she was choosing her words carefully. “The person you answer to—the woman who trained you. Is she okay with it?”

He shook his head, realized she couldn’t see him, and said, “She’s not the type. She’d just turn it into a training module.”

Zoey laughed, soft. “She sounds terrifying.”

“She’s actually kind of great. In a ‘will make you run until you vomit’ way.”

“All the best people are.”

They sat in companionable silence, distant but closer than anyone else in their respective radius. Xander looked out at the town. “You ever get the feeling that it’s all one big boundary working? Like, not just the magic, but the PTA meetings, and the mall, and every day you go home and your parents have the same argument about whose turn it is to forget to buy milk?”

Zoey said, “That’s every campus in the world, Xander. Only difference is you have actual vampires. We just have hedge fund recruiters.”

“I’ll take vampires. At least they’re honest about their nutritional needs.”

She snorted. “Are you okay? Not superhero-okay, but real-people-okay.”

He closed his eyes and weighed the answer. “If I wasn’t, I wouldn’t have called.”

“That is the least comforting answer and also the most honest.”

He smiled again, less forced. “How’s your end of things?”

Zoey described a minor Secret Service shuffling, a professor who hated her father on principle, and the elaborate choreography required to eat a sandwich in the Georgetown food court without being photographed. She finished by saying, “If you ever want to disappear, I can teach you tricks. Nobody notices a girl with her face in a book and a Starbucks cup welded to her hand.”

Xander said, “If I ever cross the boundary for good, I’ll take you up on it.”

She was quiet for a long moment, then: “They still haven’t figured out what you are, have they?”

He answered, “I’m pretty sure that’s the whole problem.”

Somewhere below, a door slammed open. Xander heard the flat thud of footsteps on the stairwell, then the slow squeal of the roof access door. He turned, expecting a vagrant or a patrol, and instead found one of the school security guards frozen in the doorway, looking at him with a mixture of horror and resignation.

“Hi,” Xander said, friendly.

The guard blinked. “You… you good?”

“Perfectly safe. Just making a call.”

“Uh, don’t… fall off?”

“Wouldn’t dream of it.”

The guard closed the door and retreated. Xander heard the footsteps echo away.

“Still there?” Zoey asked.

“Always,” he said.

“Gotta run. Ethics is back on the rails.”

He wanted to say something—something heroic, or comforting, or even normal—but Zoey beat him to it. “Don’t change the world tonight. Just keep the line open.”

He clicked off, and the sky seemed one shade lighter.

Scene 4

The library’s after-hours quiet was the kind of silence that didn’t just invite conspiracy, it demanded it. The main reading table had been converted into a war room: Giles’s annotated map of the town’s ley-line grid sprawled across one half, Willows’s laptop open to a recursive loop of highlighted intersections, and three mugs—one tea, two black coffee—circling like satellites around a central spill of yellow legal paper.

Ethan, who had acquired a bright white dress shirt since his last visit, was on the floor drawing circles in chalk with the careful, practiced flick of a man who’d done this in far dirtier places. Each stroke landed at the precise tangent, forming interlocking arcs that looked almost decorative until you noticed the sigils embedded at three points in the perimeter.

Buffy leaned against the magazine rack, one foot propped on the lowest shelf, chewing an apple with the slow deliberation of a person who knew the value of vitamin C but had other things on her mind. She watched the team, eyes tracking from Ethan’s chalk work to Willow’s code window to Giles’s muttered recitations from the Survey map.

Giles, peering through his glasses at a section marked “Old Town—Possible Node,” said, “The logic is sound. If we collapse the secondary anchor, the primary should be left exposed, if not weakened.”

Willow ran her script and made a sound halfway between triumph and exasperation. “We’ve got the candidate location. Cross-referenced with the city’s public works database and Ethan’s sigil log. It’s at the corner of Maple and Crestwood—right under a defunct pay phone.”

Ethan completed the outer ring and stood, dusting off his hands. “It’s never the primary anchor. That’s for show. The ones that matter are always under garbage infrastructure or public utilities. If I ever take over a town, I’m putting my soul jar in a Taco Bell drive-thru.”

Buffy said, “That’s a lot of effort to keep people away from free long distance.”

Willow angled her laptop to show the satellite image. “The shell company that pulled the original permit is owned by a Wolfram & Hart sub-subsidiary. Giles recognized the accountant’s name.”

Giles looked over his glasses. “Of course I did. The firm handles estate closure for the Council. They’re the worst kind of efficient.”

Buffy crunched her apple and asked, “What’s the plan?”

Ethan pointed at her, then himself. “We go. You break cover if there’s trouble, I handle the counter-seal. Giles and Willow coordinate from here. Willow patches us into the police and EMS, Giles runs interference if we trip an alarm.”

Buffy tossed the apple core into the bin. “If?”

He shrugged. “We’re being optimistic tonight.”

In five minutes they were out the door, Buffy in black hoodie and jeans with the patched elbow, Ethan carrying a nylon bag of tools and a flashlight with the lens covered by a square of red film. Giles handed Buffy a walkie-talkie and said, “Channel four. Willow has the other end.”

Maple and Crestwood was a three-block walk and a universe away from the school’s nervous lighting. The defunct pay phone was tucked into a low brick wall with a badly painted blue metal shell, the receiver missing and the coin slot covered with a square of duct tape. Buffy scoped the street, saw nothing but a single parked car, and gave Ethan the nod.

He set to work on the concrete base, chiseling away a line of mortar where the footing met the sidewalk. Buffy watched the end of the street, counting seconds and listening for anything out of pattern: a car alarm, a cough, the clang of a trash can lid.

Ethan pried loose a flat obsidian disc the size of a half-dollar. “There’s our anchor.”

Buffy said, “If you drop it, do we get seven years of bad luck or just a lot of paperwork?”

“Depends who’s holding the paperwork.” He produced a small cold iron chisel from his bag, positioned it over a notched indentation, and whispered a phrase that made Buffy’s ears ring, just slightly. He struck once, hard, and the disc split in two with a high, brittle sound.

A faint whiff of ozone curled up, and the streetlight overhead blinked out for three seconds before returning to normal.

They pocketed the pieces, covered the scar with the pay phone shell, and retraced their steps to the library, where Willow waited in the entry with the door propped open and the laptop clutched to her chest.

“Did it work?” she asked.

“Nothing exploded, so probably,” Buffy said.

Ethan placed the two halves of the obsidian disc on the table. “We’ll know if the boundary weakens at all. That was a textbook anchor, but well-hidden.”

Giles, pulling at the corners of his map, said, “Any sense of pushback?”

Buffy shook her head. “Quieter than most patrols. No sign of anyone watching.”

Ethan, staring at the broken disc, frowned. He turned both pieces over and studied the inner faces, then went very still.

“What?” Willow said, instantly anxious.

Ethan set the pieces side by side, aligning the break. The inner faces of the disc were covered with a precise grid of hashmarks and tiny engraved numbers, like the dial of a scientific instrument. He traced a finger along one quadrant and said, “This wasn’t a structural anchor. It was a gauge.”

Buffy leaned in. “As in… measuring something?”

“Measuring who,” Ethan said. “This would record the force and the nature of the breach. If you break it, it logs the energy signature—magical, kinetic, physical, whatever—and the direction. Probably the time, too.”

Willow peered at the engravings. “So… it’s a tripwire. Someone’s going to know exactly how we did it.”

Giles picked up the disc, examined it with a small jeweler’s lens he kept in his vest, and muttered, “Ingenious. A direct logging device. They’ll have a complete profile of the energy you used, and, if I’m not mistaken, the identity of the practitioner.”

Buffy stiffened. “So we just handed over all our stats to the enemy.”

Ethan smiled, but it was thin and tired. “Or we just let them know exactly who they’re dealing with, and they’ll calibrate accordingly.”

Buffy said, “Does that help or hurt?”

Willow’s face was already falling. “If it’s Wolfram & Hart, they’re building a case. Or a weapon.”

Giles placed the halves of the disc on the table, markings up. “We proceed with the plan. The information is out there. We adapt.”

Ethan nodded, but for the first time, looked genuinely nervous. “There may be other gauges. If we start removing anchors, we need to be sure they’re not all wired.”

Buffy lifted her walkie-talkie, looked at it, and said, “Maybe next time we call ahead.”

They stood in silence, four people and two halves of a very clever trap, all of them knowing that the enemy had just learned as much as they had.

Scene 5

It was forty-two minutes past the anchor break when the air in the library’s main room compressed around a spot in front of the reading table. Not with a bang or a thunderclap, not even a flicker in the fluorescents, but with the mundane wrongness of seeing a stranger standing in your kitchen at three in the morning, holding your mug.

Dr. Evelyn Rook did not radiate menace, nor did she look remotely like the sort of person who trafficked in supernatural threats. Her suit was gray, the blouse unpatterned, the hair pinned back in a style that said “adjunct faculty” more than “corporate hex-caster.” Her shoes were new, which was the only thing that marked her as out of place in Sunnydale.

She stood inside a circle of faintly geometric light, the segments and sigils of a working mapped in a language that made Willow’s head hurt just to look at. The circle was as precise as if it had been laser-etched into the floor, which, given Rook’s style, might have been exactly what happened.

Ethan raised his left arm, palm out, and said, very softly, “Do not approach.”

Buffy froze in place, one hand behind the chair, the other curled near her hip. Willow looked at Giles, who had instinctively taken two steps to block her from the circle. Xander shifted his weight forward, half a step toward Rook, and Giles stopped him with a hand to the shoulder.

Rook took in the room, catalogued the faces, and waited until she had full attention. She spoke with the flat, courteous tone of a diplomat resolving an audit.

“You have performed admirably. The anchor was never intended to be a primary defense. Its function was to profile the response. Thank you for making it clear.”

Buffy said nothing. Xander did not look away.

Rook continued, “I am not here as a threat. This is a communication, not a confrontation. I represent the interests of the Mayor’s office, specifically the protected obligations of Mr. Richard Wilkins. My instructions are to extend a final offer.”

She let the sentence rest, as if waiting for someone to ask what the offer was. No one did.

“Alexander Lavelle Harris, also known as Superman, will exit Sunnydale municipal boundaries and remain outside until the expiration of Mayor Wilkins’s current protected term. In exchange, no further pressure will be applied to his friends, his associates, or the individuals under his informal or formal protection. This includes but is not limited to school records, medical records, and residence data. The intent is to minimize disruption to his social and educational environment, which has been a cause of concern for both parties.”

She looked at Xander, her eyes not unkind. “You are not the adversary. You are the anomaly. This arrangement is not punitive; it is administrative. If you accept, the town will cease resisting your presence at the boundary. If you refuse, more targeted measures will follow. The next working will not be a gauge.”

Giles said, quietly, “She means it.”

Ethan’s arm tensed on Xander’s other side. “If you touch the circle, it will register acceptance. That’s the clause.”

Buffy stepped forward, putting herself between Xander and the projection. “He’s not going anywhere.”

Rook acknowledged her with a nod, as if this, too, was expected and perhaps even reasonable. “The choice remains. We will not pursue. The offer stands until midnight.”

Xander spoke, voice low. “What happens if I say no?”

Rook answered with perfect candor. “I would not hazard a guess. My employment does not extend to enforcement, only to negotiation and the observation of compliance. But it will not be as polite.”

Willow found her voice. “Who do you work for?”

“Wilkins,” Rook said. “But the instrument is executed through Wolfram & Hart, with full municipal authority. The scope is outlined in the records at City Hall, if you know how to read them.”

Buffy asked, “You’re just going to keep trying to push him out?”

Rook said, “It is not personal. The town cannot absorb two irreconcilable identities under the current protections. The more time he spends inside the boundary, the more it destabilizes both the local field and the constructed social reality. The Mayor’s interests are best served by restoring equilibrium. I am not authorized to discuss the cosmological implications, but the practical ones are sufficient.”

Ethan, never looking away from the sigil, said, “And if we try to break the primary?”

“Then you will have my undivided attention,” Rook said. “And the attention of entities less inclined to contract.”

Giles, under his breath, said, “That is not a bluff.”

Buffy said, “We decline.”

Rook inclined her head, accepting it as one accepts the result of a coin toss. She looked back at Xander, and the temperature in the room dropped—actual, physical, as the skin on Xander’s arms raised in goosebumps.

She said his full name: “Alexander Lavelle Harris.” Each syllable pronounced with the accuracy of a notary reading a will. Not Xander, not Superman, not any of the names that let him hide from what he was.

Rook’s projection did not fade, or flicker, or burst into light. It simply was not there, as if it had never been, leaving the library air to fill the vacuum left behind.

Nobody moved for five full seconds.

Willow was the first to speak. “I… did she just serve you legal papers? Through a magic Zoom call?”

Giles bent over the table, staring at the halves of the obsidian disc. “Yes, I think she did.”

Ethan exhaled, finally letting his arm drop. “Next time, I propose we leave the disc and simply salt the ground.”

Buffy turned to Xander. “You okay?”

He shrugged, but the room was cold and his hands trembled. “Better than last time.”

They stared at the table. The two halves of the disc lay there, the markings on their inside faces illuminated by the same library lights as always, only now everyone in the room saw them for what they were: a record, a confession, a set of coordinates for the next trap.

“She’s going to be back,” Willow said.

Buffy nodded. “So are we.”

The room slowly returned to its natural temperature, but nobody felt any warmer.

They cleaned up the chalk, closed the laptop, turned off the lights, and walked out into the night, not looking back at the disc that waited, unbroken, for whoever cared to claim the next round.
