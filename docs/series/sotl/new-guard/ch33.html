Full Disclosure

Scene 1

The Situation Room’s video wall was divided in thirds: leftmost, the Royal Navy’s high-security comms, a tilt-shifted image of London at dusk and a single bespectacled man in a dark suit; center, the NATO secure relay with its blue-badged chorus line of advisers and notetakers; rightmost, the President’s own live feed, color-corrected so that even at 4 a.m. D.C. time, no one could say Bartlet looked tired. He was, though. Jacket off, sleeves rolled, tie hanging askew like an afterthought. The mug of coffee in front of him had been cold since the first round of protocols, but the caffeine—such as it was—still counted.

Leo McGarry occupied the chair to his immediate right, a folder thick with annotations in his lap, thumb holding a spot on page seven in case he needed to circle back and drop the hammer. C.J. Cregg stood near the double doors, arms folded, shoulder propped against the frame, her hair pulled back so tight it looked like it might snap at any moment. She didn’t fidget; she rarely did. Instead, she ran internal simulations of tomorrow’s press briefing, cataloguing every word for its blast radius.

Zoey Bartlet sat along the back wall, alone in a row of observer chairs. She was there at her father’s insistence, a civilian anchor to prove, if only to himself, that the world could still have a witness when the tectonic plates shifted. She had a legal pad on her lap, two pages of doodles and exactly zero notes. The doodles weren’t nervous, just kinetic—loops and arcs and the same four-pointed star, over and over, like a stress test for graphite.

The main event started with the British national security adviser—Sir Bernard Hellyer, the kind of man who could kill a proposal with a single eyebrow—and rolled through the European coalition at a pace that left little room for ceremony. Bartlet had asked for this meeting himself, and that fact alone had set the room on edge; NATO didn’t get called to the Situation Room at four a.m. without a very good reason.

Bernard went first, as always. His accent gave him an extra ten IQ points, which he wielded with surgical cruelty. “Thank you, Mr. President. I take it we’re proceeding with the full agenda, then?”

Bartlet, hands folded on the table, nodded once. “The agenda, plus two. I’m going to walk you through them in order, and if you want to interrupt, that’s your prerogative, but I’d appreciate letting me get through the first round unmolested.”

Bernard gave a noncommittal grunt, which Leo interpreted as consent.

“Item one,” Bartlet said. “We have located the object that caused the international incident in Antarctica last month. It is currently under a joint U.S.-UK recovery operation, and the operational chain is split down the middle. As per the Antarctic Treaty, there are observers from the People’s Republic of China, France, and, as of yesterday, Australia. The object is, as preliminary analysis confirms, non-terrestrial in origin, and its provenance is—” here Bartlet checked his folder, as if to make certain the universe hadn’t changed the facts on him since the last reading “—consistent with the class of vessel described in last year’s Tok’ra transmission.” He let the implication hang.

There was no immediate response. Bartlet counted three beats, then continued: “For the purposes of clarity, the ship is immobile, the crew is nonfunctional, and our teams have managed to isolate the main memory core for diagnostic. I am told by the people who are paid to know these things that it will be months before we can risk powering it up.”

NATO’s Belgian representative, de Keyser, spoke: “Is there a risk of contamination? Biological or otherwise?”

Leo handed Bartlet a tab, which he read without looking. “No evidence of pathogen. The hull appears to have self-sealed. There is, however, a high-voltage positronic field effect in the core. Our team’s wearing EM-hard suits and double-layer isolation.”

Bernard: “And you’re sharing this why?”

Bartlet took a breath, exhaled, and said: “Because we can’t keep it quiet for long. There’s already chatter on the Chinese side about increased seismic activity. And, more to the point, the observers are not ornamental. They are credentialed, and they know what they’re looking at. If we tried to freeze them out, we’d be announcing to the world that the U.S. government is going to unilaterally break the Treaty. That’s not an option, not for us, and not for you.”

A pause. On the NATO split-screen, one of the advisers scribbled something on a legal pad, then passed it along the row.

Bartlet looked at Leo, then at the camera. “That brings me to item two.”

He closed the first folder and opened a red-bordered one, each page stamped “EXSUM” in bold at the top. “Since 1998, there has been a compartmentalized operation at Cheyenne Mountain, formerly known as Stargate Command, under the direct authority of the Joint Chiefs and the Office of the President. The program’s existence was classified Tier One. Its function—” here he let the weight build “—was to manage, operate, and exploit an ancient ring-based technology found in Egypt in the late 1920s. The Stargate.”

Bartlet watched the British adviser’s face and saw, in real time, the fifty years of poker face falter.

“The program has conducted over one hundred off-world missions. There have been conflicts. There have been losses. But for the last three years, the greatest threat has been domestic—not foreign. There are elements within our own government who view the technology as a means to a unilateral world order. I want it stated, on this record and for all time, that is not my intention, nor the policy of this administration.”

Bernard, quieter: “Why now?”

“Because,” said Bartlet, “the incident in Antarctica is going to break the compartment. The same people who want to control the Stargate are going to push for a lockdown, and they’ll succeed unless we get out ahead of them. That means bringing our partners into the loop. Not just as witnesses, but as actors.”

The room on the other end of the video feed went quiet. Not the diplomatic hush of a room waiting its turn, but the absolute, shared silence of people absorbing a paradigm shift.

Bartlet let it sit. He’d learned, from years at the front of a classroom, that silence did more to teach than any two-page speech.

Leo took over the baton. “We have prepared briefing materials for your offices. They’re coded and set to self-destruct in twenty-four hours. After you’ve had time to review, we’ll set a second call with your technical experts and ours. You will have access to the unredacted mission logs, and to all physical artifacts under joint custody.”

De Keyser: “You expect us to believe—”

Bartlet cut in, not unkindly: “I expect you to check the math. The logs are all there. The crews—many of whom were recruited from allied countries—are named and accounted for. And we are prepared to receive your own people at Cheyenne Mountain to observe current operations.”

C.J. interjected from the door, voice steady: “The press will get wind of the Antarctic dig within the week. I can spin the cover story for now, but once foreign scientists are on site, the temperature will rise. Our objective is to keep things at a simmer until we have a common position.”

Bernard: “And the Enterprise?”

“Still on ice,” said Bartlet. “It will remain so until we jointly decide on next steps. But I will not allow a repeat of the Cold War race for the spoils.”

On the Belgian side, the legal pad scribbler whispered something to a woman in glasses, who nodded and leaned into the mic: “President Bartlet, if I may—how many people in the United States government currently know about the Stargate program?”

Bartlet didn’t hesitate. “Fewer than two hundred, including technical and security support.”

The woman: “So, you have operated unilaterally for the entire length of the program.”

Bartlet: “That is correct.”

Bernard: “How can we trust you now?”

Bartlet laced his fingers and stared into the camera, letting the tired in his face show. “Because I’m telling you now, when I could just as easily have told you nothing. Because we have already put observers on the ground. Because I trust the future of this world more than I trust the future of my own country, and if that gets me voted out, then I’ll take the blame. But I will not sleepwalk into a planetary incident because we were too proud to admit what we found.”

There was a long, long silence.

Zoey watched her father from the back, the way you watch a building catch fire. He didn’t look at her, but he could sense her there, a fixed point in the gravitational math of his universe.

On the screen, Bernard pinched the bridge of his nose, then composed himself. “Thank you, Mr. President. We will review your materials and set the next call as soon as possible.”

“Thank you, Sir Bernard,” said Bartlet, and meant it.

The call ended, replaced by the blue-on-black of the White House secure desktop.

C.J. let out a breath, then pushed off the door and walked toward the table, where she took up the mug of cold coffee and sniffed it.

“Should I make a fresh pot?” she asked, but it wasn’t really a question.

Leo said, “You just detonated a secret that cost three administrations their sleep. You want the first quote tomorrow, or do we push the press until the Hill has their heads on straight?”

Bartlet didn’t answer. He stared at the mug, then at Zoey, who finally set down her legal pad.

She said, “You did good, Dad.” It was quiet, but not shy.

He almost smiled. “Good and enough, or just enough?”

She shrugged. “Enough that I don’t hate you for keeping it secret. Enough that I’d want to know before the aliens showed up on TV.”

He nodded, once.

C.J. set down the mug and walked out, making a note on her pad as she left.

Leo started to follow, but then paused. “Sir, when the British come back tomorrow, they’ll want to know if the survivors from the ship are going to be treated as persons or as artifacts. You want to draw that line now, or after the legal teams have at it?”

Bartlet rubbed his temple. “Let’s wait. Sometimes the lawyers can surprise you.”

Leo grunted, then headed out, flipping the tab to page eight as he moved.

Zoey lingered. She walked to the head of the table, took the mug, and went to the sideboard for fresh coffee. She poured it herself, brought it back, and set it at her father’s right hand.

“You really sure you want this to be your legacy?” she asked.

He looked at the mug, then at her. “I want my legacy to be that I left things a little bit less screwed up than I found them. That’s enough for me.”

She didn’t argue. She took her legal pad and left the Situation Room, her silhouette clean against the bright hallway outside.

Bartlet sat alone for a moment, the hum of the conference equipment already fading.

Then, with the practiced grace of a man who had done this a thousand times, he straightened his tie, took a sip of the coffee, and toggled the relay for the next meeting.

The future was here, and it wasn’t going to wait.

Scene 2

The Summers kitchen was the one room in the house that looked best in the morning. Yellow light came through the slider, warming the old round table and catching on the Navy-blue seal of the folder between Buffy and her mother. The refrigerator behind Joyce was still a mess of school photographs, vacation magnets, and an evolving grocery list. Today’s list was topped by “eggs” in Sharpie and, beneath it in Buffy’s handwriting, “Jell-O, the good kind.”

This was not the conversation they had the night President Bartlet left through the front door and took the last possible excuse for disbelief with him. They had already done vampires, invitation rules, the Council, patrol injuries, emergency numbers, and the location of every stake in the house. This was the conversation Joyce had demanded that night: records, not prophecy. The Navy had finally delivered them.

Joyce rested both hands on the folder. She had read it twice. The first pass had been as Buffy’s mother. The second had been with a legal pad beside her and every euphemism underlined.

“Essential personnel,” she said.

Buffy looked at the phrase upside down. “They like labels. It makes the impossible fit in a filing cabinet.”

“Critical path. Primary responder. Irreplaceable local capability.” Joyce tapped each line with one finger. “Those are not labels. Those are ways of saying they have built a plan around my daughter.”

“They built support around what I was already doing,” Buffy said. “There’s a difference.”

Joyce held her gaze. “Is there?”

Buffy considered the folder instead of answering quickly. That, more than any reassurance, told Joyce the question was being taken seriously.

“Chegwidden put the difference in writing,” Buffy said at last. “I can refuse a mission. I can refuse equipment. I can tell them to leave. The emergency clause is for people already in danger, not for sending me somewhere because Washington wants a Slayer on the scene.”

Joyce opened to the marked page. The refusal language was there, boxed in blue pencil and cross-referenced three times. Giles had added a note in the margin. O’Neil had initialed beneath it. Someone from JAG had attached a page explaining exactly how a sixteen-year-old could withdraw consent from a classified support arrangement.

“And if they decide the emergency clause means whatever they need it to mean?” Joyce asked.

“Then I call you, Giles, and the Admiral, in that order. If nobody listens, I call the President. Apparently he accepts complaints by air delivery.”

Joyce tried not to smile. She failed briefly. “We are not making that a standard procedure.”

“It wasn’t standard the first time.”

Joyce turned to the casualty summary. Unlike the Council’s histories, it treated Buffy as a person who could bleed. Dates, injuries, response times, treatment received, treatment unavailable. The count at the bottom was worse for being typed neatly.

“You told me you had nearly died,” Joyce said. “You did not tell me anyone had started counting.”

“I started counting. Then Giles did. The Navy just made a spreadsheet.” Buffy touched one entry. “Some of these are arguable. They call the Master a clinical death. I call it getting back up.”

“I am not comforted by the accounting dispute.”

“Wasn’t really going for comfort.”

Joyce read the next page more slowly. It listed the things that had changed since their first night of planning: a medical contact who would answer without questions, a vehicle close enough to matter, a protected telephone line, and people under orders to preserve evidence instead of explaining it away. None of it made patrol safe. It did make the aftermath less lonely.

“This part I understand,” she said. “A doctor. Transportation. Someone answering the telephone. Those are things you should have had two years ago.”

Buffy’s shoulders eased. “Yeah.”

The incident map came next. Joyce knew what the dots meant now. She recognized the school, Restfield, the Bronze, the hospital, and the ugly concentration around streets she drove every week. One blue circle covered Revello Drive without touching the house itself.

“Support radius,” Buffy said before Joyce could ask. “Not a target marker. Urgayle showed me the notation.”

“I know. He explained it when the car changed positions Tuesday.” Joyce turned the page. “I am learning their habits.”

“He’s probably terrified of you.”

“Good.”

Joyce reached the personnel appendix. Giles’s entry was dense with qualifications and carefully neutral language about the Council. Willow had corrected two technical descriptions in red ink. Xander’s page had a handwritten objection to the phrase “secondary field asset” and, beneath it, a different hand noting that the objection was sustained. Ethan’s name appeared throughout the working notes rather than in a separate box, as if nobody had found a safe bureaucratic category for him.

“They finally managed to describe your friends without pretending they appeared last week,” Joyce said.

Buffy leaned over to inspect the amendments. “Willow made them redo half of it. Giles made them redo the other half. Ethan objected to being documented on philosophical grounds, then submitted six pages of corrections.”

“And Xander?”

“Drew a cape on his photograph and claimed he had no further notes.”

Joyce closed the appendix. “That sounds like him.”

For a moment the kitchen was quiet except for the refrigerator motor and the faint scrape of a branch against the window.

“What do you need from me now?” Joyce asked.

Buffy did not say just keep being you. That answer had belonged to the first conversation, when both of them were trying to make knowledge small enough to survive the night.

“Learn the phone tree,” she said. “If I miss check-in, call the local number before you call the police. If the wards change color, don’t touch them. If I come home hurt and say I’m fine, make me sit down anyway. And if somebody with credentials says I agreed to something I didn’t agree to, ask for it in writing.”

Joyce nodded once. “I can do that.”

“I know.”

“And Admiral Chegwidden still owes me the meeting I requested.”

“Friday at four. O’Neil set it up yesterday.”

Joyce reopened the folder to the page of questions she had written herself. “Good. He can explain why the medical authorization requires three signatures and why none of them are mine.”

Buffy winced in advance on the Admiral’s behalf. “Maybe start with hello.”

“I will consider it.”

Buffy reached for the coffee she had forgotten to drink. It had gone cold. She drank it anyway.

Joyce looked once more at the folder, no longer as proof of a hidden life but as an imperfect plan for the life they were now facing together.

“You are still going to school today,” she said.

Buffy blinked. “That’s your takeaway?”

“Normal is harder to keep than weird, apparently. Someone very stubborn told me that.”

Buffy smiled and stood. “She sounds wise.”

“She has her moments.”

Outside, the day had gotten brighter. Inside, the folder remained open on the kitchen table, its margins crowded with the evidence that disclosure was not a single confession. It was maintenance.

Scene 3

The Harris living room was always darkest just after sunset. Some architectural flaw in the subdivision’s layout meant the porch light outside was useless, and the low ceiling trapped the dusk in a way that made the couch look even more sunken than it was. Xander walked in and, out of habit, stepped around the coffee table at the far end—one leg shorter than the others, so the whole thing tipped if you so much as breathed near it.

The TV played a cable news channel with the sound set to “submarine.” Tony Harris sat in the recliner, remote balanced on his chest, a can of Coors wedged between two fingers. The can was half empty, which meant this was probably the third or fourth of the night, but the way Tony’s head snapped up as Xander entered suggested the buzz had plateaued. Jessica Harris was on the couch, glass of white wine in her hand, her feet tucked up and her eyes fixed on some point just above the TV. The wine bottle—“Franzia,” its box squatting behind a stack of old TV Guides on the counter—was at least half-gone.

Xander slid onto the couch as close to the door as possible. The satchel with the uniform stayed between his feet, close enough that he could toe it if things went sideways.

He didn’t wait for permission. He said, “I have to talk to you both. It’s not bad, but it’s not nothing.”

Tony muted the TV, but didn’t look away. “You got a girl in trouble?”

Jessica rolled her eyes. “Tony, not everyone is you.”

Xander grinned, small and sharp, then let it fall. “No girls, no trouble. Just… There’s a government thing. I have to tell you so you don’t freak if it comes up.”

Jessica blinked, then set down the wine. “What kind of thing? Like a draft? Is there a war?”

“No war. No draft.” Xander glanced at the coffee table, then said, “Just an arrangement. I helped out with something in D.C., and now they… have a way to reach me if something comes up. But it’s not a job, and it’s not full-time. It doesn’t change anything here.”

Tony made a face, half-skeptical, half-interested. “You get paid?”

“No.”

Jessica frowned, worried now. “Is this—what, like a witness thing? Do you need a lawyer?”

“It’s not criminal. I’m not testifying. It’s just—” He hesitated. “I’m on a list. If there’s an emergency, or if they need something, they call. That’s it.”

Tony sat forward, can resting on his thigh. “You got a name, a card, something to prove this isn’t a load of—”

“Ask Mr. Giles,” Xander said. “Ask Principal Snyder, or—” He stopped. “Ask the Vice Principal. He has a contact. You can call and verify it.”

Jessica: “So you’re not leaving school?”

Xander: “Nope. Not unless it’s an emergency.”

Tony: “What kind of emergency?”

Xander shrugged. “The kind where they call, and I decide if I go.”

The silence stretched. On the TV, a B-roll of a traffic jam cycled in endless loop. Tony scratched his chin, then said, “You’re not getting paid.”

Xander: “No.”

Tony: “So what’s the point?”

Xander looked at him, then at Jessica, then at the floor. “The point is, I’m still here. I’m not running away. I’m not going to ditch the house or the school or anything. But I needed to tell you so it didn’t blindside you.”

Tony’s face twisted, then flattened. “This is one of your jokes, isn’t it.”

Xander, flat: “No joke.”

Jessica picked up the wine again, sipped. “Are you in danger?”

Xander shook his head, then, more honestly, said, “I don’t know.”

She nodded, as if she’d expected that. “So we just… go on like always?”

“Yeah,” he said, a little softer than intended.

Tony: “And if someone comes to the door—FBI or whatever—I tell them what?”

Xander: “You tell them I’m not here. Unless it’s a real emergency. Then you can call the number in the kitchen. It’s on the fridge, blue sticky note.”

Tony grunted, then sat back. “If you get paid, you tell me.”

Xander said, “Sure.”

The silence returned, but it wasn’t hostile. It was just… full.

After a minute, Jessica said, “You hungry?”

Xander shook his head. “I’m good.”

He stood, grabbed the satchel, and let himself out. No door slam. Just a clean, quiet exit.

He sat on the front step for a few seconds, letting the night settle. The neighborhood was quiet: a dog barking three streets over, the faint chemical drift of a backyard barbecue, the rattle of a sprinkler somewhere up the block. The houses all looked the same, their lights yellow and warm, and for a moment, Xander let himself believe he could just walk away, disappear into any of them, and no one would care.

He checked the watch. It was synced, green, steady.

Xander stood and started walking, hands in his pockets, the sidewalk cool under his feet, the normalness of it all a little too perfect to trust.

Behind him, the Harris house was dark. Ahead, the world waited.

He kept moving.

Scene 4

The school library after hours felt like the eye of a hurricane: the world outside spun fast and loud, but inside the stacks, everything was padded in books and filtered light. It was Giles’s favorite time, when the students were gone and the only sounds were the hum of the AC and the slow tick of the wall clock above the check-out desk.

Today, the central table was more crowded than usual. Four chairs, four occupants: Buffy, at the head, legs stretched out and boots braced on the edge; Willow, to her left, laptop open, highlighter in one hand; Giles, beside her, posture perfect, blue pen hovering as if every sentence needed annotating. Xander brought up the rear, slouched but alert, a can of soda balanced on the arm of his chair.

A secure laptop, black and boxy, sat at the far end of the table, its camera LED winking red. On the screen, Admiral Chegwidden appeared in high-res grayscale, with a JAG officer two feet behind and a USN flag arrayed over his right shoulder.

The paperwork in front of them was an inch thick, but Willow had collated the key docs into four identical packets, each stapled and stamped “UNCLASSIFIED UPON SIGNATURE.” The cover page: “Memorandum of Understanding / Joint Operational Framework / SDF-01 Task Element.” In smaller print: “Buffy Anne Summers, Lead; Giles Rupert, Civilian Coordinator; Rosenberg Willow, Technical Liaison; Harris Alexander, Field Volunteer (Independent).”

Giles started: “The protocols appear comprehensive. I do have some questions about the medical evacuation clause—”

Chegwidden cut him off, politely but absolutely. “The clause is there to ensure field assets are never left exposed. If you signal for extraction, you get it, no questions. It’s not a lever for command and control, it’s a shield. If you wish, I can walk you through the escalation tree.”

Giles, mollified, tapped the pen and nodded. “That will suffice.”

Buffy leafed through her copy, skipping to the signature page. “So, if we sign, that’s it? We’re a task force, like, Navy-official?”

Chegwidden: “You’re a voluntary element, under civilian direction. The Navy provides support—intel, logistics, medevac, airlift—but you set your own rules of engagement. Nobody’s going to order you anywhere.”

Xander grinned. “Not even for pizza runs?”

Chegwidden didn’t blink. “Mr. Harris, in the event of a pizza emergency, you’ll have the full backing of Naval Aviation.”

Giles allowed himself the faintest of smiles. Willow actually giggled.

Chegwidden: “Now, if you’ll initial section four, page nine, and sign the last page, we’ll have our agreement.”

Buffy flipped to page nine, scrawled a quick BA SUMMERS, and passed the packet to Giles, who signed with old-world flourish. Willow took extra time, reading every line, highlighting one phrase—“critical civilian input”—then signed in neat, looping script.

Xander got the packet last. He read the relevant paragraph, then looked up at the screen. “Just so we’re clear—this doesn’t mean I take orders.”

Chegwidden: “Mr. Harris, if I wanted someone who took orders, I would have drafted you.”

Xander signed, then leaned back, clearly pleased.

Willow scanned each signed page, uploaded the PDFs to the secure portal, and sent the originals via the overnight envelope provided by the Navy courier that afternoon. The JAG officer on the other end clicked a button and the screen registered: “RECEIPT ACKNOWLEDGED.”

Chegwidden looked at each of them in turn, then said, “Thank you, all. You’ve done more than anyone could ask. You’ll have the support you need. And the autonomy.”

Giles: “We appreciate the trust, Admiral.”

Buffy: “And the helicopters.”

Chegwidden smiled, dry and brief. “Don’t crash any. They’re expensive.”

The screen faded to the Navy seal, then to black.

The four sat in silence. Giles removed his glasses and cleaned them on his handkerchief. Willow sorted her highlighters by color, then started a new folder on her laptop: “TASK FORCE.” Xander tapped his watch—the LED blinked once, then glowed steady green, in perfect sync with the Navy server.

Buffy folded her signed copy once, tucked it in her jacket, then looked around the table.

“So that’s it? We’re official?” Willow asked.

Buffy shrugged, not unkind. “We were always official. Now there’s paperwork.”

Outside, the last of the afternoon sun cut a thin gold line through the blinds, then vanished as the world slipped to night. Inside, the air was calm, the signatures drying, the tick of the clock louder than anything waiting in the dark.

They sat a while longer, not in a hurry to leave.

Scene 5

The first version of the integrated array occupied three library tables, two rolling carts, and enough extension cord to violate several fire codes at once. None of its pieces were new. That was the point. Willow’s perimeter monitor had been collecting the town’s fluctuations since the first anchor break. Ethan’s sigil logs gave those fluctuations a grammar. The portable ward rods from the SGC shipment could impose a known pattern, and the rugged sensor core Carter and Jenny had designed could tell them what the Hellmouth did in response.

What had not existed before was a way to make the pieces speak to one another.

Willow sat in the center of the sprawl with her laptop balanced on a milk crate, three adapter cables disappearing beneath the table. The program on her screen had begun as a timestamp matcher for Ethan’s meter readings. Over a month of late nights it had become a ward-resonance parser: part signal analysis, part municipal map, part translation layer for phenomena no computer manual admitted existed.

“The code can follow a repeating frequency,” she said, dragging a green trace over an amber one. “But magic doesn’t stay on a frequency just because we ask nicely. Every time the wards react, the signal shifts. Sometimes by a fraction, sometimes by a whole harmonic.”

Ethan leaned over her shoulder, his left hand holding the battered occult meter against the laptop’s plastic case. “It is not changing at random. It is answering. Push here, it moves there. Threaten the boundary, it gathers at the nearest lawful anchor.”

Giles stood at the far table with the municipal map under a transparent grid. He had marked the school, Restfield, City Hall, and the old utility lines in different colors, then connected them to the ward rods arranged around the room. “Then we do not need the machine to understand magic,” he said. “It needs to recognize the shape of the reply.”

Jenny set one of the sensor modules beside Willow. She had been back from Antarctica for six hours and had spent four of them replacing the module’s cold-weather shielding with a mesh ground that would not turn every active ward into static. “Carter’s array reads field pressure, phase, and energy density. Ethan gives us the expected relationships. Willow’s program compares the two in real time. If the pattern moves, we move with it.”

Buffy, sitting backward on a chair, said, “So it’s a magic radar.”

Four people objected at once.

“Magic radar,” Buffy repeated, satisfied.

The Navy communications specialist on the ladder pretended not to hear. He fed a cable through the open transom and up to the school roof, where four directional antenna loops had been fixed to the corners of the east wing. They were ordinary copper inside weatherproof housings, tuned low enough to catch the sensor modules without broadcasting the data beyond the local relay. A fifth loop, mounted on the library roof, served as the reference point.

O’Neil checked each installation against the diagram. “Passive receive unless Summers authorizes active mode. The data stays local. The Navy gets a copy after the event, not a live window into the school.”

Willow looked up. “And nobody remote can turn the transmitters on.”

“Physical switch only,” the specialist confirmed. He held up a red safety cover. “Your console, your hand.”

That earned him the first unqualified smile of the evening.

They tested the system with a ward Giles knew well enough to fail safely. He planted the white-oak stave at the center of a chalk-and-salt circle while Ethan opened a hairline seam at the western edge. The roof antennas registered the pressure change first. Carter’s sensor core translated it into a blue rise on Willow’s screen; half a second later, Ethan’s meter twitched and the chalk line brightened.

“Three sources, same event,” Willow said. She tagged the peak. “That’s our baseline.”

Ethan shifted the seam clockwise. The signal slid across the antenna grid, one node handing it to the next. Willow’s parser chased the movement, adjusting the comparison frequency in small steps until the traces overlapped again.

“Frequency chase,” she said. “It can stay with a moving pattern.”

Jenny watched the power draw climb. “Until the pattern starts feeding on the chase. If someone builds a recursive working, continuous correction becomes an amplifier.”

Ethan glanced at her. “An antenna instead of a lock.”

“Exactly.” Jenny reached past Willow and added a second control beneath the first. “We need a pulse mode. One sharp interruption, then silence before the working can use our output as a carrier.”

Willow named it SPIKE and gave the button a confirmation window. “Frequency chase for tracking. Spike mode for breaking a feedback loop. We do not press the exciting button unless somebody says the words ‘positive feedback.’”

“Or unless the sky opens,” Xander said.

“If the sky opens,” Giles replied, “we shall endeavor to be more specific.”

The portable command station took shape around the software. A folding camp table held Willow’s laptop and the sensor interface. Ethan drew an interlocked ward array directly onto its metal surface, each loop corresponding to one antenna node and each break giving him a place to interrupt the geometry by hand. Jenny marked the safe contact points in Starfleet blue and stocked her field kit with insulating patches, solvent, conductive markers, burn dressings, and the neon restraints Urgayle favored because nobody could pretend not to see them.

They ran the drill three times.

Giles held the axis and controlled the main ward. Willow stayed inside the defensive perimeter and called the frequencies. Ethan worked the hostile logic from the edge, where he could read or break a secondary pattern without crossing the primary line. Jenny moved between diagnostics and casualties. Buffy and Xander stayed mobile, one on the ground and one above it. The Navy watched the streets and did not enter the magical perimeter unless called.

On the first run, Willow chased the signal too long and the practice ward threw a spark through the laptop’s power brick. On the second, Ethan crossed behind Jenny without warning and earned an elbow to the ribs. On the third, the parser found the moving peak, Giles held it in place, Willow fired a controlled spike, and every light on the table went from amber to blue.

O’Neil stopped the clock. “Twenty-seven seconds. Again tomorrow.”

Buffy looked at the cables, the chalk, the roof feed, and the people standing in their assigned places. “Still calling it magic radar.”

This time nobody bothered to argue.

They packed the command table beneath the library steps with the cables already tied in sequence. The antenna grid stayed live in passive mode, listening to the ordinary pressure of the Hellmouth and teaching Willow’s parser what quiet looked like. By midnight, the blue baseline had become almost boring.

It stayed that way for three nights.

✳

The allied readiness call ended with no one pretending the situation was under control. Britain committed airlift, Australia tracking corridors, France medical support, and China and Russia observer access with every legal objection preserved.

The exercise ran without Superman. Simulated orbital strikes took out two command nodes, but evacuation held because the response did not depend on one person crossing the sky. Only after the baseline held did Carter add Xander as an optional branch.

That was the difference between a miracle and a capability. Miracles were welcome. Capabilities got scheduled.
