The City Line

Scene 1

The advance element arrived in the parking lot of a Sunnydale strip mall that advertised psychic readings, tax preparation, and all-you-can-eat Chinese. Chegwidden, O’Neil, and Urgayle wore civilian jackets over uniforms that could have been anything from government-issue to the discount rack at Sears. The rented SUV still smelled of plastic and low-mileage shame. Urgayle said nothing about the paint match between the Suburban and the faded curb stripes, but his scowl measured the tactical possibilities of each parked car.

Sunnydale did not notice. Sunnydale never noticed.

They made a slow circuit before entering the office space the Navy had leased on forty-eight hours’ notice. It came furnished with an ancient copy machine, two plastic palm trees, and a conference table sticky from the last tenant’s enthusiasm for both donuts and litigation. Chegwidden surveyed the windowless room, said “Better than half the places I’ve commanded from,” and set his briefcase in the exact center.

O’Neil examined the security panel. “Main entrance is glass, deadbolt’s civilian grade. Two internal lines already installed, but the comms team needs another hour for full secure. Copier is a bust.”

“Define bust,” Chegwidden said.

“Tonally matched to the coffee, sir.”

Urgayle had brought three bags: one for the comms gear, one for logistics, and one for the field medicine. He unpacked nothing, surveyed the perimeter, and assigned the first two civilian security specialists their posts. The third was still en route; Urgayle left her a note in the form of a black watch cap and an open energy bar.

They ran first protocols on whiteboards stripped of any branding.

Buffy and Giles arrived at the back door seven minutes early. Giles still had the aura of a man who believed the official world could be tamed by tweed and patience, but the dark lenses he now wore told another story. Buffy wore a jacket with new seams under one arm, and the look of a person grading every grown-up she met for any sign of liar or zealot.

Xander came two minutes later, dressed as Alexander Harris in every way except for the loose-fitting gloves that would not have passed at the Army-Navy surplus. He had a manila folder under one arm, a school ID in his back pocket, and the posture of a demolition expert waiting for the all-clear. Willow, apparently, had been left in reserve.

Chegwidden invited them into the main room without a handshake. He introduced himself, the officers, and the mission in the language of arson investigation: “My job is to prevent secondary fires. Everything else, including us, is containment.”

He gestured at the table. “Slayer at the head. Mr. Harris, next. Giles, you may float.”

Buffy raised an eyebrow, but took the head of the table without comment.

Chegwidden began. “We are here to provide infrastructure, not management. That means our orders are to support, not to command. I am required to say that out loud at the start of every meeting until you believe it.”

“How many meetings are you planning?” Xander said.

“Enough that you can break the schedule with a phone call, but not enough to become the next problem.”

O’Neil ran through the briefing. Each offer was listed by category: medical, communications, logistics, research, and emergency extraction. Each had a clause for voluntary refusal, with the refusals to be logged but not second-guessed.

“Refusing help doesn’t end the help,” O’Neil said. “It just means we try again with a different approach.”

Giles glanced at the research annex. “Will you expect reciprocal access to Watcher records?”

“Offer only,” O’Neil said. “We will not request the Council’s proprietary material, but if you choose to share a lead, we’ll treat it as privileged. The trade is: you get a Navy research assistant and all the open source we can scrape, and we never ask where you got your own.”

Buffy said, “What happens if you see something before we do?”

Urgayle answered. “We inform you. We offer resources. If you decline, we observe. If lives are at risk, and only if, we act independently—but only after making the refusal clear and logging it. You get the next review, and you can appeal every action up the chain.”

Giles looked at the appeals flowchart. “I see Admiral Chegwidden is the top of the chain.”

“I picked the job,” Chegwidden said. “If I’m not the right adult for the appeal, you’ll know immediately.”

Xander flipped through his own copy of the protocol sheet. “Medical support says ‘independent physician, no sample retention, no research without specific consent.’ Does that include blood draws after a... situation?”

O’Neil did not smile. “Only for treatment. Not a research sample unless you sign a fresh form every time. The base has already shredded a dozen requests from the Science Directorate to use you for baseline studies.”

“They’re relentless,” Xander said.

“Yes, but not our problem,” O’Neil said.

Buffy scanned the rescue section. “If I get hit and you have to evac me, is my name in every Navy database by the next morning?”

“Patient identity is protected. You have a case number, not a record. If you want it wiped after treatment, you can request deletion. If you want it shared with anyone, you can direct that too. But until then, it’s sealed above most military secrets.”

Buffy closed her copy. “And if you get a direct order from higher up? White House, Pentagon?”

“Admiral Chegwidden takes the call,” O’Neil said. “We go through him or not at all.”

Chegwidden waited for the challenge.

It came from Xander. “What’s the catch? You don’t field this much support unless somebody’s expecting a mess that can’t be solved with a phone call and a nice letter.”

Chegwidden’s expression shifted half a degree. “The catch is you are already operating at a level that would require three special-ops teams, five intelligence shops, and a support battalion to keep up. The world can only pretend to ignore that for so long. My job is to make the world never notice unless you want it to.”

Buffy folded her arms. “So what happens if we say no?”

“Then you continue as before, but with better phone numbers and a Navy base willing to answer them at 3 a.m. If you want the base to leave, the base leaves.”

“Do you believe that?” she asked Xander.

He studied the faces at the table. “I believe that if we say go, Chegwidden makes it happen. I also believe the first time I punch a hole through something with ‘classified’ on the side, half the world’s going to notice and the other half will want to own me.”

Giles nodded. “That sounds plausible.”

O’Neil passed a copy of the field medical form to Buffy. “Try not to need this, but if you do, the medic will ask only what you want recorded. None of this gets to your mother or anyone else unless you approve it.”

Buffy met O’Neil’s gaze. “You’re not what I expected.”

“Neither are you,” O’Neil replied.

Urgayle interrupted, his tone formal. “Three minutes to secure the exterior.”

Chegwidden closed his folder. “That is your cue to see if the team can move through Sunnydale without waking the whole town. First test: show them the park, the school, and anything you consider important. Not the Hellmouth, not yet.”

Buffy exchanged looks with Xander. “We taking them on patrol?”

“That’s the idea,” Chegwidden said.

Buffy rose, buttoned her jacket, and beckoned Xander to her side. Giles followed, already reviewing the field notes and muttering about procurement rules for crossbow bolts. O’Neil and Urgayle followed at an unobtrusive distance, their posture somewhere between personal security and very polite observation.

Outside, the town looked unchanged, but the air had the weight of secrets waiting to tip.

They walked the route past the park, under the half-lit lamplight of a playground that doubled as both a supernatural hot zone and a place for toddlers to experiment with gravity. Buffy explained the difference between normal and not-normal vampire behavior. O’Neil asked if the park had ever been used as a muster point by the opposition. Xander said, “Every week. They’re as unoriginal as high-school vandals and twice as lazy. If they were smart, they’d use the mall.”

Urgayle took silent notes on a small, waterproof pad.

When the route swung by the school, Xander hesitated at the sight of the blue mailbox. The repaired sidewalk still bore a half-moon scar from his last encounter. O’Neil noticed, but did not ask.

They passed through the cemetery, where Angel waited. He wore the expression of someone who had arrived early and spent the time inventing reasons to regret it. He nodded at Buffy, ignored O’Neil, and gave Urgayle a look that suggested he had already memorized the new team’s gait, scent, and probable intentions.

O’Neil surprised everyone by asking Angel to brief her on Sunnydale’s post-midnight traffic patterns.

“Your people use patrol routes,” she said. “So do the others. How often do they repeat?”

“Too often,” Angel said.

“Do they avoid surveillance or approach it?”

“Depends who’s in town. The smarter ones use the sewers or split up before anyone can follow.”

“Any rules about who gets first pick of new ground?”

Angel gave her a flat look. “Nobody’s written them down, but yeah. They don’t like a crowd unless they’re hunting. If they’re moving in numbers, there’s a reason.”

O’Neil scribbled on her pad. “Thank you.”

The conversation moved to surface level again. They completed the round trip in under forty minutes, never once drawing the attention of a police cruiser, a wandering night jogger, or any of the three actual vampires Xander saw shadowing them from the far side of the street. It felt like a test, and the town passed.

Back at the base, Chegwidden had arranged coffee, disposable cups, and what might have been a donut had it not lost its will to live somewhere near Bakersfield. He gestured everyone back to the table.

“Now you know the field,” he said. “What do you want to change?”

Buffy answered first. “Don’t trail us. Don’t shadow the cemeteries unless we call. And if you see something, say something, but don’t try to ‘intervene’ without a reason you can explain in one sentence.”

Chegwidden wrote that down, word for word.

“Mr. Harris?” he prompted.

“Keep the science team off my back,” Xander said. “If they need a sample, they get it from the hardware, not the software. And if you have a plan for what to do if I snap, you tell me about it. I don’t want a secret fail-safe nobody can talk about.”

“Done,” Chegwidden said.

“Giles?” O’Neil asked.

“Procurement forms,” Giles said. “Less than four pages, please.”

O’Neil gave a half-smile. “We’ll do our best.”

The final protocol was signed. The command was functional. The offer—support, not command—was real.

Buffy took the first donut and broke it in half. She passed one half to Xander, then ate her own with slow, skeptical defiance. Urgayle sampled the coffee and pronounced it “less hazardous than expected.”

As the meeting broke, Xander caught O’Neil at the door.

“You really don’t care what we do as long as nobody makes the news?”

“Not true,” she said. “We care. We just recognize you’re the only ones with a clue what you’re doing. If we take that away, we lose.”

“That’s not what I expected.”

“You’ll get used to it,” O’Neil said.

Xander doubted it, but for now, the system held.

In the rented office, Chegwidden watched the monitors and saw nothing amiss. He made a note: “First contact: operational. Trust: baseline. Friction: high, but contained.”

He almost smiled.

The world was not ready for Sunnydale.

But it was now, at least, on the clock.

Scene 2

At 11:12 a.m., Joyce Summers was stapling shipping manifests at the gallery office when her new cell phone erupted with a sound like a broken smoke alarm. She had assigned the “federal line” ringtone to something subtle, but the configuration had reset during the last software update. The gallery intern dropped an entire stack of acrylics. Joyce thumbed the green icon and tried to keep her voice level.

“Summers.”

A voice she didn’t know—female, precise, practiced at customer-service vowels—announced: “This is an operational readiness test. Please state the emergency protocol password and verify the secure code.”

Joyce’s mind blanked for half a second. She reached for the envelope she’d placed at the bottom of her bag, only to realize it was still on the kitchen counter where she’d last reviewed it. The intern attempted to restack the acrylics but succeeded only in turning a minor mess into a field of plastic dominoes.

“I, uh—could you hold a moment?” Joyce clamped the phone between shoulder and ear, spread the gallery files in front of her, and scanned for the half-remembered protocol printout. “This is a drill,” she repeated to herself. Not an attack. Not a real call. The context helped, but only slightly.

She found the packet, fumbled the tab open, and read off the code phrase. The woman on the other end repeated it back, asked her to verify the numbers at the bottom of page two, and then explained that, in the event of an “active kinetic event,” Joyce would be required to shelter in place, activate the local comms kit, and initiate a call tree which included at least three other named parties. Only one of whom she actually recognized: Giles.

Joyce wrote the instructions on a sticky note as the protocol officer read them off. Her handwriting, normally a point of pride, was almost illegible, and her fingers left a faint smudge where she clutched the pen too tightly.

“Do you understand the protocol, ma’am?”

“Yes,” Joyce lied. “I—yes, I do.”

“Would you like to run through a dry test?”

She hesitated. The intern had finally rebuilt the acrylic tower, and three other gallery staffers were now peering in from the hallway. It was a Tuesday, which meant the next hour was unlikely to require her expertise. Or so she hoped.

“Yes,” Joyce said, and as soon as she hung up she was dialing Giles.

He answered on the first ring. “Summers residence?”

“It’s me. They’re doing the drill.”

“Excellent,” Giles said. “It’s important to establish a baseline—”

“I can barely remember my own password. They want me to call two more people. Are you one of them?”

“I am. The second is likely Mrs. Rosenberg, and the third may be a liaison at the Navy office.”

Joyce exhaled. “Shouldn’t you be doing this, Giles?”

There was a pause. “They’ve determined, not without reason, that redundancy is preferable to sole reliance on an individual currently under observation for, as the admiral put it, ‘chronic overwork and probable sleep deficit.’”

Joyce closed her eyes. “That would be you.”

“I can confirm,” Giles said dryly.

She called Willow’s mother, got voicemail, left the required message, and then dialed the Sunnydale Navy number. The woman at the other end responded with the same sequence, as if this were the fourth time she’d done it today. Which, Joyce realized, it probably was.

By the time she hung up, the staffers had drifted away and the intern was pretending to sweep the rug with a two-inch paintbrush.

Joyce sat down. Her hand trembled a little. The phone vibrated again—this time, a text from the Navy: DRILL COMPLETE. RESPONSE: TIMELY. NO ACTION REQUIRED. She stared at the message, then scrolled up to see the last real conversation with Buffy, from the night before. The juxtaposition nearly undid her.

In a windowless office, Chegwidden reviewed the drill report.

“She hesitated, but completed the tree,” O’Neil said.

“She’s never had a protocol,” Chegwidden replied. “That was the point.”

Urgayle said, “Stress response: nonzero, but within parameters.”

“Repeat in three weeks,” Chegwidden said. “If she fails, we repeat in one. If she succeeds, we move to actual event simulation.”

O’Neil watched the data come in. “She’s worried she’s the weak link.”

Chegwidden drummed a finger on the desk. “That’s the strength of it. She knows the stakes. The ones who don’t worry—those are the ones I want off my list.”

Urgayle said, “Summers family response has improved in every metric.”

Chegwidden made a note. “Keep it that way.”

Back at the gallery, Joyce refilled her mug, wrote “ACTIVATE CALL TREE” on a fresh post-it, and stuck it to the phone case. She wasn’t sure if the exercise had made her more ready, or just more anxious. But she had not failed, and nobody had called to replace her with a specialist or a consultant or, God forbid, a professional parent.

For the rest of the day, every time the phone buzzed, she felt ready enough to answer.

Scene 3

“Field day,” O’Neil called it, and none of them laughed until Xander nearly killed himself trying to demonstrate a proper lifting technique on the park’s commemorative boulder. He wore civilian clothes, but his sneakers were one size too large—on Chegwidden’s recommendation, in case he needed to shed them in an emergency. Urgayle supervised the perimeter as if expecting a sniper in every family sedan. The comms specialist, who had not been introduced by name, spent the whole time staring at the sky and scribbling on an unmarked legal pad.

Xander took the support element out the southern edge of town, where the pavement thinned and the valley dropped into chaparral and dry riverbed. Sunnydale was visible behind them as a haze of palm trees, cheap tile roofs, and electrical substations.

“This is the fun part,” Xander said, gesturing with both arms. “No traffic, no cameras, and nobody to see you fly except the odd, very disappointed hawk.”

O’Neil checked her phone. “We have fifteen minutes before the check-in call.”

“I’ll have you back in ten. Twenty if you want to see something cool.”

Urgayle scanned the horizon. “Show us.”

Xander jogged to the old county line, now a battered road sign almost invisible behind yucca and sage. The comms guy timed the run with a stopwatch, then made a note that included “unexpected lateral acceleration.” Xander reappeared a full thirty meters past the line, slowed, and jogged back with barely an extra breath.

O’Neil tilted her head. “That was…”

“About seventy miles per hour, give or take the wind,” Xander said. “The boulder thing is for comic effect. This is the real trick.”

He leaned against the sign, waiting for one of them to ask the question.

Urgayle said, “Can you sustain that speed into the city?”

“I’ve done it,” Xander said. “But I usually slow up before residential areas. Debris, pets, kids—don’t want to make the news.”

O’Neil drew a small circle in her notebook. “And your top speed?”

“Tested at… I don’t know, Mach 2? 3? But only in a straight line, and not for long. You get weird stuff at that speed. Air pressure, heat, and sometimes—”

Xander stopped. Something about the line had changed. He felt it through his toes, like the static from an ungrounded carpet, but it was rooted and thick and threaded with something he couldn’t name.

O’Neil looked up. “Problem?”

Xander raised his hand. “Weird thing, but did you notice the sign?”

She looked. “Sunnydale, population forty-two thousand. Why?”

“Read the jurisdiction tag.”

She squinted at the bottom of the metal: “Maintained by City of Sunnydale, County of—” It was scratched, but the city seal was visible. “What am I looking for?”

“The marker’s different. It’s…”

He reached out, but his hand hit an invisible wall. It did not bounce, and it did not tingle, but it absolutely refused to let him through. He tried again, this time with both hands, and pressed as if pushing on a stubborn bathroom door. Nothing.

Urgayle tried to walk past him, but the boundary did not exist for anyone else. The comms guy, curious, jogged straight through with a wave. Xander tried again, and again, until O’Neil said, “Do you want to show us or not?”

He put his shoulder to it, found the pressure doubled, tripled, then simply failed to translate into movement. Xander stepped back.

“Try going the other way,” O’Neil said.

He did, walking into the wild nothing for ten meters, then circled back. This time, when he tried to re-enter Sunnydale, the resistance was absolute.

Xander looked at the ground. No physical markers, no pentagrams, nothing. The city itself had decided, somewhere between the cheap sign and the first fire hydrant, that he was no longer welcome.

Urgayle moved up next to him and tried to drag Xander forward, hand on his arm. Urgayle went through, Xander’s hand stayed stuck.

O’Neil called Chegwidden.

“Boundary event,” she said. “Subject Harris cannot re-enter city limits. Supernatural or otherwise, effect is total. Request guidance.”

On the other end, Chegwidden had already received a security alert. He toggled the speaker. “Harris, do you read?”

“Yeah, I read,” Xander said. “This is… not great.”

“Report sensation,” Chegwidden said.

Xander tried to keep the panic out of his voice. “It’s like the sidewalk is glue, but only for me. I can see the other side, I just can’t get there. No pain, no resistance for anyone else.”

Chegwidden said, “Repeat attempt with civilian escort.”

O’Neil called back the comms specialist, who had been recording everything on a GoPro hidden in a cell phone case. The specialist took Xander’s wrist and tried to tow him forward. The skin on Xander’s hand stretched, then simply stopped, like it was glued to glass. Xander looked at O’Neil, then back at his stuck wrist.

“Try at speed,” Urgayle said.

“That’s—”

“Try it,” O’Neil said.

Xander backed up, squared his shoulders, and ran straight at the line. He hit the boundary at full stride and fell hard, bouncing off nothing, rolling onto his shoulder. The boundary did not so much as ripple.

He sat up, rubbing his arm.

O’Neil offered a hand. “Anything broken?”

“Not physically.”

She pulled him to his feet. “We’re in uncharted territory.”

“Literally,” Xander said.

O’Neil placed a call to Giles and Buffy. “We have a boundary anomaly. Xander is currently outside the city and cannot return. Recommend regroup at the office.”

Buffy answered, “Is he hurt?”

O’Neil glanced at Xander. “No, but he’s… annoyed.”

Buffy’s voice, through the phone: “Tell him we’ll figure it out.”

They returned to the Navy office, minus Xander, who stood outside the sign and stared back at Sunnydale with a look reserved for people denied re-entry to their own birthday party.

Buffy and Giles arrived five minutes later. Willow had class, but texted from the library.

Buffy asked, “What happened?”

O’Neil explained, then produced the GoPro footage. Giles examined the video frame-by-frame. “It doesn’t have the characteristics of a force field,” he said. “No light, no diffraction. He simply ceases to move forward.”

Buffy shook her head. “So magic?”

Giles shrugged, for the first time admitting he had no answer. “Possibly. But it is not one I recognize.”

Urgayle suggested, “We try a different identity.”

Buffy perked up. “If Xander is in costume, does it still block him?”

O’Neil called him. “Superman up and try re-entry.”

They watched from the window as Xander took off his jacket, produced the suit from the duffel bag, and changed. He was visible from two blocks away, blue and red in the morning sun.

He walked to the boundary and tried again.

It didn’t work.

Xander gestured, frustrated, then tried to fly over the line. The effect was instantaneous: he rebounded thirty feet into the scrub, landing unhurt but deeply humiliated.

He called O’Neil. “Still a no-go. I don’t even make it a foot.”

Buffy turned to Giles. “What if it’s about the name?”

“Explain,” Giles said.

She pulled out her phone, showing the text from Willow: “Rook’s identity working separated Xander from Superman. Maybe only one can enter at a time?”

Giles pondered. “So what would the city recognize?”

O’Neil dialed Chegwidden. “Sir, if the theory is correct, we may need a legal or civic identity to permit re-entry.”

Chegwidden said, “Does the town possess an official record of his other identity?”

Buffy snorted. “They have his school files, disciplinary reports, attendance. All of it.”

Giles added, “There is a copy at City Hall, Principal Snyder’s office, and possibly the county records office.”

Chegwidden: “Retrieve them. We’ll test with the most recent record. Perhaps if the identity is physically present, it may allow entry.”

Buffy and Giles left for the school. O’Neil and Urgayle supervised, but kept to the background.

In Snyder’s office, the school secretary gave them a look like she expected a search warrant and a team of news reporters to follow. Giles asked for Xander’s file, saying he needed to review an old incident for a disciplinary review. The secretary retrieved a thick folder, banded in red, and handed it over with a look that said “You only think you have the whole story.”

Buffy took a copy of Xander’s most recent ID card. She snapped a picture and texted it to O’Neil.

O’Neil forwarded it to Xander. “Try again, but carry your ID. Announce your name as you cross.”

Buffy’s text arrived: “Willow thinks you need to introduce yourself as Alexander Harris, not Superman.”

He groaned, but tried it.

Xander walked up to the line, held up the ID, and said, “Alexander Lavelle Harris, returning from authorized absence. Please let me in.”

He pressed forward.

It gave.

Not much, but enough for him to get one foot past the line before he felt the pressure again. He staggered, then, holding the card in front of him, walked the last ten meters in slow, deliberate steps.

O’Neil watched from the window as Xander crossed into city limits, then immediately collapsed on the grass, breathing hard.

He called the office.

“It worked,” he said. “But I feel like I got mugged by a DMV clerk.”

Buffy and Giles drove back and found Xander leaning against the bus stop, still in the suit, ID in one hand.

Giles said, “You’re you again?”

“For now,” Xander said. “But it’s not a great feeling. I think if I put the card down, I get locked out again.”

Buffy looked at O’Neil. “What does this mean?”

O’Neil said, “It means the opposition has figured out how to weaponize bureaucracy against superpowers.”

Buffy shuddered. “That’s actually more terrifying than vampires.”

Giles said, “Is there a workaround?”

O’Neil thought for a moment. “We could forge IDs, rewrite the town registry, or hack the boundary directly. But I suggest we treat this as a temporary state, test for vulnerabilities, and avoid sending Xander outside the city unless we have a backup plan.”

Buffy nodded.

Urgayle looked at Xander. “Can you fight with one hand?”

“I can try,” Xander said.

O’Neil updated Chegwidden: “Boundary now keyed to legal identity. Supernatural asset cannot re-enter city unless carrying official documentation. We’ll recommend in-person status checks before any external deployments.”

Chegwidden replied, “Put it in the next report. Also, prepare for the possibility of civilian unmasking.”

O’Neil signed off.

Buffy watched Xander fumble his way back toward the office, the suit stretched at the knees, the ID held like a passkey to the worst amusement park on earth.

“Never thought I’d see the day,” she said.

“Which part?” Giles asked.

“Where the city hates Superman more than vampires.”

Giles smiled, small and sad. “I suspect the city hates the idea of anyone being two things at once.”

Buffy watched Xander walk, and for the first time since Sunnydale’s latest disaster, she wondered what would happen if the line finally closed behind them.

On Wednesday, Joyce was in her car, halfway to the post office, when the emergency line pulsed twice and went dead. She turned down the radio, waited for the second notification, but none came. A block ahead, the traffic light flickered yellow, then blue, then the usual red. Joyce hesitated. Sunnydale traffic did not justify stop-and-go, not at this hour, and most mornings the town behaved as if physics were still a live debate.

The phone vibrated in her purse. She parked at the curb, ignored a muttered horn from the only other driver on the block, and checked the alert.

SECURITY NOTICE: SUSPICIOUS ACTIVITY NEAR YOUR LOCATION. REMAIN IN VEHICLE. AWAIT INSTRUCTIONS.

She took a breath, snapped the purse shut, and watched the sidewalk.

Thirty seconds later, a follow-up message arrived.

IF SAFE, CALL 415-XXX-XXXX. VERIFY CODE ALPHA-ONE-NINE BEFORE DISCUSSION.

Joyce did as instructed, only realizing afterward that her hands were slick on the phone. The number picked up on the second ring; the voice was O’Neil’s, clipped and unhurried.

“Summers?”

“Yes—this is Joyce.”

“Remain in your car. Keep the doors locked. We have a transient anomaly in the vicinity of the library.”

“Is it—”

“Containment only. No direct threat. However, a local entity was observed attempting to enter the library during open hours. We do not believe it is aware of you.”

Joyce processed this. “Are Buffy and Giles—”

“They are safe. Asset Harris is in place. This is a containment drill, but with real observation. You are the closest responder.”

Joyce’s voice threatened to crack. “I don’t know what I’m supposed to do.”

“Monitor your surroundings. Do not attempt to engage. If you observe anything unusual, describe it as directly as possible. If you see the entity, note direction and clothing. If not, wait for the all-clear.”

The instructions ended. Joyce waited, feeling the engine idle vibrate under her shoes. She made a list of possible “unusual” things: vampires, demons, men in suits, or perhaps nothing at all.

Four minutes passed before she saw the shape: a very thin man, pale as bleached paper, wearing a charity-run t-shirt, pushing a battered shopping cart loaded with electronics, two spiral notebooks, and what appeared to be a cello case.

She called in the description, feeling faintly ridiculous.

“Entity observed,” she said. “Eastbound, cart, possible violin or… cello case. No threat. Just walking.”

O’Neil’s voice: “Copy. Watch for any interaction with public, or approach to vehicles.”

The shape paused at the end of the block, then crossed the street in front of her car. Joyce ducked low, just for a second. The figure ignored her, adjusted the cart, and kept moving. Joyce let out a breath.

Within seconds, two men in city-employee jumpsuits emerged from a nearby maintenance shed. They trailed the entity at a safe distance. A civilian vehicle—definitely not a real cop, but possibly the Navy medic from the day before—idled into view.

Joyce updated the line. “Approaching Park Avenue. No contact with public. Looks… sad, honestly.”

“Observed. Maintain position.”

Five minutes later, O’Neil called back.

“Situation resolved. Stand down.”

Joyce released her seatbelt, set the phone down, and then realized she had not checked her mirrors the whole time.

She started the car. Her hands were still trembling.

At the Navy office, Urgayle entered a brief report.

CITIZEN RESPONSE: PROMPT. DESCRIPTIVE. NO ESCALATION. SUBJECT MAINTAINED VISUAL WITHOUT COMPROMISING POSITION.

Chegwidden reviewed it. “She handled it.”

O’Neil added, “She’ll feel like she didn’t.”

“That is better than the alternative,” Chegwidden said. “Put her on monthly drills. Increase to weekly if response time drops.”

Urgayle nodded. “Message discipline?”

“She tells the truth,” Chegwidden said.

“Even when she’s scared.”

He signed off.

At home that night, Joyce went through the after-action report with Buffy.

“I know it wasn’t a real attack,” Joyce said, “but I was useless. I just sat there and stared at the man.”

Buffy frowned. “If you’d gotten out, it would have gone wrong. The rules are to not get involved.”

Joyce touched her daughter’s hand. “I don’t want to mess up and get you hurt. Or anyone else.”

“You did everything right,” Buffy said, and for the first time, it sounded like she meant it.

The next day, Chegwidden mailed Joyce a thank-you card. It was unsigned, but it contained a post-it: “Perfect execution of protocol. You are an asset.”

Joyce put it on her fridge. Under the magnet with the yellow dog.

Scene 5

They planned the next crossing as a controlled test. O’Neil, Urgayle, and the comms specialist set up cameras and physiological sensors at the edge of town. Buffy and Willow brought backup, both magical and medical, and Giles arrived with two briefcases of books and three more crossbow bolts than he had hands for.

Xander paced the line. He felt it before he saw it—a pressure behind the eyes, a building charge in the molars, the urge to shiver despite the ninety-degree air. The boundary remembered him, and this time it was not merely bureaucratic. It was personal.

Buffy handed him the ID card. “You want to try it, or should we call it off?”

He held up the card. “If this doesn’t work, I might get stuck out there. Or in-between.”

She nodded. “It’s your call.”

“Yeah. That’s the problem.”

He approached the sign. There was no physical marker, but to his senses it burned like a weld. He steeled himself, then walked forward.

The first step was easy. The second felt like wading through honey.

On the third step, something inside Xander split—an internal seam, a doubled voice, a loud, relentless accounting of everything he was, and everything he was not.

He heard O’Neil’s voice over the headset. “Harris, you okay?”

Xander wanted to answer, but his mouth couldn’t pick a side. The words hung in his throat, fighting for purchase.

He took a fourth step, the world shearing like a mirror in an earthquake. He saw himself reflected in both directions: on one side, Superman, blue and rigid and defined by certainty; on the other, Alexander Harris, sixteen, average height, a kid who spent every night wishing for something impossible.

The boundary made a choice.

It tried to keep both, and in the process, tore Xander sideways.

He collapsed to his knees at the edge of the sidewalk, one hand gripping the ID so tight it cut his palm, the other stretched out behind him, fingers splayed and trembling. To everyone else, it looked like a faint shadow in the air—a ripple, then a flicker, and then a second Xander, blurry and indistinct, a step behind.

Buffy ran forward, but O’Neil held her back. “Wait. It’s not stable.”

Giles watched, jaw clenched. “The working is attempting to separate the two identities.”

Willow whispered, “Like a split personality. Only real.”

Buffy said, “We have to help him.”

O’Neil nodded. “Let it finish. Otherwise we don’t know what we’re rescuing.”

The two images of Xander strained against each other. The Superman side tried to stand, but the Harris side clung to the ground, resisting. The air snapped, once, and a pulse of ozone rolled across the lot.

Then the Xanders merged, momentarily, and the body at the line collapsed into one—trembling, alive, but breathing so shallowly that O’Neil signaled the medic.

“Get him clear. Move him to the shade.”

Buffy and Willow dragged Xander out of the direct sun. He was pale, cold, sweating. Giles knelt beside him.

“Harris? Can you hear me?”

Xander opened his eyes, and for a second they were blue, then brown, then blue again. He blinked, and the effect faded.

“Yeah,” he said. “I’m here. We’re here.”

He tried to sit, but the medic held him down and checked his pulse, then flashed a light in each eye.

Buffy asked, “Can you feel both?”

Xander nodded. “They’re not separate. But they’re not together either. It’s like having two radios, and both are always on.”

O’Neil said, “Is it manageable?”

Xander shrugged. “Ask me after I try to sleep.”

Urgayle helped him to his feet. “You functioned as two, then one. Is there a chance you could stay that way?”

Giles answered, “Given the strength of the working, it’s possible the separation is now a permanent risk. The intent was not just to bar entry, but to force a schism in identity.”

O’Neil said, “If it happens again, will you warn us?”

Xander smiled, small and grim. “Hard not to.”

The medic taped a gauze square over the cut in his palm and gave him water. Xander drank, then looked at the others.

Buffy squeezed his shoulder. “We’re not letting it split you. We’ll figure it out.”

He nodded, but the look in his eyes said it was already too late for figuring.

Back at the office, Chegwidden read the incident report, then wrote a new protocol in red ink: “If identity working is suspected, all crossings to be supervised and logged. Monitor for duplication, schism, and any evidence of parallel existence. Report immediately. Do not assume re-integration is benign.”

He closed the file.

Buffy waited with Xander in the shade, watching as he finished the bottle and tried to joke about failed science experiments. The support team packed up the gear. Willow started researching city records for a way to reverse the working, but Xander knew, deep down, that the effect was not designed for reversal.

He watched the sun move across the pavement, and wondered how many versions of himself would be left by morning.
