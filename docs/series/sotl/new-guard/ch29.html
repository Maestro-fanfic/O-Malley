Tech Transfer

Scene 1

The cargo bay wasn’t designed for humans, let alone four SGC engineers, two scientists, and enough salvaged technology to outfit a regional diagnostic lab. Every time the door cycled, a new sheet of Antarctic air curled in, fogging up the first meter of space and coaxing visible breath from anyone not lucky enough to be fully covered in thermal layers. The lighting rig overhead was SGC surplus, twenty halogens bolted to a lattice of Starfleet conduit, and it pulsed at a frequency that made time feel slower, or at least flatter.

Jenny Calendar had Beverly Crusher’s hands. She never thought about it in those words, but the way her fingers aligned a circuit, the way the calibration tool hovered above the contact, that came from thirty years of experience that were never supposed to be hers. Across the workbench, Carter watched the feed on a rig-mounted monitor, her eyes flicking between the numbers and the live trace, not quite smiling but fully present.

The sensor core was a stripped Goa’uld array, lifted from a ribbon device they’d neutralized last month. Carter had dissected it with a scalpel and a jeweler’s loupe, then rebuilt it in a form factor slightly larger than a paperback—thick, dense, the kind of thing you could drop from a roof and expect to work. The display was a repurposed Starfleet data padd, but its interface had been rewritten from scratch; the sweep line glowed a jittery green, stuttering every time Jenny tweaked the micro-tuner at the base.

The SGC engineers crowded close, four of them, their jackets still dusted with snow from the walk across the bay. One of them, a bearded guy with the name tape STANTON, took notes with an actual pencil on a clipboard the color of dried mustard. The rest watched in a way that made Jenny think of grad students at a department seminar: eager, ready to pounce on a misstep but also hoping to witness something they could someday claim as their own.

Carter said, “Ready on three,” and Jenny nodded, fitting the final coupling into the slot with a satisfying metallic click. The sweep line on the padd stilled, hovered, then jumped from green to a low, steady amber.

The room went quiet except for the soft rush of the air circulator.

“That’s a target lock,” Carter said. She said it with the calm of someone who’d just observed a chemical reaction she predicted but never quite believed would happen in the wild.

Jenny set the tool down, stretched her fingers, and made herself look at the display before she looked at Carter. “What’s the accuracy?”

Carter pressed a sequence on the side panel. “Six milliradians, plus or minus two, at this temperature. Better than the ribbon’s original.” She looked over at the engineers. “Mark it—full success on the static test.”

Stanton made a note on the clipboard. The others leaned in, squinting at the readout, maybe hoping to catch a glitch or an unexpected jump in the trace.

Jenny flexed her left hand, shook off the pins-and-needles that still showed up when she worked too long without stopping. She reached for the power bypass, disengaged it, then began walking the engineers through the reversal sequence. “You go cold before the power cell swap. If you don’t, you risk a resonance spike—see here, the insulation’s not built for continuous load. Pull the secondary, then remove the core. If you get a red bar, restart from zero and let the system drain for fifteen seconds.”

Carter repeated the steps, then had one of the engineers try it, hands shaking just a bit as he handled the module. When he got the sequence right, Jenny said, “Good,” and didn’t bother hiding the small note of pride. She turned to Carter. “Want to show them the frequency calibration?”

Carter picked up the padd, toggled the diagnostic menu, and guided them through the process. “For a naquadah-specific resonance, you cycle the sweeps from twenty-two hertz to fifty. If you go higher, you start to pick up interference from the ship’s hull. We’ll have to isolate the spectrum when we get this outside, but for now—” she gestured at the sweep line, “—the pattern is distinct enough to run a real-world calibration without pulling too much power.”

One of the engineers—long hair, nose chapped from the cold—asked, “What about nulls? The last test, the signal just dropped off the board.”

Jenny said, “You get a null if the field is shielded by something more dense than what the probe can handle. Or if the EM background here—” she tapped the bulkhead, “—is bleeding too much. The Hellmouth’s resonance can overload a standard SGC probe in about four minutes. This model runs a three-step reboot: full power down, cold restart, and a secondary sweep with a lower integration time.”

Carter watched the faces as she said it, then added, “If you’re running field tests in Sunnydale, or anywhere in a three-mile radius, expect to lose the signal every so often. That’s not a failure. It’s the environment pushing back.”

The engineers copied that word for word. Jenny could see their hands moving in near-perfect unison, the ritual of transcribing something from a mentor whose respect they wanted to earn.

They spent another half hour running the procedure, each engineer cycling through the steps: power down, battery swap, cold restart, recalibrate. Stanton logged each round, his notes getting tighter and more organized as the sequence became muscle memory. Jenny watched, corrected only when she needed to, and let herself enjoy the feeling of actually building a thing with people who cared if it worked.

When the fourth test was finished, Carter motioned everyone back. “You’ve got the protocol,” she said. “Now we write the checklist. Nobody runs a field test without this signed off by both labs.”

She pulled a legal pad from her vest pocket and began drafting the steps in her block handwriting. Jenny dictated from memory, adjusting Carter’s phrasing where the SGC protocol diverged from what Starfleet would expect. “We need to add a caution about the zeroing—if the core gets stuck, you run the risk of a brownout.”

Carter nodded, penciled in the caveat, and then made a bold heading: FIELD DEPLOYMENT CHECKLIST. She wrote each step as a bullet point, then handed it to Stanton. “Make three copies. Everyone signs.”

Jenny watched as the engineers took turns with the pad, their gloves coming off for the signature, each one treating the act like the final step in a ritual.

There was no fanfare at the end. Carter and Jenny took the signed sheets, then walked to the side alcove where the SGC team lead waited with a clipboard of his own. The man—gray hair, windburned cheeks, reading glasses perched on the end of his nose—held up the one-page memorandum of understanding.

“Are you ready?” he asked.

Jenny said, “Let’s do it.”

They signed, Carter first, then Jenny, the pens making tiny indentations in the paper as the ink soaked in. The team lead witnessed, signed, then set the page in a folder labeled JOINT TECHNICAL SHARING.

Carter shook Jenny’s hand, once, firm and professional, and the SGC team lead gave a brisk “Thank you, Doctor, Major,” before heading off to the next protocol in the queue.

Jenny looked at the prototype on the bench. She picked it up, turned it over in her hands, and set it back down, just so.

The amber light from the display was the only thing that felt even a little bit warm in the bay.

Scene 2

There was a distinct flavor to Situation Room mornings, even at nine p.m. The upholstery never aired out, so the room always smelled a little like suit lining and nervous breath. The coffee—if you could call it that—had the personality of burnt hair, and there was a faint, undercurrent tang of ozone from the projector that hadn’t been serviced since the Clinton years.

President Bartlet sat at the head of the table. His jacket hung on the chairback in that deliberate way that said “informal, but only just.” His tie was loosened but not sloppy. The binder in front of him was still open to the classified summary sheet, but he hadn’t touched it in at least ten minutes. Instead, he rotated a capped pen in his right hand, over and under, over and under, never once dropping it.

The Joint Chiefs lined both sides of the table, each with their own binder and their own brand of fatigue. General Akers, Air Force, was halfway through a legal pad of scribbled notes and on his third coffee. Admiral Fitzwallace, Navy, had his hands folded on the table, unmoving. General Staub, Army, glared at the cover of his binder as if it were personally responsible for every procurement boondoggle in the last decade.

Weir was on the screen, muted for the time being, a half-page of comments already flagged for her next input window.

The briefer, a civilian from the National Security staff, did not waste time on the metaphors. “In summary: the sensor suite’s detection range is approximately five kilometers, depending on mass. The minimum threshold for naquadah resonance is four hundred grams, with a margin for error at high-EM sites. The sweeps are externally powered—no internal battery to spoof—and the signature profile is distinct from natural uranium or plutonium. The core is a Goa’uld artifact, but the assembly is all-American. The attached annex covers field deployment and maintenance.”

He paused, let the silence find its way into the corners of the room, then said, “The debate is on how to share the technology.”

General Akers leaned in. “Our SIGINT partners need this as soon as we can get it to them. If the Chinese get wind of a deployment and we haven’t already seeded the Five Eyes, we’re inviting a global incident.”

Staub cut in: “We classify it at the highest level, issue it only to Tier One response teams, and keep the specs in-house until we have a countermeasure.”

Admiral Fitzwallace’s voice was the gravel and smoke of old command: “If we move too slow, we get caught with our pants down. If we move too fast, every adversary on the planet knows we’ve got it. Either way, there’s no putting the toothpaste back in the tube.”

Bartlet twirled the pen again. “What about the Starfleet origin? If someone takes one apart and sees what’s in the core?”

The briefer said, “We’re repackaging all exterior casings with SGC hardware. The internal signature can only be detected by specialized tools—and those don’t exist outside of SGC and Starfleet. As long as no one does a full reverse-engineer—”

General Akers: “Someone always does a reverse-engineer.”

Bartlet set the pen down, flat on the table. “If we send these into the field, and something happens, and a partner government runs with the detection but doesn’t know the source, and then people die—whose call is that?”

The silence was longer this time.

Staub: “We can’t share what we don’t understand, sir. Not without risk.”

Bartlet tapped the binder, twice. “So the debate is between sharing a black box and risking the consequences, or not sharing and accepting the risk of being caught off guard.”

Fitzwallace nodded. “That’s the trade, Mr. President.”

Bartlet reached for the coffee, grimaced, and set it aside without drinking. “Here’s what we’ll do. You share the detection data with partner agencies, but on a need-to-know, case-by-case basis. If it’s operationally necessary, you use existing liaison channels. But the specs—the tech, the Starfleet provenance—those stay in this building and nowhere else, unless and until I say otherwise. You have forty-eight hours to get me a draft protocol.”

He let that land, then closed the binder, slow and final.

“Any questions?”

Nobody had any. He looked around the table, and for a moment, the only sound was the projector fan, laboring against the heat of a world that did not want to slow down.

“Good,” said Bartlet. “Let’s get it done.”

The Chiefs filed out in silence, each with their own reading of what had just been decided. Bartlet stayed behind, eyes on the muted Weir, who now sat on her end of the video link in a room even less forgiving than the Sit Room.

From the doorway, Leo McGarry gave a quick, tight nod: the silent heads-up that the MOU was signed, that Carter’s team had the prototype, that the next phase was already in motion.

Bartlet met the look, nodded back, and let out a breath.

There was always a next phase.

Scene 3

It was the kind of dusk that made the whole school look like a set for a TV pilot: the old gym’s exterior wall lit in pink and blue from the reflected sky, the parking lot empty except for a battered field van with the SGC logo stenciled discreetly near the rear bumper. The basketball court was a patchwork of cracked concrete, the paint lines so faded they might have been from another era entirely. Off in the distance, a sprinkler kicked on, its stutter and hiss just audible above the scrape of sneakers on the court.

Buffy leaned against the wall, arms folded, watching the two-person tech team wheel the sensor array out from the van. The device was everything Jenny had promised and Carter had iterated: boxy, rugged, no concessions to aesthetics except the green sweep line that ran across the data padd face in a smooth arc, back and forth, like a heartbeat at rest.

Xander, for his part, stood close enough to see the display but far enough to give the SGC operator his perimeter. He looked like a civilian observer in a war movie—aware, attentive, but not pretending to understand the script. The tech team wore SGC parkas over school-district-issue polos, which somehow made them look less official and more determined. The taller of the two, Santos, ran a standard grid pattern with the unit, calling out coordinates every four meters and logging them with a clicker that looked like it had been borrowed from a wildlife survey.

The sweep line glowed a steady green as Santos walked the length of the court. It cleared the parking lot, cleared the gym, then started a new pass aimed at the strip of grass between the gym and the library annex.

Buffy tracked the sensor with her eyes, almost bored, until the operator’s hand froze mid-step.

The sweep line stuttered, then went flat—a hard, cold red. The operator stared at the padd, eyebrows knitted, then tapped the power cycle. The line reset, tried a sweep, and flatlined again.

Xander asked, “Is it broken, or is that…?”

The operator hit the comm on her shoulder. “Carter, do you see this?”

On the hood of the van, a ruggedized laptop ran a video link straight to Cheyenne Mountain. Carter’s face, more gray in the image than usual, appeared above a scrolling bar of live data. Her voice, clean and tight through the laptop speaker, said, “Go to the secondary mode. Run the spectrum at quarter speed.”

The operator did as instructed. The sweep line crawled, so slow that Xander could see every micro-jump, every attempt the unit made to resync to baseline. This time, instead of red, the sweep paused over a three-meter section of lawn, oscillated between amber and crimson, then held steady at a blinking orange.

Buffy’s hand was already at her belt, stake out and gripped, before anyone spoke.

Carter’s voice: “That’s not a ghost reading. That’s a containment signature.” She rattled off three numbers, her tone going flatter by the second. “It’s below the detection threshold for the Mark I, but the new model is pulling the phase shift. Santos, can you mark the coordinates?”

Santos, who had not blinked since the first red sweep, said, “Marked. Should I go closer?”

Carter said, “No. Pull back. Export the log to the external drive, then run a compare against the Antarctic baseline, side-by-side.”

The operator did so, and within seconds, both screens—on the padd and on the laptop—showed a pair of jagged signatures. The Antarctic one was as expected: sharp, regular, like a hospital EKG. The Sunnydale trace was warped, the whole left side of the spike stretched, bent, almost as if something heavy had been sitting on it.

Buffy, eyes still locked on the grass, said, “Is it a bomb?”

Xander, not taking his eyes off the display: “Or is it alive?”

Carter, from the van: “We don’t know. The signature is masked. The EM bleed from the Hellmouth overlays everything, but this is the first time we’ve seen a secondary profile under the distortion.”

Santos asked, “Did we miss this in the first sweep?”

Carter: “It wasn’t visible before. The Hellmouth’s phase interference must have shifted, maybe after the last event.” There was a pause, the sound of Carter double-checking her own numbers. “Whatever it is, it’s been there a while.”

Buffy stepped forward, the motion almost lazy but perfectly controlled. “Show me where.”

Santos pointed, not with a gesture but with the whole device, holding it like a divining rod. The sweep line trembled as it neared the marked coordinates, then flared orange again.

Xander said, “That’s right under the north fence. Where the ground dips.” He turned to Buffy. “Remember when you said that part of the lawn felt off?”

She nodded. “Not in a Hellmouth way, either. Just… off.”

The operator held the device steady, then started a slow, circular motion. The orange spike grew sharper, a small volcano erupting in the middle of a calm sea.

On the van’s hood, the laptop beeped—a warning, not loud but sharp enough to make everyone turn. The second operator, a guy in glasses with the patience of a monk, ran a quick script and said, “There’s a cross-signal. The mask isn’t just hiding the signature, it’s redirecting it. Every time we run a sweep, the return shifts, like it’s tracking us back.”

Buffy didn’t move. She said, “Is it dangerous?”

Carter’s voice: “We don’t know yet. But don’t go closer. I’m pinging the Sunnydale site for a support team, just in case.”

Buffy’s eyes narrowed, but she stepped back. “If something comes out, I handle it.”

Xander looked at the numbers again. “Can we tell how deep it is?”

The operator checked, did a quick calculation, and said, “Less than two meters. Maybe a meter fifty, hard to say with the masking.” She looked at Xander. “You want to dig it up?”

He shook his head. “Not my call. But if it’s been here since before the new field sweeps, it’s probably not an accident.”

On the laptop, Carter and Jenny were now side by side in the video window, both watching the Sunnydale data stream in real time. Jenny leaned in, her face unreadable, and said, “Try a harmonic sweep at 17 hertz. It’s a resonance point for naquadah, but also for certain organic substrates.”

The operator nodded, set the sweep, and waited.

The sweep line slowed, then paused. The orange became yellow, then green, then—at the end of the sweep—jumped to a blinding white spike that saturated the display.

Everyone froze. The only sound was the distant sprinkler, ticking over the last arc of its cycle.

Carter, quietly: “That’s new.”

Buffy looked at the lawn, then at the sensor, and then at Xander. “What does it mean?”

Xander shook his head. “If it’s a trap, it’s a good one.”

Jenny’s voice, faint but clear: “It’s a communication. Or a warning.”

Carter said, “Get the unit back to the van. Upload the data to me, full packet. We need to run a comparative analysis before anyone touches the site.”

Buffy watched as the operators packed up, then holstered her stake. She didn’t put it away—just let it rest against her leg.

Xander said, “You want to call Willow?”

Buffy nodded. “Tell her we need every archive on signal masking, demon and otherwise.”

They walked back to the van together, the light from the gym wall fading as the sun dipped below the horizon.

On the hood, the laptop flickered. Carter and Jenny stared at the anomaly, their faces mirrored in miniature on the screen.

The sprinkler on the far lawn clicked off. The whole school fell silent.

For a moment, nobody moved. The only thing that seemed alive was the white spike on the padd, burning a hole through the quiet.

Xander looked at it, then at the sky, and wondered what you called a ghost when it was still alive and waiting.

Buffy watched the grass, her face unreadable.

On the laptop, Carter and Jenny exchanged a look. Neither smiled.

A breeze moved across the court, cold and sharp, and the world started up again.

✳

Carter and Jenny inventoried what the Enterprise could contribute if the invasion reached orbit: sensors, shield harmonics, power routing, and relay geometry. Every line carried a second column for what could be used without compromising the ship or violating Calendar’s authority.

“We can make the ship an early-warning platform,” Carter said. “Possibly a relay. Not a weapon unless we have no other choice.”

The defense was becoming real in layers: information, sensors, logistics, and only then force.
