Chapter 48

Three weeks into the college's first term, Alberton sent word through the hub door that he needed Willow's opinion on something, urgently, and could she please come without asking too many questions in advance because he wanted her reaction unfiltered.

Willow, who did not enjoy being managed even by people she liked, went anyway, mostly out of curiosity, and dragged Xander along because he'd been threatening to see the finished east wing for a month and this was as good an excuse as any.

They found Alberton and Dara in the small stone workshop, the one that had been bare and empty on Dara's first day, now covered floor to ceiling in chalk diagrams that Xander didn't recognize and Willow clearly did, based on the way she went very quiet and very still the moment she stepped through the door.

"Tell me what you're looking at," Alberton said, watching Willow's face with barely contained excitement. "Before I say anything. I want to know if it means the same thing to you it means to us."

Willow walked the perimeter of the room slowly, tracing one line of chalk with her eyes without touching it, careful and reverent, the way you'd look at something both very old and very dangerous. "This is a hub-door schematic," she said finally. "But it's not like any I've seen. Ours are — simpler. Cruder, honestly, compared to this. Whoever drew the original version of what you've reconstructed here understood something about the geometry that got lost by the time anybody I ever studied under was working."

"Dara drew it," Alberton said. "From memory. She watched Meren repair a failing hub door once, years ago, and apparently retained every single line of it without understanding what she was retaining."

Dara, standing off to one side with chalk dust on her hands, looked exhausted and wrung-out, like she'd spent days pulling something enormous out of her own head. She gave a small, tired shrug. "I didn't know it was important. It was just — how the lines went. I remembered how the lines went the same way I remember how a good weld looks versus a bad one. I didn't think anyone would care."

"Everyone should care," Willow said, quiet, still tracing the diagram with her eyes. "This piece here—" she pointed, careful not to touch, "—this is the part that fails on ours. The seam where the two anchor points meet. Ours crack there, eventually, always, no matter how careful the original build was. It's the reason every hub door I've ever heard of needs constant small maintenance." She looked up at Dara, something like awe creeping into her expression despite herself. "Yours doesn't crack there. This design doesn't crack there. Whoever built it that way solved a problem people have been quietly living with for generations."

"Meren solved it," Dara said. "I don't know how. I just know what it looked like when she did it."

"That may be enough," Alberton said. "That may genuinely be enough, if we can get the geometry precise instead of just remembered — and this is where I need you specifically, Willow, because Dara's memory is extraordinary and her hands are steady, but neither of you has actually built the working end to end. I have theory. I don't have a hub door to test theory against. You have one."

Xander, who had been quietly absorbing all of this from the doorway, felt a slow sinking feeling — this conversation was heading somewhere he wasn't going to love — and cleared his throat.

"When you say 'test theory against,'" he said, "you don't mean the hub door in Willow's basement."

"I mean precisely the hub door in Willow's basement," Alberton said. "It's the only working, stable hub door any of us has direct access to that isn't a royal installation guarded by people who would ask a great many questions about why a girl's basement in another world suddenly had scholars crawling all over it."

"That door is how we get groceries," Xander said. "And go to school. And how Jesse gets home when he stays too late playing video games and misses curfew by exactly the amount his mom will actually notice."

"I'm not proposing we take it apart," Alberton said, mildly offended. "I'm proposing we study it. Carefully. Non-invasively. With considerable respect for the fact that several people's daily lives depend on it continuing to function exactly as it currently functions."

"That's a very reasonable-sounding way of saying you want to poke at the thing that's basically load-bearing for my entire life," Xander said.

"Yes," Alberton agreed, entirely unbothered. "That is, in fact, precisely what I'm saying, phrased more reasonably than you phrased it. Was I successful?"

Xander looked at Willow, who was still staring at the chalk diagram like it had personally reordered her understanding of a subject she'd thought she knew cold, and at Dara, standing quiet and uncertain in the corner of a room she'd filled with more forgotten knowledge than she seemed to fully grasp she possessed, and sighed, because he already knew how this was going to go.

"Fine," he said. "Carefully. Non-invasively. And somebody tells me before anything happens, not after."

"Naturally," Alberton said, already turning back to the diagram, Willow drifting toward it like she couldn't help herself, the two of them falling into the easy, absorbed shorthand of people who'd found a problem worth losing an afternoon to.

Dara caught Xander's eye across the room, something wry and grateful in the look. "Thanks for not making this weirder than it already is."

"Don't thank me yet," Xander said. "Wait till Willow starts drawing chalk diagrams on our basement floor. Then we'll see how weird it gets."
