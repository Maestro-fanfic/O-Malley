Chapter 57

The meeting happened on neutral ground — a small inn two villages over from where Corrin had spent fifteen years being careful, chosen by Elan for its quiet and its distance from anywhere either of them called home, so that whatever happened, happened somewhere that belonged to neither of them and could be walked away from cleanly if it needed to be.

Dara had asked to keep it small — no crowd of well-meaning adults, no audience if it went badly — and Elan, after her own quiet assessment of Corrin as no danger to a child, agreed to take her alone, on the condition that Kestrian know exactly where they were and precisely when to expect word back. "You get your say in how this feels," Elan told her, the night before. "I still get the final say on whether it's safe. That's not up for negotiation, even for this." Dara had accepted the terms without much argument, mostly because she suspected, correctly, that she wasn't going to win them.

Xander noticed, watching her step through the hub door that morning, that she'd tucked Meren's old cord of warding beads into her coat pocket where she could reach it, the way some people reached for a lucky coin.

She came back that evening, and the whole household did its level best to look casual about waiting up for her, an effort that fooled absolutely no one, Dara least of all, who took one look at the kitchen full of people pretending very hard to be doing unrelated tasks and let out a small, watery laugh.

"You could've just asked how it went."

"We were giving you space," Sharon said, abandoning all pretense of the crossword puzzle she'd been staring at without writing in for the last forty minutes.

"You were sitting in the kitchen at nine at night pretending to do a crossword you don't even like doing."

"Giving you space while being nearby," Sharon amended. "It's a nuanced position."

Dara laughed again, steadier this time, and sat down at the table, and the whole room leaned in without quite meaning to.

"It was hard," she said. "I want to say that first, because I don't want anyone thinking it was easy just because I'm not crying right now. It was hard. She cried again, the way Elan said she did the first time. I think I did too, at some point, though I honestly lost track of exactly when." She wrapped both hands around the mug of tea Amy had already pressed into them without being asked. "But it was good-hard. Not bad-hard. She told me things about Meren I never knew — how they met, what they'd been like as students together, before whatever happened between them happened. Small things. What Meren's laugh sounded like before I ever knew her, back when she still laughed easily, before she'd learned to be as careful as she was by the time she raised me."

"Did she tell you about the falling out?" Alberton asked, and then, at Elan's sharp look, added quickly, "Only if you want to share that. I'm not — I wasn't asking for the scholarship of it. I was asking because I care."

"It's all right," Dara said. "I don't mind telling it. I think Meren would want it told, honestly, now that it's safe to tell." She took a breath. "Corrin believed the work should scale. Meren believed it shouldn't. That's the short version, the version Kestrian already more or less had right. But the longer version is that Corrin didn't believe it out of greed, or hunger for power for its own sake — she believed it because she'd grown up somewhere poor and forgotten, somewhere the old kingdoms never bothered sending help to, and she thought, if the doors could connect everywhere to everywhere, maybe nowhere would ever have to be forgotten again. She thought big doors meant nobody got left behind."

"That's not an unsympathetic reason," Willow said, quiet.

"It's not," Dara agreed. "That's what made it so hard for Meren to argue against, I think, at least at first. But Meren had seen enough of the world by then to know that whoever ended up holding a network like that wouldn't be the forgotten villages Corrin was trying to help. It'd be whoever was already powerful enough to seize control of it first. Big doors don't erase who's powerful and who isn't. They just hand the powerful a bigger lever." Dara turned the mug slowly in her hands. "Corrin didn't believe that, for a long time. Believed it enough to spend years chasing the scaled version anyway, quietly, on her own, after she and Meren parted ways over it. And then, by her own telling, she got far enough into the actual work to see exactly who was starting to circle it — powerful people, patient people, the kind who don't announce themselves until it's too late to stop them — and she understood, all at once, that Meren had been right the whole time. So she stopped. Burned most of her own research. Vanished into that little village and spent fifteen years making sure nobody else ever found what she'd built, or rebuilt it after her."

The kitchen was quiet for a long moment.

"She's spent fifteen years alone," Xander said slowly, "punishing herself for almost doing something she never actually did."

"That's more or less what I told her," Dara said. "She didn't entirely believe me. I think she's spent so long thinking of herself as the person who almost got it wrong that she's forgotten how to be anything else." A small, fierce note crept into her voice. "I want to help her remember. If she'll let me."

"What did she say, when you offered?" Elan asked, gentle.

"She said she'd think about it," Dara said. "Which, from a woman who's spent fifteen years alone by choice, I think might actually be a yes, just moving at the speed of someone who's forgotten how to trust good things showing up uninvited." She looked around the table, at all of them, waiting up in a kitchen at nine at night for news that mattered to every single one of them in a different way. "I told her about all of you, by the way. About the household. She asked what kind of people take in a wet, terrified stranger and a wizard and turn them both into family inside of a few months."

"What'd you tell her?" Jesse asked.

"I told her the honest kind," Dara said. "The kind that doesn't ask you to explain yourself before they decide you're worth keeping."

Nobody had anything to add to that, and for once, nobody tried.
