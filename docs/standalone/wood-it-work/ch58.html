Chapter 58

Corrin said yes six weeks later, and arrived through the hub door on a gray Tuesday afternoon carrying nothing but a small traveling case, wary and stiff — she'd forgotten, over fifteen years alone, how to walk into a room full of strangers without automatically calculating every exit.

She was older than Dara had described, though not old, exactly — the kind of weathered that came from years of careful, solitary work rather than from age itself, gray threading through dark hair pulled back plain and practical, hands scarred in a way Xander recognized instantly — a lifetime spent close to a forge.

"You're the household," she said, not quite a question, standing very still just inside the doorway like she wasn't yet sure she'd been fully invited past it.

"We're the household," Willow agreed, warm but not pushing, giving her room. "Dara's out back. She wanted to be the one to show you around, if that's all right."

"That's all right," Corrin said, and something in her shoulders eased, fractionally, at the plain permission of it.

Dara came in from the back yard at the sound of voices, wiping her hands on a rag, and stopped short at the sight of Corrin standing there, and for a moment neither of them moved, the whole kitchen holding its breath around the two of them.

"You came," Dara said finally.

"I said I'd think about it," Corrin said. "I thought about it for six weeks. I concluded I was being a coward, and I don't much care for being a coward, whatever comfort it's occasionally offered me." She looked at Dara properly then, taking in the whole of her — the calluses, the careful stance, the small worn cord of warding beads Dara still wore at her wrist most days now, not hidden in a pocket anymore. "You do have her hands."

"Elan told you that."

"Elan told me a lot of things," Corrin said. "I wanted to see for myself whether any of it was true."

"And?"

Corrin's mouth did something that wasn't quite a smile yet, but was clearly working its way toward one. "And I find I'm considerably less braced for disappointment than I expected to be an hour ago."

Dara laughed, unsteady, and crossed the room, and after a moment's hesitation that the whole kitchen watched with held breath, Corrin opened her arms, and the two of them stood there a long moment, not quite strangers anymore, not yet anything with an easy name either, but something real regardless.

"I brought something," Corrin said eventually, stepping back, opening her traveling case with careful hands. Inside, wrapped in cloth soft with age, was a small leather-bound book, its pages dense with handwriting Dara clearly recognized on sight, her breath catching.

"That's her hand," Dara said. "That's Meren's hand."

"It's her working journal," Corrin said. "From before we parted ways. I kept it, all these years, and I never quite knew why, except that some part of me always suspected somebody would need it someday, even if I didn't expect that somebody to be her student, decades later, in a house I'd never heard of, on the other side of a door I helped teach her to build." She held it out, and Dara took it with both hands, careful as though it might dissolve. "It's yours now. It always should have been."

Dara opened it slowly, running her fingers over the first page, and Xander, watching from across the kitchen, saw her throat work, saw the effort it took to hold herself together — falling apart, right now, felt like it would be too big a thing to come back from cleanly.

"Thank you," Dara said, quiet, not looking up from the page.

"Don't thank me yet," Corrin said, echoing, without knowing it, the exact phrase this whole household had apparently worn smooth from overuse. "There's a great deal in there I never fully understood myself. I suspect working through it properly is going to take considerably longer than one afternoon, and I find, rather to my own surprise, that I'd like to be there for at least some of that work, if you'll have me."

"I'll have you," Dara said, immediate, no hesitation at all. "For as long as you want to stay."

"I don't entirely know how long that is," Corrin admitted. "I've been alone a long time. I'm not certain I remember how to be otherwise, gracefully."

"Nobody here's asking for graceful," Amy said, from the doorway, arms crossed, warm and matter-of-fact. "We're mostly asking for present. Graceful's optional. Ask literally anyone in this room."

"She's not wrong," Jesse said. "I once cried actual tears over a broken engine in front of a king's herald. Graceful left this house a long time ago and it's not coming back."

Corrin looked around the kitchen — at Amy's crossed arms and easy warmth, at Jesse's grin, at Willow already moving toward the stove out of pure instinct because someone new had arrived and instinct said feed them, at Xander watching quietly from the corner, steady, taking her full measure before deciding how much trust to extend — and something in her whole posture shifted, some old, careful weight finally setting down after fifteen years of carrying it alone.

"I think," she said slowly, "I might actually be able to manage that."
