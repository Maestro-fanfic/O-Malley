Chapter 55

Elan came back on the ninth day, and the household knew something had happened before she said a word, simply from the way she stepped through the hub door — slower than usual, careful, like she'd rehearsed how to say something difficult and hadn't quite finished rehearsing.

"She's alive," Elan said, before anyone could ask, because some news wasn't fair to make people wait for. "I found her. She's alive."

The room let out a breath as one, Dara's hand flying to her mouth, and for a moment it looked like that might be the whole of it — relief, simple and complete — until Elan's expression, still careful, still braced, made it clear there was more.

"But," Willow said, reading the room correctly.

"But it's complicated," Elan said. "As I suspect everyone already guessed it would be." She set her pack down, took the offered chair Sharon pulled out for her, gathered herself for a moment before continuing. "The village was easy to find. Corrin was harder — she doesn't use that name anymore, hasn't in years, goes by something else entirely to the handful of people in that village who know her at all. It took most of the nine days just earning enough trust from ordinary villagers to get pointed in the right direction, and even then, she nearly didn't see me."

"But she did," Dara said.

"She did. Eventually." Elan looked at Dara directly now, gentling her voice. "She's older than I expected. Tired, in the specific way of someone who's been carrying something heavy alone for a very long time. When I told her Meren had died, and that Meren had a student, and that the student had made it safely through Meren's old contingency door — she cried. Actually cried, in front of a stranger, which by every account I gathered of her, is not a thing she does."

"Did she say anything about the falling out?" Alberton asked, unable to help himself. "About what the difference in what the work was for actually was?"

"She did," Elan said, "though not easily, and I think she'll want to tell Dara herself, in her own time, rather than have it relayed secondhand through me. What I can tell you is this much: Kestrian's fear wasn't wrong, exactly, but it also wasn't the whole truth. Corrin did once believe the old techniques should scale — should connect kingdoms, should concentrate power in careful, responsible hands. She spent years chasing that idea. And then, by her own account, she chased it far enough to see clearly what it would actually cost, and who would actually end up holding that power once it existed, and she didn't like the answer, and she stopped. Walked away from it entirely, the same as Meren had years before, just later, and by a harder road."

"So she's not dangerous," Xander said.

"I don't believe she is, no. I believe she's a woman who made a serious mistake in her convictions once, corrected course at real personal cost, and has spent fifteen years quietly making sure nobody else picks up where she left off." Elan looked around the table. "Which is, I'd point out, not so very different a story from several people sitting at this table right now."

Nobody argued with that.

"Does she want to see me?" Dara asked, quiet, the question that mattered most finally surfacing.

"She asked about you before I'd even finished explaining who you were," Elan said, gentle. "She asked what you looked like. Whether you had Meren's steady hands. Whether you were kind." A small, warm smile. "I told her yes, to all three, and she asked me — carefully, like she didn't quite believe she had the right to ask it — whether you might ever want to meet her. Not immediately. Not if it was too much, too soon, too complicated a thing to walk into. Only if, and only when, you were ready."

Dara sat very still for a long moment, and then, slowly, nodded, tears finally spilling over that she didn't bother wiping away.

"I'm ready," she said. "I don't know how to be more ready than I already am."

"Then we'll arrange it," Elan said. "Carefully. On your timeline, not anyone else's — not mine, not Kestrian's, not Alberton's very obvious scholarly impatience." A pointed look at Alberton, who had the grace to look sheepish. "This is your story, Dara. Whatever happens next, it happens on your terms."

"Thank you," Dara said, and then, because the moment seemed to call for it, added, "all of you. For finding her. For not letting me carry this half-finished forever."

"That's what family does," Amy said, simply, from across the table, and nobody in the room, not even Alberton with his college full of theory and formal titles, felt the need to complicate that particular sentence with a single additional word.
