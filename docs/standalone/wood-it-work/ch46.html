Chapter 46

Back at the shop, life did its best impression of ordinary, which lasted almost a full week before Sharon showed up at the gate with a face that meant trouble long before she opened her mouth.

"Okay," she said, "before anybody panics, nobody's hurt, nothing's on fire, and it's not a king thing."

"That's a very specific list of things it's not," Jesse said, from under the hood of a sedan. "What is it, then?"

"My mom wants to meet Elan."

The wrench Xander was holding clanked against the fender, loud in the sudden silence.

"Your mom," Xander repeated. "Wants to meet. Elan."

"She's been asking about the 'foreign exchange thing' for weeks," Sharon said, miserable. "I've been dodging it, but she saw Elan through the kitchen window last Tuesday when she dropped off the casserole dish, and now she's convinced I've been hanging around some grown woman nobody's ever introduced her to, which — okay, fair, I have — and she wants her over for dinner. Wants to meet whoever's apparently been keeping an eye on all of us. This Friday."

"We don't have a cover story for Elan," Willow said, coming out of the workshop with grease on her hands and a frown already forming. "We've got a cover story for Alberton — visiting scholar, foreign university, nobody checks those too hard. We don't have one for a diplomatic envoy who talks like she's reading off a very polite legal document."

"She can talk normal," Amy said. "I've heard her talk normal. She does it on purpose so grown-ups underestimate her."

"That doesn't help us with Sharon's mom," Jesse said. "Sharon's mom underestimates everybody less than most people give her credit for. She figured out I was lying about the science fair project in fourth grade in under a minute."

"That's because you told her it was a perpetual motion machine," Xander said, "and then it stopped moving in front of her."

"The timing was unfortunate."

Sharon dropped her head into her hands. "This was a mistake. I should never have let her see the kitchen window. I should've closed the blinds. I should've—"

"Sharon." Elan's voice, calm and even, from the doorway, where she'd apparently been listening for longer than any of them had noticed. "Breathe. This is a solvable problem."

"Is it?" Sharon looked up, hollow-eyed. "Because from where I'm standing it looks like either I tell my mother the truth, which she will not believe and which will get us all committed, or I lie to her face at her own dinner table, which she will absolutely notice, because she notices everything."

"There's a third option," Elan said. "I come to dinner as exactly what I appear to be — an adult who checks in on this household because I care what happens to the children in it, who's from somewhere far away, who's a little formal because that's how I was raised, and who is not going to volunteer any information nobody asks for." She crossed her arms, considering. "Most lies fail because people try too hard to sell them. The truth, told selectively and with confidence, is nearly always more convincing than a story built entirely out of invention. I am, in point of fact, from somewhere very far away. I am, in point of fact, formal. Neither of those is a lie. We simply won't be specifying how far, or how formal."

"You make it sound easy," Sharon said.

"It generally is," Elan said, "provided the person doing the talking believes what they're saying while they're saying it. I've had considerably more practice at that than a dinner with one worried mother ought to require."

Xander caught Jesse's eye across the garage, and neither of them said anything, but they were both thinking the same thing: that somewhere in the last several months, without anybody quite deciding it on purpose, "considerably more practice at deception than a situation requires" had become one of the more reassuring sentences a member of this household could say.

"Fine," Sharon said, straightening up, some color coming back into her face. "Friday. Seven o'clock. Please, please don't let anything catch fire."

"I make no promises about the kitchen," Elan said, "but I promise your mother will leave that dinner considerably less worried about who's watching after her daughter's odd little group of friends, which is, in my experience, most of what a first dinner like this needs to accomplish."
