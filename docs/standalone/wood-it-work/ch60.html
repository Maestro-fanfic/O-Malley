Chapter 60

Winter came on slow that year, the kind of gradual cold that let a household ease into it a layer at a time instead of getting caught flat-footed by the first hard frost, and by the time the first real snow fell, the shop had settled into a rhythm that felt, to Xander's quiet surprise, almost entirely peaceful.

Dara split her time between the college and the household with the easy fluidity of someone who'd finally stopped treating either place like it might disappear if she looked away too long. Corrin, slowly, cautiously, had started teaching a small handful of Alberton's most promising students — not the scaled, dangerous version of hub-work she'd once chased, but the smaller, careful version Meren had always believed in, the kind that connected one place to one other place and asked nothing more of the world than that.

"I never thought I'd teach again," she admitted to Xander one evening, watching snow fall past the workshop window while Dara helped one of the younger students correct a chalk diagram across the room. "I thought that door closed with the rest of it, fifteen years ago."

"Doesn't look closed from here," Xander said.

"No," Corrin agreed, something almost wondering in her voice. "It doesn't, does it."

Out in the main room, Jesse was in the middle of losing an argument with Amy about whether the keychain business should expand to include a winter line — "snowflakes, Xander, tell her snowflakes would sell" — and Sharon was helping Elan wrap a stack of presents in paper that kept tearing because Elan, for all her diplomatic composure, had apparently never once in her life wrapped a gift and refused, on principle, to accept help until the paper had defeated her at least four more times. Brack sat by the fire with Willow, the two of them quiet, comfortable, the specific easy silence of people who'd earned the right to not need conversation to enjoy each other's company.

Xander stood in the doorway for a moment, taking it all in — the whole sprawling, impossible, thoroughly adopted shape of a family that had started with a machine shop and a door in a girl's basement and had somehow, one improbable stranger at a time, grown into this — and thought, not for the first time, that whatever came next, whatever kings or scouts or forgotten kingdoms of hub-work still waited somewhere out past the horizon of what they knew, this particular room, on this particular snowy evening, was worth every bit of the trouble it had taken to build.

"You're doing the thinking face again," Jesse said, appearing at his elbow with two mugs of hot cider, handing one over without being asked. "The one where you get all quiet and philosophical about the shop being more than a shop."

"Am I that predictable?"

"You're extremely that predictable," Jesse said. "It's fine. It's kind of your whole thing at this point." He raised his mug in a small toast. "To the shop being more than a shop."

"To the shop being more than a shop," Xander agreed, and they drank, and outside the snow kept falling, quiet and steady, over a street that looked exactly as ordinary as it always had, hiding, as it always had, considerably more than anyone passing by would ever have guessed.
