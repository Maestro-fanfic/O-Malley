Chapter 53

The lead, when it finally came, arrived almost two weeks later, and not from Kestrian at all.

It came from Brack.

He turned up at the shop on a Saturday morning with the particular set to his shoulders that meant he'd been carrying something heavy in his head for a while and had finally decided it needed saying out loud, and asked, without much preamble, whether they could all sit down for a minute.

"That's never a good start," Jesse said, but he sat.

"I should have said something sooner," Brack began, once they were all gathered around the kitchen table, Willow and Amy included, Elan hovering near the doorway with the alert stillness she got when she suspected a conversation was about to matter. "When Alberton first mentioned the name Corrin, it didn't register right away. It took me a few days to place it. I'm an old soldier, not a scholar — names from academic disagreements two generations back aren't the kind of thing that stays sharp in my memory."

Brack paused, taking in the row of small, attentive faces waiting for him to continue, not one of them looking the least bit rattled by a grown soldier saying words like dangerous and scholarly disagreement over their own kitchen table.

"You've got that face again," he added, almost to himself.

"What face?" Amy asked.

"The one grown-ups make right before they decide something's wrong with all of you," Willow said, not looking up from her tea. "You get used to it. Adults expect kids to be scared of the scary parts. We stopped being scared of the scary parts a while back, and it throws people off every single time."

"It's a little funny, honestly," Jesse said. "Watch, he's gonna do the thing where he decides we're either very brave or very broken and can't quite pick."

"I was leaning brave," Brack said, dry.

"Everyone leans brave at first," Willow said. "But it registered eventually," she added, nodding for Brack to go on.

"It did." Brack folded his hands on the table, slow and deliberate, choosing his words. "Years ago — before any of you, before this household, when I was still doing the kind of work that put me in rooms I don't talk about much — I was posted for a season to a village in the northern territories. Quiet posting. Boring, mostly. Watching a border nobody was actually threatening, the kind of duty you get assigned when somebody higher up wants you somewhere safe for a while." He paused. "There was a woman in that village. Kept to herself. Ran a small forge, did careful, unusual metalwork nobody quite understood the purpose of. The other soldiers called her odd. I mostly left her alone, the way you leave alone anyone who isn't causing trouble and clearly wants to be left that way."

"Corrin," Dara said, very quietly, from where she'd come through the hub door an hour earlier with Corrin, for what was supposed to be a simple visit.

"I don't know for certain," Brack said. "I never learned her name. She wasn't the type to offer it, and I wasn't the type to demand it of someone doing no harm. But the timing fits. The description Kestrian gave — solitary, secretive, unusual metalwork with a purpose nobody official ever fully pinned down — fits close enough that I couldn't in good conscience sit on it."

"Is she still there?" Alberton asked, leaning forward, unable to help himself.

"I don't know," Brack admitted. "That posting ended eleven years ago. I never had reason to go back. Villages like that don't exactly send letters." He looked at Dara, something careful and apologetic in his expression. "I'm sorry it's not more than that. A maybe, eleven years old, from a soldier who never even learned her name."

"It's more than we had yesterday," Dara said, steadier than Xander expected. "It's a place. A real place, with a real name, instead of a sentence in a water-stained book. That's worth something."

"It's worth a great deal," Kestrian said, from the hub doorway, having apparently arrived partway through the conversation and let it run without interrupting. "A village, even an eleven-year-old maybe of one, is considerably easier to search than an entire kingdom's worth of quiet, unremarkable places someone might have vanished into." He looked at Brack with something like genuine respect. "You have good instincts, soldier. I don't say that lightly."

"I have old instincts," Brack said, "and a guilty conscience about not saying something the moment the name came up instead of waiting two weeks to be sure. Don't credit me too much."

"I'll credit you exactly as much as you've earned," Kestrian said, "which is considerable, whether you're comfortable hearing it or not." He turned to the room at large. "I'll have someone quiet make inquiries. Not soldiers — soldiers asking questions in a small village draw exactly the kind of attention we're trying to avoid, if Corrin really is there and really is as careful as everyone describes. Someone ordinary. Someone who can pass through and ask the sort of questions a traveler asks without anyone remembering the asking."

"I could go," Elan said, from the doorway. "I've done quieter work than this before, and I'm considerably better at passing unremarked than most of your soldiers, no offense meant."

"None taken, because it's entirely true," Kestrian said. "I was rather hoping you'd offer."

Dara looked between them, hope and fear tangled together in her expression — the same look Xander had come to recognize as her default state whenever this whole thread of her life came up. "You'd really do that? Go looking, just on a soldier's decade-old maybe?"

"I'd do considerably more than that for considerably less certainty," Elan said, gentle now, the diplomat's mask softening into something plainer. "You've spent your whole life on the outside of a story that's actually yours. If there's any chance at all of bringing you further inside it, I intend to take that chance seriously."

Dara didn't say anything for a moment, just nodded, throat working, and Xander, watching the whole table — Brack's guilty relief at finally having said his piece, Kestrian's careful diplomatic calculation already turning toward logistics, Elan's quiet resolve, Alberton practically vibrating with scholarly anticipation he was visibly trying to suppress out of respect for the moment — thought that this particular household had gotten remarkably, thoroughly good at closing ranks around one person's hard question until it stopped being quite so lonely to carry.
