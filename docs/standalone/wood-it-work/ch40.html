Chapter 40

The Fall Festival booth was, against every single one of Xander's quiet reservations, a runaway success.

They'd set up a folding table under a borrowed canopy, a hand-lettered sign reading HARRIS FABRICATION — LITTLE METAL THINGS that Amy had drawn and Jesse had insisted on decorating with a small doodled wrench that took him four tries to get looking less like a snake, and a shoebox of aluminum keychains laid out in careful rows: gears, tiny wrenches, a little truck with wheels that actually turned, and — over Sharon's strenuous objection and by popular vote of everyone else — one single, small, deliberately-not-too-on-the-nose dragon, which sold out within the first twenty minutes and prompted three separate kids to ask, with real disappointment, whether there'd be more.

"Told you," Jesse said, smug, watching a fourth kid walk away disappointed.

"You did not tell me," Sharon said. "You voted for the dragon along with everyone else. There is no version of events where you get to say 'I told you so' about a democratic process you participated in."

"I'm choosing my own version of events. It's more satisfying."

By early afternoon the shoebox was nearly empty, the little cashbox Willow had insisted on for actual bookkeeping purposes was pleasantly heavy, and Xander had spent most of the day doing something he genuinely hadn't expected to enjoy nearly as much as he did: talking to strangers, ordinary strangers, about ordinary metal shapes, with no hidden currents underneath any of it, no kings or heralds or scrying wards, just a kid buying a keychain for his little sister because she liked trucks, or a mom picking out a gear for her husband's toolbox because it was cute and cheap and made in town.

"You're good at this," Amy said, during a lull, watching him make change for a ten-year-old who'd saved up specifically for the dragon's replacement, a slightly-less-detailed gear that he'd nonetheless declared, with great seriousness, "actually way cooler, I changed my mind."

"Good at what, selling keychains?"

"Good at the whole thing. Talking to people who don't know anything about any of it. You don't get weird or cagey, you're just — normal. Friendly. Like none of the rest of it is even happening."

Xander considered that while he counted out change, careful with the coins the way he was careful with everything mechanical. "I think that's the part I actually like best, honestly. Not the magic, not the diplomacy, not even the building, exactly. Just — this. Making something small and good and handing it to somebody who's happy to have it, no strings, no story behind it bigger than 'I thought you'd like this.'"

"That's basically your whole personality, though," Amy said. "You just do it with bigger stakes most of the time."

"Maybe," Xander said. "Today the stakes are just whether a kid's happy with his gear instead of his dragon. I could use a few more days like that."

"You'll get some," Amy said, with more confidence than either of them probably had any right to feel, given the last month. "Alberton's gone, the king's signed off, the scout thing blew over. For once we might actually get to be bored for a while."

"Don't jinx it."

"I'm not jinxing it. I'm hoping out loud. There's a difference."

"Same energy as Elan's 'this is the good possibility' comment," Xander said. "You people keep hoping out loud like it's a strategy."

"It's worked so far," Amy said, and grinned, and turned to help the next customer — a woman buying three little wrenches for what she explained, unprompted, was her whole book club, all mechanics' wives, all going to think this was the funniest gift she'd brought in years — and Xander watched the booth do its slow, steady, entirely unremarkable business for the rest of the afternoon, and thought that unremarkable, for once, was exactly the flavor of good he'd been missing.
