Chapter 64

The throne room was smaller than Xander had pictured, which he supposed was becoming something of a pattern for him — expecting grandeur and finding, instead, something more human-scaled and considerably more nervous-making for exactly that reason.

King Then'tal sat not on an elevated throne but in a plain, well-made chair only slightly grander than the ones arranged in a loose half-circle before it, flanked by a handful of advisors whose expressions ranged from carefully neutral to openly curious, and Kestrian stood just to one side, close enough to be present, careful to make clear through his posture that this was the household's audience to navigate, not his to manage for them.

"Thank you for coming," Then'tal said, once they'd all settled, his voice measured, unhurried, the voice of a man who'd learned long ago that rushing important conversations rarely improved them. He looked around the loose half-circle — Elan, Corrin, Kestrian, and then, seated together near the back, a cluster of children who could not have been much past ten, one of them swinging her feet because they didn't reach the floor — and something in his face flickered, just once, before he smoothed it back into diplomatic calm. "I confess Kestrian's reports did not fully prepare me for how young this household actually is."

"They didn't fully prepare me either, Your Majesty," Kestrian said, dry, "and I've had considerably longer to get used to it."

"I understand this is not a comfortable position for anyone to be placed in," Then'tal went on. "I'll try to make it as brief as the subject allows, though I suspect it may not allow for particular brevity. I'll also say, before we begin — nothing asked here today is meant to be asked of the children in this room. I have questions for the grown-ups responsible for them. The children may listen, and may speak if they wish to, but no one in this room expects a ten-year-old to answer for the fate of old and dangerous magic."

Elan, seated at the front of the group, inclined her head, something like real gratitude crossing her face for just a moment. "We appreciate that, Your Majesty. More than you know."

"Let's begin there, then," Then'tal said. "Kestrian's reports tell me a girl in this household carries inherited knowledge of the old hub-work — knowledge thought lost for generations. They tell me a woman once associated with a dangerous theory of that same work has resurfaced and now resides, apparently peacefully, at the college my crown funds. I would like to understand, plainly, what I'm actually looking at."

Corrin rose, unhurried despite the visible effort it clearly cost her, and Xander watched her steady herself before she spoke — plain, careful, done, once and for all, with being a rumor other people got to shape.

"My name is Corrin, Your Majesty," she said. "Fifteen years ago, I believed the old hub-work should scale — should connect kingdoms, not just households, in the name of reaching places power had always forgotten. I chased that belief further than I should have, and when I saw clearly what it would actually cost, and who would actually end up holding that power, I stopped. I burned what I'd built. I spent fifteen years alone, making certain nobody else picked up where I'd left off." She paused. "I'm telling you this myself, in this room, because I've learned that hiding a story only ever leaves room for someone else to tell a worse version of it. I would rather you judge me on the truth than on whatever half-formed version reached you first."

Then'tal studied her for a long moment, unreadable. "That took courage to say plainly," he said finally. "More courage, I'd wager, than most who've stood in this room have shown me." His gaze moved to Dara, and gentled deliberately, the careful softening of a man reminding himself, on purpose, that he was looking at a child. "And you're the one who found the pieces Corrin left behind. You don't need to explain yourself to me, but I understand you'd like to. That's your choice, and only yours."

Dara looked at Elan, who gave the smallest of nods, permission rather than instruction, and then looked back at the king. "I never wanted power, Your Majesty," she said, her voice smaller than she probably meant it to be, but steady. "I just wanted to understand what my teacher taught me before she died. I fix broken doors. I don't want to build big scary ones."

"That's all I needed to hear from you," Then'tal said, gentle. "The rest of this conversation, I'll have with the grown-ups in the room. Thank you." He turned back to Elan and Corrin, and something in his posture shifted, formal again. "What I need to know is who stands between that knowledge and anyone who'd misuse it, going forward — not from a child, but from the adults who've taken responsibility for her."

"That would be all of us, Your Majesty," Elan said. "Myself. Corrin. Kestrian, in his official capacity. Alberton, at the college. And this household, which has been raising these children with more care than most royal nurseries manage, if I may say so." A faint, dry edge crept into her voice. "None of us intend to let a ten-year-old's inheritance become anyone's weapon. That's not a promise we're making lightly."

Then'tal was quiet for a long moment, considering the whole room — Corrin's steady honesty, Elan's careful diplomatic composure, the children seated together, present and clearly well looked-after in a way that plainly wasn't lost on him.

"I'll be direct," he said finally. "I don't fully trust knowledge this old and this dangerous simply because the adults currently guarding it seem, by every account, to be good people. Good people age. Good intentions get inherited by people who don't share them. That is the entire history of why kingdoms fear knowledge like this in the first place." He paused, considering his next words carefully. "But I also don't believe punishing honesty is the way to keep dangerous knowledge safe. If I make an example of this household for telling me the truth plainly, I teach every household after you to hide instead. That serves no one, least of all me."

The room held its breath.

"Here is what I propose," Then'tal said. "Formal, quiet oversight — not control, not seizure, but a standing arrangement, renewed yearly, in which the adults responsible for this household and this college report significant developments in the old hub-work to the crown, and in which the crown, in turn, commits to protecting rather than exploiting what's been rebuilt — and to protecting the child who rebuilt it, from anyone who might come looking for more than she's willing to give. I would rather have careful partners than something I have to watch from a distance with suspicion."

Elan glanced at Kestrian and Corrin, a silent question passing between them, and got two small nods in return.

"We accept, Your Majesty," Elan said. "Gratefully."

"Good," Then'tal said, something in his posture easing for the first time since the audience began. "Then I believe we've concluded the difficult part of today's business considerably faster than my advisors predicted." He looked back at the children, and the formality in his voice eased into something almost fond. "I understand, from Kestrian's rather extensive reports, that this household also makes remarkably good aluminum keychains. I don't suppose I might trouble one of you for a look, entirely unofficially."

Jesse, unable to help himself, grinned. "Very reasonable rates for kings, Your Majesty."

Then'tal laughed — a real laugh, surprised out of him, the sound of it echoing through a throne room that had clearly not heard much genuine laughter in recent memory — and the tension in the room broke all at once.

"I may take you up on that," the king said, still smiling, "though I suspect my treasury advisors will have opinions about a king carrying a keychain shaped like a dragon."

"We can do a discreet gear instead," Jesse offered. "Very dignified. Very kingly."

"A dignified gear," Then'tal repeated, amused. "Yes. I believe that would suit me considerably better."

And just like that, improbably, the audience that had kept the whole household braced for weeks ended not in fear or formality, but in something closer to the easy, ordinary warmth this household had gotten remarkably good at building, one unlikely relationship at a time, wherever it found room to take root.

Omake: What the Court Recorder Wrote Down (And What She Didn't)

Mirelle had been the crown's court recorder for eleven years, and in eleven years she had transcribed treaty negotiations, an attempted poisoning, and one memorably unhinged ambassador from a merfolk delegation who insisted on conducting the entire audience underwater in a borrowed washtub. She considered herself difficult to rattle.

She was rattled.

Her official notes read, in her small, tidy hand: Household representatives presented testimony regarding historical hub-work practices. Corrin (surname withheld) gave account of prior involvement with scaled construction theory, now renounced. Girl, D., corroborated inherited technical knowledge. King proposed standing oversight arrangement. Terms accepted.

What she did not write down, because it was not the sort of thing that belonged in an official transcript, was that at no point had any of them looked frightened. Not the woman confessing to having once chased a kind of power that could unmake kingdoms. Not the little ones seated near the back, barely past being babies by court standards, calmly present for a conversation that scholars twice their age were still struggling to fully understand. Not the tall dark-haired woman at the front, who had the specific, unhurried stillness Mirelle associated with senior diplomats three times her age — grown, unmistakably grown, and yet somehow devoting the whole of that adult composure to standing guard over a room full of children as though nothing in the world mattered more.

Most delegations who stood before the king left something of themselves behind in that room — a little fear, a little deference, the visible weight of understanding exactly how much power sat in that plain chair. This household had walked out afterward laughing about keychains.

She'd served under two kings and outlasted four court wizards, and she'd learned, over the years, to recognize the particular quiet that settled over a room in the presence of something genuinely dangerous. It wasn't the tension of soldiers or the menace of a hostile envoy. It was something else — the calm of people who had already faced down worse than a king's questions and come out the other side mostly unbothered.

She filed her report, as required, and went home, and did not sleep particularly well, and told no one, because there was truly nothing to tell that wouldn't sound, written down properly, like the ravings of an overtired recordkeeper: the children who aren't, somehow, entirely children, and I don't think anyone in that room but me noticed, and I don't think it would have mattered if they had.

She kept that thought to herself. Some things, she'd learned, were safer left out of the official record.
