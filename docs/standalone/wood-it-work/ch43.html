Chapter 43

Kestrian, when Elan reached him through whatever quiet diplomatic channel she kept for exactly this kind of thing, turned out to be exactly as inclined to help as she'd predicted, and considerably faster about it than anyone had expected.

"Two days," he reported, stepping through the hub door with the same careful, hands-visible entrance he always made, though there was less formality in it this time — he'd started to consider the trip routine, and it showed. "The patrol in question has been reminded, politely but firmly, that harassing unaccompanied travelers over the mere ownership of tools is not local watch business, and that any further such incidents will be reported directly to the crown. I don't expect you'll hear from them again."

"That was fast," Xander said.

"My king takes a dim view of his subjects frightening children," Kestrian said. "Even children he's never met, in villages he rarely thinks about. It was not a difficult case to make." He turned his attention to Dara, who'd gone very still and very formal the moment a king's herald walked into the room — an old instinct, from a whole life spent two social rungs below people like him. "You needn't look so alarmed. I'm not here about you specifically, beyond making sure the matter's closed. You're free to go where you like."

"Thank you," Dara said, quiet, still not quite believing it.

"Don't thank me," Kestrian said. "Thank this household, who apparently cannot go more than a fortnight without adopting some new complication and then talking a king into smoothing it over on their behalf." There was real warmth under the dryness, though — he'd started, against his own better professional judgment, to actually enjoy these visits. "Though I confess I'm curious. Meren's name isn't one I expected to hear again. She was something of a legend, in certain circles, before she vanished."

"You knew of her too?" Elan asked.

"Everyone who studies the old hub-work knows the name," Kestrian said. "I don't know a great deal beyond that myself — I'm a herald, not a scholar. But the man who could tell you properly happens to run a college devoted to exactly that subject, and I suspect he'd rather hear it from me than read about it in a report a week from now."

As if summoned by the very mention of him, the hub door hummed to life a second time that evening, and Alberton stepped through still dressed for a late night at the college, ink on his fingers and a stack of notes tucked under one arm, looking like a man who'd dropped everything the instant word reached him.

"Word reached me two days ago that a girl with unusual training and an unusually old master's tools had turned up at a house I know well, and I decided the college could survive one more day without me while I came to meet my newest student in person," Alberton said. He looked at Dara, and something in his expression gentled. He remembered, better than most, what it felt like to be the person everyone suddenly wanted something from. "Whatever your history is, whatever it turns out to mean, I promise you this much: at my college, you'll be a student first. Everything else is a distant second, and if anyone ever makes you feel otherwise, you come tell me, and I will handle it with considerably less patience than the herald here has been showing everyone."

"I heard that," Kestrian said.

"You were meant to," Alberton said, and the room laughed, and Dara, for the first time since she'd stumbled through the door soaking wet and terrified, looked like she actually believed, all the way down, that she'd landed somewhere safe.
