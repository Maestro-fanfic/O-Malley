Chapter 74

Summer arrived properly in early June, and with it came the shop's first real slow season in longer than anyone could remember — no scouts, no heralds, no kings, no wells accidentally opened into by ambitious apprentices, just heat and long days and the unhurried rhythm of a household that had, somehow, earned the right to be a little bored.

"I don't know what to do with myself," Jesse admitted, sprawled across the workshop floor in the shade, three days into what he'd started calling, with real anguish, "the Great Quiet."

"You could work," Xander suggested, not looking up from the truck he was under.

"I could," Jesse agreed, making no move to do so. "I could also lie here and contemplate the fact that we have gone an entire ten days without anyone showing up through that door with a diplomatic crisis, and honestly, I don't know what to do with that kind of peace. It's suspicious. It's making me nervous."

"You're bored and calling it suspicion."

"I contain multitudes," Jesse said. "It's possible to be both."

Amy, tending her flowers with real focus a few feet away, glanced over with a grin. "You could help me expand the garden. I've been wanting to add a second bed."

"Is it dangerous?"

"Extremely not."

"Then I don't know if I'm interested," Jesse said, though he was already getting up, already reaching for a shovel, because ten days of genuine quiet, it turned out, was exactly enough time for even Jesse to rediscover the specific, uncomplicated pleasure of doing something with his hands simply because it was there to do, no crisis attached, no story behind it bigger than a second flower bed and an afternoon with nowhere else to be.

By evening, the second bed was dug, the whole household had somehow ended up out back again for the second impromptu gathering in as many months, and Xander, watching Jesse argue with Amy about proper spacing for whatever seeds they'd decided on, watching Willow and Dara compare notes on some small working neither of them was in any hurry to finish, watching Corrin laugh at something Sharon's little brother said on one of his increasingly frequent visits — thought that this, precisely this, unhurried and small and entirely undramatic, was the exact shape of what all of it, every hard door and every kept promise, had actually been for.

No crisis on the horizon. No letter waiting to be opened. Just a garden, growing slow and ordinary in the summer dirt, and a whole household content, for once, to let it take its time.
