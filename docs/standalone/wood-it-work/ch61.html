Chapter 61

The letter that broke the quiet came, fittingly enough, on the first truly cold morning of the year, delivered not by runner or herald but by Kestrian himself, who stepped through the hub door looking like a man who hadn't slept properly in several days and was doing his best not to show it.

"I need to speak with all of you," he said, without his usual opening pleasantries, and that alone was enough to bring every head in the kitchen up sharp. "Something's happened. Something I should have anticipated sooner, and didn't, and I'd rather explain it plainly than dress it up."

"That's an ominous start," Sharon said, quiet.

"It is," Kestrian agreed. "King Then'tal has requested a formal audience. Not a courtesy visit. A formal audience, with full diplomatic weight, in three weeks' time." He looked around the table, at Xander, at Elan, at Dara and Corrin, who'd come through for breakfast and now sat very still. "He's asking to meet the household directly. All of it. And he's specifically requesting the presence of anyone with knowledge of, and I'm quoting his letter precisely here, 'the old hub-work, its history, and its current practitioners.'"

The kitchen went very quiet.

"He knows about Corrin," Willow said slowly.

"He knows there's a story worth asking about," Kestrian said. "Whether he knows the specifics — whether he knows about the scaling theory, about what Corrin once chased and walked away from — I genuinely can't say. But someone has clearly told him enough to make him curious, and Then'tal's curiosity, once engaged, doesn't tend to go back to sleep on its own."

Corrin's hands had gone very still around her mug. "Fifteen years I kept this quiet," she said, low. "Fifteen years, and it takes less than a season back in the world for it to reach a king's ears."

"That's not your fault," Dara said immediately, fierce. "That's not on you."

"Perhaps not," Corrin said, "but it's still my problem to answer for, if a king's asking."

"It's not just your problem," Elan said, calm, cutting through the rising tension with the particular steady authority she brought to exactly these moments. "It's this household's problem, the same as every other complication we've walked through together. We handle this the same way we've handled everything else — carefully, honestly where honesty's safe, selectively where it isn't, and together." She looked at Kestrian. "What does he actually want? Has he said?"

"He hasn't specified," Kestrian said. "Which is, I'll admit, not entirely reassuring. Then'tal doesn't request formal audiences over small matters, and he doesn't leave his intentions vague by accident. I suspect he wants to see for himself, in person, exactly what this household is — and exactly how much of the old hub-work knowledge has resurfaced under his notice without his prior awareness."

"Is that dangerous?" Amy asked, quiet.

"It could be," Kestrian said, honest, the way he'd learned this household preferred. "Then'tal is not a cruel king, but he is a careful one, and careful kings don't love discovering that dangerous old knowledge has been quietly reconstructing itself within a day's travel of their border without their prior say-so."

"Should the kids even be there?" Corrin asked, low, glancing at Xander and Jesse and Amy. "If he wants answers about the old work, he can have them from me and from Elan. There's no reason to put children in a throne room over something none of them chose."

"I asked him that myself, before I came," Kestrian said. "He specifically wants to meet the household — but I made it plain, and he agreed, that any real negotiating gets done by the grown-ups in the room. The children can be present, can be seen, can even speak if they want to. Nobody's asking them to carry this."

Xander, who had opinions about being talked over even when the talking was clearly meant kindly, opened his mouth, caught Elan's small, warning look, and settled for, "I can still say stuff, though. Right?"

"You can still say stuff," Elan agreed, the corner of her mouth twitching despite herself. "Just not the parts that end a kingdom if you get them wrong. Those parts are ours."

"Deal," Xander said, relieved despite himself, because as much as he hated being managed, some days it was genuinely nice to have grown-ups around whose whole job was standing between him and the parts that could end a kingdom.

"Three weeks," Kestrian said. "What do we need to do to get ready?"

"Honestly," he added, something almost like relief crossing his tired face, "I think that's the first sensible question anyone's asked all morning. Let's start there."
