Chapter 44

Getting Dara settled took the better part of a week, which surprised no one more than Dara herself, who kept apologizing for how little there actually was to settle.

"It's a bag," she said, for the third time, holding up the same worn canvas satchel she'd arrived with, tools and all. "You don't need a week to unpack a bag."

"You need a week to unpack a person," Willow said, not looking up from a stack of drawings spread across the kitchen table — Corrin's suggestions for where Dara could sleep, which shelf was hers, which chores she'd share with the rest of them, because that was how this household did things: you got a bed, a chore chart, and a say in dinner, same as everybody else. "The bag's the easy part."

"What's the hard part?"

"Figuring out where you're allowed to just exist for a minute without somebody needing something from you," Amy said, flat and certain, like she'd thought about this longer than the question deserved. "Took me almost a year here before I stopped waiting for the other shoe to drop every time somebody was nice to me for no reason."

Dara looked at her for a long moment, wary, weighing how much truth to trade for how much truth offered. "How'd you get past it?"

"Xander fed me pancakes until it stopped mattering," Amy said. "Not, like, a metaphor. Actual pancakes. Repeatedly. Over months."

"That's not a coping mechanism," Jesse said, "that's a diet."

"It worked," Amy said, unbothered. "Don't knock a working system."

Dara laughed, small and surprised, the kind of laugh that seemed to catch her off guard as much as anyone else, like she hadn't expected there to be room for it yet. "I don't think anyone's offered me pancakes since I got here."

"That's an oversight," Xander said, already up and moving toward the stove, "and one I intend to correct immediately, because apparently it's load-bearing for this household's whole onboarding process."

"You don't have to—"

"I know," Xander said. "I'm doing it anyway. Sit down."

Dara sat, slow and a little unsteady, like sitting down and being fed was a foreign enough experience that her body hadn't fully worked out the choreography of it yet, and Willow slid the drawings a little closer, tapping one with the end of her pen.

"Alberton wants you coming through to the college a few afternoons a week, for lessons," Willow said. "Not to live there — just to learn. Somebody grown-up will always take you through and bring you back, so you're not going anywhere alone. Corrin already said she'd walk you over most days, once she's settled in properly herself."

"I know," Dara said, quieter now, the ease draining back out of her posture. "I just — I don't know what I'm supposed to say I am, when people ask. Apprentice? Hedge witch's student? Nobody's ever asked me to put a name to it before. Meren just taught me things because I could hold a chisel steady and didn't ask too many questions."

"You don't have to have a name for it yet," Elan said, from the doorway, arms crossed, watching the whole scene with the particular stillness she got when she was turning a problem over from several angles at once. "If Meren really was what Kestrian thinks she was, that's not a small thing you're carrying. That's knowledge maybe a handful of grown-ups anywhere still remember. But you're ten. You don't have to be anything but a kid who's good with tools yet."

"That's not comforting," Dara said. "That's the opposite of comforting."

"It wasn't meant to comfort you, exactly," Elan said. "It was meant to tell you the truth, so nobody blindsides you with it later the way Kestrian nearly did the first day." She uncrossed her arms, came and sat across from Dara at the table, and for a moment the careful diplomat's mask slipped into something plainer, something closer to a grown-up who remembered what it was like to feel very small next to something very big. "Nobody in this house is going to make you grow up faster than you want to. Not for Meren, not for the college, not for anybody. That's a promise."

"And if I want to grow up faster anyway?"

"Then we'll talk about that when you're older," Elan said, gentle but firm. "Right now you get to just be a kid who's good with tools. The rest can wait."

Xander set a plate of pancakes down in front of Dara without comment, syrup already poured in the specific, slightly-too-generous way that had become something like a house signature, and Dara looked at it, and then around the table at all of them, and didn't say anything for a moment.

"Thank you," she said finally, and it came out steadier than anything she'd said since she'd arrived, like the thanks had found solid ground to stand on at last.

"Eat first," Willow said. "Thank us after. Pancakes get cold fast and Xander takes it personally."

"I do," Xander agreed. "Deeply personally."

Dara ate, and the drawings sat forgotten at the edge of the table for a little while longer, and nobody minded.
