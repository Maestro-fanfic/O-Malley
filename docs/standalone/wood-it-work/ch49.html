Chapter 49

The basement, by the following weekend, had chalk lines on the floor.

Not many, and not close to the door itself — Willow had been firm about that, a good six feet of clearance on every side, "because I like this door, and I like all of you continuing to use it, and I am not risking either of those things for a research project" — but enough that Xander's uncle, coming down for a spare fan belt, stopped dead in the doorway and stared for a long moment before deciding, wearily, the way he'd learned not to ask certain questions anymore, that he simply hadn't seen it.

"New hobby?" was all he said, backing back up the stairs.

"Geometry club," Xander called after him, which was, strictly speaking, not a lie.

"Geometry club," his uncle repeated, flatly, already retreating. "Sure. Have fun with your geometry club."

Willow knelt at the edge of the chalk lines with a brass instrument in each hand, murmuring measurements to Dara, who wrote them down in a battered notebook, her handwriting careful and cramped — she'd learned to write small because paper had always been scarce. Alberton hovered a few feet back, forcibly restraining himself from crowding closer, a discipline that was visibly costing him.

"The resonance is different than I expected," Willow said, frowning at the reading on her instrument. "Stronger, near the base. Like the anchor's doing more work than the theory says it should need to."

"That tracks," Dara said, not looking up from her notebook. "Meren used to say the anchor carries the door's temper. Doors with a calm temper need less anchor work. Doors that get used hard, that get emotional weight put on them — grief, longing, that kind of thing — need a heavier anchor to keep from drifting."

"Doors have tempers?" Jesse said, from the stairs, where he'd been sitting for the last twenty minutes, rapt, watching something he only half understood and found completely fascinating anyway.

"Everything you use enough starts to have a temper," Dara said. "Machines too, probably, the way you talk about that truck that only starts if you kick the bumper first."

"That's not a temper, that's a loose connection," Xander said, from the couch, where he was ostensibly reading and actually listening to every word.

"Sure," Dara said, a faint, dry smile on her face — she'd clearly decided this was an argument not worth having. "A loose connection. That responds to being kicked. In the same spot. Every time."

"Okay, when you say it like that—"

"This door," Willow interrupted, tapping her instrument against the frame with careful precision, "has been used constantly for over two years by people who love each other and worry about each other and use it to bring each other home safe. If Dara's theory holds, that's exactly the kind of use that would put weight on an anchor."

Everyone went quiet for a moment, the room absorbing something that felt bigger than the words used to say it.

"So the door's basically been listening to us the whole time," Amy said slowly. "Like — everything we've ever said walking through it. Every time somebody said 'be safe' or 'text when you're home' or—"

"I don't know that it listens, exactly," Dara said, careful now, aware she'd wandered into something that mattered more to this particular room than she'd realized when she started explaining it. "I just know the anchor's doing more work than a calm door's anchor should need to, and Meren always said that kind of extra work came from weight put on the door by the people using it. Whether that's the door understanding what it's carrying, or just — physics, reacting to how often and how hard it's used, I genuinely don't know. I'm not sure Meren knew either. She just knew what to build for it."

"I like the idea that it's listening," Willow said, quiet, running her hand along the frame with something that looked almost like affection. "Even if it's not. I like it anyway."

"Yeah," Xander said, and found he meant it more than the moment probably called for. "Me too."

Alberton, who had been very good for a remarkably long stretch of minutes, finally couldn't hold it in any longer. "This is extraordinary. Do you understand what this means for the theory? We've been treating hub-work as pure mechanism for a generation — geometry, materials, binding formulas — and here's a construction principle that accounts for emotional load as an actual structural variable—"

"Alberton," Willow said, not unkindly.

"Yes?"

"Not tonight. Tonight we're just measuring. The theory paper can wait for daylight, when you've had sleep and I've had a chance to check these numbers twice."

Alberton deflated, visibly, and then, after a moment, nodded, some of the boyish excitement folding back into the steadier, more patient shape of a man who'd learned, slowly, over the last several months, that good work usually survived being done carefully more often than it survived being done fast.

"Daylight," he agreed. "Fine. Daylight." He glanced at the door one more time, though, unable to help himself — a man who'd stumbled onto the best problem of his career and had to go home without solving it tonight. "It really is extraordinary, though."

"It really is," Dara agreed, quiet, and closed her notebook, and looked, for the first time since she'd stumbled soaking wet through that same door weeks ago, like someone standing not just in a safe place, but in the exact right place — the place all her scattered, half-understood inheritance had apparently been waiting for her to find.
