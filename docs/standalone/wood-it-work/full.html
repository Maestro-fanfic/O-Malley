Chapter 26: Chapter 26

The workshop was bigger than it had any right to be, which Xander had stopped questioning around the time he'd found out closets could lead to spice shops.

Alberton set the engine block down on a workbench like it was made of eggshells and immediately started rearranging three other projects to give it more room. "It needs its own table," he muttered. "A pedestal, perhaps."

"It's aluminum, not the Ark of the Covenant," Jesse said.

"To you," Alberton said.

Willow wandered along the shelves, trailing a finger over jars of things Xander had learned not to ask about. "You said tattoos would take longer to figure out than rings," she said. "Why?"

"Rings I understand," Alberton said, not looking up from the block. "Enchant an object, bind the effect, done. Tattoos aren't objects. They're skin. Skin moves, skin heals, skin argues with the magic if you don't get the binding exactly right." He frowned. "I had a teacher who lost a finger that way. The tattoo decided the finger wasn't skin enough to keep."

"Good to know," Brack said. "Add that to the list of things we're not telling the boys before they agree to it."

"They're going to find out eventually," Elan said.

"Eventually," Brack agreed, "is a fine word. It means not today."

Xander leaned against the workbench and watched Alberton run his thumb along the machined edge of the engine block again, the way a man might touch a photograph of someone he missed. "You okay there?"

"I keep thinking about how you made this," Alberton said. "Not the metal. The evenness of it. Every groove the same depth as the last." He shook his head like he was trying to clear water out of his ears. "We carve by hand and call it fine work if two craftsmen can't tell whose hand did which half."

"We've got machines that do the carving," Xander said. "People just tell the machines what to carve."

"Machines that carve," Alberton repeated slowly, "on command."

"Don't," Willow said.

"I wasn't going to ask," Alberton said, in the tone of a man who was absolutely going to ask, later, when he thought no one was listening.

Amy poked her head out of the hub doorway. "The movie's over. Sharon wants to know if we're doing dinner here or if we're all walking back before Xander's uncle notices his engine block walked off."

"He noticed weeks ago," Xander said. "He just doesn't believe it's gone until he actually goes looking for it, and he never goes looking for it."

"That's not reassuring," Amy said.

"It's accurate," Xander said. "Different thing."

Alberton finally tore himself away from the block long enough to look at the room full of children discussing theft of a stranger's property like it was a chore rotation. "Does anyone else find it odd that the three of you talk about wonders and thefts and gifting away kingdoms' worth of goods with the same tone you'd use to plan a picnic?"

"We've had a while to get used to it," Xander said.

"You're ten," Alberton said.

"Ten's relative," Jesse said. "Ask us again in a decade."

"I intend to," Alberton said, and went back to his notebook, already three lines into a new list before he'd finished sitting down.

Elan caught Xander's eye across the room and tipped her head toward the door, a small motion, the kind that meant walk with me rather than follow me. He fell into step beside her without being asked twice.

"You gave him a fortune today," she said once they were out of easy earshot. "You do that a great deal, from what I've seen. Willow isn't wrong that you overpay."

"We're not exactly hurting for aluminum," Xander said.

"That's not what I asked."

He glanced at her. She wasn't smiling the way she usually did when she teased him; she had the same look Willow got right before she asked a question she already suspected the answer to.

"Where I'm from," Xander said carefully, "people who have more than they need and don't share it end up alone with it. That's not a fate I'm interested in."

"That's a very old answer for someone who's supposed to be new to all this," Elan said.

"I've had good teachers," Xander said, and left it there, and after a moment, so did she.

Not if. When."

"And?"

"And he thinks you should be worried about that a great deal sooner than you currently are."

"I worry about it," Xander said. "I just don't let it slow anything down."

"That's not the same as worrying."

"It's the only kind I've got time for," he said, and there was nothing performed about it this time, no tidy line waiting to land — just the plain shape of the fact, said the way a person says something they've already made peace with. "We'll deal with it when it's an actual problem instead of a maybe."

Elan didn't answer right away. Somewhere behind them Alberton's voice rose in delighted disbelief over something Jesse had apparently just offered him, and Brack's low laugh followed a beat later.

"For what it's worth," she said finally, "I don't think it'll be as sudden as Brack fears. Kings move slowly when they're not sure yet what they're looking at."

"And when they are sure?"

"Then you'll have wanted every pound of goodwill you ever gave away," Elan said. "So keep going. I'll tell you when to worry properly."

"Deal," Xander said.

"Also," she added, already turning back toward the workshop, "if Alberton names anything after a vegetable, I want it on record that I objected."

"Noted," Xander said. "Loudly and on the record."

They walked back in together, and neither of them mentioned it again that day.

Dinner ended up being pizza, because Sharon pointed out that nobody had actually cooked anything and Alberton had made it three notebooks deep into cantrip theory without noticing the sun had gone down.

"You people eat like this regularly?" Alberton asked, holding a slice at an angle that suggested he suspected it of being a trap.

"Regularly is generous," Willow said. "Xander eats like this. The rest of us eat like this when Xander's cooking isn't an option."

"Hurtful," Xander said, not looking up from his own slice, "but fair."

"What is it, precisely?" Alberton asked, turning the slice another quarter turn.

"Bread, cheese, tomato sauce, whatever you want on top," Jesse said. "Ours has pepperoni, olives, and Xander's contribution."

"Which is?"

"More cheese," Xander said.

"There is such a thing as too much cheese," Amy said.

"There is a rumor of such a thing," Xander corrected. "I've never actually witnessed it."

Alberton finally bit into the slice, chewed once, twice, and then went very still in the specific way that meant a man was recalculating his entire relationship with food. "This," he said slowly, "is going to be a problem."

"Welcome to pizza," Jesse said, saluting him with his own slice.

"No, I mean it will genuinely be a problem," Alberton said. "I have responsibilities. A college to found. Apprentices to train. I cannot in good conscience spend the rest of my days sitting in this room eating flatbread with cheese."

"Sure you can," Sharon said. "You just did the math wrong. Add it to the college. Founder's privilege."

Alberton considered that with the seriousness of a man weighing a genuine theological argument, and took another bite.

Brack leaned over to Elan. "We are going to lose him."

"We lost him around the aluminum," Elan said. "This is just him noticing."

By the time the last box was empty, Alberton had fallen asleep sitting upright in his chair with a half-finished diagram of a Ring of Cantrips still open on the table in front of him, one hand resting on the notebook like he was afraid it might wander off without him.

"Should we wake him?" Amy asked.

"No," Willow said, already pulling a blanket out of the chest she kept in the corner for exactly this kind of situation. "He'll be embarrassed and rush off before he's actually rested, and then he'll make a mistake tomorrow because he's tired, and then he'll blame the mistake on us instead of himself."

"That's oddly specific," Brack said.

"He's not the first mage to fall asleep at that table," Willow said, draping the blanket over Alberton's shoulders with the same brisk competence she used for everything else. "He won't be the last."

Xander caught Jesse's eye across the room, and neither of them said anything, because they'd both learned a long time ago that some things about Willow's hub were easier to accept than to ask about.

Chapter 27: Chapter 27

The man came in a little after ten, and Jesse clocked him from the counter before he'd made it past the gate — dark coat too heavy for the weather, boots that had walked farther than any boots bought locally, and a way of scanning the lot that wasn't shopping, it was cataloguing.

"Customer," Jesse said, low, to Xander, who was under a truck with his legs sticking out and didn't have a free hand to do anything but grunt in acknowledgment.

Jesse wiped his hands on a rag that made them dirtier instead of cleaner, and went out to meet the man slow and unhurried, like he had nothing to hide — the single most useful skill either of them had ever learned to fake.

"Help you find something?" Jesse asked.

The man studied him for a moment before answering, sizing him up the way a man decides how much someone's worth talking to. "I am told," he said, in English that was correct in every way except the rhythm, "that this establishment produces work of unusual quality. Metalwork. Precise metalwork."

"We do all right," Jesse said. "CNC machine does most of the precision. Guy who runs it's kind of obsessive about tolerances. You looking for something specific, or just checking out the shop?"

"Checking," the man said. "I have heard stories. I like to see for myself whether stories are true."

"Stories, huh." Jesse leaned against the gate post, unbothered, the picture of a bored kid killing time on a slow morning. "People around here like to exaggerate. We had a guy tell his cousin we built engines out of gold once. It was aluminum. Regular aluminum. He just liked the way gold sounded better in the story."

"And is that the truth? Regular aluminum?"

"Come look," Jesse said, and led him past a row of scrap cars toward the workshop, chatting the whole way about torque specs and supplier discounts and the guy two towns over who'd tried to undercut their prices and failed because his machines couldn't hold a tolerance to save his life, a steady stream of specific, boring, entirely true details that painted a picture so mundane it was almost aggressive in its lack of mystery.

Xander rolled out from under the truck at the sound of footsteps and got up slow, wiping his hands, playing the part of a kid who did this every day because he did do this every day, and none of what he was about to say would be a lie.

"Hey," Xander said. "You need something?"

"He's checking out a rumor," Jesse said, before the man could answer. "Somebody told him we do gold engines."

Xander laughed, easy and unforced, because the joke actually was funny, and because he'd learned a long time ago that the truest way to sell a lie was to build it entirely out of things that were true. "Man, that story again. No. It's aluminum. It's always been aluminum. You want to see the invoices, I can show you the invoices, but they're extremely boring."

"I would not object to seeing them," the man said.

"Sure," Xander said, already walking toward the office, unbothered, and pulled a battered binder off a shelf and flipped it open to a page of supplier receipts, real ones, ordinary ones, the paper trail of a genuinely mundane teenage business that happened to also be extremely good at what it did. "Here. Delivery from Hendricks Metal Supply, third of the month. Here's the invoice from the batch before that. You want, I can pull the receipts back two years, they're all in the file cabinet, alphabetical."

The man looked at the binder for a long moment. Looked at Xander. Looked at the workshop, at the machined engine block sitting exactly where it should be sitting for a place like this, ordinary and unremarkable and entirely too plain to be what someone had apparently traveled a very long way to ask about.

"You are younger than I expected," the man said finally.

"Everybody says that," Xander said. "I get it a lot. Kid running a shop, people assume there's a story. There isn't. My uncle got sick, I kept the lights on, turned out I was good at it. No gold. No magic. Just aluminum and a lot of hours nobody wants to work."

The man's eyes flicked to Jesse, who shrugged, unbothered — he'd never had to fake being unimpressed by his best friend's business, because he genuinely wasn't.

"He works too much," Jesse offered. "Ask anybody."

Something in the man's posture eased, fractionally, the tension of a hunter who has followed a scent to its source and found only an ordinary rabbit hole — though something else lingered underneath it, something he didn't quite have words for, a small persistent wrongness he couldn't pin to any single fact in the room. Two boys, alone at a shop, talking to a stranger who'd walked in asking pointed questions about gold — and neither of them had so much as flinched. He nodded once, more to himself than to either of them, and turned to go.

"Thank you for your time," he said. "I apologize for the intrusion."

"No problem," Xander said. "Come back if you ever actually need parts. We're cheap and we don't ask questions."

The man walked back through the gate, back down the street, glancing back once, briefly, at two children who should have been at least a little rattled by a strange man asking about their family's business and plainly weren't, and didn't look back again after that, and it wasn't until he'd turned the corner two blocks down that Jesse let out a breath he'd apparently been holding for the better part of ten minutes.

"Well," Jesse said. "That happened."

"That happened," Xander agreed, closing the binder slow and careful, hands not quite steady in a way he was making sure Jesse was the only one who'd notice.

"You think it worked?"

"I think," Xander said, "we're about to find out exactly how good Elan's read on this guy actually was."

Chapter 28: Chapter 28

Elan listened to the whole account without interrupting, which was itself unusual enough that Jesse kept glancing at her sideways to check she was still breathing.

"Boots too worn for a courtier," she said finally. "Coat too heavy for the season. Polite, unhurried, satisfied by a binder of receipts." She was quiet a moment, turning it over. "That's not what I expected from Then'tal's people. His scouts are usually sharper than that. Suspicious of anything too convenient."

"You think it's fake," Brack said. "The whole encounter."

"I think," Elan said slowly, "that either he genuinely believed the story Xander gave him, in which case we're fine and this was exactly as boring as it looked — or he didn't believe it at all, and let you both think he did, because a scout who reveals he isn't convinced gets a harder problem sent after him next time. The polite, satisfied exit is what a professional does whether or not he's actually satisfied."

"That's a comforting thought," Jesse said. "Really. Thank you for that."

"I'm not in the business of comforting thoughts," Elan said. "I'm in the business of not getting anyone killed by assuming the easy answer. There's a difference, and it's usually the one that keeps people alive."

Willow had been quiet through most of this, sitting cross-legged on the counter with a notebook open on her knee, and now she looked up with the specific expression she got when a problem had stopped being about people and started being about mechanics. "Can we find out where he went? After he left the shop?"

"Track him, you mean," Elan said.

"Not track like following him down the street," Willow said. "Track like — I know what a scrying working looks like when it's aimed at us. If somebody's watching this house, I want to know before it's a surprise."

"You have that kind of magic?"

"Enough of it," Willow said. "I can't see faces or names, nothing that specific. But I can tell if something's watching this house the way you'd feel eyes on the back of your neck in a crowded room. If the scout left something behind — a ward, a marker, anything meant to report back — I should be able to find the thread of it."

"Do it," Elan said. "Carefully. If there is something there, I don't want it to know we noticed."

Willow nodded and slipped off the counter, already reaching for the chalk and salt she kept for exactly this kind of quiet, defensive work, and the kitchen settled into the particular hush of people trying very hard not to get underfoot while something important happened around them.

It was into that hush that Alberton finally spoke, from where he'd been sitting very still at the table through the whole conversation, notebook forgotten in front of him for once.

"I should go," he said. "Back through my own door. Before this becomes something that costs you more than it's worth."

The room turned to look at him.

"That's not what any of us want," Xander said.

"It's not about what you want. It's about what's safe," Alberton said. "I am, whatever else I am, a foreign complication sitting in the middle of your ordinary life, and today a scout came looking for exactly that kind of complication. If I leave, there's nothing left here for anyone to find."

"If you leave," Willow said, not looking up from the chalk circle she was drawing, "you leave with a half-finished ring, no aluminum contact anywhere else in this kingdom or any other, and a scout report already filed that already mentions this shop by name. Leaving doesn't erase today. It just means you did all the risky part for nothing."

"She's got a point," Brack said. "Running now protects you from the wrong danger and walks straight into the one that actually matters."

"That is not entirely reassuring," Alberton said.

"It's not supposed to be reassuring," Brack said. "It's supposed to be true. There's a difference, and you'll want to get comfortable with it if you're staying."

Alberton looked between them — at Xander's calm, at Brack's flat certainty, at Elan's careful watching stillness — and something in his shoulders settled, not into ease, exactly, but into the particular resolve of a man deciding a risk was worth taking with his eyes open.

"Then I'll stay," he said. "And I'll work faster. If there's a version of the ring that's finished before anyone official comes asking harder questions than a man with a binder of receipts can answer, I would like to build that version."

"Now you're talking," Jesse said, grinning.

"I am talking," Alberton agreed, "about not dying in a foreign kingdom over a business dispute. I would appreciate everyone treating that with appropriate seriousness."

"We do," Xander said. "That's why nobody's panicking. Panicking gets people killed faster than scouts do."

Outside, the sun kept climbing, ordinary and unbothered, over a street that looked exactly like it had looked yesterday, and the day before that, and every day for two years — which was, as far as anyone in that kitchen was concerned, exactly how it needed to keep looking for as long as they could manage it.

Chapter 29: Chapter 29

Willow's scrying setup took over the back half of the hub, a circle of chalk and salt and small brass instruments Amy had learned to hand over without being asked, in the order Willow needed them, the two of them moving through it with the practiced rhythm of a routine they'd built themselves over a hundred smaller workings.

"Anchor point," Willow said, and Amy passed her a shard of the aluminum they'd worked that week, still faintly warm from the machine. "If anyone's got a working keyed to the metal itself, this'll show me the thread."

"And if nobody's got a working keyed to it?"

"Then I'll know that too, which is still useful. Knowing nobody's watching is worth almost as much as knowing somebody is."

Amy settled in with the reference book open across her knees, ready to read out the correction phrases the moment Willow's working started to drift, which it would, because scrying always drifted, because trying to look at something that didn't want to be looked at was inherently an argument, and arguments never went perfectly to plan.

It took the better part of twenty minutes, Willow's brow furrowed in concentration, the chalk lines glowing faint and then fading and then glowing again as she chased the thread of the working through layers of nothing, until finally, with a small, sharp intake of breath, she found it.

"There," she said. "Small. Old-fashioned. Not aimed at us specifically — more like a general listening charm, the kind you'd leave on any place worth keeping an eye on, not proof anybody suspects anything in particular." She traced the shape of it in the air without touching the actual working, careful not to disturb it. "It's reporting back somewhere. I can't tell where, not with what I've got. But it's definitely reporting."

"Can you turn it off?" Amy asked.

"I can dull it, though. Not remove it — that'd probably trip something, tell them somebody noticed and fought back, which is worse than leaving it alone. But I can make our little pings quieter. Buy us time."

"Do it," Amy said.

"That's the plan," Willow said, already reaching for a fresh handful of the counter-components she kept for exactly this kind of quiet, defensive work, the kind that didn't announce itself, that just made a house a little duller to look at, a little less interesting to whatever was doing the looking.

It took another hour, careful and unglamorous, the kind of magic that never made it into anybody's stories because it didn't do anything as dramatic as explode or glow — it just made a doorbell ring a little softer, made an address slide a little further down somebody's list of things worth checking on, made the ordinary version of the truth Xander had handed the scout that morning into something the universe itself was a little more willing to believe.

When it was done, Willow sat back on her heels, tired in the specific bone-deep way that came from holding a working steady for longer than was entirely comfortable, and Amy handed her a water bottle without being asked, because that was its own kind of routine too.

"Well?" Xander asked, from the doorway, where he'd apparently been standing for a while without either of them noticing.

"We've got time," Willow said. "Not unlimited time. But time." She took a long drink, caught her breath, looked at him with the flat, tired honesty she saved for moments that mattered. "Use it well."

"Always do," Xander said, and didn't quite believe it himself, but said it anyway, because some things you built by insisting on them until they were true, and this — all of it, the shop, the aluminum, the goodwill, the family that had assembled itself around a hub door in a girl's basement — was the biggest thing he'd ever tried to build that way.

Chapter 30: Chapter 30

Alberton worked the way other people breathed — automatically, constantly, and with visible discomfort whenever anyone tried to make him stop.

By the third day after the scout, he'd colonized a full workbench of his own, and the workshop had rearranged itself around him the way a house rearranges itself around a new pet: tolerantly, with occasional muttering, and a general acceptance that certain corners now belonged to him whether anyone had agreed to it or not.

"Talk me through it again," Xander said, pulling up a stool. "The ring. Where's it actually stuck?"

"It's not stuck," Alberton said, with the wounded dignity of a craftsman defending his methods. "It's deliberate. A Ring of Cantrips needs three separate bindings layered without any one of them dominating the others, or the wearer ends up favoring whichever cantrip got bound strongest and the rest atrophy from disuse. I am being careful. Careful looks slow from the outside. I assure you it is not the same thing."

"How much longer, careful-not-slow-wise?"

"A week. Maybe less, if the aluminum keeps holding a charge as cleanly as it has been." Alberton turned the half-finished ring over in his fingers, admiring it despite himself. "Your metal is extraordinary, you know. I keep saying this and I don't think any of you understand what I mean by it. Where I come from, this kind of purity takes a master smith a season and a small fortune. You hand it to me by the pound like it's nothing."

"It kind of is nothing, to us," Xander said. "That's the whole trade, right? Nothing to us, everything to you."

"That," Alberton said, "is either the most generous or the most alarming sentence anyone's ever said to me, and I genuinely cannot decide which."

Chapter 31: Chapter 31

Two weeks later

The ring finished on a Tuesday, and Alberton insisted on a small, entirely unnecessary ceremony to mark the occasion, which mostly consisted of everyone standing around the workbench while he held it up to the light with both hands like it might dissolve if he breathed on it wrong.

"It's beautiful," Willow said, and meant it. The band caught the light in three distinct colors depending on the angle, evidence, Alberton explained, of the layered bindings working exactly as intended rather than fighting each other for dominance.

"Try it," Jesse said. "Come on. Cast something."

Alberton slid it on, closed his eyes, murmured something under his breath, and a small, warm light bloomed in his open palm, steady and unhurried, the kind of cantrip that would have taken real concentration to hold a minute ago and now sat there like it cost him nothing at all.

"It works," he said, and there was something almost boyish in the way he said it, all his careful scholarly composure cracking for just a moment into pure, uncomplicated delight. "It actually works."

"Course it works," Xander said. "You're good at your job."

"I am good at my job," Alberton agreed, still staring at the light in his hand like he'd never seen anything like it, even though he'd built it himself, "but good has never once, in my entire career, meant fast, and this took a fraction of the time it should have, because your metal doesn't fight me the way ordinary metal does. Do you understand what this means? If I can teach even a handful of other craftsmen to work with aluminum this pure, we could change what magical items even are. Cheaper. Faster. Available to people who could never have afforded them before."

"That sounds like a big deal," Amy said.

"It is a very big deal," Alberton said, sobering slightly. "Which is also, I suspect, going to be a problem, because big deals tend to attract attention, and I don't think either of our worlds is quite ready for the kind of attention this could bring."

Chapter 32: Chapter 32

The attention, when it came, arrived formally, which somehow made it worse.

Kestrian stepped through the hub door on a gray Wednesday morning with none of his usual easy warmth, a sealed letter in hand and the particular careful blankness of a man delivering news he didn't personally agree with but was obligated to deliver anyway.

"The crown has taken notice," he said, without preamble. "Not of you specifically — not yet — but of Alberton's absence from the college registry, of a scout's report about a shop that answered every question a little too smoothly, of rumors of unusually fine metalwork changing hands in ways nobody official can quite trace." He held out the letter. "King Then'tal requests Alberton's presence at court. Formally. To account for his activities."

The kitchen went very quiet.

"That's not good," Jesse said.

"It's not necessarily bad," Kestrian said, though the caution in his voice suggested he didn't fully believe that himself. "Then'tal is not an unreasonable king. But an unaccounted-for mage working with an unaccounted-for source of extraordinary materials is exactly the kind of thing that makes a careful ruler nervous, and nervous rulers make decisions that are hard to walk back."

Alberton had gone very pale. "If I go, and they ask where the aluminum comes from—"

"Then you'll need an answer that satisfies a king without endangering the people who gave it to you," Elan said, already thinking several steps ahead in the way she always did when the stakes turned real. "Which means we need to decide, carefully, exactly how much truth this situation can survive."

Chapter 33: Chapter 33

They settled on the same principle that had gotten Xander through the scout's visit — truth, arranged carefully, rather than invention.

Alberton would tell the king he'd found a trading partner from a distant, well-warded land whose people valued discretion above all else, whose goods were extraordinary, and whose only price for continued trade was privacy. All of that was true. None of it mentioned children, or a machine shop, or a door in a basement.

"He'll ask who," Kestrian warned. "Kings always ask who."

"Then I'll tell him what I can and no more," Alberton said, steadier now than he'd been the day before, the fear settling into something more like resolve. "I've never lied to my king in my life. I don't intend to start today. I simply intend to be selective about which truths I volunteer."

"That's basically our family motto at this point," Xander said.

"I've noticed," Alberton said, dry, and for the first time all morning, something like a real smile crossed his face.

Chapter 34: Chapter 34

The audience, when it happened, happened without any of the household present — Kestrian had been firm on that point, gently but immovably, and Elan had agreed with him, which settled the matter more thoroughly than any argument could have.

"Not because we don't trust you," Elan told Xander, when he'd pushed back on being left out. "Because a king meeting an unaccounted-for mage is one kind of complicated conversation. A king meeting an unaccounted-for mage and a house full of children with an inexplicable source of royal-grade metal is an entirely different, much harder conversation, and I'd rather Alberton have the easier one first."

So they waited, the whole household gathered in the kitchen doing a poor job of pretending they weren't waiting, until Alberton finally stumbled back through the hub door late that evening looking like a man who'd run a race he hadn't trained for and somehow, against expectation, won it.

"Well?" Willow asked, before he'd even fully caught his breath.

"He believed me," Alberton said, sinking into a chair. "Or believed enough of me. He pressed, of course — asked twice more who my trading partner was, asked whether this partner might be persuaded to open trade more broadly — but when I held firm on discretion being the whole point of the arrangement, he didn't push a third time. I think," he added, still slightly stunned by his own success, "he respected that I was protecting something rather than hiding something. Kings understand the difference, even when they don't like it."

"So we're safe," Jesse said.

"For now," Alberton said. "He wants updates. Regular ones. Nothing invasive — just enough to know I haven't vanished into something dangerous. I told him I would provide them personally, which means the two of you—" he nodded at Xander and Willow, "—never need to appear in a royal report at all. I intend to keep it that way."

Chapter 35: Chapter 35

A month later

The regular updates turned into a rhythm of their own — Alberton visiting the palace once every few weeks, carefully vague, always truthful, always careful — and somewhere in that rhythm, a second idea began to take root in him, one he raised almost shyly, over dinner, like he expected to be laughed at for it.

"I've been thinking," he said, "about a school."

"A school for what?" Amy asked.

"For this. All of it. Enchantment, properly taught, with materials nobody's had access to before, methods refined by working with metal this pure." He turned his water glass slowly between his hands, not quite meeting anyone's eyes. "I've spent years watching promising students fail simply because they couldn't afford proper materials, or because nobody bothered teaching them the fundamentals properly before throwing them at the hard problems. I think I could build something better. Something that actually gives ordinary people a real chance at this work, not just the wealthy few who can already afford good tutors."

"That sounds amazing," Willow said, and meant it.

"It would require leaving," Alberton said, quieter now. "Setting up somewhere real, somewhere permanent, not just borrowing a corner of your workshop forever. I don't say that to make anyone feel obligated to keep me. I say it because I think it's time, and I wanted you to hear it from me before it became a rumor Kestrian had to explain."

The table was quiet for a moment.

"We're gonna miss you," Xander said, plain and simple, and Alberton's careful composure cracked, just slightly, at the honesty of it.

"I'm not leaving you," he said. "I'm leaving the workbench. There's a considerable difference, and I intend to make full use of that door of yours to make sure everyone remembers it."

Chapter 36: Chapter 36

Getting the king's blessing for a college turned out to be, against everyone's cautious expectations, the easiest part of the entire process.

Omake: What the Court Recorder Wrote Down (And What She Didn't)

Mirelle had been the crown's court recorder for eleven years, and in eleven years she had transcribed treaty negotiations, an attempted poisoning, and one memorably unhinged ambassador from a merfolk delegation who insisted on conducting the entire audience underwater in a borrowed washtub. She was rattled.

Her official notes read, in her small, tidy hand: Household representatives presented testimony regarding historical hub-work practices. King proposed standing oversight arrangement. Terms accepted.

What she did not write down, because it was not the sort of thing that belonged in an official transcript, was that at no point had any of them looked frightened. Not the woman confessing to having once chased a kind of power that could unmake kingdoms. Not the little ones seated near the back, barely past being babies by court standards, calmly present for a conversation that scholars twice their age were still struggling to fully understand. Most delegations who stood before the king left something of themselves behind in that room — a little fear, a little deference, the visible weight of understanding exactly how much power sat in that plain chair. This household had walked out afterward laughing about keychains.

She'd served under two kings and outlasted four court wizards, and she'd learned, over the years, to recognize the particular quiet that settled over a room in the presence of something genuinely dangerous. It wasn't the tension of soldiers or the menace of a hostile envoy. It was something else — the calm of people who had already faced down worse than a king's questions and come out the other side mostly unbothered.

She filed her report, as required, and went home, and did not sleep particularly well, and told no one, because there was truly nothing to tell that wouldn't sound, written down properly, like the ravings of an overtired recordkeeper: the children who aren't, somehow, entirely children, and I don't think anyone in that room but me noticed, and I don't think it would have mattered if they had.

She kept that thought to herself. Some things, she'd learned, were safer left out of the official record.

Autumn came around again, the way it always did, and the household did what it always did with an autumn: settled into it, unhurried, letting the year turn without much ceremony.

Dara's lessons continued, steady now, no longer a source of anxiety so much as just another part of the week, like chores or dinner. Corrin taught her small classes and walked home most evenings with flour on her sleeves from whatever Willow had talked her into baking that week. The desert trade quietly hummed along in the background, water and keychains and small good-natured squabbles over shipment schedules, Tomas's little waystation growing a little more each season without ever quite losing the feel of a place built on a favor between friends rather than a formal enterprise.

The letter from R. finally got answered, a short, careful note Dara spent three evenings drafting and redrafting before she was satisfied, and the reply that came back a month later was warm and unhurried and asked nothing of her she wasn't ready to give.

Nobody kept precise count anymore of how many worlds the hub door had quietly connected to, how many small kindnesses had rippled out into how many unlikely places. It had simply become the shape of things — a household that kept its doors open, literally and otherwise, and trusted that whatever walked through them, sooner or later, turned out to be worth the trouble of getting to know.

"You've got the face again," Jesse said, one evening, finding Xander leaning in the workshop doorway watching the whole ordinary sprawl of it — Willow and Dara bent over some new small working, Amy elbow-deep in aluminum shavings at the lathe she'd finally started learning properly, Corrin laughing at something Sharon's little brother said, the desert trunk sitting quiet and unremarkable in its corner, doing nothing more dramatic tonight than existing.

"Can't help it," Xander said. "Look at all this."

Jesse looked, the way he always did now, without needing convincing anymore. "Yeah," he said. "I know."

They stood there together a while longer, and didn't say anything else, because there wasn't anything else that needed saying — just a shop, and a door, and a whole improbable family that had built something worth keeping, one small kindness and one open door at a time.
