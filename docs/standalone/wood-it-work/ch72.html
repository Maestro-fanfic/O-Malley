Chapter 72

The pollinator garden's first real crisis arrived not from any bee-related catastrophe but from Xander's uncle, who wandered out back one morning, took in the neat rows of flowers now occupying what had until recently been an unremarkable stretch of dirt behind his own machine shop, and stood there for a long moment in visible, deepening confusion.

"When did this happen," he said. Not a question, exactly. More a statement of a man recalibrating his understanding of his own property.

"Gradually," Xander said, which was true.

"There's a beehive."

"There's a bee box," Xander corrected. "Small one. Very manageable. Willow's been reading up."

"Willow reads up on everything," his uncle said, which was also true, and which he'd apparently decided, at some point over the last two years, to simply accept as a fact of the household's operation rather than a thing worth further investigation. He studied the garden a moment longer, then shrugged, loose and easy — he'd long since stopped trying to fully account for everything happening on his property and made a kind of peace with it. "Looks nice, though. Real nice, actually."

"Thanks," Xander said, faintly surprised by how much that landed.

"You kids," his uncle said, shaking his head, not unkindly, already turning to head back toward the office, "you've turned this place into something I don't half recognize anymore."

"Good different or bad different?"

His uncle paused at the door, considering the question with more weight than Xander expected. "Good different," he said finally. "Real good, actually. Your dad would've—" He stopped himself, cleared his throat, the way he always did when that particular sentence threatened to go somewhere he wasn't quite ready to finish out loud. "Anyway. Nice garden. Don't let the bees near the paint booth, they don't like the fumes."

He disappeared back into the office, and Xander stood there a moment in the morning sun, looking out over a pollinator garden and an aluminum keychain stand and a workshop full of people who'd started as strangers and become, one impossible door and one ordinary Tuesday at a time, something that genuinely felt like the word home — and thought that whatever his uncle had almost said, whatever unfinished sentence still sat there, warm and a little sad, in the space between them, it was, on the whole, a pretty good problem to have.

He'd get there eventually. Some doors were worth leaving open, after all, rather than rushing through before either of them was ready.
