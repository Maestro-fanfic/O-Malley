Honest Trailer

Bonus Chapter: The Honest Trailer

A fluorescent shop light flickered on above a lathe, a pile of aluminum stock, and a wardrobe that was absolutely not supposed to connect to another universe.

The voice began.

“In a world where ten-year-old boys have access to machine tools, magic, and only the most theoretical concept of adult supervision…”

Xander checked a measurement. Jesse opened a door into somewhere impossible.

“…two children will turn one workshop into the least regulated interdimensional development agency in existence.”

The music swelled.

“Return to the shop where precision machining looks like sorcery, sorcery looks like a solvable engineering problem, and every harmless favor becomes critical infrastructure by Thursday.”

Quick cuts: aluminum shavings curling away from a cutter. Willow studying a working. Amy learning the lathe. Sharon watching the boys explain something with far too much confidence. Brack inspecting craftsmanship from a world that should not exist.

“Meet Xander and Jesse.”

They grinned the grins of children who had just found a responsible use for an irresponsible idea.

“They are machinists.”

A perfect part dropped into a waiting hand.

“Merchants.”

Gold changed hands.

“Diplomats.”

An offended supernatural visitor accepted tea.

“And, through a clerical error in reality itself, regional transit planners.”

A new door opened.

Then another.

Then another.

“Watch as the household connects fantasy kingdoms, desert settlements, magical schools, and any other civilization within wardrobe range—while maintaining the ironclad secrecy protocol known as ‘technically telling the truth on the invoice.’”

A suspicious man studied records for the alleged gold engines.

The records were aggressively mundane.

He became more suspicious.

“Because nothing conceals an impossible magical economy like accurate bookkeeping.”

This summer, discover a story where enchanted rings, protective tattoos, water rights, education, machine tolerances, and pizza all receive roughly equal geopolitical weight.

Someone tried the pizza.

The room changed forever.

“Yes. Once again, humanity’s greatest contribution to first contact is melted cheese.”

But opening a door is easy.

Keeping it open means deciding who gets a key, who gets help, who gets protected, what gets traded, and whether giving someone a small useful object will accidentally reorganize their local economy.

It usually will.

“Thrill as Xander and Jesse repeatedly attempt one modest act of kindness and are punished with long-term consequences.”

A desert waystation grew around reliable water.

Dara’s lessons became part of the week.

Corrin began teaching classes.

A favor became a route. A route became a schedule. A schedule became a dispute process.

Alberton beamed.

“You built a trade network.”

Elan’s expression said that nobody had better call it that where outsiders could hear.

“Fine,” the narrator amended. “A completely ordinary, secret, multi-world exchange of goods, knowledge, labor, hospitality, and magical door access.”

Elan continued staring.

“Very quiet. Barely civilization at all.”

The music softened.

Dara drafted a careful letter to someone from whom she needed distance, time, and the right to choose the terms. The reply asked nothing she was not ready to give.

Corrin walked home with flour on her sleeves. Ishara returned not for business, but because she liked the tea and the household’s particular flavor of foolishness. People who had expected transactions found themselves becoming neighbors.

The narrator lowered his voice.

“We’re leaving that part alone.”

A beat.

“It knows exactly what it’s doing.”

The shop lights snapped bright again.

“Instead, keep count of—”

The numbers appeared on a chalkboard.

An unknown and apparently uncountable number of connected worlds.

Several miraculous alloys dismissed as ‘shop stuff.’

One suspicious investigator defeated by receipts.

Enough keychains to alter desert commerce.

At least one second moon shaped like a mouse.

And zero adults able to stop Jesse once he says, ‘It’s good business.’”

Starring:

Xander—as The Face He Gets When He Realizes They Accidentally Helped Everybody Again.

Jesse—as Unrepentant Capitalism, But Weirdly Community-Minded.

Willow—as Magical Research and Baked-Goods Escalation.

Amy—as Apprentice Machinist, Because Apparently the Shop Needed Another Prodigy.

Sharon—as The Increasingly Flexible Definition of Normal.

Dara—as Proof That Safety Can Be Quiet, Patient, and Chosen.

Corrin—as Teacher, Door Technician, and Volunteer Stabilizer of Everyone Else’s Nonsense.

Alberton—as The Scholar Who Absolutely Is Not Documenting Any of This, Especially Not in Detail.

Elan—as Operational Security’s Last Nerve.

Ishara—as Repeat Customer for Tea and Foolishness.

And the wardrobe—as Public Transportation With Better Woodwork.

The alternate titles assembled themselves from engraved plaques:

The Lion, the Witch, and the CNC Machine.

How to Train Your Supply Chain.

The Sisterhood of the Traveling Doorframe.

Fast & Furious: Tolerance Drift.

The narrator examined the last one.

“That is not what tolerance means.”

Jesse shrugged.

“It is now.”

The real title was carved into a wardrobe door.

# WARDROBES AND WOULD-WORK

Built by: two boys with a shop.

Expanded by: everybody they trusted with a door.

Held together by: precision fittings, chosen family, and the social force of feeding visitors.

The screen went dark, then returned for one final shot.

Autumn settled over the household. Willow and Dara bent over a new working. Amy stood at the lathe. Corrin laughed. The desert trunk waited quietly in its corner. Xander and Jesse watched the whole ordinary, impossible sprawl.

“It turns out,” said the narrator, “the most powerful thing in the shop was never the machine, the magic, or the door.”

Xander looked at his family.

“It was leaving room for one more person to come through.”
