Chapter 45

The college, when Dara finally saw it, was not what she'd been picturing.

She'd built some version of it in her head over the week of settling in and pancake-based recovery — towers, probably, or at least something suitably imposing, the kind of place a wizard who talked about founding institutions would build. What the hub door actually opened onto was a low stone building still half under construction, scaffolding along one wing, the smell of fresh-cut timber sharp in the morning air, and Alberton standing in the middle of it all in a leather apron dusted with sawdust, gesturing at a bewildered-looking mason with the particular intensity of a man who had strong opinions about load-bearing walls and no formal training in expressing them diplomatically.

"You're doing the joint wrong," Alberton was saying. "It needs to flex, not lock. If it locks, the first hard frost splits the stone."

"With respect," the mason said, in the weary tone of a man who had clearly had this exact argument several times already that morning, "I have built four colleges. This is not my first winter."

"And I have watched three enchantments fail because a stone mason thought locked was the same as strong," Alberton said. "Humor me. It costs you an afternoon. It saves us both a much worse spring."

The mason sighed the sigh of a professional conceding a point he still privately disagreed with, and turned to redo the joint, and Alberton spotted the hub door opening and lit up like a kid himself, delighted, the two weeks since he'd last seen anyone from the household clearly having felt much longer to him than to them.

"You're here! Come, come, mind the scaffolding, the east wing's not load-tested yet." He turned to Corrin, who'd come through holding Dara's hand, and gave a small, formal nod, the particular careful courtesy of an adult making sure another adult knew he understood whose responsibility the child in the room actually was. "Thank you for bringing her. I'll walk you both back myself when we're done, or send word if we run long."

"We're in no hurry," Corrin said, and gave Dara's hand a small squeeze.

Alberton caught sight of Dara's face, taking in the half-built walls and the exposed timber and the general chaos of active construction, and softened. "It doesn't look like much yet. It will. Give it a season."

"It's not what I expected," Dara admitted.

"Good," Alberton said. "Expectations are usually wrong, in my experience, and the disappointing kind of wrong at that." He clapped his hands together, sawdust scattering. "Now. Let's find you a proper spot to sit for your lessons, and then we'll talk about what exactly it is you know, because Kestrian's letter was long on urgency and remarkably short on specifics."

He led them through the half-finished building with the easy familiarity of a man who'd memorized every beam and gap in it through sheer repeated presence, narrating as he went — this would be the forge room, that the library, though the shelves were still just frames waiting for wood, this the courtyard where the actual working happened because indoor spaces made half the enchantment work unreliable for reasons nobody had ever satisfactorily explained to him.

"And this," he said, stopping at a small room near the back, bare stone walls, a single window, a low table with two stools already set up, "is where you'll have your lessons. Corrin can sit right here with you, or wait in the hall, whichever you'd both prefer. I didn't want you learning somewhere that felt like nowhere at all."

Dara looked at the little room, the low table, the light coming through the one window at an angle that would, she guessed, be good for close work in the mornings.

"You didn't have to do that," she said.

"I know," Alberton said, echoing Xander without either of them realizing it, the particular phrase that had apparently become load-bearing in its own right across two very different households. "I did it anyway."

Corrin set Dara's small satchel of tools down on the table, careful with it in a way that suggested she understood exactly what it represented, and settled onto the second stool to stay for the lesson.

"Now," Alberton said, sitting down carefully across from Dara, gentling his whole manner the way he did whenever he remembered, mid-sentence, that he was talking to a child and not a colleague. "Tell me about Meren, if you'd like to. Not the legend Kestrian's been fretting over — the actual woman who taught you. What did she teach you first?"

Dara opened the satchel, slow, and pulled out a chisel worn smooth along the handle from years of use, turning it over in her hands before she answered.

"Patience," she said. "She taught me patience first. Said a hub door doesn't care how badly you want it finished, it only cares whether you did each step right, and if you rush the seventh step because you're excited about the eighth, you'll be doing the whole thing over from the third."

Alberton went very still, the particular stillness of a man who'd just heard something far more significant than the words themselves accounted for.

"She taught you the steps," he said slowly. "Not just the idea of it. The actual order."

"Is that not normal?"

"Dara," Alberton said, careful and kind, "grown-ups all over this kingdom have been trying to piece that order back together for years, and mostly failing. You may have just made a great many very serious scholars' jobs extraordinarily difficult, without meaning to at all."

"Is that bad?"

"That," Alberton said, smiling now, "is the single best problem this college has had since I laid the first stone. Now — no more big scary talk for today. Corrin, is a story about carrots an acceptable way to end a first lesson?"

"It generally has been in the past," Corrin said, dry, and Dara, despite everything, laughed.
