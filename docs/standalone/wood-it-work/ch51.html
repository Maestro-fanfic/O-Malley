Chapter 51

Kestrian, when Alberton finally caught up with him three days later, listened to the whole account unreadable and still, the way career diplomats do when they're filing away a great deal more than their face lets on.

"Corrin," he said, when Alberton finished. "That's not a name I expected to hear said out loud in polite company."

"You know it," Alberton said, sitting forward. "Then you know something."

"I know a little," Kestrian said, choosing his words with visible care. "Enough to tell you this is not a simple errand of finding an old friend. Corrin was — is, if the sighting fifteen years back holds — considered dangerous by the crown. Not dangerous the way a soldier is dangerous. Dangerous the way an idea is dangerous, when the wrong people get hold of it."

"What idea?"

Kestrian was quiet for a moment, weighing something. "Meren and Corrin trained together, as your book said. What it didn't say, because scholars writing generations later rarely know the actual reasons behind old fallings-out, is what they disagreed about. Meren believed hub-work should stay small. Personal doors, quiet doors, the kind that connect one household to one other place and ask nothing more of the world than that. Corrin believed it should scale. Believed the old techniques, properly understood, could connect whole cities, whole kingdoms — and believed, further, that whoever controlled that network would hold power no crown in living memory has held."

The room went very quiet.

"That's not a scholarly disagreement," Willow said slowly. "That's a different thing entirely."

"It very much is," Kestrian agreed. "Meren, by every account I've ever heard, walked away entirely — vanished, built her small quiet life somewhere ordinary, taught what she taught to whoever needed it and nothing more. Corrin did not walk away. Corrin kept working, quietly, for years, on a scale nobody official was ever able to fully track, until fifteen years ago, when the trail simply stopped."

"Stopped how?" Dara asked, quiet, and Xander could hear the effort it took her to keep her voice steady.

"Nobody knows," Kestrian said, and there was something almost gentle in how he said it, an awareness of exactly what this meant to the girl standing in front of him. "That's the honest answer, not a diplomat's dodge of one. Corrin could be dead. Could have finally succeeded at something and be sitting on more power than any of us would be comfortable knowing about. Could simply have gone as quiet and careful as Meren did, for entirely different reasons. The crown has wondered about it for fifteen years without an answer, and I won't pretend I can hand you one now."

"But you'll look," Alberton said. It wasn't quite a question.

"I'll look," Kestrian confirmed. "Carefully. Quietly. Because if Corrin is alive and still working toward what Corrin was working toward fifteen years ago, my king would very much like advance warning of it — and because," he added, and here something warmer crept back into his voice, some of the formal caution easing, "because that girl standing there deserves better than a half-finished story about the woman who raised her, and I've grown rather fond of this household's habit of insisting on whole answers instead of convenient ones."

Dara let out a breath she seemed to have been holding for the entire conversation. "Thank you."

"Don't thank me yet," Kestrian said, echoing, unknowingly, half this household's favorite phrase. "This may take time, and it may end somewhere none of us particularly want it to end. I'd rather promise you honesty than false comfort."

"Honesty's fine," Dara said. "Honesty I can work with. It's the not-knowing that's hard."

"Then we'll trade the not-knowing for something, one way or another," Kestrian said, and for once, the careful diplomatic phrasing gave way to something that sounded, plainly, like a genuine promise.
