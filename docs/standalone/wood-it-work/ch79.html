Chapter 79

The message came through an old door nobody had opened in months — the trunk-shaped one, half-buried under spare tarps in the back of the hub, the one Willow had built to pour out either lake or ocean depending which way you turned it before lifting the lid.

It wasn't the trunk itself that moved. It was the small brass bell wired to its hinge, a signal Willow had rigged ages ago and mostly forgotten about, which had never once rung in all the time it had hung there — until, on an ordinary Thursday evening, it did.

"That's the desert bell," Willow said, going very still over the dishes she'd been drying. "That's never rung before."

"Meaning?" Xander asked.

"Meaning somebody on the other end wants to talk to us, and they know how to ring for it, which means it's not the friend we gave the chest to originally — he never learned the signal, we never taught him — which means somebody else over there knows this door exists."

Elan was already reaching for her coat. "Then we go carefully, and we go with grown-ups first."

The desert, when the trunk opened proper — turned upright, lid braced, Brack standing ready in case anything other than words came through — was exactly the kind of heat that made you understand, immediately and completely, why anyone living there would build their whole culture around not wasting a single drop of water.

Waiting on the other side, at a careful, deliberate distance, stood a small group of desert people wrapped head to foot in pale wrapped cloth, faces mostly hidden against the wind and sun, and at their center, looking considerably more nervous than any of them, was Tomas — the settler the household had gifted the water-chest to, years back, now weathered brown and lean in a way that made him almost unrecognizable.

"Xander," Tomas said, relief cracking his voice. "I didn't know if the bell would even work. I didn't know if anyone was still listening on your end."

"We're here," Xander said. "What's going on?"

Tomas glanced sideways at the wrapped figures beside him, and one of them — a woman, sun-lined, ancient eyes in an otherwise ageless face — stepped forward and pulled her wrap down enough to speak clearly.

"Your friend's water did not go unnoticed," she said, in careful, formal trade-tongue. "A settlement with an endless well, in country where wells are the difference between a people and a memory — word of that travels. We came to see for ourselves whether it was theft, miracle, or trap. We have watched him three seasons before approaching. We do not think it is a trap."

"It's not a trap," Elan said, stepping forward herself now, calm and even. "It was a gift. Given because he needed water and we happened to have some to spare. Nothing more complicated than that."

The woman studied her for a long moment — the same unhurried, cataloguing study Xander recognized from the scout with the binder of receipts, a lifetime ago. "Nothing is ever nothing more complicated than that," she said. "Not out here. A gift of water this size is a gift of power. Whoever controls it could starve a rival clan into the sand or raise one from nothing. We have come to understand your intentions before we decide whether your friend keeps his well, or whether it becomes something we must take from him for the safety of everyone else who depends on scarce water out here."

Tomas went pale. Elan didn't.

"Then let's talk about intentions properly," Elan said, "adult to adult, so nobody has to guess. My name is Elan. I speak for this household in matters like this one. What would put your people at ease?"

The woman considered her, then glanced past her — at Xander, at Willow, at Amy peering through the trunk's opening with open curiosity rather than fear — and something in her weathered face eased, fractionally.

"Your children are not afraid of us," she observed. "That tells me something too."

"They trust the grown-ups around them to handle the parts that could go wrong," Elan said. "That's not the same as not understanding the stakes."

The woman nodded slowly, apparently satisfied by that answer in a way she hadn't expected to be. "I am Ishara, of the deep-sand clans. We do not ask you to take the well back. We ask instead for a treaty — that access to it be shared fairly among the clans, overseen jointly, rather than left in the hands of one settler who could, however unwittingly, become a target for it. We would rather build something durable together than let scarcity turn a gift into a war."

"That sounds," Elan said, glancing back at Tomas, who nodded fast and grateful, "like exactly the kind of arrangement we'd be glad to help build. Though I'll admit, we're going to need someone who actually understands your water customs properly, because I suspect we'd get the details wrong on our own and cause exactly the harm you're worried about."

"I can teach," Ishara said. "If you are willing to learn properly, and not simply take what looks useful and discard the rest."

"We're generally pretty good at the properly part," Xander offered. "Ask anyone we've ever traded aluminum with."

Ishara's eyes moved to him, sharp and assessing, and something that might almost have been amusement crossed her sun-worn face. "The aluminum boy," she said. "Your reputation reaches even the deep sand, small as it is. I did not expect you to be quite so small."

"I get that a lot," Xander said, unbothered.

Somewhere out past the edge of the gathering, low and enormous, the ground shuddered — once, twice, a long rolling tremor that sent a spray of sand hissing up from a dune half a mile off, and every single one of the desert people went instantly, perfectly still, hands raised in a shared, practiced gesture Xander didn't recognize but understood immediately as don't move, don't speak, wait.

"What is that," Amy whispered, eyes huge.

"Wait," Ishara said, very quiet, very calm, not remotely as alarmed as the tremor seemed to warrant. "It is only passing. They rarely surface near a gathering this size. Too many voices, too much noise on the sand." She waited another few seconds, listening to something none of the household could hear, and then relaxed all at once, the whole group easing with her like a single held breath let go. "It has gone. You may speak again."

"Was that," Xander said carefully, "a worm?"

"You have heard of them," Ishara said, surprised.

"We've read about them," Willow said. "In a book. About a desert kind of like this one."

"A book," Ishara repeated, something dry and knowing flickering behind her eyes, as though she'd met exactly this reaction from exactly this kind of outsider before and found it endlessly, quietly funny. "Of course you have. Everyone who visits the deep sand for the first time seems to have read some book about us." She shook her head, not unkindly. "The old ones are dangerous, yes, but they are not monsters looking to hunt you. They are simply enormous, and simply old, and simply there, the same as any weather. Respect the sand, and the sand mostly leaves you be."

"Good policy," Brack said, from where he'd been standing quiet and watchful the entire negotiation, visibly recalibrating his risk assessment of the afternoon by several notches.

"We should discuss terms properly," Elan said, steering things gently back. "Somewhere out of this heat, ideally, before anyone melts."

"There is shade, an hour's walk," Ishara said. "Or," she added, glancing at the water-chest still propped open behind them, glinting cool and impossibly out of place against the endless dry gold of the dunes, "you could simply bring us through your door, and we could discuss this somewhere considerably less likely to try to kill either of us."

Elan considered that, glanced at Xander, at Willow, at the whole strange, improbable shape of what this household kept turning into, one open door at a time.

"I think," she said, "that could be arranged."
