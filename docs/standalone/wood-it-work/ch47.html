Chapter 47

Dinner at Sharon's went, against every reasonable expectation, well.

Elan arrived in a plain dress Willow had helped her pick out — nothing that would draw a second look, nothing that would draw a first one either, which had apparently been the entire point — and spent the first twenty minutes doing something Xander hadn't quite seen her do before: asking Sharon's mother question after genuine question, about her job, about the neighborhood, about how she'd met Sharon's father, listening to each answer with the same total, unhurried attention she gave a king's herald or a suspicious scout, as though there were no more important business in the world that evening than the specific details of one ordinary woman's ordinary life.

"You're very easy to talk to," Sharon's mother said, partway through the second course, sounding faintly surprised by her own observation. "Sharon said you were shy. You're not shy."

"I'm careful," Elan said. "It looks similar from the outside, I think, but it isn't quite the same thing."

"Careful how?"

"I come from a family that valued listening more than speaking," Elan said, which was, Sharon thought, watching from across the table with her heart in her throat, both completely true and completely useless as actual information. "It's served me well. People tell you a great deal, if you let them, and rarely regret having told you, provided you've actually listened rather than merely waited for your turn."

Sharon's mother laughed, delighted. "That's a very grown-up way of putting it."

"I've had good teachers," Elan said, and Xander, biting back a smile over his plate, thought that particular phrase was getting a genuine workout across every corner of this whole strange, expanding, thoroughly adopted family.

By dessert, Sharon's mother had extracted a promise that Elan would come to Thanksgiving, had insisted on sending her home with a container of leftovers "because you look like you could use a good meal, sweetheart," and had pulled Sharon aside in the kitchen, ostensibly to help with dishes, actually to deliver a verdict.

"I like her," she said, quiet, drying a plate with more focus than the task strictly required. "I don't fully understand what she is to you kids — and honey, I know there's more to it, I've been your mother for sixteen years, I can tell when there's more to it — but she clearly looks out for the lot of you, and I trust you enough not to push on the rest of it unless you need me to."

Sharon's throat went tight in a way she hadn't expected. "Thanks, Mom."

"Don't thank me. Just don't make me find out you all needed real looking-after from somebody else, someday, when it matters." Her mother set the dried plate down, met Sharon's eyes, serious now, underneath the warmth. "That's the whole ask. That's it."

"Okay," Sharon said, and meant it, even though she had no earthly idea, yet, how she'd ever manage to keep that particular promise if the day ever actually came.

Out in the living room, Elan was letting Sharon's little brother show her his entire collection of toy dinosaurs, one at a time, with the same unhurried, total attention she'd given his mother all evening, and something in Sharon's chest loosened at the sight of it — not relief exactly, something steadier than relief, something closer to the comfort of watching two entirely separate worlds fit together at the seams without either one having to break to manage it.
