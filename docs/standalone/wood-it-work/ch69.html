Chapter 69

The Great Bee Incident, as it came to be known, started with Jesse's spring keychain line and ended, three weeks later, with an actual, honest-to-goodness beekeeper showing up at the shop gate looking deeply confused.

"Somebody told me you sell bees," the man said, adjusting a hat that had clearly been designed for considerably more serious apiary business than a conversation at a car lot.

"We sell bee-shaped keychains," Xander clarified, wiping his hands. "Aluminum. About an inch long. Not, uh. Not live bees."

"Ah." The man looked deeply relieved and faintly disappointed at the same time, a combination Xander hadn't previously known a human face could achieve. "My nephew's been asking for weeks. Said his friend had a metal bee. I thought — well. I thought there'd been some kind of local livestock innovation I hadn't heard about."

"There has not," Xander said. "There will not be. We're a machine shop."

"Shame," the man said, entirely sincere. "Real bees have been having a rough season. Wouldn't have minded the competition being metal for a while."

He left with three keychains for his nephew and, somehow, an extended conversation with Willow about pollinator decline that resulted in Willow spending the following two evenings drafting an actual, serious proposal for a tiny magically-assisted garden bed behind the shop, "for the real bees, not the fake ones, though I do think the fake ones have been unfairly maligned in this whole conversation."

"You're defending the honor of a keychain," Jesse said, watching her sketch out planting rows with more seriousness than the subject strictly required.

"I'm defending pollinator solidarity," Willow said. "It's a real thing. Look it up."

"I don't need to look it up, you're saying it directly to my face right now."

"Then believe it directly to your face," Willow said, unbothered, and three days later there was, in fact, a small pollinator garden taking root behind the shop, tended mostly by Amy, who'd apparently decided that "finding her thing" could reasonably include "growing flowers that make actual bees happy while also selling their aluminum cousins for a dollar fifty apiece," a business model Sharon declared, with real admiration, "unreasonably wholesome, honestly, borderline suspicious."
