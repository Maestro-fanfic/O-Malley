Chapter 50

The letter arrived on a Tuesday, carried not by Kestrian but by a runner none of them recognized, a boy of maybe fourteen who looked like he'd walked considerably farther than any fourteen-year-old should have to and was doing his level best not to show it.

"For the household of the door," he said, formal and rehearsed, clearly a line he'd been given rather than one he'd have chosen himself. "From the college. Urgent, they said."

Willow took the letter, thanked him, offered him water and a seat on the porch steps while he caught his breath, and he accepted both looking faintly stunned, like he hadn't expected kindness to be part of the delivery.

The letter was in Alberton's handwriting, cramped and urgent in a way his usual notes weren't.

Willow — Need you and Dara both, as soon as you're able, with a grown-up along for Dara as always. Found something in the old college archives, a reference to a second hub-work practitioner from Meren's generation, still living, or was as of the last confirmed sighting some years back. If accurate, this changes everything about what we can verify versus what we're reconstructing from one child's memory alone, no offense meant to Dara, who has been extraordinary, but corroboration matters and I would like her to have it. Kestrian is making inquiries through official channels but official channels move slowly and I don't entirely trust that this person, if they're findable at all, wants to be found by anyone official. Come when you can. Bring Xander if he'll come — I may need someone who thinks like a craftsman rather than a scholar, and he's better at that than any of us. — A.

Willow read it twice, then handed it to Xander, who read it once and looked up flat, quiet, already recalculating his entire evening.

"A second person," Xander said. "Who knows the old hub-work. Who might not want to be found."

"That's what it says," Willow said.

"That sounds like exactly the kind of thing that goes fine right up until it very suddenly doesn't."

"Probably," Willow agreed. "Are you coming anyway?"

Xander thought about Dara, three weeks into finally feeling like she belonged somewhere, finally starting to look less like a girl bracing for the floor to drop out from under her and more like a girl standing on solid ground. He thought about what it might mean for her — for all of them, really, but mostly for her — if there really was somebody else out there who'd known Meren, who could tell Dara things about her teacher that Dara herself had never had the chance to learn.

"Yeah," he said. "I'm coming."

Corrin came too, of course — nobody so much as suggested Dara go through the hub door without her these days — and they found Dara in the little lesson room at the college when they arrived, chalk dust on her hands as usual, hunched over a diagram with Alberton pacing behind her, too keyed up to sit still.

"Show them," Alberton said, the moment they walked in, gesturing at an open book on the desk, ancient and water-stained, clearly pulled from somewhere deep in the college archives that hadn't seen daylight in a long while. "This. Page forty-one. Third paragraph."

Willow leaned over the book, Xander reading over her shoulder, and found the passage — a brief, scholarly aside in a much longer, drier text about hub-door theory, mentioning almost in passing that the practitioner known as Corrin, last confirmed active in the northern territories some fifteen years past, was said to have trained alongside Meren of the vanished school before the two parted ways over what the sources describe only as "a difference in what the work was for."

"A difference in what the work was for," Willow read aloud, slow. "That's ominous."

"It could mean anything," Alberton said, though the way he said it suggested he didn't entirely believe his own reassurance. "Scholarly disagreements. A falling out over methodology. It doesn't have to mean anything dramatic."

"You don't sound convinced," Xander said.

"I'm not," Alberton admitted. "But I'd rather find out what it means from Corrin directly than spend a month guessing at it from one water-stained sentence in a book nobody's opened in a generation."

Dara had gone very quiet, staring at the passage with an expression Xander couldn't quite read — hope and dread tangled together, the look of someone being offered a piece of a person she'd lost, with no way of knowing yet whether the piece would comfort her or hurt worse than the not-knowing had.

"Do you think they're still alive?" she asked, quiet.

"I don't know," Alberton said, gentler now, some of the scholarly excitement dialing back in favor of something plainer. "Fifteen years is a long time. But I think it's worth finding out, if you want to know. Nobody's making you chase this if you'd rather not."

Dara was quiet for a long moment, tracing one finger along the edge of the old book without quite touching the page.

"I want to know," she said finally. "Whatever it is. I've spent my whole life knowing about half of everything — half of who Meren was, half of what she taught me, half of why she sent me through that door instead of just telling me the truth herself while she still could. I'm tired of half of things." She looked up, steadier now, some of the dread folding into something more like resolve. "If there's a whole answer somewhere, even a hard one, I want it."

"Then we'll find it," Willow said, quiet and certain, and Xander, watching the four of them — Willow's steady conviction, Alberton's restless scholarly hunger, Dara's hard-won resolve — thought that whatever this Corrin turned out to be, whatever the difference in what the work was for had actually meant, this household had gotten remarkably good, over the last several months, at walking straight into hard answers instead of around them.

"Okay," he said. "Where do we start looking?"

"That," Alberton said, closing the old book with careful hands, "is the part I haven't solved yet. But I know someone who might have."

"Kestrian," Willow guessed.

"Kestrian," Alberton confirmed. "Officially, he can't chase down someone who deliberately vanished fifteen years ago without cause. Unofficially, I suspect he'll find the cause the moment I explain whose old friend we're looking for."
