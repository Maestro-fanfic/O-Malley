What Gets Built

The new charter didn't have a proper name yet, three full months in, which Giles had come to suspect was less an oversight than a quiet, collective admission that nobody actually involved had fully decided yet what they were building, or what to call something still so visibly in progress.

He'd been asked to lead it. Twice, formally — once by a State Department liaison with a folder full of language he found almost aggressively bloodless, all passive voice and careful hedging, and once, more meaningfully, by a handwritten note from the Queen's own office that simply read: You have earned the right to refuse this. I hope, very much, that you won't.

He hadn't refused. He had, however, insisted on terms that nobody at the first proper meeting had expected a former field Watcher to insist on quite so bluntly.

"No Potentials monitored without their families' full knowledge and consent," he said, standing at the head of a conference room table in a building that had, until recently, belonged to a Council that no longer existed in any meaningful sense. "No removal from home without genuine consent past the age of majority, and even then, only with complete transparency about what's being asked of them and why. Slayers receive proper compensation, proper ongoing medical care, proper psychological support... actual, sustained support, not a Watcher unilaterally deciding what a seventeen-year-old girl needs on her behalf without ever asking her directly. And Buffy Summers sits on the founding board, not as a symbol, not as a figurehead, as a genuine voting member, with real authority to veto anything that treats a Slayer as an asset rather than as a person first."

"That's a considerable amount of structural change," the liaison said, carefully, glancing down at notes that clearly hadn't anticipated any of this, "for an organization that hasn't technically finished being founded yet, Mr. Giles."

"Yes," Giles agreed, entirely unmoved. "That's rather the point of insisting on it now, at the very start, rather than later. I'd like it built correctly the first time, given how spectacularly badly it was built the last time around, and given what that failure ultimately cost."

They agreed to all of it eventually, in no small part because the alternative, explaining to a president who had thrown one senator bodily out of his office over this exact subject why the new organization looked, even slightly, suspiciously similar to the old one, held absolutely no appeal whatsoever for anyone seated at that table.

A man near the far end of it, a career administrator named Colston, inherited from the old Council's finance office, competent enough at his actual job and utterly tone-deaf about everything else, made the mistake, on the second day, of referring to Buffy, in her own absence, as "the primary asset" during a budget discussion.

Giles didn't raise his voice. He set his pen down, very precisely, in the exact center of the folder in front of him, and looked at Colston for a long moment before he spoke.

"Say that again," he said, quietly, "and mean it the way you just meant it, and I will personally ensure you never work in any capacity that puts you near a Slayer again, for the rest of your professional life. I am not being dramatic. I am telling you, plainly, what will happen." He picked the pen back up, and his voice returned, instantly, to its usual mild register, as though nothing at all had happened. "Now. Shall we continue with the budget?"

Nobody at that table asked Colston what, exactly, he'd heard in Giles's voice that had made him go so pale. Nobody needed to ask. Colston himself adjusted his vocabulary within the week, and stayed, carefully, on his very best behavior for the whole of the following month.

Buffy attended her first board meeting with no ceremony made of her at all, no formal robes offered or requested, just the same plain, well-worn suit she wore every other day, deliberately unremarkable, and said almost nothing for the first full hour, watching the shape of the conversation the way she'd once watched a fight before deciding where to throw the first punch.

"You've been quiet," Giles observed, during the first break, pouring tea nobody in the room but him actually seemed to want.

"I'm figuring out if this is actually different, or if it's just better-dressed than the last version." She accepted a cup anyway, mostly for something to hold onto while she thought. "So far it's — better. Not perfect, not by a long shot. There's a guy down at the end of the table, Colston, I think... everybody keeps going quiet whenever he opens his mouth, like they're waiting to see if he's learned his lesson yet."

"I noticed that too, as it happens."

"You gonna say something?"

"I have," Giles said, with the dry satisfaction of a man who had genuinely enjoyed doing so more than he was letting on. "He'll either adjust his vocabulary considerably, or he'll find employment somewhere rather less consequential. I find people generally adjust their language quite quickly, once they properly understand the alternative being offered them."

Something in Buffy eased at that, warm and real, a small huff of genuine amusement carrying where a smile no longer could. "You've gotten scarier since Halloween. I mean that as a compliment, mostly."

"I've gotten more honest," Giles corrected, gently. "I find I no longer have much patience left for pretending otherwise, for anyone's comfort. Yours included, though I hope you know I still hold your comfort in rather higher regard than most people's in that room."

"I'm not complaining." Buffy meant it. "I think I need someone like this now, running things. Someone who isn't gonna let it turn back into what it used to be, just because being polite about it would be easier for everyone in the short term."

"Then we're in complete agreement," Giles said, "which is, I confess, a rather novel enough sensation between the two of us that I intend to enjoy it thoroughly while it lasts."

General Hammond made his first visit to Sunnydale in person a week later, on the reasonable grounds that a man who had spent three months authorizing missions to a town he'd never actually set foot in had, by his own private accounting, let that arrangement run on rather longer than it should have.

He was, by nature and by four decades of practiced discipline, not an easy man to rattle. He had stood in front of live Goa'uld motherships, briefed presidents on threats that would have driven lesser men out of the service entirely, and buried more good soldiers than he ever discussed with anyone outside his own quiet hours. He had, in short, seen a great deal.

He had not seen this.

O'Neill, trailing a half-step behind him down the ramp specifically so he could watch this exact moment happen, would later describe Hammond's face in that first instant as "a man doing long division in his own head, badly, on live television."

Destiny's shuttle bay swallowed the transport plane whole, and Hammond stood at the base of the ramp for a long moment, hands clasped behind his back in the parade rest that had become, decades ago, his own substitute for standing still and simply staring, and said absolutely nothing at all.

"General," Xander said, coming to meet him, suddenly, uncomfortably aware of how a teenager in a borrowed hoodie must look welcoming a two-star general aboard an Ancient warship. "Welcome aboard, sir. I know it's a lot."

"Son," Hammond said, after a silence that stretched several seconds longer than protocol strictly demanded, "I have read every report that has crossed my desk about this vessel for the past three months, and I want you to know, man to man, that not one single word of any of them properly prepared me for actually standing inside it."

"Yeah, that tracks. Everybody says that."

"I imagine they do." Hammond's eyes moved slowly across the bay — the scale of it, the alien architecture, a shuttle craft resting nearby that belonged to no engineering tradition Earth had ever produced. He took his own sweet time about it, the way a man does when he intends to remember every detail properly rather than simply absorb the shock and move past it. "I've spent thirty years learning not to let my face show what I'm actually thinking, Mr. Harris. I'd like you to understand that keeping that discipline right now is taking me considerably more effort than it generally does."

When Buffy was introduced to him a short while later, sealed visor and all, Hammond extended his hand exactly the way he'd have extended it to anyone else, gave it precisely one extra second of stillness once the shape of what he was shaking hands with had properly registered, and did not let go early.

"Ms. Summers," he said, evenly. "It's an honor. I mean that plainly, and I'd ask you to believe I don't say things like that when I don't mean them."

"Thank you, sir." Something in Buffy's posture eased, fractionally, at the steadiness of it — no flinch, no visible effort to perform composure, just a man absorbing something enormous the only way three decades of discipline had ever taught him how: quietly, completely, and without ever once looking away.

He toured the ship for the better part of two hours, asked sharp, specific questions of Rush that left even Rush grudgingly impressed by the second one, and said, on his way back down the ramp at the end of it, to no one in particular, in a voice pitched just quiet enough that only Xander and O'Neill caught it properly:

"I am going to need a rather stiff drink tonight, and I am going to need several people not to ask me why."

O'Neill waited until the general was safely out of earshot, watched him go with something that might, on a lesser man, have been called genuine tenderness, and turned to Xander with the deeply satisfied expression of a man who had just witnessed a personal historical event.

"You understand what you just saw," he said. "That man briefed three separate presidents on Goa'uld invasion contingencies without so much as a raised eyebrow. I have personally watched him stare down a sarcophagus-revived System Lord without blinking. The unflappable," O'Neill said, with real, quiet reverence, "has been flapped. I'm gonna remember this day for the rest of my natural life."

Willow came to exactly one board meeting, sat through exactly forty minutes of it, and left in the middle of a discussion about standardized power-assessment protocols for newly Called Potentials — a phrase that had made something in her chest go cold and tight the moment it was said out loud.

"They want a scale," she told Nada, later, walking fast down a hallway that smelled like new carpet and old bureaucracy in equal measure. "A number. One to ten, how dangerous is this kid's magic, how much of a resource is she, like it's — like it's inventory."

It is an old habit, Nada said, unhurried, matching her pace without needing feet to do it. Measuring a thing is often the first step toward owning it. Small ones do this instinctively, even the well-meaning ones. It rarely occurs to them that some things are better left unmeasured.

"I could fix it," Willow said, and heard, as she said it, exactly how much that sentence cost her to say out loud. "I could just... go in there, make everyone see it the way I see it, no scales, no numbers, done. It'd take ten minutes."

You could, Nada agreed, entirely without judgment. You have that in you now, and will for the rest of your life, however long that turns out to be. The question was never whether you could, small one. It has only ever been whether you will let yourself want to.

Willow stopped walking. Stood there a long moment in the new-carpet hallway, breathing, feeling the old hunger sit quiet and patient somewhere just under her ribs, exactly where it always sat now, exactly where it would probably always sit from here on.

"I'm not gonna fix it that way," she said, finally, mostly to herself. "I'm gonna go back in there and just... say what I think, out loud, like a person, and let them actually listen or not listen on their own. That's slower. That's so much slower."

Yes, Nada said, and there was something, underneath the ancient patience, that might have been the closest thing to pride either of them had a proper word for yet. It is. I find that is generally how the things worth doing tend to go.

She went back in. She said her piece, plainly, without a trace of anything but her own voice behind it, and the standardized scale got quietly shelved by the end of the meeting, not because anyone had been made to believe anything, but because enough people in that room had actually, finally listened.

Giles caught her on her way out that evening, both of them the last to leave, and fell into step beside her without asking.

"You did well in there today," he said.

"I almost didn't." Willow shrugged, a little sheepish. "I almost just fixed it the easy way."

"But you didn't."

"But I didn't." She looked over at him, something steady in her own face now that hadn't quite been there a few months ago. "I'm trying to get good at the harder way. Turns out it takes practice, same as anything."

"Most worthwhile things do," Giles said, and they walked the rest of the way out together, in the kind of comfortable quiet that no longer needed filling.

✳

She went back to the Anomaly two days later, properly, for the first time since that early conversation with Polo just inside the shadow-doorway, not because Nada had asked her to, and not because anything was wrong, but because Polo had promised, months ago now, that the introductions would come in their own time, and Willow had decided, somewhere in the last week, that their own time had finally arrived.

Polo met her at the landing pad with the same undimmed delight as always, talking before she'd fully stepped off the platform. "You've come to meet the others! Wonderful, wonderful, I did wonder how long it would take you, though I confess I never doubted it would happen eventually. Come, come, there is someone in I think you'll enjoy."

The someone turned out to be a small, precise figure tucked into an alcove near a ramp, all polished metal and quick, birdlike movements, surrounded by a floating display of items that shimmered faintly even in the station's already-strange light.

"This is the Quicksilver Synthesis Companion," Polo said, with the pride of an inventor introducing his own creation. "Though the small ones who visit tend to simply call it Quincy, which it has decided, after some consideration, that it rather likes."

"A being fashioned from metal and soul," the Companion said, in a voice that clicked and hummed at the edges, turning to regard Willow with what might have been genuine curiosity, "by Polo's own paws, no less, though I've always suspected he undersells his own cleverness in the telling."

"I do not undersell it," Polo said, wounded. "I am simply modest."

"You are never simply anything," Quincy said, and something in the exchange had the easy, well-worn rhythm of two people who had been quietly ribbing each other for a very long time.

Willow found herself laughing, real and unguarded, the first properly light moment she'd had in days. "Okay, I like him."

"Most do, eventually," Polo said. "He grows on people. Like a very well-mannered fungus."

"I am not a fungus," Quincy said, with what could only be described as dignity, and Willow laughed again.

They walked further in after that, Polo narrating as they went — a dozen other Travellers scattered through the station's wide, mismatched halls, each caught up in their own quiet business, none of them in any hurry to be introduced all at once. A small, strange creature Polo called Cronus wound briefly around Willow's ankles before losing interest and wandering off toward the Nexus. Somewhere deeper in, behind a door Polo didn't offer to open yet, something hummed with the patient, contained energy of a whole simulated star system, saved once from a destruction Willow didn't ask about and Polo didn't volunteer.

"It's a lot," Willow admitted, standing in the middle of it all, feeling the scale of the place settle over her properly for the first time.

It is, Nada agreed, quiet, warm, present in the way she'd come to recognize as entirely her own. It took me a very long time to think of all of it as home rather than simply as somewhere I happened to be standing. I do not expect it to take you any less time. I only wanted you to know, finally, that you are welcome to start.

"Thanks," Willow said, out loud, to Nada, to Polo, to Quincy still muttering indignantly behind them about fungus. "I think I'm actually ready to."
