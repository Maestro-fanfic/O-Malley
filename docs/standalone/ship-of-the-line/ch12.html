When the President Stands

Kinsey arrived expecting leverage and found, instead, a room that had decided how this meeting was going to go.

He noticed the two Secret Service agents posted just outside the Oval on his way in, standing at a distance and in a configuration that struck him, in the brief half-second he spared it, as slightly unusual — not the ordinary rotation, positioned closer to the door than protocol generally called for — and dismissed the thought almost as quickly as he'd had it, already rehearsing his opening line, already certain of exactly how this meeting was going to go.

Leo was there, standing near the window rather than sitting, arms loosely crossed. Abbey was there too, standing slightly apart from everyone else, arms crossed in a mirror of Leo's own posture, saying nothing yet, which somehow made her presence more unsettling than if she'd spoken the moment Kinsey walked through the door. Sam stood near the window with the stillness of a man holding back words he'd clearly prepared in advance and clearly intended to use, given even the smallest opening.

"Mr. President." Kinsey's smile was working, reaching for the register that had carried him through a hundred smaller confrontations like this one. "I appreciate you making the time for this. I assume it's about the O'Neill matter—"

"Sit down, Bob."

Kinsey sat, a half-second slower than he'd have liked, something in Bartlet's voice telling him this wasn't going to unfold the way he'd planned it on the drive over.

"I've read your file," Bartlet said, not sitting himself, standing instead behind his own desk with both hands flat against it. "All of it. Every supplementary request, every subpoena, every quiet insinuation you've personally floated to at least three different reporters who were kind enough, when my press office called to fact-check, to mention exactly whose name kept coming up." He let that land, watched it land. Kinsey's smile faltered, just fractionally, at the corners. "You built a scandal out of a genuine mystery, Bob, because a mystery doesn't get you anything politically and a scandal might. And in the process, you spent six weeks of federal resources trying to destroy two good men's careers over something neither one of them actually did... and did not, at any point in those six weeks, have the basic human decency to notice, or care, that the real story sitting underneath all of it is about a seventeen-year-old girl who's been carrying a war the rest of us never even knew existed, entirely alone, for years, with nobody's help at all."

"Mr. President, with respect, I don't see how one teenager's personal circumstances have any bearing on legitimate oversight of—"

"When the President stands," Bartlet said, standing straighter now, though he'd never actually sat, "nobody sits."

Kinsey stood anyway, fast, an old, deep reflex from a lifetime spent in rooms with clear hierarchies, before his own sharper political instincts caught up and told him, a half-second too late, exactly how badly that reflex had just cost him in front of everyone watching.

"You had nothing," Bartlet said, quiet now, colder than shouting would ever have managed to be. "Six weeks. A subpoena. Three off-the-record calls to reporters who talk to my staff more than they talk to yours, apparently. And at the very end of all of it, you have nothing, Bob, because there was never anything real to have. What you actually had, the whole time, was a genuine opportunity to be the first person in this entire government's history to properly notice what's been happening in that town, and to ask, seriously, what we might do to help. You chose a witch hunt instead. I want you to sit with that."

"This investigation is closed," Bartlet said. "Effective immediately, as of this conversation. But we're not finished, Bob, not by a considerable distance, because closing it isn't the same as forgiving how you conducted it." He didn't raise his voice. He didn't need to. "You leaked details of a classified operation to three separate reporters for personal political advantage. That's unauthorized disclosure of classified national security information, Bob, which is a federal crime, and I want to be very clear that I am not speaking hypothetically when I use that phrase. You used subpoena power that exists for legitimate oversight to conduct what amounts to a harassment campaign against a seventeen-year-old girl who has never once, in her entire life, been given a say in any of this. And you did all of it either fully aware that you were spending a child as currency in your own reelection campaign, or willfully unaware of it, which I actually find worse, Bob. Not knowing isn't an accident when you've spent this much effort making sure you never had to."

Abbey didn't say anything. She simply looked at Kinsey, level and unhurried, for exactly long enough to let the silence itself do the work Bartlet had left open for her, and that silence, somehow, landed worse than any line she could have delivered.

"Your subpoena power on this committee is finished," Bartlet said, quieter now, the fury banking into something colder and considerably more final. "Your chairmanship is finished. And the Justice Department will be in touch shortly, because unauthorized disclosure of classified information carries real weight, Bob, even for a sitting Senator, and I intend to see every ounce of it applied."

Kinsey opened his mouth, some last, doomed instinct for the last word rising up out of pure habit, and got exactly one syllable out before Bartlet's temper finally, fully broke its banks.

"Get this man out of my house!" he snapped, on his feet now, one hand slicing through the air toward the door.

The two agents from the hallway were inside the room before the sentence had finished leaving his mouth, moving with the specific, well-drilled efficiency of people who had clearly been briefed, in advance, on exactly which moment they'd be needed. Kinsey found himself airborne between them almost immediately, feet barely troubling the carpet, propelled toward the door with a speed and a total absence of ceremony that made Sam, watching from the window, briefly, helplessly bite down on a laugh he knew better than to let escape out loud.

"Senator." Abbey's voice caught him just before he cleared the threshold, sweet as anything, sharp enough underneath to draw blood. "Do give my best to whoever writes your concession speech. I imagine they're going to have considerably more time to work on it than either of you originally planned."

Kinsey didn't manage a response to that either, still moving fast, feet finally finding the floor again somewhere out in the hallway, where a second pair of agents was already waiting, and where the small federal seal on the nearer one's jacket read Bureau rather than Service.

"Robert Kinsey," the lead agent said, already reaching for his own restraints, "you're under arrest for unauthorized disclosure of classified information. You have the right to remain silent—"

Whatever fight had been left in him after the first ejection didn't survive the second one. He went still, went quiet, and let the agents take him by each arm, the particular, total stillness of a man who had only ever performed strength and had just, very publicly, run out of the performance.

The door closed with a soft, unhurried click, considerably quieter than the sound of an entire life permanently upending, though everyone still standing in that office understood perfectly well the two sounds had just become the same thing. The story that moved through Washington over the following weeks was not, in the end, about a scandal at all... it was about a Senator who had walked into the Oval Office fully expecting to win, and had instead been physically thrown out of it before being formally arrested in the hallway outside, in front of witnesses who would remember, for a very long time, exactly how thoroughly, how publicly, and how permanently, he'd lost it.

Sam let out a breath once the door had closed, the first easy movement his shoulders had made in the entire meeting. "I've wanted to see that happen since the first week I read the file," he admitted.

"Don't get used to it," Leo said, dry, gathering his own notes. "That was a once-a-term event, if we're lucky."

"Worth it," Bartlet said, sitting back down properly now, some of the tightness finally easing out of his jaw. "Worth every bit of the sleep I lost over it. Leo — the girl and the kid are coming in this afternoon, aren't they? I want a few minutes to actually think about what I want to say to them, rather than just running on whatever's left of this."

"This afternoon," Leo confirmed. "You'll have your few minutes."

He didn't, quite, in the end. The day never gave anyone in that building quite as much room as they asked for, but by the time Xander and Buffy were shown into the Oval a few hours later, whatever fury still lived in Bartlet's chest had settled into something quieter, ready to make room for whoever walked through that door next.

Mrs. Landingham herself brought them up from the lobby, a duty she generally delegated to junior staff without a second thought, and which she had, on this occasion, quietly reserved for herself the moment she'd read the visitor list. She had, over the course of a great many years in that building, personally walked kings, dictators, and no small number of overconfident young senators past her own desk without her expression so much as flickering, and she held that same composure admirably right up until the exact moment Buffy turned to thank her for the escort, sealed visor and all, and something in Mrs. Landingham's perfectly arranged face genuinely, visibly slipped.

It was gone again almost as fast as it had come — replaced, briskly, by the same brand of warm, unhurried care she extended to absolutely everyone who walked through that lobby, teenager or head of state alike — but Xander caught it, and filed it away, and made a private note to mention it to precisely no one, because some things, he was beginning to understand, a person earned the right to recover from in private.

"Right this way," was all she said, voice not wavering by so much as a syllable, though her hand did find the strand of pearls at her throat, briefly, before she seemed to catch herself and let it go. "The President's expecting you both. And for what it's worth, dear," she added, to Buffy specifically, gentler now, "you'll find he's kinder than the building makes him look."

✳

Xander had not, in either of his two sets of memories, ever once prepared for the specific experience of meeting the sitting President of the United States, and it showed the moment he walked through the door.

"Oh my god," he said, the second he was actually standing inside the Oval Office, all composure gone entirely, all of Eli's careful borrowed competence apparently taking an extended, well-earned coffee break somewhere else entirely. "Oh my god, this is the actual — this is the actual room, I've seen pictures, this is insane, I'm so sorry, I know I'm supposed to be composed right now, there's probably a whole etiquette thing I should know, but I did not prepare for the room to be this round—"

"It's an oval, technically," Bartlet said, and the sheer, unguarded delight spreading across his face was immediate and real, the delight of a man who spent most of his days managing composed, careful adults, suddenly confronted with someone completely incapable of performing anything at all. "Most people don't actually notice that on the first pass. You noticed the roundness."

"I notice a lot of things when I'm having a nervous breakdown, sir, it's kind of my whole thing lately—"

"Sir," Bartlet repeated, delighted further still. "I like him," he said, to no one in particular, to the room at large. "Leo. I like him."

"Everybody likes him, Mr. President," Leo said, dry, arms still crossed by the window. "That appears to be rather a significant part of the problem, from where I'm standing."

Whatever levity the room had briefly found didn't survive Buffy's arrival a moment later.

Nothing in the briefing folders had properly prepared any of them for it: Bartlet had read the words no longer entirely human a dozen times over the past week without once translating them into an actual, standing, breathing person, and the gap between the phrase and the reality of a sealed visor with something like slow starlight moving behind the dark glass hit the room like a physically felt thing.

"Jesus—" Josh's hand found the edge of his chair without his quite deciding to reach for it, the word out before he could stop it, low and involuntary, and it was followed almost immediately by a second, considerably less contained "Okay, that is — somebody want to tell me why nobody put that in the briefing, because I read the briefing twice and the briefing did not prepare me for—" before Leo shot him a look sharp enough to actually stop him mid-sentence.

Toby, for his part, made it exactly as far as standing very still, mouth falling open half an inch before he caught it, and then undid all of that composure in a single stride by muttering, not quite as quietly as he clearly intended, "That's not in the file. I read the file. That is not what was in the file," to nobody in particular, over and over, like repeating it might eventually make it stop being true.

Sam Seaborn, to his credit, recovered fastest, though even he needed a visible beat, and one sharp, audible breath through his nose, before he managed to arrange his own face into something other than open astonishment.

Bartlet, who had at least had the advantage of reading the file more times than anyone else in the room, was the only one who didn't visibly flinch, though even he was quiet a full second longer than his usual easy rhythm allowed, watching her cross the carpet with the careful, deliberate attention of a man determined not to let the shock show any more than he could help.

She came in a step behind Xander, quieter, more visibly controlled, but there was something coiled tight in her that hadn't been in the room a moment before, the tension of someone who had spent the last several weeks being managed, discussed, and reorganized-around by every institution that had ever touched her entire life, however good their intentions had been, and who had, by now, gotten thoroughly, exhaustingly used to watching rooms full of strangers fail to hide their reaction to how she looked.

"Ms. Summers." Bartlet was extending a hand, warm, genuine, entirely unbothered by whatever unfamiliar shape might close around it. "I've read a great deal about you. I want you to know—"

"Please don't." The words came out sharper than she'd clearly meant them to, sharp enough that the whole room went instantly, completely still. Buffy took a breath, visibly steadying herself, and when she spoke again her voice was raw, real, nothing careful left in it at all anymore. "I've had adults deciding things about my life since I was fifteen years old. Good adults, bad adults, adults who thought they loved me and adults who thought I was just a problem that needed managing. And now I've got the Queen of England signing papers about my own life, and Watchers rebuilding an entire organization around what happened to me, and generals and senators and... and you... all of you, in rooms I'm not even in, deciding what happens to me next, and I know, I know everyone in this building means well, but I need, just once, just this once, for someone with this much power to actually ask me what I want, instead of deciding, on my behalf, what I need."

The room held its silence for a long, heavy beat. Leo's jaw tightened, the old instinct to smooth things over visibly warring with the good sense not to try. Sam, by the window, looked like he might actually cry. Josh shifted his weight, clearly uncertain what to do with his own hands, his whole body, in the face of something this raw and unguarded. Toby had gone very still, very quiet, in the way of a man who felt this kind of thing more deeply than he ever let show on his face.

Bartlet didn't say anything for a long moment. When he did, his voice had lost every trace of the delight from a minute earlier, replaced with something careful and completely serious.

"You're right," he said. "I'm sorry. What do you want, Ms. Summers? Actually want, not what anyone else in this room thinks you should want."

Something in Buffy's posture went very still, visibly thrown by being asked so plainly, so directly, with no hedging at all.

"I want—" She stopped. Started again, quieter this time. "I want to be asked. That's actually most of it, honestly. I don't need you to fix everything, I know that's not even possible. I need to be in the room when decisions get made about my own life, instead of finding out about them after the fact, from a folder somebody hands me once it's done."

"Then you will be," Bartlet said. "From this point forward, on anything that touches you directly, I will personally make sure of it, myself." He held her gaze, steady, entirely sincere. "That's not nothing, coming from me, though I understand it's also not everything you're owed after everything you've been through. I'm sorry it took a room full of powerful people, and a Senator getting thrown out of my office an hour ago, before someone properly asked you the question."

Buffy let out a breath that shook only slightly at the edges. "Thank you," she said, and meant it, even through the anger still sitting close under the surface, not yet gone, maybe never fully gone.

Later, once the room had emptied out of everyone but family, Bartlet sat behind the Resolute desk for a long while, turning the whole conversation over slowly in his mind.

"Whatever we build to replace the Council," he said, finally, quietly, to Abbey, who'd stayed behind. "It gets built with her in the room. Not as a symbol. Not as an asset, the way that man kept describing her. As a person whose life it actually is, first and always."

Abbey, arms still crossed, allowed herself a small, fierce smile. "Good," she said. "That's the first thing you've said all week that I don't have a single thing to add to."
