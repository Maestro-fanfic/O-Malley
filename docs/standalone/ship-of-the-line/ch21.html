The Music

Marcus Whitfield had told himself, for months on end, that patience was a virtue worth any cost. That the old Council would only ever be properly rebuilt by someone willing to wait, to gather resources carefully, to plan — not by the sentimentalists currently running the new organization straight into what he was privately certain would prove total irrelevance within a year, maybe two.

He had told himself that so many times, across so many long, isolated nights in the storage unit, that it had gradually stopped feeling like patience at all and started feeling, in his more honest moments, like nothing more than fear — fear of admitting he no longer mattered to the shape of anything happening in the world anymore.

He worked from a rented storage unit outside Fresno, three encrypted drives spread across a folding table under a single hanging work light, piecing together fragments of two systems he understood only in rough outline and not at all in true substance — a containment working pulled from the Council's oldest surviving archive, meant, centuries ago, for subduing a Slayer who had turned violently against her own Watchers, and a piece of salvaged tech he'd acquired through a contact who claimed, with more confidence than actual evidence, that it had come off a black-market seller dealing quietly in what the contact vaguely called "off-world scrap." Marcus didn't fully understand what that phrase actually meant. He told himself, repeatedly, that it didn't matter. Power was power, in his calculation, and enough of it, properly stacked together, surely amounted to real control in the end.

He activated the hybrid device on a Tuesday night, alone in the unit, pointed at a photograph of Buffy Summers he'd lifted years ago from an old Council personnel file, murmuring the containment ritual's opening words over a piece of technology that had never once, in its entire existence, been meant to receive them. He thought, in the last clear moment before he began, of Corinne — told himself, one final time, that this was for her, for every Slayer who'd ever died because nobody with proper authority had been willing to impose the discipline the work actually required. He did not let himself think, even now, about how little that girl's face had ever actually looked like the reasons he kept giving for what he was about to do.

It did not work the way he'd intended it to.

The two systems met each other the way oil meets water forced violently into the same container under pressure — not blending, not cooperating in any way, simply fighting, magic and machinery each trying desperately to impose its own internal logic onto a process neither one fully recognized or could properly interpret. The rupture that followed was not clean, not controlled in any sense. It went out from that cramped storage unit in Fresno in a spike of raw, chaotic signature, still aimed, by pure original intent if nothing else, at the woman in the photograph three hundred miles away.

Marcus was thrown backward hard against the unit's corrugated metal wall, ears ringing, hands badly burned, understanding, for the first time in months, with sudden and total clarity, that he had just done something larger than he had any right whatsoever to control.

Willow felt it before anyone properly explained anything to her at all — a wrongness moving through the air, sharp and sudden, like a held musical note gone flat all at once, mid-breath.

"That's not us," she said, out loud, to Nada, to the empty classroom she'd claimed as her own quiet workspace ever since that first overwhelming week after Halloween. "Something just — happened. Somewhere out there. Something big, and something bad."

It has, Nada agreed, and for the first time in weeks, something in that ancient, patient calm sharpened suddenly into genuine, focused attention. It is reckless, and poorly made, and dangerous precisely because of how poorly it was made. Small one — I believe we are about to have a very difficult night ahead of us.

The call came within the hour: an operation Marcus's own scattered network had apparently been running for months was now collapsing, badly, its containment failing across multiple points simultaneously, and whatever he'd triggered was accelerating fast enough that the new organization's response team — still thin, still finding its own feet, only months old at best — simply didn't have the resources on hand to contain it properly in time.

"We need more than what we currently have," Giles said, grim, over the phone, from wherever he was currently coordinating the response effort. "Willow. I genuinely hate to ask this of you, of all people. But if there's any way to reinforce the containment field before it fully collapses—"

Willow felt the old, familiar hunger rise before she'd even finished hearing the full request — the part of her that had always, quietly, in some private corner of herself, wanted to know how much more she could do if she simply, stopped being so careful all the time. More power. Just this once. Just enough to fix it fast and clean, before anyone gets seriously hurt because I hesitated.

I feel it too, Nada said, quiet, watchful, offering no judgment at all in the observation. The wanting. It is not wrong of you to want it, small one. It is only dangerous to reach for it without first knowing what answers when you do.

"What answers?" Willow asked, already moving, already gathering what she'd need for the drive across town.

I do not know yet, Nada admitted, honestly. But something is listening tonight that was not listening yesterday. I would ask you to be careful, small one. Not because you are wrong to want to help. Because the shape of the help matters a great deal more than its raw size ever will.

Willow worked through the entire night with exactly that warning sitting heavy in her chest — pulling steadily from the deep well of power she now knew, with total and unshakeable certainty, that she could reach if she simply let herself, and stopping, again and again, right at the very edge of it, choosing instead the slower, harder, more careful path: precision instead of raw force, patient containment instead of overwhelming correction. It cost her far more effort than the shortcut would have. It took considerably longer. Twice, over the course of that long night, she felt her own hands shake with how badly some deeper, hungrier part of her wanted to simply stop being careful and end this, all at once, with absolutely everything she had available to give.

She didn't.

By dawn, the immediate crisis was contained — not resolved, not clean by any measure, but stable, holding steady, buying everyone precious time. Willow sat on the classroom floor afterward, exhausted down to her bones, shaking visibly, and alive in a way that felt, for the first time in a very long while, entirely and uncomplicatedly like herself again.

You did not reach for the door, Nada said, something in the words that might have been quiet pride, threaded through them. That was not a small thing, small one. I have watched a great many travelers reach for it, over a very long time. Fewer manage to stop themselves in time, the way you just did.

The scan came first — an anomalous reading that Sam flagged from orbit within hours of the initial rupture, something that didn't match any known signature in any database available to her, sweeping slowly, methodically, patiently across the entire region where the chaos had originally spiked.

"It's not searching randomly," Sam said, frowning hard at the display in front of her. "It's narrowing its focus. Like it's actively triangulating something specific."

The presence followed two full days later — not an attack yet, not quite, just a shape settling into high orbit that hadn't been there before, vast and coldly geometric and without the warmth of anything human-built, watching, patient, the way a hunter watches before deciding whether the prey in front of it is worth the considerable trouble.

"Sentinels," Buffy said, out loud, the word arriving from somewhere that wasn't quite her own ordinary vocabulary, cold certainty settling heavy into her chest as she said it. "That's — that's what those are called, I think. They watch for change. Too much change, moving too fast, and they come to correct it."

Yes, Artemis confirmed, grim. I did not expect to see them here, of all places. I did not expect there to be enough of me left, in this particular reality, to properly draw their notice. But there is. There has been, since the very day this all began, whether either of us wanted it that way or not.

"So this is my fault, then. All of it."

It is no one person's fault, Artemis said, careful. It is simply what happens when enough accumulated change gathers in one single place. Yours, certainly, in part. The witch's, tonight, however she managed to hold herself back from the worst of it. The reckless man's, most of all, by far — his was loud, and poorly shaped, and loud, poorly shaped things draw attention faster than anything quiet ever could hope to.

The attack, when it finally came, was smaller than it might otherwise have been — Willow's hard-won restraint had kept the combined signature from ever crossing whatever precise threshold would have summoned something far larger and worse — but it still came regardless, and it still came directly for Buffy, the largest single standing irregularity already sitting in that particular town by the time the presence overhead finally decided to act.

She met it alone, out in the same empty stretch of desert where she'd found the crashed ship just days before, because some instinct — hers, or Mike's, or Artemis's own — told her clearly this was not a fight to bring anyone else into, not this time.

Something in her moved before she'd consciously decided anything at all — hands open and visible at her sides, weight settled even between both feet, shoulders square but not squared at anything, the specific, deliberate posture of a person making very sure a nervous, watching system could read her, at a glance, as no threat worth escalating over. It wasn't a Slayer's stance. It wasn't even quite a stance she recognized as her own, though her body had apparently known it perfectly well long before her mind caught up to why. De-escalation. Open hands. No sudden gestures. Let it look as long as it needs to look. The voice supplying it was quiet, flat, entirely certain of itself, unmistakably Mike's — the particular, hard-won calm of a man who had learned to survive an unfamiliar universe the plain way, by never once giving a wary, watching thing a reason to strike first.

The Sentinel unit that descended toward her was smaller than the vast presence still waiting in orbit, a single geometric drone, cold and precise, scanning her slowly with a light that felt, somehow, like being properly read rather than simply seen or measured.

It is assessing you, Artemis said, steady now, the grief momentarily set carefully aside in favor of something more procedural, more immediately useful. It will act if it judges you a continuing, ongoing threat to overall balance. It will withdraw if it judges the current disruption sufficiently contained.

"So I need to convince a robot I'm not dangerous," Buffy said, standing very still, "while also being, historically, kind of extremely dangerous, in point of fact."

You need to be still, Artemis corrected, gently. Not passive, not frozen. Still. There is a real difference between the two, and I believe you already understand it, somewhere underneath the fear.

Buffy stood her ground, didn't reach for a weapon, didn't run, didn't fight back in any way — simply stood there, breathing slow and even, letting the scanning light wash steadily over her, letting whatever ancient, careful competence lived quietest and deepest inside her carry her through the one single thing panic would have ruined entirely: patience, held steady, for as long as it took.

The drone hovered a long moment, motionless, considering. Then, without further incident of any kind, it rose smoothly away, retreating back toward the vast waiting shape still sitting in orbit, which folded itself away into nothing at all shortly afterward, leaving the sky, for the first time in days, simply, blessedly empty again.

Buffy let out a breath she felt like she'd been holding for what seemed like a very long time indeed.

"Great," she said, out loud, to the empty desert, to Artemis, to nobody at all really. "You play a concerto, and I'm apparently the one who has to face the music."

An accurate complaint, all things considered, Artemis agreed, something almost like real warmth threaded through the words now. For what it's worth, small as it may be — you played your own part very well tonight.

Buffy sat down in the sand where she'd been standing, legs suddenly unsteady beneath her now that there was nothing left requiring stillness, and let herself feel the whole shape of it — not just the fear, finally allowed to catch up with her, but something else too, quieter, stranger, sitting underneath. That was well done, she thought, and the thought arrived, for just a moment, without an I attached to it at all — not I did that well, just the plain fact of it, observed rather than owned, the way Artemis herself might have put it. She noticed the absence a beat too late to catch it properly, turned it over once, uncertain, and let it go, too tired tonight to chase down what it might mean.

Marcus Whitfield was found four days later, not through any deliberate pursuit, not by any justice finally, belatedly catching up to him after all this time, but simply because the storage facility where he'd triggered the original rupture had been swept up incidentally in the very same Sentinel scan that had eventually found its way to Buffy — flagged automatically, investigated as a matter of routine, and turned over, almost as an afterthought, to a federal team that had already, separately, been tracking anomalous signatures across the wider region for weeks.

He offered no resistance at all when they finally came for him. There was, by that point, very little real fight left in a man who had spent his entire adult life believing himself important enough to matter to the shape of world events, and had, undeniably, unambiguously been shown otherwise — undone, in the end, not by an enemy who'd cared enough about him to hunt him down deliberately, but by something so vast and so indifferent that it had never once, in its entire existence, considered him worth the effort of noticing on purpose at all.

Sitting in the back of an unmarked car, hands zip-tied loosely in front of him, watching Fresno slide past a window he wasn't permitted to lower, Marcus found himself thinking, unexpectedly and for the first time in eleven years, not of Cordwell, and not of duty, but simply of a fifteen-year-old girl who had voiced a field instinct nobody in authority had bothered to listen to. He did not reach, this time, for the old comfortable lie about what her death had proven. He found, turning the silence over for what felt like the first time in a very long while, that he no longer entirely believed it himself, and that the not-believing hurt considerably more than the arrest did.
