Rude About It

Rush found him three weeks later, in the middle of the night, in the observation deck where Xander had first woken up carrying two lives instead of one — the same worn scuff pattern still on the deck, the same low hum underfoot, though the room had lost most of its strangeness for Xander by now, worn smooth by months of quiet, late visits nobody else knew about.

"You're not sleeping," Rush said, by way of greeting, settling into the seat across from him without waiting to be asked.

"Neither are you. Seems to be the theme around here lately."

"I've been thinking about our first conversation." Rush's tone was even, the tone of a man who had rehearsed this and very much did not want it to show, though it showed anyway, at the edges. "About what didn't happen. I expected a particular reaction from you that day, and I didn't get it, and I've spent three weeks deciding that fact bothered me more than it reasonably should have."

"You mean you tried to make me feel small, the way you used to make Eli feel small, and it just didn't work, and now you can't stop thinking about why it didn't."

Rush's jaw tightened, fractionally, a small muscle jumping once near his temple. "That's an ungenerous way of putting it."

"It's an accurate way of putting it." Xander didn't say it unkindly. There wasn't any real heat in it at all, just plain, flat fact, laid out the way Eli's memories had learned, eventually, over six long months of solitude, to lay difficult truths out flat rather than dress them up in anything softer. "You're not wrong that something's different in me. I've got his skills. I've got six months of his memories, his loneliness, the whole shape of what it cost him to keep this ship running alone with nobody to check on him. What I don't have is his need for you, specifically, to think he's worth something. That was never really about you being smart, Rush, whatever you told yourself at the time. That was about a kid who didn't believe he mattered unless someone smarter than him said so out loud. I don't have that particular hole in me. Not the same way he did."

"And you think that makes you better than him." Something sharp under the words, defensive in a way Rush clearly hadn't intended to let show.

"No." Xander looked at him directly, steady. "I think it makes me luckier. He earned every single bit of who he became, the hard way, alone, out here, with nobody checking whether he lived or died on any given week. I got handed the finished version without paying for it the same way he did. That's not superiority. That's just — I got the good parts without six months of nobody caring whether I made it. Feels a little like cheating, if I'm honest with you. Some nights it feels like a lot like cheating."

Rush was quiet for a long moment, something working behind his eyes that looked, for once, less like calculation and more like genuine, uncomfortable reassessment.

"I don't know what to do with you," he admitted, quietly. "You're not a problem I recognize the shape of. I've spent thirty years learning to read people by finding the seam where they'll bend. I keep reaching for yours and there's simply nothing there to find."

"Yeah, well." Xander allowed himself a small, tired smile. "Welcome to the club. I don't always know what to do with me either, some days. Ask anybody."

Rush didn't smile back, but something in his posture eased, fractionally, the coiled readiness of a man expecting a fight he apparently, genuinely hadn't gotten. "The Colonel wants a briefing at oh-six-hundred," he said instead, standing, brushing invisible dust from his knees. "Apparently Homeworld Command's found something they'd like your ship's sensor range for. Something rather larger than either of us is going to enjoy, if the preliminary data's anything to go by."

"My ship," Xander repeated, half to himself. "See, that still sounds weird every single time you say it out loud. Like I'm gonna turn around and somebody's actually gonna own it."

"Get used to it," Rush said, already walking toward the hatch, not looking back. "I have a distinct feeling it's not going to stop being true anytime soon, however weird it sounds to you."

Xander watched him go, and sat with the quiet a while longer, turning the whole strange conversation over — not resolved, not fixed, but different now, somehow, than it had been an hour ago. Different in a way he suspected neither of them would ever fully name out loud to the other, not for a long while yet.

✳

The briefing was short, and Jack O'Neill delivered it with the particular flatness he reserved specifically for things he found genuinely unpleasant to have to say out loud in front of a room full of people.

"Apophis," he said, tapping the display, the name landing in the room with the weight of old, unfinished business. "Same System Lord whose leftover hardware very nearly killed me and Teal'c a few weeks back — remember that, the whole reason any of you met this kid in the first place. Turns out he's been rebuilding faster than anybody tracked, and he's got a fleet massing near a planet we've got people currently stationed on. Regular Earth assets can't get eyes on it in time to do anything useful. Yours can, apparently, if the sensor specs Rush sent over are accurate. Which, side note, means we're relying on MacGyver here for round two. Try not to need a whole roll of duct tape this time."

"So we're doing this," Xander said, feeling something settle low in his stomach — not quite fear, not quite excitement, some third thing he didn't have a clean name for yet.

"We're doing recon," O'Neill corrected, holding up one finger. "Recon that might turn into more than recon, depending on what we actually find out there. You up for that, or do we need to have a different conversation first?"

Xander looked around the bridge — at Young, arms crossed, already running risk calculations behind careful, weathered eyes; at Rush, at a console, pretending with limited success not to be as quietly excited as he clearly was underneath the professional composure; at Teal'c, steady as ever, a fixed point in a room full of moving ones; at Sam, already elbow-deep in a data feed she hadn't fully understood an hour ago and clearly, visibly intended to understand fully within the next sixty minutes regardless of what it cost her in sleep.

"Yeah," he said. "I'm up for it."

✳

Destiny came out of a short FTL jump close enough to read hull markings on six Ha'tak-class motherships arranged in a formation that had absolutely nothing peaceful about its geometry, and for one very long, silent moment, nobody on the bridge said anything at all.

"Well," O'Neill said, into the silence, breaking it the way he always seemed to. "That's a lot of pointy ships. We are definitely not in Kansas, and also I'm fairly sure Kansas never had this many motherships hovering over it, so, bad comparison, but you get the idea."

"Sensors are already reading our arrival," Sam said, fast, hands already moving across her console. "They know we're here. No point pretending otherwise, we don't have a cloak that good."

"Options," Young said, sharp, snapping into full command-voice without a beat of hesitation.

"We could run," Rush offered, not looking up from his own display. "We could also, and I want to be very clear I am not formally endorsing this course of action, use the fact that we appear to massively outclass everything currently sitting in that formation to do something less cautious than running."

"You're not endorsing it," O'Neill said, dry, "but you brought it up anyway."

"I'm a scientist. I note that the option exists. What any of you choose to do with that information is, strictly speaking, your business, not mine."

Young looked at Xander for a long moment. "This is your ship," he said, and there was something in the way he said it — not quite deference, not fully, but close, an acknowledgment that clearly hadn't come easy to a career military officer and wasn't offered lightly. "Your call, if you want it to be."

Xander felt the old flinch rise — the part of him that wanted, badly, to say why are you asking me, I'm seventeen, somebody else with actual training should be deciding this — and felt, underneath that flinch, Eli's steadier voice, not overriding the fear, but standing right beside it, patient, already halfway to an answer of its own.

"We don't run," he said. "Not because I'm looking for a fight. Because there's people on that planet who don't have anywhere else to run to, and we're the only thing out here today that can actually do something about it." He looked at the display, at six ships that would have been an unbeatable wall three weeks ago and now just looked, in the light of what Destiny could actually do, like a difficult problem with an available solution. "Sam. Can you get me a targeting solution that disables without killing the crews aboard, if I ask you for one? I want options that don't require anyone dying today who doesn't have to. This is not gonna be a Death Star situation. We're doing this the Ewok way — outnumbered, technically shouldn't work, works anyway."

Sam blinked, clearly not having expected that particular request. "I— yes. Given a minute, yes, I can build that."

"Then let's give them the minute." Xander looked around the bridge once more, and felt, for the first time since Halloween, something settle into place inside him that wasn't quite confidence, exactly, and wasn't quite fear either, but was steady enough to actually stand on. "Let's go save some people."

The engagement that followed took the better part of an hour, careful and methodical rather than dramatic — disabling strikes rather than killing ones, Sam calling targeting solutions in a calm, clipped voice while Rush ran interference on the enemy's own sensor sweeps, Teal'c manning a weapons station with the total focus of a man who had fought this exact enemy under worse circumstances more times than he cared to count. By the time it was over, all six ships sat dead in space, disabled, their crews alive, and a planet full of people who would never fully understand how close they'd come to a very different afternoon got to keep living the ordinary lives they'd woken up into that morning.

Xander stood on the bridge afterward, exhausted in a way that had nothing to do with his body, and let himself, quietly, feel something that wasn't quite pride but stood somewhere in its neighborhood — the specific, hard-won satisfaction of having made a difficult call, and having it actually work out, for once, the way he'd hoped it would.
