Chapter 16 of 27

Property of a Ship That Does Not Exist Yet

Ethan Rayne had been many things over the years, a chaos worshipper, a con man, an occasional catalyst for events larger than he'd ever actually intended them to become, but he had never, until the precise morning three federal agents in unmarked suits appeared at the door of his newest rented storefront, been quite this thoroughly, comprehensively out of his own depth.

"Mr. Rayne." The lead agent didn't bother producing a badge, which told Ethan a very great deal about which part of the government he was actually dealing with this morning. "We'd like to discuss the events of this past Halloween, if you have a moment."

"I sold some costumes," Ethan said, with the easy, well-practiced innocence of a man who'd talked his way out of worse situations than this one over the years. "I'm afraid I really can't be held responsible for whatever nonsense my customers get up to afterward, on their own time, of their own free will."

"Three of your customers currently possess an Ancient starship, a space station roughly the size of a small moon, and, in one case, a physiology that no longer entirely qualifies as human by any standard medical measure." The agent's expression didn't change at all. "We'd very much like to understand how a costume shop accomplished all of that, Mr. Rayne, and whether you intend to do it again, deliberately or otherwise, any time soon."

Ethan considered his available options... considered, briefly and with genuine longing, whether a well-timed bit of chaos magic might get him cleanly out of this particular room... and decided, on balance, weighing everything he'd personally witnessed in the last several months, that a man who'd recently watched a seventeen-year-old casually pilot an Ancient warship into a full fleet engagement was perhaps not the wisest possible person to go testing with cheap parlor tricks today.

"It was Janus," he said instead, with the practiced, put-upon air of a man grudgingly telling the complete truth. "The God of Chaos took a fancy to that boy some considerable time ago, for reasons I confess I still don't fully understand myself. I merely provided the occasion, the costume, the doorway, if you will. I promise you, gentlemen, I am more surprised by the eventual outcome than any of you currently sitting in judgment of me."

"We'll need you to come with us," the agent said, not unkindly, gesturing toward the door. "There are people who'd very much like to understand how much of this was, and was not, within your actual control that night."

"One moment, if you don't mind." Ethan hadn't meant to ask it, hadn't planned to, but something about the tone of the last several weeks' rumors, Travers gone, an entire Council leadership quietly, thoroughly gutted, made the question rise up before he could stop it. "There's a story going round certain circles I still, apparently, have some access to. About Rupert. About what happened to Travers."

The agent's expression didn't so much as flicker. "I couldn't say."

"No," Ethan agreed, quiet, something that wasn't quite fear and wasn't quite fondness moving behind his eyes. "No, I don't suppose you could." He'd given Giles that nickname himself, decades ago, in a Bath cellar neither of them liked to discuss, watching a younger, wilder Rupert Giles do something with a summoning circle that had frightened even Ethan, who frightened rather easily. He had assumed, for twenty years, that Ripper had died quietly somewhere in a library, replaced by tweed and careful disapproval. He found, turning the rumor over now, that some old, canny part of him wasn't remotely surprised to learn otherwise... and was, if he was entirely honest with himself, rather glad he hadn't been the one standing in Travers's office that night.

✳

The interview room they eventually put him in was windowless, government-beige, the kind of room designed to make a man feel like the walls themselves were taking notes. Ethan sat across a plain metal table from a woman who introduced herself only as "Agent Reyes" and a folder considerably thicker than a mere costume shop transaction should have warranted.

"Let's start with Janus," Reyes said, not opening the folder yet, just watching him. "You keep using the word god. I'd like to understand whether you mean that literally, or whether it's simply the framework you've built your business around."

"Both, as it happens." Ethan folded his hands on the table, settling into the register he'd used to talk his way past considerably more dangerous people than a government agent with a folder. "I don't expect you to believe me. I've stopped expecting most people to believe me about most things, a very long time ago. But I've been a devotee of Janus since before you were born, Agent Reyes, and I can tell you with complete sincerity: what happened on Halloween was not chaos. It was attention. He noticed something in that boy long before I ever sold him a hoodie."

"Noticed what?"

"I haven't the faintest idea," Ethan said, and for once, it was the plain, unglamorous truth. "I've spent decades trying to read that god's intentions and I've never once succeeded. I simply provide doors. What walks through them has never been my business, and I've learned, the hard way, not to ask too many questions about it."

Reyes studied him a long moment, the flat, unreadable look of someone deciding how much of a story to actually believe. "You understand how this sounds."

"I understand precisely how it sounds. I also understand that you've read the same reports I have, secondhand as mine may be, and I suspect somewhere in that folder is a description of a boy piloting a ship considerably older than recorded history through a fleet engagement he had no business surviving. If you've got a better working theory than a god got involved, Agent Reyes, I would genuinely love to hear it."

She didn't have one. She closed the folder instead, and the interview, for all its length, produced very little Ethan hadn't already offered freely in the first five minutes.

✳

Ethan went, in the end, without much real resistance, not out of any sudden, newfound sense of civic duty, but because some old, canny survival instinct, honed over decades of narrow escapes, told him clearly that the alternative to cooperation, in a world that now apparently included both alien starships and a president willing to personally dress down a sitting United States Senator in his own Oval Office, was less appealing than a long, tedious series of very thorough interviews conducted in a windowless room.

He did, however, permit himself one small, private, genuine smile as they led him out to the waiting car, thinking of a boy in a three-dollar hoodie who had absolutely no idea yet how much further Janus's particular favor might still have left to run, nor how much further it would eventually take him.
