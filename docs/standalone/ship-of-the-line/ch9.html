Chapter 9 of 27

What Remained

The meeting was never entered onto any official calendar, in either country, by anyone with the authority to enter such things.

Bartlet flew commercial, under a name that wasn't his own, on a red-eye out of Andrews that left him gray-faced and stiff-jointed by the time they touched down, with a security detail thinned down to the smallest number Leo would agree to sign off on without turning it into a formal fight in the Situation Room first. He'd read the full brief twice on the flight over — once with something close to disbelief, and a second time, slower, with a kind of grim, deliberate patience, forcing himself to sit with every line of it rather than skim toward the parts that made him angriest. By the time the wheels touched down at a private strip outside London, he'd stopped being surprised by any of it. He hadn't stopped being angry.

He was driven from the airfield in an unmarked car, no motorcade, no flags on the fenders, to a residence that was neither Buckingham Palace nor Windsor — a modest country house an hour outside the city, ivy climbing one whole wall, gravel crunching underfoot in a drive that could have belonged to any comfortable, unremarkable estate in the English countryside. The sheer ordinariness of it was itself a kind of message, Bartlet understood the moment he stepped out of the car, one chosen and carefully received by both parties before either of them had said a single word to the other: this conversation does not officially exist, and we would both very much prefer to keep it that way.

A footman showed him through a low-ceilinged hall lined with portraits of people whose names he didn't recognize and into a sitting room where a fire had been laid but not yet lit, autumn light coming pale and gray through tall windows onto furniture that had clearly been in the family considerably longer than the country itself had held its current borders.

The Queen received him alone, standing when he entered — a small courtesy that told him, more than anything said aloud yet would, how seriously she was taking this — but for a single lady-in-waiting positioned discreetly near the door, taking notes in a hand nobody outside that room would ever be permitted to read, and a full tea service laid out on the low table between two facing chairs that neither of them would touch once, not for the entire length of the conversation.

"Mr. President." Her voice was even, careful, entirely composed, though something behind her eyes — tired, Bartlet thought, in a way that had nothing to do with age — suggested the composure was doing real work this morning. "I understand you've had rather an eventful month."

"Ma'am, I've had rather an eventful month would be considerably underselling it." Bartlet sat when she indicated he should, in the chair across from her, close enough that the fire, once it was lit, would eventually reach them both. He didn't reach for the tea. He noticed, distantly, that she didn't either. "I appreciate you seeing me like this. Off the books. I know that's not how either of our countries usually likes to do things."

"Neither of our countries usually finds itself discussing an organization that has operated, in near-total secrecy, for over three centuries," she said, and there was something dry in it, not quite humor, but standing close enough to its shadow. "I find the usual channels feel rather inadequate to the occasion. I think you already know some part of why I've agreed to receive you at all, Mr. President, rather than referring this to the Foreign Office as protocol would technically demand."

"I know a version of it," Bartlet said. "I'd like to hear yours."

She was quiet a moment, folding her hands in her lap with the particular economy of a woman who had spent seventy years learning to say a very great deal while appearing, to any casual observer, to say almost nothing at all. Outside, somewhere across the grounds, a dog barked once and went quiet.

"There has been, for a very long time — longer than my own reign, longer even than my father's, stretching back, as I understand it, to well before any living person could properly account for its origins — an arrangement." She said the word carefully, as though testing whether it still held. "A charter, quietly renewed generation after generation, quietly forgotten by most who ever so much as brushed up against it in passing. An organization tasked with a burden most of my subjects have never once known existed, and would, I rather suspect, thank neither of us for telling them about now, even if we could find a way to do it that anyone would actually believe."

"The Watchers."

"The Watchers," she agreed, folding her hands neatly in her lap. "I was aware of their existence, in the broadest possible terms — a name mentioned once or twice by my father, in passing, never explained fully, the way certain things simply weren't explained fully in that generation. I was aware of their general purpose, in outline, if not in the sort of operational detail your people have apparently spent the last several weeks compiling. I was not aware — and I want to be plain with you about this, Mr. President, because I believe you've earned plain speaking from me today, if nothing else, having flown all this way on so little sleep — of what the man standing at the head of that organization proved willing to do, in the end, to a child."

Something in Bartlet's jaw tightened, visibly, before he could school it back into something more composed. "He sent people to kill her. Trained people. A team, sent after a seventeen-year-old girl, and when it failed, he tried to reconstitute the order under a different name rather than admit any of it had been wrong in the first place. He called it operational necessity, in writing, in a memo my people have a copy of."

"So I have since been told, in considerable and rather harrowing detail." The Queen's composure didn't crack. Bartlet doubted very much that it ever fully did, not after seventy years of practice. But something underneath it went very still, very quiet, the way a held breath goes still right before it's finally released. "I have read the reports your people were kind enough to share with mine. I have read what she is, this girl. What she carries. What's apparently been asked of girls like her, generation after generation, stretching back further than either of our countries has kept reliable records, in complete silence, answerable the entire time to men who evidently believed her, believed all of them, replaceable enough to order murdered without a second thought, the moment one proved inconvenient." She was quiet a moment, some old memory visibly surfacing and resettling behind her eyes, the way memories do in people who have carried a great many of them for a very long time. "I met a Slayer once, myself. Very briefly, during the war: 1941, if my memory of that whole period hasn't grown too unreliable, though a great deal of it has, if I'm honest. I was quite young. There was an incident near Windsor, hushed up entirely, the way everything unusual was hushed up in those years, what with so much else already needing explaining to the public. A girl, not much older than I was at the time, appeared out of the dark during a blackout and dealt with something in the grounds that nobody in my family ever fully explained to me afterward, then simply left again before anyone could properly thank her. I asked my father about it once, years later. He told me only one thing, and made me promise never to repeat it, a promise I find myself breaking now for the first time in sixty years: there is a girl, always, somewhere, holding a door shut that the rest of us are grateful never to have to look through ourselves. I did not understand, at the time, quite what door he'd actually meant. I understand it rather better now, having read your reports."

"I'd like your help closing the book on this properly," Bartlet said, leaning forward slightly, elbows on his knees, the practiced formality of the last several minutes giving way, just slightly, to something more direct. "Not politically. Not through the usual diplomatic channels, where this sits in some committee's inbox for six months while lawyers argue over whose jurisdiction a magical assassination attempt technically falls under, assuming anyone could even get a committee to take the premise seriously to begin with. I want this handled the way you'd handle something inside your own family, if you discovered one of your own had ordered a child's death and then dressed it up afterward as necessity in a filing cabinet somewhere."

The Queen was quiet for a long moment, hands still folded, studying him with an expression he found himself, for once, unable to read: not coldness, not quite warmth either, something more considering than either.

"The Council's assets," she said, "its properties, its archives, its accumulated funds, every single thing built or gathered under that charter across three full centuries of quiet operation, remain, formally, still answerable to the Crown, however loosely that particular thread of accountability has actually been exercised in recent decades. My own father, I believe, never once thought about it at all, in all his years. I confess I hadn't either, until three weeks ago." She said it simply, without any real ceremony in her voice, the way a woman signs something she has already, privately, fully decided upon well before she ever sat down at the table across from the person asking for it. "I am prepared to deed all of it, in its entirety. Property. Archives. Funding. Every last shilling and every last dusty file. To whatever body you intend to build in its place, provided that body is constructed with rather more genuine conscience than the one it will be replacing, and provided the girl herself has some real say in the shape it eventually takes."

"That's a considerable amount of trust to extend on the strength of a single conversation, Ma'am. No treaty. No formal signing ceremony either country could ever acknowledge happened."

"Mr. President, I am eighty years old." She said it plainly, without self-pity, the simple flat fact of it. "I have buried a husband, outlived several prime ministers I quite liked and rather more I didn't, and I have just this month been told that an organization operating, however loosely, under my own family's quiet blessing chose to attempt the murder of a child rather than simply admit their entire system carried a flaw in it worth examining." A thin, dry note entered her voice now, not quite humor, but standing close enough to its shadow to be recognizable as a distant cousin of it. "I find I have rather less patience remaining for careful, incremental trust-building than I once did, at your age, Mr. President. Draft the papers. I'll sign whatever you put in front of me, provided the language is honest about precisely what it's replacing, and why it needed replacing in the first place."

"I think," Bartlet said, something in his own chest finally loosening for the first time since he'd boarded the plane, "that's more than I had any right to hope for, coming in here. Thank you, Ma'am."

Travers never saw it coming, which seemed, in retrospect, the kind of oversight the man had spent his entire long career punishing mercilessly in others.

Giles came to London himself for this part, in person. He hadn't originally planned to: the survivor's message had seemed, at the time, sufficient on its own — but word had reached him, through the same quiet channel that had warned him the first time, that Travers was already moving to reconstitute the retrieval order under a different name, a different justification, the same tired bureaucratic sleight-of-hand of a man who sincerely believed the failure had been purely tactical rather than moral.

Giles found him alone in his own office, late at night, the building otherwise empty, the way men like Travers so often were, Giles reflected, once you finally stripped away the institutional weight that had, for so long, made them feel entirely untouchable.

"Rupert." Travers didn't look up from his desk, didn't even pause his pen. "I'd heard you'd gone rather feral. I confess I didn't credit the report. Though I suppose I should have — there's a note in your old file, isn't there, from your Bath days. Ripper. I always assumed that was youthful exaggeration on some junior Watcher's part. I don't assume that any longer."

"You should have."

"The girl is an asset. A remarkably capable one, admittedly, more capable than any Slayer in living memory, which is why the Council cannot possibly allow her to continue operating outside our—"

"You sent people to kill her." Giles's voice didn't rise once. It didn't need to. "Not to retrieve her. Not to discipline her. To kill a seventeen-year-old girl, so that the asset, as you keep calling her, could simply be replaced with one more compliant."

"The line requires proper management, Rupert, surely even you understand—"

"I am not here to debate policy with you." Giles crossed the room without hurrying, without any particular urgency at all. "I am here because you are a rabid animal wearing a very expensive suit, and rabid animals do not get reasoned with. They get put down, quietly, before they have the chance to bite anyone else."

Travers had time to reach for the small drawer where everyone in the Council knew he kept a pistol he'd never once, in his entire career, had actual cause to use.

He did not reach it in time.

It was quick, in the end. Quicker than the wetwork team had gotten, because there was no separate message left to send through Travers specifically — that message had already been sent, weeks earlier, carried home in one man's carefully, deliberately broken bones. This was simply the arithmetic finishing itself out, the equation resolving to its inevitable conclusion. Giles left the office as quietly as he'd entered it, and by morning the story moving through what remained of the Council's loyal ranks was not one of a coup, or a murder, but of a man who had, in the end, proven as replaceable as he'd once so casually believed Buffy to be.

The concerned member who'd made that first phone call sent no further messages after that night. Giles understood, without ever needing it confirmed, that the silence itself was its own quiet form of thanks.

What remained of the Council's leadership scattered within the month that followed — some resigning into private life, hoping simply to be forgotten; some absorbed, vetted, into the new structure Bartlet's people were already drafting on both sides of the Atlantic; and a handful, loyal past all reason to a system that had just visibly eaten its own architect, going to ground, disappearing into shadow. One of them — a lieutenant of Travers's, competent, patient, entirely unwilling to accept that the old order was actually finished — would resurface eventually, in smaller ways, in places nobody was watching closely enough yet. But that was a problem for a later day.

For now, in a modest country house outside London, a queen signed papers she'd had drafted overnight, deeding three full centuries of secrets, resources, and quiet accumulated power to a president who intended to build something better out of their bones — and neither of them spoke again, that particular day, about the man whose death had made the entire transaction possible in the first place.

The Queen asked for one further thing before Bartlet's plane departed, quietly, almost as an afterthought, in a tone that made it clear it had not been an afterthought at all.

"I understand the girl is in the country with you." It wasn't a question. "I would like to meet her, if she's willing. Not as a head of state meeting an asset, Mr. President. I made my feelings about that way of thinking about her clear enough this morning, I think. Simply as one person meeting another, however briefly, before you both fly home."

Buffy arrived at the country house an hour later, considerably more nervous about this specific meeting than she'd expected to be, given everything else she'd already survived that particular week.

The Queen received her alone, standing when she entered, the same small courtesy she'd extended to Bartlet that morning, and studied her for a long moment before either of them said anything at all. Not the polite, practiced study of royalty greeting a stranger, but something slower, more personal: the look of a woman checking a memory against the person now standing in front of her.

"You're younger than I expected," the Queen said, finally, "though I suppose I should have expected that. She was young too. The one I met."

"I heard about that," Buffy said. "Windsor, 1941. Your dad told you about doors."

"He did." Something moved behind the Queen's composure, brief and unguarded. "I have carried that sentence for sixty years and told almost no one, and I find I am rather glad, now, to finally be handing it to someone who'll actually understand what it means, rather than simply hearing polite words about duty and nodding along." She gestured to the chair opposite her own, an invitation rather than a command. "Sit, please. I find I have rather a lot of questions I've never once had anyone properly qualified to ask."

They talked for the better part of an hour, not about policy, not about the transferred assets or the new organization taking shape across two governments, but about the actual weight of it, the specific loneliness of carrying something nobody else could properly see, the particular exhaustion of being thanked in the abstract by people who'd never have to understand what the thanks was actually for. The Queen asked what it felt like, the calling itself, and listened to the answer with the whole of her attention, the way she'd apparently promised her own father she'd remember to do, sixty years too late for him to see it kept.

"I never got to thank her," the Queen said, toward the end, quiet now, some old grief finally surfacing after decades held carefully in reserve. "The girl at Windsor. She was gone before anyone even properly saw her face. I've thought about that, on and off, most of my life: that somebody did something extraordinary for my family and my country, and simply walked back into the dark afterward, expecting nothing, receiving nothing, and I never once had the chance to tell her it mattered."

"I don't think they usually stick around for the thank-you," Buffy said, gently. "I don't think we're really built for it."

"No," the Queen agreed. "I don't imagine you are." She reached out, then, and took Buffy's hand in both of hers, briefly, the gesture of an old woman rather than a monarch. "So let me say it now, to you, on her behalf as much as your own, since I never got the chance properly the first time. Thank you. For the door. For all of them, every single one, whoever they were, however little any of us ever properly understood what it cost."

Buffy didn't have a ready answer for that. She sat with it instead, letting herself, for once, simply be thanked rather than deflecting it into a joke the way she usually did, and found, to her own quiet surprise, that she didn't mind the weight of it nearly as much as she'd expected to.
