What the Wound Remembers

The hole in the world didn't look like much from the surface.

That had always been the strange, cheating thing about the Hellmouth. You could stand in the library basement, or the mouth of a cave off Kingman's Bluff, or any of the half-dozen access points Giles's books insisted existed, and see nothing but rock and old water stains. It took a kind of attention to feel the wrongness underneath. Buffy had spent three years learning to feel it the way you'd learn to feel a draft under a door. Now, standing at the lip of it with Artemis's borrowed awareness laid over her own, she didn't feel a draft anymore. She felt the door itself.

"It's not a hole," she said, mostly to herself, though her voice carried further in the basement's stillness than she'd intended. "It's a wound that never closed right."

Nobody asked her to clarify. Giles was kneeling at the chalk circle he and Jenny had spent two hours arguing into its final shape, each line and symbol debated, adjusted, checked twice against three separate sources before either of them would commit to it. Jenny didn't look up from the page she was translating, low and fast under her breath, in a language that predated the building around them by a very long time — older, even, than the Council archives she'd grown up half-believing were only stories.

Willow crouched a few feet off, hands loose in her lap, not casting anything yet. She'd stopped needing to grip things to feel steady these past weeks. That was new, and she still hadn't fully decided whether she liked it.

It listens before it opens, said the part of her that wasn't only her. Speak to the wound as you would to a frightened animal — not louder. Slower.

"I'm not scared of it," Willow murmured.

I did not say you were the frightened one.

Rush's voice cut across the chamber before she could sit with that observation any longer. "If everyone's quite finished being poetic about it..." He didn't look up from the tablet in his hands, its screen throwing pale blue light across a face that hadn't slept properly in longer than six months of stasis could account for. "This wound is putting out a waveform. An actual, measurable waveform. Which means it has a frequency. Which means it can, in principle, be matched."

"That's rather the working theory, yes," Giles said, without looking up either, his patience for Rush's tone visibly thinning after only two days of working alongside him.

"Then can we please stop treating it like it's going to be offended if someone points that out plainly."

Xander, crouched by the makeshift rig Rush had cobbled together, didn't look up from the readout either. "Nobody said it gets offended, we said it listens. Those aren't the same thing, and you'd know that if you'd actually read the material Jenny put together instead of skimming it for anything you could argue with. It's not The Terminator, Rush, it's more like — okay, actually, it's a little bit The Terminator. But an old one. An emotionally complicated one."

"They're functionally identical if the plan requires everyone to be quite so careful about tone."

"The plan requires us to be careful because a fourteen-hundred-year-old rip in reality doesn't come with an instruction manual, and you don't get to skip the part where people who've actually studied this thing for years tell you how it behaves, just because you don't like that the answer isn't a clean number on a screen."

Rush's jaw worked. He didn't say anything back. That, more than an actual retort, was its own kind of concession, the second one in as many days, and Xander clocked it and let it go without comment, because there wasn't time to enjoy it properly, not with what was coming.

"Positions," Giles said, standing, chalk dust clinging pale to the knees of his trousers. "Jenny?"

"Ready." She didn't look up from the page, still murmuring the last of the preparatory verses under her breath.

"Willow?"

Willow breathed out, slow and deliberate. Something in her chest that wasn't quite panic and wasn't quite calm settled into a shape she recognized now, weeks into learning what her own held breath was supposed to feel like with company inside it. "Ready."

"Rush?"

"The array's keyed to the waveform Sam's people logged an hour ago." He didn't look up from the tablet, fingers moving fast across it. "If the resonance holds..." He didn't finish the sentence. He didn't need to. Everyone in the room understood the shape of if it doesn't well enough that nobody made him say it out loud.

Buffy stepped up to the edge of the circle last, because that was where she belonged, and looked down into a darkness that had never once, in three years of patrolling this town, looked back at her the way it looked back at her now.

It knows what's coming, Artemis said, quiet, careful, an unfamiliar tenderness threaded through something colder underneath. It has known for longer than you have been alive that something would, eventually, come to close it. It has simply never quite believed, until this moment, that it would actually happen.

"Good," Buffy said out loud, and heard her own voice come out steadier than she actually felt underneath it. "Let's not disappoint it, then."

Giles began the invocation. It wasn't loud. That surprised her, somewhere underneath everything else... some old expectation of magic as spectacle, thunder and wind and dramatic gestures, and instead what she got was a middle-aged man's careful, precise Latin, unhurried, almost gentle, and Jenny's voice beneath it in something older still, weaving through the same rhythm the way two instruments find each other partway through a song neither one had rehearsed alone. Willow's hands moved, slow and deliberate, tracing a shape in the air that wasn't quite a gesture and wasn't quite writing, something in between the two that neither Latin nor English had ever needed a word for.

Rush's array began to hum.

The sound came up from the floor first... not really a noise at all, more a pressure, something felt in the teeth and the soles of the feet before it ever reached the ears properly. The chalk lines on the ground caught a light that had no obvious source and held it, burning cold-blue at the edges, deepening by degrees. Somewhere far below them, deep in rock that had been broken since before anyone alive had words for what had broken it, something enormous, ancient, and until this moment unchallenged, turned its slow attention toward the surface.

"Frequency's shifting," Rush said, sharp now, all his earlier irritation burned clean away by the one thing he apparently cared about above all else: the problem in front of him, actually working. "It's fighting the match."

"It doesn't want to close," Willow said. Her voice had gone strange, doubled, the way it did sometimes now when she wasn't paying close enough attention to keep the two voices carefully separate from each other. "It's been open so long it doesn't remember how to be anything else. It's afraid, in whatever way a thing like this can be afraid."

"Then remind it what closing feels like," Giles said, not unkindly, and kept chanting, his voice never once wavering.

The light at the circle's edges deepened from blue toward something that didn't have a proper color, something Buffy's eyes kept trying and failing to name. The pressure in the air built steadily until it felt like standing too close to a held musical note about to catastrophically break. She thought, distantly, of six months of solitude that weren't hers, and of a corridor full of people she'd never met and never saved, and let both of those things sit heavy in her chest instead of pushing them down the way she might once have, because some instinct, hers or Artemis's, she genuinely could no longer tell the difference and had stopped being frightened by that not-knowing sometime in the last few weeks, told her clearly that this was not a moment for flinching away from what she carried.

"Now," Rush said, sharp, urgent. "Now, now, now—"

Willow's hands closed.

The world didn't so much shake as exhale: one long, final breath let out of something that had been holding it for fourteen centuries without ever once setting it down. Then the light went out all at once, chalk lines snapping dark, the pressure in the room collapsing into ordinary basement air so suddenly and so completely that something in Buffy's sealed helmet clicked and re-equalized against it, sharp and startling.

Silence. The particular, enormous silence of a thing that has, at long last, actually stopped.

Rush looked at his tablet. Looked at it again, longer this time, checking and rechecking. "Frequency's flat," he said, and for the first time since Buffy had known him, he sounded something close to unguarded. "There's no waveform anymore. There's nothing down there at all. It's just rock. Ordinary rock."

Giles let out a breath he'd clearly been holding since well before the invocation had even started, his shoulders dropping several visible inches. Jenny sat back on her heels, hands trembling slightly, and made no effort to hide it.

"Willow?" Buffy said.

"I'm okay." The light behind Willow's eyes flickered, just once, and for a second something ancient and patient looked out through it before receding, gently, back to wherever it lived when it wasn't needed. "It's — it's actually closed. It's just a hole in the ground now. It's just rock, like Rush said."

Nobody said anything for a while after that. There didn't seem to be anything left that needed saying, not yet.

Buffy looked down into the dark, ordinary, empty space where a wound had sat for longer than anyone standing in that basement had been alive, and felt something in her own chest loosen that she hadn't fully realized, until this exact moment, had been clenched this entire time.

It's over, she thought, and it was, completely her own thought, no one else's... that fact, more than the silence in the ground itself, was the thing that made her believe it was actually true.

She stood there a while longer, alone in the quiet with the rest of them, letting it settle. Behind her, she could hear Rush starting to argue with Giles about whether the readings warranted a follow-up survey, and Willow laughing softly at something Jenny had said, and the ordinary, blessed sound of people who had just done something enormous together, returning, piece by piece, to the shape of an ordinary evening.

Jenny caught Xander's eye across the basement, once the celebration had settled into something quieter, and gave him a small, tired nod, the acknowledgment of someone who'd fought two days of arguments to get exactly here, and knew he'd been the one to actually find the words that made the argument stop.

"Delivery truck," she said, dry, walking past him toward the stairs. "I'm putting that in my notes. Historians are going to hate you."

"Historians already hate me," Xander said. "I'm the guy who brought a spaceship to a Buffy movie. I made my peace with that a while ago."
