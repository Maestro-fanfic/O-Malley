Chapter 23 (14 of 54 in this book)

The Eirene Constantia was a beautiful ship.

That was the first thing, arriving before the tactical assessment, the aesthetic fact of the vessel sitting in berth seventeen of Landing's commercial harbor in the early afternoon light with her lines clean and her hull maintained with a care that exceeded what commercial necessity required. Someone took pride in her. That was visible from the quay.

She filed it. Pride of ownership meant the master had a relationship with the ship that went beyond function, which meant the ship was not just a vehicle but an identity, which meant the master would not easily abandon it.

That was useful.

The boarding team assembled at the harbor authority checkpoint in the guise of a routine inspection team, which they looked like because Usher's people had dressed them like one and had provided documentation that would hold up to any check the Eirene Constantia's crew was likely to run. Commercial inspections happened. A system at war inspected commercial vessels. This was normal.

Buffy was not part of the inspection team's official manifest. She went in separately, through the maintenance access that Brandis had identified in the harbor's structural records, a route that was technically a fire egress and practically a way onto the vessel without going through the main boarding point.

She went in ten minutes before the inspection team arrived at the main hatch.

The maintenance access brought her into the ship's lower engineering spaces, which were warm in the way of active drives and smelled of lubricant and the specific recycled-air quality that all ships shared regardless of their age or purpose. She moved through it without turning on lights, not needing lights, the Slayer sense doing what it did in the dark which was more and more precisely than it did in the light.

The ship had eleven people aboard.

She could feel the shape of them, distributed through the vessel's spaces, the pressure of occupied rooms and corridors against the general background hum of the ship's systems. Most were in the forward sections. Two were in the engineering spaces she'd come through, close enough that she'd worked around them on the way in.

She counted again.

Eleven. Of those eleven, four had the quality she was looking for, the specific wrongness that said what she was reading was not entirely human. Three were the distributed kind, spread through the forward sections, old enough to be settled in their operational profile. The fourth was something different.

The fourth was very old.

She'd felt very old before. She'd fought very old. The Master had been very old, and the Master had been the oldest thing she'd encountered in two years of slaying, and the Master had been a presence she could feel from the other side of a warehouse before she saw him.

What she was feeling now from the forward section of the Eirene Constantia was in the same register as the Master.

She adjusted her assessment of the next twenty minutes accordingly.

The inspection team would be at the main hatch in approximately eight minutes. She had eight minutes to understand the ship's layout as it actually was rather than as the harbor's records showed it, which for a vessel with a long-standing irregular operation were likely not the same thing.

She moved forward.

The engineering spaces gave way to crew quarters, a short corridor of doors that were mostly closed, and she went through it without stopping and into the main cargo section, which was large and dim and smelled of the specific mixture of scents that accumulated in spaces where many different things had been stored over many years. The cargo was partially loaded, pallets and containers in configurations she didn't try to read, and she moved through it and found what she'd suspected she would find.

A secondary space. Below the main cargo deck. Accessible through a hatch that was not on any schematic she'd been given but which was exactly where you'd put a secondary space on this class of vessel if you'd had this class of vessel long enough to know where the structural voids were.

She stood over the hatch and felt what was below it.

Very old. And not alone.

She had six minutes.

She didn't go through the hatch.

She moved forward to the main corridor and positioned herself in the intersection that would give her sightlines to the bridge access, the forward compartments, and the cargo deck hatch, and she waited.

At the four-minute mark the main boarding hatch opened and she heard the sounds of the inspection team coming aboard. At the three-minute mark something in the forward section moved.

She felt it before she heard it, the specific quality of something very old deciding to do something. She moved toward it.

The corridor outside the bridge was short and well-lit, and the door at the end of it was closed. She stood in the corridor and waited.

The door opened.

The man who came through it was tall and lean with the look of someone who had been the same age for a very long time, the specific quality of preserved youth that vampires developed after enough centuries. He was wearing clothing that was expensive and understated and that had been fashionable for long enough to have lapped itself and come back around.

He saw her.

She saw him.

The moment before.

He was fast. She'd felt how old he was and she'd recalibrated for it and the recalibration was still insufficient by a margin that she only understood once he was already moving, the kind of speed that came from centuries of practice layered over whatever the base capability had been.

She was faster.

Barely.

The corridor was not a good fighting space. It was narrow and straight and favored whoever had the longer reach, which was him by approximately eight centimeters that mattered significantly in the exchange of the first three seconds. She accepted two impacts that she would have accepted nothing from a younger opponent and used the acceptance of them to get inside his reach and from inside his reach the length advantage was neutralized.

He was strong. She was stronger.

He knew techniques she hadn't encountered before, the accumulated library of every martial tradition it had encountered across centuries. She worked through the unfamiliar patterns by feel rather than recognition, the Slayer sense doing the pattern-analysis work faster than conscious thought could manage.

The Girl was very present.

He went for the wall at one point, trying to create space, and they went through a door that gave way into a small compartment that was a communications room, and she got the stake into position and he saw it and the expression that crossed his face was old enough to be interesting.

Not fear. Something more complex, the expression of something that had survived long enough to have a sophisticated relationship with its own ending.

"You're the Slayer," he said.

"Yes."

"I didn't know one still existed."

"People keep saying that," she said. "It keeps being wrong."

He looked at the stake and then at her. The calculation in his eyes was visible. "You want the network."

"I want information about the network," she said. "There's a difference."

"And if I give you information."

"I don't make deals with vampires," she said. "The information you get from a deal is less reliable than the information you get from the alternative, because the thing making the deal shades what it tells you toward whatever outcome is best for it."

"And the alternative provides accurate information?"

"The alternative provides whatever information is accessible in the operational environment," she said. "Which includes your ship's records and communications logs and whatever your crew knows, which I'm going to have access to regardless of this conversation."

He looked at her.

"You're very young," he said.

"Yes."

"And already this precise about it." Something crossed his face. "How long have you been doing this?"

"Two years." She met his eyes. "You?"

A pause. "Longer than your civilization has existed," he said.

She thought about what that meant.

"Then you know how this ends," she said.

"Yes," he said. Something that was almost regret, too old to be simply regret. "I know how this ends."

He came at her again.

She was ready.

~~~

The dust settled.

She stood in the communications room and breathed and let the category that didn't have a name do what it did when a fight that mattered was finished. Not triumph. Not grief exactly. The completion of a necessary and irreversible act.

She'd gotten something in the last moments. Not a deal. But the very old ones were sometimes legible at the end in ways the younger ones weren't, and what she'd read was a direction.

Not a name. Not coordinates. A direction. The sense of something above the master in the hierarchy that was older still and further away, and that the master had been connected to with a long leash rather than close operational control.

The master had been running this network with significant autonomy.

She filed it.

She picked up the communicator. "Castellan. I'm done forward. Three more in the cargo section. Don't let anything off the ship."

"Copy." A pause. "Are you all right?"

She looked at the dust. "Yes. Give me five minutes on the cargo deck before you bring anyone in."

"Copy. Five minutes."

She went to the cargo deck.

~~~

The three in the cargo section were younger than the master and older than the Landing drones, and they knew she was coming. She dealt with the one positioned over the secondary hatch first, accepting a second impact to her left side, ribs, deep enough to matter in the short term. She accepted it and dealt with the hatch-guardian and then worked through the remaining two.

Three more in the dust.

She stood over the secondary hatch and breathed through the rib pain and opened the hatch.

The space below was a living space, long-established. Furniture from different eras. Books, actual physical books, stacked and shelved with care. Objects she couldn't immediately categorize, some of which were old enough to have been in a museum in her time.

No one in it.

She moved through it carefully.

The books first. Several languages. Dates ranging from the sixteenth century to something within the past fifty years.

Then one thing, on a shelf by itself. A small case of dark material she didn't have a word for, sealed with a mechanism she didn't recognize, that the Slayer sense was registering the way a compass registered north.

Something was in the case.

Something that was not natural.

She looked at it for a long time without touching it.

Then she picked up her communicator and called Fritz.

"I need you at berth seventeen," she said. "Now. And bring whatever you have for imaging something without opening it."
