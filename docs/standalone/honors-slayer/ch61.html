Chapter 61 (52 of 54 in this book)

Three years.

She spent them the way she spent all the years now, which was fully, in the work and the life and the people, the texture of the ongoing thing rather than the waiting for the significant thing.

The Vigil at fifty-one was the Vigil that she could look at and recognize as the thing she'd been building toward in the early conversations with the Queen and the late nights in the first year's residence and all the iterations between. Not finished. Still becoming. But recognizably itself, the character of it established in the way that characters established themselves through being tested enough times that the testing confirmed rather than changed.

Yeva was the operational leader in full now, which meant Buffy's operational role was advisory rather than primary, which she'd thought was going to feel like loss and which felt instead like the specific satisfaction of something you'd built finally running on its own momentum.

She still took the highest-complexity operations. Not because she couldn't delegate them but because the highest-complexity operations required the most complete synthesis of the Slayer sense and the channel and the lineage knowledge and her thirty-three years of doing this, and that synthesis was still specific to her in ways that Yeva's was not yet. The yet was still honest. Yeva's ceiling continued to not be visible.

She advised on the rest.

The advising was different from the leading in a way she hadn't fully anticipated. Leading required the full engagement of everything she was at the level of execution. Advising required the full engagement of everything she was at the level of understanding, which was a different skill and one she was finding she was well suited to, the accumulated knowledge of what things looked like from the inside making her a particular kind of useful to the people inside them.

She talked to the woman on Old Earth every week.

The woman's name, when she shared it, was Adisa. She shared it in the fourth month, which was the right timeline, the point at which the communication had built enough that the name was an extension rather than an exposure.

Adisa was sixty-two when they'd first spoken and was sixty-five when the third window approached, and in those three years Buffy had learned Adisa the way she'd learned the people who became important in her life, through the accumulated specific weight of many conversations about many things.

Adisa was brilliant.

Not in the way of credentials or formal training, though she had both. In the way of someone who had been thinking about a specific set of problems for forty years with the intelligence and care of someone who had no choice but to be thorough because there had been no one else to be thorough for her.

The oral tradition she'd described in the first conversation was the surface layer. What the conversations over three years had revealed was a theoretical framework she'd developed for the lineage's nature that was, in some ways, more advanced than what Allison and Fritz had developed from the biology, because Adisa had been working from the inside of the experience rather than from the outside of the data.

Allison, when Buffy had begun summarizing the conversations, had said: "I need to talk to her directly."

She'd arranged it.

The first conversation between Allison and Adisa had lasted six hours and produced, by the end, the specific quality of two people who had arrived at the same place from completely different directions and were discovering that the convergence was real.

They'd been in regular communication since.

The seventeen-year-old the woman had mentioned � the one who might be fully expressed � had been told what she was.

Not by Buffy directly. By Adisa, using the words from the chip and the years of conversations with Buffy as the framework. The telling had gone, Adisa reported, the way tellings went when they arrived at the right time to a person who had been ready for them, which was with the specific quality of something clicking into place rather than something foreign being installed.

The girl had said: I knew something was different.

Adisa had said: yes.

The girl had said: is it always going to be this much?

Adisa had said: no. It gets different. The amount changes. You learn to hold it.

The girl had said: how do you learn?

Adisa had said: you talk to people who already have.

The girl had said: are there people who already have?

Adisa had said: yes. Many. On many worlds. And you're going to meet them.

She'd reported this conversation to Buffy and Buffy had read the report and thought: yes.

She thought: that's the thing.

She thought: you're going to meet them.

The third window opened in October.

She went with Yeva and Eris and two others.

Not for an operation. For the meeting.

Old Earth from the surface was something she hadn't anticipated.

She'd thought she'd known what to expect. She'd read everything that had been compiled about Old Earth's current state � the environmental history, the two millennia of human civilization building and adapting and rebuilding on the planet that had originated them all. She'd thought the reading would prepare her for the looking.

It hadn't.

The surface of Old Earth was not Sunnydale and it was not the Earth she'd read about in history and it was not the Earth of the stories and it was entirely, specifically itself, a planet that had been through more than most planets and was still here, still inhabited, still the specific place where humanity had begun even if almost nothing she recognized remained of the specific version of it she'd known.

She stood outside the cave entrance, which was recognizable only through the Slayer sense and the data from the previous visits, and she looked at what was around her.

The California coast. Changed beyond recognition in terms of its specific features and entirely itself in terms of its fundamental character, the light and the angle of the land and the quality of the air in October, which was wrong in the ways of two thousand years of climate change and managed recovery and was still October light in California in a way she felt in the specific part of her that was still from here.

Adisa was there.

And the girl, who was seventeen and whose name was Mara and who looked at Buffy with the expression that Buffy had seen on the faces of seventeen-year-olds who were newly told what they were, which was a specific combination of recognition and overwhelm and the specific alertness of someone whose understanding of what they were had just dramatically expanded.

Mara looked at Buffy and said: "You're the one from the chip."

Buffy said: "Yes."

Mara said: "You're from here originally."

Buffy said: "Yes."

Mara looked at the landscape around them.

She said: "What was it like? When you were here?"

She thought about Sunnydale and the high school and the library and Giles and Willow and Xander and her mother and the specific quality of that time, the weight and the grief of it and the love and the work.

She said: "It was a lot. I was seventeen and it was the whole world and it was very large." She paused. "It turned out the world was larger than I knew. But that was the world I knew at the time."

Mara said: "Do you miss it?"

She held the question.

She said: "I miss the people." She paused. "The place is � present. In me. The origin thing doesn't go away. But what I miss are the people and they're not here anymore in the sense of being reachable, so the missing is what it is." She paused. "I built other people. The missing didn't prevent the building."

Mara looked at her.

She said: "That's what Adisa says. That the loss doesn't prevent the building."

She said: "Adisa is right."

Adisa, beside them, had the specific quality she'd come to know through three years of conversations made fully visible for the first time in person. Sixty-five and present in the way of someone who had been doing the long work for forty years without support and had found the support and was now in the specific quality of a person receiving confirmation that what they'd built alone was real.

She looked at Adisa.

She said: "I owe you an apology."

Adisa looked at her.

She said: "The chip arrived thirty years ago. The response took twenty years to reach us. That's fifty years you spent doing this without the connection that the Vigil was built to provide." She held Adisa's gaze. "We should have found you sooner."

Adisa said: "The window physics�"

She said: "Yes. I know the window physics. I'm not talking about the windows. I'm talking about the forty years before the first window. The framework should have been in place before the first transit." She held Adisa's gaze. "It wasn't and I know why it wasn't and it was still a failure of what the Vigil was built to do. I wanted to say that directly."

Adisa was quiet for a moment.

She said: "You were building it while I was here. You didn't know I was here."

She said: "I know. The not-knowing doesn't change the fact of the gap." She held Adisa's gaze. "What you built here in the gap � the oral tradition, the framework, the communication network � is real and I'm not minimizing what you did without us. I'm saying you shouldn't have had to do it without us." She paused. "We're here now."

Adisa held her gaze.

She said: "Yes." She paused. "You are."

They held each other's gaze in the October light of the California coast on Old Earth and Mara stood between them with the alertness of someone who was taking in more than she could fully process and who was going to be processing it for years.

She looked at Mara.

She said: "You're going to come to Manticore."

Mara looked at her.

She said: "Not immediately. When you're ready. There are people there who need to meet you and things you need to learn that are better learned in the place where the Vigil exists." She paused. "And there are people there who are your age who are doing the same work. You should know them."

Mara said: "What are they like?"

She thought about the current generation of fully expressed candidates, the ones who had come through the Vigil's training in the past several years, who were older than seventeen and younger than thirty and who were the future of the work in ways she was still discovering.

She said: "They're remarkable. Each of them in their own specific way." She held Mara's gaze. "You'll fit." She paused. "Differently than any of them. You always fit differently than any of them. That's the nature of this." She held her gaze. "But you'll fit."

Mara looked at her for a long moment.

She said: "How do you know?"

She said: "Because you found the cave. Because you've been doing this for two years without anyone telling you what it was. Because when Adisa told you what you were, you recognized it." She held the girl's gaze. "The ones who fit recognize it. The ones who don't, don't recognize it." She paused. "You recognized it."

Mara held her gaze.

She said: "Okay."

She said: "Yes."

Yeva, who had been present and quiet through all of this, said to Mara: "I want to hear about what you can do. Your specific version of it." She held Mara's gaze with the direct quality that was specifically Yeva's. "No one's version is the same. I want to know yours."

Mara looked at Yeva.

She looked at Buffy.

She looked back at Yeva.

She said: "Okay."

She started talking.

Buffy listened to her talk and thought about seventeen and what it was and what it had been and what it was now, the specific quality of a generation that had been born into the world the Vigil had been building and who didn't know what it had been like before the building and who were going to do things with what they had that she couldn't predict.

She thought: good.

She thought: that's the shape of it.

She thought about the work and the document and the seven things she'd written and the data chip on the cave floor and the letter she'd left and the woman who had found them and was now here, in this light, in this specific October.

She thought about coming back to here and what here had always been and what it was now, not the home of her memory but the origin of her and everything that had come from her.

She stood in the October light of Old Earth and let it be what it was.

The Keeper was very present.

The Girl was present.

The lineage was running through her and through Mara and through Adisa and through the channel and through the oral tradition and through the archive and through every version of the knowledge that had been kept and was being kept and would be kept.

She thought: always forward.

She thought: this is what always forward looks like.

The California light did what it always did, the specific quality of it in October, and she was here, fifty-four years after she'd been born in a version of this place that no longer existed, and she was here, and it was good.

It was very good.
