Beowulf was old in a way that Manticore was not old and Grayson was not old, an oldness that was qualitatively different from either, the specific character of a civilization that had been a civilization since before the concept of interstellar civilization had been fully invented.

She'd read about it extensively and the reading had not prepared her for what direct experience produced, which was the same thing she'd felt on Manticore but older and stranger and more layered, the specific weight of eleven hundred years of continuous human habitation doing something to the fabric of the place that you couldn't measure but could feel.

The Keeper felt it too. She knew because the accompanying quality changed when they entered the system, a heightened attention that was different from what it brought to Manticore or Grayson, more alert, more directed. She'd come to trust the Keeper's reactions as information even when she couldn't fully interpret them and this one she interpreted as: something here is relevant.

She'd already known that. But now she knew it in the way you knew things you felt rather than reasoned.

Honor's mother met them at the docking facility.

Dr. Allison Chou Harrington was not what she'd built from Honor's description, which was itself information about the limits of Honor's capacity to describe her own mother. She was small, which the description had included, and had dark hair going silver at the temples and eyes that were doing the assessment thing the moment they found Buffy's face and didn't stop doing it.

She was also, immediately and without preamble, warm in the specific way of someone who had decided that warmth was not a performance but a mode of operation, the kind of warmth that was its own kind of precision.

She took both of Buffy's hands when they met, which was not a Manticoran greeting custom and was apparently a Beowulfan one, or possibly just an Allison one. "You're younger than the pictures," she said.

"The pictures are accurate," Buffy said. "I'm actually this young."

"I know. That's what I said." She looked at Buffy's face with the specific kind of looking that Fritz did, the medical version of seeing, except that Allison's had more layers. "You're also tired. Not the way someone is tired who hasn't slept. The deeper kind."

"I've been busy."

"I know. Honor told me." She released her hands. "We'll fix the tired. Come with me."

Honor's expression behind Allison's shoulder was the expression of a daughter watching someone she loves do something completely characteristic and finding it exactly right.

Allison's home was the home of someone who had been somewhere long enough to make it exactly what they wanted it to be, not large but complete, with the specific quality of a space where serious work and serious living happened in the same rooms without conflict. Books and instruments and the tools of medical research were present alongside the evidence of a life that was also a life, art on the walls and plants that were alive and a kitchen that Buffy could smell from the front door.

"Kitchen first," Allison said. "Then I'll show you the rest."

The kitchen produced, over the next ninety minutes, the most significant meal Buffy had eaten since her mother's kitchen two and a half years and two thousand years ago, which was a comparison she made in the middle of the third course and then held quietly because it was hers and didn't need to be said.

Allison watched her eat with the frank pleasure of someone who cooked seriously and was watching someone receive it seriously. "More," she said, twice, when Buffy's plate was empty, and when Buffy said I eat a lot she said I know, Honor told me, which was what people said to Buffy regularly now.

It never got old.

Over dinner Allison talked about Beowulf the way people talked about places they'd lived long enough to have a relationship with rather than just a location in. She talked about its medical research tradition, about the Beowulf Code which governed genetic research and which she had opinions about that were complex and which she expressed without self-consciousness. She talked about the political situation, the League relationship, the things happening in the Solarian system that she followed carefully and that connected to the war in ways she traced with the specific intelligence of someone who paid attention to more than her immediate specialty.

And she asked questions.

Not the forty-seven thousand. The ones that got to the point.

"The record you opened," she said. "The information in it. Fritz's preliminary notes describe it as received rather than read." She looked at Buffy. "What's the difference?"

"Reading is sequential," Buffy said. "Information in a linear structure that you move through from one end to the other. What I received was�" she looked for the word� "simultaneous. Like the information was complete and I received the whole thing at once and then spent time afterward sorting what I'd received."

"Like a gestalt."

"Yes. Except with content that was specific rather than impressionistic."

Allison looked at her with the layered seeing. "Was it painful?"

"No." She thought about it. "It was disorienting. There was a period after when I wasn't completely sure where I ended and the content began." She paused. "Carson pulled me back. Having someone in the room who knew me helped."

"It would." Allison looked at the table and then back up. "The research I've been doing since Honor told me you were coming. The dimensional interface theory, which I'd read because Haverstock published preliminary notes six months ago and I know Michael Haverstock and I follow his work. And the Slayer lineage documentation, which Carson Clinkscales sent me copies of." She paused. "I have a hypothesis."

Buffy looked at her.

"The healing factor," Allison said. "Fritz has characterized it as anomalous but hasn't identified a mechanism. The enhanced physical capabilities � same. The sensory processing. The connection to the lineage through the dream-space." She paused. "These aren't supernatural in the sense of being outside physics. They're physics we don't have a complete framework for yet. But I think the framework exists."

"The dimensional interface," Buffy said.

"The dimensional interface. And specifically what happens at the interface to an organism that has a stable, heritable connection to it." She held Buffy's gaze. "The Slayer line is what it is because something at the interface created a stable heritable modification in an ancestral individual. The modification has been expressing itself in every generation since." She paused. "Which means it's in the genome. Which means I can find it."

Buffy held her gaze for a moment.

"You want to study me," she said.

"I want to study the modification," Allison said. "You are the most accessible and thoroughly documented example of its full expression. But the modification is what I'm after, not you specifically." She paused. "With your permission. Everything with your permission."

"Fritz has been saying that for months."

"Fritz is correct." She paused. "I have equipment here that Fritz doesn't have and methods he hasn't considered and thirty years of experience in exactly this category of problem." She paused. "I can help you understand what you are."

Buffy thought about this. She thought about the Council, which had studied the Slayer as an asset to be optimized rather than a person to be understood. She thought about the difference between those two things and how she read it in the way Allison was asking.

"Yes," she said. "With Fritz coordinating and me reviewing everything."

"Of course." Allison looked at her with something that was satisfaction and something else, something more personal. "Thank you."

"Don't thank me yet," Buffy said, without thinking.

Allison almost smiled. "You sound like the Queen."

"She says that too," Buffy said. "I've been spending time with her."

"I know," Allison said. "Honor told me."

Honor, across the table, had the expression of someone who had been listening to this conversation with complete attention and was now feeling something she wasn't going to put into words, and Nimitz on her shoulder was looking at Buffy and Allison with the green-eyed satisfaction of something that had assessed a situation and found it exactly right.

Beowulf had three days of preliminary work before the other thing.

The three days were Allison and Fritz, who had arrived on a later ship, in Allison's research facility doing the things that Allison and Fritz did in a research context, which was proceed with rigorous systematic precision and argue about methodology in ways that were professionally respectful and entirely serious. Haverstock was present by remote link because Haverstock had opinions about the interface question that he wanted to contribute directly. Petrakis had sent notes.

She was a patient, which was a role she'd accepted and which she was getting better at accepting, the specific discipline of holding still while people who knew things she didn't used those things on her behalf.

She was also doing her own work in the evenings, walking the parts of Beowulf that were walkable in the specific way she walked new places, with the Slayer sense at its mapping register and the Keeper's attention directed in the way that said relevant and she was building a picture.

The picture was what she'd expected and more detailed than she'd expected.

Something very old was in the infrastructure of this civilization. Not obviously. Not in any way that would be visible to the ordinary processes of an eleven-hundred-year-old society. But present in the specific way of things that had adapted to institutional structures long enough to become structural themselves, not parasitic exactly, more like symbiotic in a relationship that served one party considerably more than the other.

The something was not a vampire.

She kept returning to this because it was the thing she had the least framework for. The active supernatural threats she'd encountered in her career had been in categories she had names for: vampires, demons, hellgods, various arcane entities with specific operating principles she could research in the Watchers' journals. This was in a category she didn't have a name for. The Keeper registered it in a way that was different from how it registered vampires or the residue of the Hellmouth or the mystical energy in the lineage itself, a different register entirely.

On the third evening she told Honor.

They were on the terrace of Allison's home in the specific quality of Beowulf's late evening, which was different from Manticore's late evening in ways she was cataloguing. Honor had a glass of something and Nimitz was on the railing and the city was doing its evening configuration below them and Buffy sat across from Honor and told her what the Keeper was registering and what she was reading from the walks.

Honor listened.

"Not a vampire," she said, when Buffy finished.

"No."

"Then what?"

"I don't know yet." She paused. "The Keeper knows. Or knows something I don't have access to yet. I can feel it knowing." She paused. "Which is frustrating."

"Can you ask it directly?"

"I can try. The communication isn't�" she stopped. "It's like trying to ask someone a question in a language you're still learning. I can feel it respond but I can't always read the response with precision." She paused. "Haverstock and Prieto are working on the interface mapping. If the map gets more detailed maybe the communication gets more precise."

Honor looked at the city. "Should we be concerned about the current approach? If something here is aware of what you are�"

"I've been thinking about that," Buffy said. "I don't think it knows I'm here yet. Whatever it is, it's not reading the way the older vampires read, which was active awareness of the Slayer sense. This registers as�" she tried to find the word� "less present. Like something that doesn't need to actively monitor because it's been undisturbed long enough to have stopped expecting disturbance."

"Complacent," Honor said.

"As much as something eleven hundred years old can be complacent." She looked at the city. "I don't want to disturb it before I understand it. If it leaves Beowulf because I showed up, I lose the chance to understand what it is and what it's doing here."

"So we're in the observation phase."

"We're in the observation phase." She paused. "Allison is helping with that too. She knows this system. She has relationships I can use without those relationships being connected to me directly."

Honor looked at her. "You've been planning this."

"I've been thinking about it since the Grayson operation," she said. "Thinking is planning with lower commitment." She paused. "Allison is�" she tried to find the right word� "she's exactly what I needed here. I didn't know that until I was sitting across from her at dinner."

"She has that effect," Honor said, in the tone she used when she was talking about her mother, the layered warmth that was both personal and precise.

"How did you become who you are," Buffy said, "when she's this much?"

Honor looked at her. Something moved in her face. "Slowly," she said. "And she made it possible rather than impossible. There's a difference." She paused. "She never asked me to be her. She asked me to be whatever I was going to be and then she was interested in it." She paused. "That's the harder version of love. Interested rather than invested. She's always been interested."

Buffy held that.

She thought about Joyce Summers in the lit window of a house she would never see again, who had been scared of the Slayer part and who had been her mother anyway, who had been both of those things simultaneously without pretending one of them away.

"Mine too," she said.

Honor looked at her.

"She was scared of it," Buffy said. "My mom. She didn't want it to be true. She spent about a year trying to pretend it wasn't and then she stopped and she was just�there. Scared and there at the same time." She paused. "I think that was harder than what Allison did."

"Probably," Honor said. "Both are love. Different shapes."

"Different shapes," Buffy agreed.

They sat in the Beowulf evening and the city did its things and Nimitz was on the railing being present in the way treecats were present, completely and without apology.

After a while Honor said, "She would be proud of you."

Buffy looked at her.

"Your mother," Honor said. "What you're doing. What you're building." She held Buffy's gaze. "She would be proud."

Buffy sat with this.

It landed the way certain true things landed, not with the impact of surprise but with the weight of something she'd known and needed to hear confirmed. She sat with it and let it settle and when it had settled it was just present, warm and specific and hers.

"Yeah," she said. "I think so too."

On the fourth day Allison's contact produced the thing they'd been waiting for.

The contact was a woman named Dr. Ingrid Tomas who held a position in Beowulf's Biological Survey Institute that was her official function and also, as Allison had established over fifteen years of professional relationship, a secondary function that involved the kind of awareness that Usher's people had on Manticore and that the Grayson contacts had on Grayson. The informal infrastructure.

Tomas came to Allison's home on the fourth afternoon and sat across from Buffy with the expression of someone who had prepared for this meeting and was now discovering that preparation was insufficient.

She said, "I've been aware of the anomaly in our institutional structure for eleven years. We call it the Constant. It's been in the record under various names for as long as there are records." She paused. "What it is, we have never determined. What it does�" she stopped.

"Tell me what you think it does," Buffy said.

"We think it maintains certain conditions," Tomas said carefully. "Not actively. Not in ways that are visible as action. More like�" she paused� "like a standing arrangement. Conditions that favor certain outcomes over others. A bias in the probability distribution." She looked at Buffy. "Eleven hundred years of subtle influence on the oldest research civilization in human space."

Buffy thought about what that meant. Beowulf's medical research tradition, which had shaped the development of every inhabited system in human space. The Beowulf Code, which governed genetic research across the League and beyond. The specific distribution of knowledge and capability that had accumulated in this system over eleven centuries.

"What conditions?" she said.

"We don't fully know," Tomas said. "That's the honest answer. We know the Constant exists and we know it's old and we know it's present in ways we can trace in the historical record but we don't know its objective." She paused. "Until now possibly."

Buffy looked at her.

"You're connected to something that understands it," Tomas said. "The Keeper, as you call it. Allison told me." She paused. "Does it know what the Constant is?"

Buffy checked the Keeper's accompanying quality.

It was different from usual. More directed, more focused, the attention not of something encountering something familiar but of something encountering something it had specific knowledge of and had been waiting for the moment to convey.

"Yes," she said. "It knows." She paused. "I don't yet. We're working on the communication." She looked at Tomas. "Give me what you have. All of it. Every year of the record. I'll cross-reference it against what the Keeper is giving me and we'll figure it out together."

Tomas looked at Allison.

Allison said: "Give her everything."

Tomas gave her everything.

She spent two days in Allison's study with the files and the Keeper's attention and the specific kind of work that was receiving and comparing and building toward a picture. She didn't leave the house much. Allison brought food and didn't ask how it was going until the evening of the second day when she asked once and accepted the answer we're not there yet without pushing.

She was getting there.

The Keeper's communication was not becoming language. But it was becoming more readable, the way something you'd been listening to for months in a foreign language started producing comprehension not of words but of intent. She couldn't have transcribed what the Keeper was telling her. She could map it against the historical record and find where the map corresponded and where it didn't.

What she assembled was this:

The Constant was old enough to predate Beowulf's colonization. It had been here before humans arrived in this system, established in the dimensional interface in the way the Keeper was established at a different interface, a presence at the boundary between dimensions that was organized and intentional and that had been here when the first humans arrived and had been present ever since.

It was not malevolent. The Keeper's quality when she tried to read the Constant's nature was not the quality it brought to the vampire network, the specific register of threat. It was something different. Something that the Keeper seemed to recognize not as an enemy but as a different category of thing entirely.

Old. Waiting. Patient in a different way than the Keeper's patience.

The Constant had been influencing Beowulf's research direction, she was fairly confident of that from the historical record cross-reference. The influence was subtle and long-term and aimed at something she could see the direction of without seeing the destination.

The direction was the dimensional interface.

Beowulf's research tradition, over eleven centuries, had been slowly moving toward a complete understanding of the interface between dimensions. The work Haverstock was doing was the most recent iteration of a line that went back centuries, each generation building on the previous one in ways that had the character of directed progress rather than random exploration.

Something had been guiding Beowulf toward understanding the interface.

She held this for a long time.

She went out on the third morning and walked and thought about it and let the Keeper's attention move with her through the city and sat at a waterfront that was the Beowulf version of what waterfronts were and looked at the water.

Then she called Haverstock on the communicator.

"Your interface theory," she said. "The fifteen years of work. Does it feel like you developed it or discovered it?"

A pause. "That's an unusual question."

"I know. Answer it anyway."

Another pause, longer. "Discovered," he said finally. "Always discovered. Like the framework was already there and I was finding it." He paused. "I've always assumed that was the nature of physics. Physics is what it is regardless of whether we understand it."

"Yes," she said. "That's true." She paused. "What if it was also true that the framework was already there and something was making it easier to find?"

A silence.

"That's a significant implication," he said.

"I know." She looked at the water. "Michael. I need you on Beowulf."
