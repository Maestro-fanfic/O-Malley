The treecat's name, when it chose to have one, was something she couldn't hear directly.

Treecats named themselves in their own language, the complex combination of sound and empathic communication that humans couldn't fully perceive, and what humans called the naming was the approximation, the nearest thing in human sound to whatever the treecat was expressing about itself. Nimitz's name was the human word that came closest to whatever Nimitz communicated about himself, and over the months of her relationship with the treecat in the Tremaine property's canopy she'd asked enough people who knew enough about treecats to have a working sense of the process.

She asked Honor on a Tuesday in late winter when Honor was briefly on Manticore for reasons the war still required.

Honor said: "They choose. The human approximation of the name is usually something the treecat finds acceptable. The communication goes both directions." She paused. "What are you getting from it?"

Buffy thought about the quality the treecat had when it was most directly communicating, which was more frequent now that the approach had become a regular feature of the Sphinx visits. "Something that's�" she looked for the word� "like watching. But not passive watching. Watching that's also participating." She paused. "Like a vigil."

Honor looked at her.

Buffy looked back.

"Vigil," she said again, testing the weight of it as a name.

Nimitz, from Honor's shoulder, made a sound that was not his usual sound, something different, a note she didn't have a name for.

"What is that?" she said.

"He's�" Honor paused. "He says that's right."

She thought about the treecat in the canopy who had been watching her property and watching her for a year, who had followed her across the snowy garden, who came closer each visit and received her reading each time she offered it, and she thought about the word vigil and the thing she'd built and the quality the treecat expressed when it was most directly present.

"Vigil," she said. To the canopy and to the room and to the Keeper and to the sense of the lineage running through her.

The treecat in her memory of the most recent morning came through the channel at the specific angle of recognition.

"Yes," she said. "Okay. Vigil."

Honor looked at her with the layered expression. "You've been bonded," she said.

"I don't think that's what's happening."

"I don't think you get to decide what's happening," Honor said. Not unkindly. With the precision of someone who had direct experience. "The treecat decides."

She thought about this for a long moment. She thought about what it meant, the specific relationship she'd read about in the briefings she'd done on her first weeks on Manticore, the bond that treecats chose and that was understood in Sphinx culture as one of the most significant things that could happen between a person and a treecat.

She thought about the Keeper, which was a consciousness that accompanied her through the channel. She thought about Nimitz, who was Honor's and whose bond was visible in the quality of everything Honor did.

She thought about the treecat who had been watching her property for a year and who had a quality she'd just named Vigil and who had been coming closer every visit.

"What does it require?" she said. "The bond."

"Nothing," Honor said. "It's not a transaction. It's a recognition." She paused. "The treecat recognizes something in you that it wants to be in proximity to. You recognize something in the treecat that you want to be in proximity to." She paused. "The rest follows from the recognition."

Buffy held this.

She went back to Sphinx the following weekend.

She stood in the garden in the early morning with the snow still on the ground and the winter doing its last cold before the turn toward spring, and she waited.

Vigil came down from the canopy.

Not all the way. To the low branch, the branch it had been using as the approach point for months, and it looked at her with the green eyes that were doing what they always did.

She looked back.

She said, very quietly: "I know what you're doing."

The treecat's whiskers moved.

She said: "I don't have a framework for this yet."

Vigil made the sound. The low questioning one.

She answered it the way she always answered it, by being present and readable, and this time she added something she hadn't added before. She opened the channel slightly, not to the Constant, not to the lineage in its full expression, but to the specific ambient quality of what she was, the signature of the thing that treecats apparently read and that Vigil had been reading for a year.

Vigil came the rest of the way down.

It sat on the wall beside her at the edge of the garden and they were both there, in the winter morning, and it put one true-hand on her wrist the way Nimitz had done once, briefly, the way treecats touched when the touching was communicating something that couldn't be communicated otherwise.

She held still.

The touch lasted approximately ten seconds.

Then Vigil withdrew it and looked at her and she looked at it and the winter was around them and the snow was the snow and the world was doing its world things.

"Okay," she said.

Vigil's tail tip moved.

She reached out and scratched between its ears, which she'd been wanting to do for a year, and it permitted this, which was the word, permitted, deliberate and clear about it, and she thought: yes. This is what this is.

She told Scotty that evening.

He looked at her and then out the window toward the canopy, where Vigil was presumably present and doing whatever Vigil did in the hours between interactions.

He said: "Nimitz chose Honor."

"Yes."

He said: "Is this the same thing?"

She thought about how to answer that. "I don't know if it's identical," she said. "Honor and Nimitz have a specific quality between them that I've been watching for over a year and it's�" she looked for the word� "complete. In the way of something that's both of them fully." She paused. "What Vigil is doing feels like the beginning of that rather than the same thing at the same stage." She paused. "But the direction is the same."

He looked at her steadily. "Then the property is going to need some reconfiguring," he said. "If a treecat is going to be spending more time in it."

She looked at him.

He said: "There are things treecats prefer. Structures they can access at the canopy level. Certain kinds of perches. Sascha probably knows." He paused. "Or I can ask."

She held his gaze.

"You're not surprised," she said.

"I've been watching Vigil watch you for a year," he said. "I had some time to prepare."

She almost smiled. "Ask Sascha," she said.

He almost smiled back. "Already planning to."

Spring came to Sphinx.

She was there for it, which was the version of the property she hadn't seen yet, and it was as different from the winter as the autumn had been from what she'd expected, its own specific thing, the landscape coming back from what winter had done to it in a way that was neither the spectacular color of autumn nor the bone-bare clarity of winter but something else, the urgent green of things that had been waiting and were now doing what they'd been waiting to do.

She planted the garden.

Not by herself. Sascha was involved, substantially, in the planning and much of the execution, but the choices were hers, the specific plants chosen for reasons that were partly practical and partly personal and partly the question of what kind of garden she wanted to be in.

She planted things that were Sphinx things and things that were as close as she could get to California things and things that were in between. She planted the Grayson variety of something that was approximately rosemary, because that was what was available and because the shape of a rosemary plant was something she wanted in the garden regardless of the specific chemistry of the leaves.

She planted and Vigil supervised from the canopy and occasionally from the wall, watching with the specific quality of interest that treecats brought to things they found relevant.

Scotty was between postings for an extended period, a gap in the operational demands of the war's changed shape that had given him more continuity in one place than he'd had in the previous two years. He worked from the property when he could, the naval administrative work that followed officers everywhere, and he was present in the specific way of someone who had decided this was where they were and was fully in it.

They had an argument in the second week of the planting.

Not a large one. The kind that came from two people with strong opinions about specific things encountering a specific thing they had different opinions about, which was the placement of a particular structural element in the garden that she wanted in one location and he wanted in another.

The argument was resolved by Sascha, who appeared during it with the expression of someone who had seen this argument before with different people about different gardens and who had a view, and whose view was correct, and who expressed it without preamble.

After Sascha left they were quiet for a moment and then she said: "She's right."

"Yes," he said.

"We should have asked her first."

"Yes," he said again.

She looked at the location Sascha had indicated. "It's better there."

"It is," he agreed.

She looked at him. He looked at her.

She said: "I want to argue with you sometimes."

He said: "I know. I want to argue with you sometimes too."

"Good," she said. "It's going well so far."

"The argument or the garden?"

"Both," she said.

He almost smiled. She almost smiled back.

Vigil, in the canopy, made a sound she was beginning to interpret as amusement.

"Don't," she said.

The sound persisted.

Allison arrived in Manticore in late spring.

Not just for a visit. With the specific quantity of personal effects and research equipment that said she was not visiting but relocating, the organized deliberateness of someone who had made a decision and was executing it with characteristic thoroughness.

Honor was at the docking facility when Allison's ship came in and Buffy was beside Honor and Nimitz was on Honor's shoulder and the reunion was the reunion of people who loved each other and had been in different places for long enough that the reunion had weight.

She stood slightly to the side and gave it space.

Then Allison turned to her and said, "You asked me to come," in the tone of someone presenting a fact rather than making a statement about the asking, and Buffy said, "I did," in the same register, and then Allison looked at her for a long moment and did something she hadn't done the first time, which was put her arms around her.

Buffy held still for a moment in the way of someone receiving unexpected contact and recalibrating, and then she returned it, and it was the hug of someone who had decided she was a person worth hugging rather than an interesting research subject and Buffy felt the distinction clearly.

She appreciated it.

She pulled back and said: "How was the transit?"

"Long," Allison said. "And productive. I wrote two papers." She looked around the docking facility. "Is there food? I've been on a ship for ten days."

"Allison," Honor said, with the warmth that was specific to her mother.

"Honor, I've been on a ship for ten days. I want real food." She looked at Buffy. "Your quarters. Is that still where you eat an enormous amount of things?"

"My quarters are where I live," Buffy said. "There's food."

"Good," Allison said. "Let's go."

They went.

~~~

Allison's integration into the Vigil's research structure happened quickly in the way that things integrated quickly when the structure was ready for them and the person was ready for it, the two readinesses meeting and producing momentum.

She and Fritz had been in correspondence for months and the correspondence had produced a collaboration that was already running before Allison arrived, the specific efficiency of people who knew what they were doing and had been coordinating their approaches so that the arrival was not a beginning but a continuation.

Haverstock was more initially cautious, not because he doubted Allison's capability but because his work existed at the intersection of physics and biology in ways that he'd developed his own frameworks for and that Allison's approach, which was more directly biological, required some negotiation with.

The negotiation happened over three dinners, because Allison negotiated scientific methodology at dinner rather than in formal settings, which was a thing Buffy had learned about her and which she suspected was deliberate rather than incidental.

After the third dinner Haverstock said, in Buffy's hearing: "She's right about the channel interface. The biological substrate is what I've been trying to account for without having the tools for it."

Allison said: "I've been trying to account for the interface physics without having the framework for it. Now we both have what the other had."

Haverstock said: "Yes."

They went back to work.

She watched this with the specific satisfaction of someone who had placed two people in proximity to each other because she thought they needed to be in proximity and had been right, which was one of the better feelings available.

She told Scotty about it that evening.

He said: "How often are you right about putting people together?"

She thought about it. "More than I expected," she said. "I used to not trust my read on that. I thought I was bad at the people part." She paused. "I wasn't bad at it. I was doing it without infrastructure and the lack of infrastructure made it look like failure when it was actually just unsupported success."

He looked at her.

"That's a generous reading," he said.

"It's an accurate reading," she said. "I checked." She paused. "The Scoobies. We were good at being together. What we were missing was the structure that would have made being good at it less costly." She paused. "The cost was real. I'm not arguing with the cost. But the capability was always there."

He held her gaze for a moment.

"You've been thinking about them," he said.

"I think about them," she said. "Not the way I used to, which was the kind of thinking that was grief wearing the clothes of memory. More like�" she looked for the word� "accounting for them. Making sure they're included in what I'm building. Not personally, I can't do that, they're not here and they're two thousand years gone and that's a real thing." She paused. "But the work I'm doing is for everyone who was ever in the position they were in. Standing next to a Slayer without a framework for it. Doing the work without the support." She paused. "I'm building the support. For them, in retrospect, and for everyone after."

Scotty was quiet for a moment.

"That's not grief," he said.

"No," she said. "It's not grief anymore." She held his gaze. "It's love. You can do things for people who are gone. The doing doesn't require their presence."

He held her gaze.

She held his.

After a while he said: "I want to tell you something."

She waited.

He said: "I've been thinking about what we're doing here. On Sphinx. The property and the garden and the way it's becoming a place we're building rather than a place we're visiting." He paused. "I want that to be clear. Not just happening because we're here and it's convenient." He met her eyes. "I want to build this with you."

She held his gaze.

She thought about the garden and the winter and the argument about the placement and Sascha's correct answer and Vigil in the canopy. She thought about the city on Manticore that had become home and the work that was in it and the people who were part of it and this, the property in the Sphinx northern range that was becoming something alongside all of that.

"Yes," she said. "I want that too."

He reached across and took her hand.

Vigil, from the canopy, made the sound that she'd come to interpret as approval.

She looked up at the canopy and said: "I know."

The approval persisted.

She looked back at Scotty.

"Come to the garden tomorrow," she said. "Sascha says the Sphinx equivalent of the rosemary is coming in."

He said: "I'll be there."

She looked at his hand holding hers and thought about the garden and the winter bones of the trees and the spectacular autumn and the spring that was doing what springs did and the summer that was coming after, and she thought about all the seasons of a place and how knowing a place through its seasons was how you came to know it, and she thought about seasons of a life and how knowing a person through the seasons of what they were becoming was how you came to know them.

She was going to know this person through many seasons.

She thought: good.

She thought: yes.
