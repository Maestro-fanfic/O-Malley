Grayson was not what she'd expected.

She'd expected what Carson had described, cold and forested, the northern range and the particular quality of the light he'd described, but she arrived at the capital first, Mayhew, which was different from the capital of Manticore in ways that went beyond the obvious and which she needed several hours of direct experience to begin to characterize.

It was serious, was the first word she found. Not grim. Serious. The architecture and the people moving through it and the specific quality of the attention they paid to each other, all of it had the weight of a civilization that had survived something genuinely difficult and had organized itself around that survival in ways that were still structurally present even generations after.

She'd read the history. She knew about the near-extinction and the recovery and the specific shape of the Grayson character that had emerged from that history. Knowing was different from being in the middle of it.

The other thing she noticed, immediately and then continuously, was how people looked at her.

Not the way they'd looked at her in Manticore, which was the recognition look, the I know who you are from the news coverage look. This was something older. People on the street didn't know her face but some of them � older people, people from families with deep roots, people with the specific look of the more committed believers � registered something when they saw her that she couldn't quite read.

"They feel it," Carson said, walking beside her. "Some of them. The older Graysons especially. The awareness that was in the original tradition." He paused. "They don't know what they're feeling. But they feel something."

She thought about what that meant, an entire civilization with a vestigial awareness of the lineage built into its religious tradition. "Is that going to be a problem?"

"For the operation, no. For everything else�" he paused� "we'll manage."

The Clinkscales family home was outside the capital proper, which in Grayson terms meant twenty minutes in an air car and in physical terms meant a landscape transition from urban to something that was not quite rural and not quite suburban, a specific Grayson configuration that she didn't have a category for. Large properties. Old trees that were the Grayson version of old trees, different from the Manticoran ones in the specific ways that parallel evolution produced. A quality of space between structures that said people here had decided space mattered.

The house was large in the way of houses that had accumulated additions rather than been designed at full size, each addition visible in the architecture as a layer of decision. It had a garden that was clearly a serious garden rather than a decorative one, planted with intention and tended with care, and it smelled of things she had no names for that were the Grayson equivalent of the smells she associated with gardening, close enough to be recognizable.

Carson's mother came out to meet them.

She was a small woman with dark hair going gray and the quality of someone who had been managing a complicated household for decades and had arrived at a state of competence so complete that it had become ease. She looked at Buffy with the frank assessment of a Grayson woman who had decided that the situation required directness and said, "You're thinner than Carson described."

"I've been eating more," Buffy said.

"Not enough more." She looked at her son. "You didn't tell me she was this young."

"I told you she was seventeen, Mother."

"Telling me isn't the same as seeing." She turned back to Buffy and did something that Buffy hadn't expected, which was reach out and briefly touch her face in the way of someone confirming the reality of something they'd heard about for a long time. "Come in," she said. "There's food."

There was food. There was a significant amount of food, distributed across a kitchen that was the heart of the house in the way that kitchens in houses that were actually lived in always were, with the accumulated evidence of regular serious cooking in every surface and implement.

The lamb dish Carson had described was on the stove.

She ate three portions while Carson's mother watched with the expression of someone filing information for future use and Carson watched with the expression of someone who had been watching this for months and was not surprised but was still fond of watching it.

Thomas Clinkscales came in during the third portion.

He was eighty-three and moved with the specific deliberateness of someone whose body required more management than it had used to and who had made peace with this without being resigned to it. He was tall and had been taller, the compression of years visible in the way he carried himself, and his face was the face of someone who had spent a very long time thinking about something specific and had the lines to show it.

He stopped in the doorway and looked at her.

She set down her fork and looked at him.

The room was quiet.

He crossed it with his deliberate careful walk and stood in front of her and looked at her for a long moment with eyes that were very dark and very clear, and then he did what Carson had said he would do, which was that his face did the thing that it did, the specific configuration of a man who had been right about something for thirty years and was now standing in the direct presence of that rightness.

He didn't weep immediately. He held himself together with the controlled dignity that Carson also had and that she was coming to understand was a Clinkscales quality rather than a Grayson one specifically, and he said, "Lady Buffy."

"Mr. Clinkscales," she said.

He sat down. His wife brought him tea that he didn't look at. He looked at Buffy.

"My father told me," he said. "When I was a boy. He sat me down the way his father had sat him down, and he said: the family has a responsibility. There is a lineage. It is real. We keep the record and we watch for the signs and when the time comes we will know it." He paused. "I believed him. I've believed it every day since. There were days when believing was�" he stopped. "When the thing you believe has been dormant for long enough, believing becomes a practice rather than a response. You practice it every day regardless of whether it responds." He paused. "It responded."

She looked at him. She thought about what she wanted to say, which was what she actually wanted to say rather than what was appropriate to say, and she said it. "I read the record you kept. Carson showed me." She paused. "It's the most complete documentation I've encountered. In this century or the last one." She paused again. "You did something that was necessary and that didn't look necessary for thirty years. That's the hardest kind of work. I wanted you to know that I know that."

He held her gaze for a moment.

Then he wept.

Not dramatically. Quietly, in the way of someone who had been holding something for a long time and had simply been given permission to set it down, and she sat with it and didn't try to address it because it didn't need to be addressed, it needed to be witnessed, and she witnessed it.

Carson's mother put her hand on her husband's shoulder.

Carson sat quietly to Buffy's left.

After a while Thomas Clinkscales took a breath and looked at her again with the clear dark eyes, clearer now. "The girls Carson told me about," he said. "You've met them?"

"I have an appointment tomorrow," she said. "Both of them. You'll be there, Carson said?"

"If you'll allow it." He paused. "I've been watching for them for years. I didn't know what to say when I found them."

"I'll say the first things," she said. "You can say the things that come after. The lineage's history. The record." She paused. "The context I didn't have when I was called. The thing that would have helped me most." She looked at him. "You know the record better than anyone alive. That matters."

He straightened slightly. Not performatively. The straightening of someone receiving a task they recognize as theirs. "Yes," he said. "All right."

The Grayson candidates were different from each other in most of the specific ways that people were different from each other and shared the things that the lineage shared, the physical profile and the dreams and the quality of someone navigating a gap between what they knew about themselves and what they were.

The first was fourteen, which was young, the youngest she'd encountered, and sat in the Clinkscales sitting room with Thomas beside her and her own family present and did the thing that fourteen-year-olds did in serious situations that adults had organized around them, which was be more present and more alert than the adults expected and ask questions they hadn't prepared answers for.

The question she asked that no one had prepared an answer for was: will it hurt?

Buffy looked at her and thought about the honest answer, which was yes, some of it, and the context the honest answer needed, which was considerable.

She said: some of it will hurt. I'm not going to tell you otherwise. But the hurt is not the point of it and it's not all of it, and you'll have people with you, which I didn't always have, and that makes the hurt different.

The girl looked at Thomas, who was beside her with the dignity of thirty years of faith made present, and then back at Buffy.

She said: what's the good part?

Buffy thought about running in the dark. About the operation on Hades and the boarding of the Eirene Constantia and Yeva's face when the explanation arrived. About Scotty's letter from the Talbott Cluster that had arrived two days ago and that she'd read twice.

She said: you're enough for something that matters. That's the good part.

The girl sat with this.

Then she said: okay. Tell me the rest.

The second candidate was seventeen and had the controlled watchful quality of someone who had been managing something alone for long enough that alone had become her default. She listened to Buffy with a stillness that said she was receiving and filing rather than performing reception, and when Buffy finished she said: I knew something was different about me. I thought I was broken.

You're not broken, Buffy said. I know that's what I'm supposed to say and I'm saying it because it's true.

The girl looked at her. How do you know?

Because I thought I was too, Buffy said. For a long time. And I was wrong.

She held the girl's gaze.

I was wrong, she said again.

The girl looked away and then back. She said: what do I do now?

We figure it out, Buffy said. Together.

Thomas Clinkscales wept again on the way home, quietly, in the air car, and Carson sat beside him and Buffy sat across and watched the Grayson landscape go past the windows, the old trees and the space between properties and the sky that was slightly the wrong shade of blue in the specific way that all skies were slightly the wrong shade.

She thought about the fourteen-year-old asking what the good part was.

She thought about Yeva and the four Manticoran candidates and the two on Grayson and the ones she hadn't found yet, across Grayson and Manticore and the Alliance and beyond that, everywhere the lineage had scattered in two thousand years of forgetting.

She thought: this is the thing that matters.

Not instead of the other things. Alongside them. The work that was going to be present regardless of what else was happening, the work that would still be there when the war ended and when the political situation resolved and when all the immediate things became the things that had happened.

The Keeper was present.

She felt it orient toward the landscape around her the way it oriented toward her when she was focused on something relevant, the specific quality of attention it brought to the work rather than to her specifically.

She looked out the window.

The trees were the wrong trees and the sky was the wrong sky and she was here, in this air car, in this century, on this planet, doing this specific thing.

She was here.

The Grayson operation took four days to plan and one night to execute.

Castellan arrived on the second day, which was also the day Buffy met with Usher's Grayson contacts, two people who worked in a structure that had no official name and whose relationship to the official Grayson security apparatus was lateral rather than integrated, the kind of parallel institutional awareness that was apparently a feature of civilizations that had been dealing with things they couldn't fully categorize for long enough to develop informal infrastructure for it.

They knew about the node. They'd known about it for longer than the Landing situation, which told her something about the node's age and establishment. They'd been managing it in the same way the Landing operation had been managed, which was documentation and monitoring and the specific frustration of an awareness without a capability.

She told them what she was going to do.

They gave her everything they had, which was more than she'd expected and better organized than she'd hoped, the specific gift of people who had spent years preparing information for exactly this kind of transfer.

Castellan spent a day reviewing the site information and came back with observations and one significant recommendation about the approach that Buffy incorporated because Castellan was right and being right mattered more than whose idea it was.

On the third day she did what she'd learned to do before operations, which was to sleep and train and eat and be present with the people around her rather than the operation ahead of her, because the operation ahead of her was as prepared as it was going to be and the people around her were what they were.

She ate dinner with the Clinkscales family that night.

Thomas was there and Carson's mother and two of Carson's sisters who had returned home for reasons related to the news that something significant was happening, which in a family like this moved through the structure quickly even without explicit communication. The sisters were different from each other, the elder one with a quality like Thomas and the younger one with a quality like Carson's mother, and they looked at Buffy with the combined assessment of two women who loved their brother and wanted to know if the situation was what it appeared to be.

She was apparently what it appeared to be, because by the end of dinner the elder sister had asked if she wanted to learn the Grayson card game that was apparently a family tradition and the younger one had found and played a piece of music on an instrument Buffy had no name for that was the Grayson equivalent of a piano in that it was large and keyboard-based and produced a sound that was close to what a piano produced in the specific way of parallel development toward the same function.

It was a good dinner.

She thought about her mother's kitchen and let the thought be present without being displaced by it, and the Clinkscales kitchen held what it held around her, warm and inhabited and full of the evidence of a family that had been a family for long enough to have a character that was the sum of all its accumulated decisions.

She went to sleep at ten and woke at one and went to work.

The node's consolidated position was a facility in an industrial area outside Mayhew's eastern limits, a storage and logistics facility that had been in legitimate use until approximately eight years ago when a transaction had transferred it to an ownership entity that Usher's contacts had spent considerable effort trying to trace and had traced as far as a Solarian legal construct that stopped being traceable at that point.

The facility had three structures and a perimeter that was functional rather than decorative and which had been augmented in ways that were consistent with a population that was being careful.

Being careful meant, among other things, that they knew she was coming.

She'd assumed this. A network that had been warned by the Landing situation and had consolidated would not be surprised by the arrival of the thing that had addressed Landing. The element of surprise was not available. What was available was being faster than their prepared response and better than their consolidated capability.

She went in from the east, which was the direction that was hardest to approach and therefore the direction the prepared response would weight least.

The first two were outside. Old enough to have some capability and young enough to have overestimated it, which was the combination she'd been hoping for when she'd made the eastern approach choice. She addressed them with the speed the situation required and moved to the first structure.

The first structure was empty of vampires and full of the evidence of recent occupancy, sleeping areas and the stored provisions that vampires who had humans operating for them sometimes kept for those humans, and she moved through it and went to the second.

The second structure was where eight of the remaining ten were, which was more than she'd faced in a consolidated position before but which was also the specific concentration that made the Keeper's advice � she'd started thinking of its pressure as advice � useful in a way she was still developing the practice of.

The advice, insofar as she could read it, was about sequence.

The Keeper understood something about the population's internal hierarchy that she couldn't read directly but could receive through the pressure, and the pressure said oldest first, and oldest first was counterintuitive from a conventional tactical standpoint because it committed you to the hardest engagement immediately before dealing with the remainder.

She trusted the advice.

The oldest one in the second structure was not the node's oldest � that was in the third structure, she could feel it, and it felt like something that had been here for a very long time. This one was old enough to be the oldest one she'd faced other than the Eirene Constantia's master and she went at it directly and with everything she had in the first three seconds because the first three seconds were when the tactical initiative was available and the time after that was when you were responding.

She won the first three seconds.

The remainder of the second structure took twelve minutes and cost her a cut to the right shoulder and a bruised rib she wouldn't fully assess until later and produced seven more instances of dust.

She went to the third structure.

The node's oldest had been here, she thought, since before the Eirene Constantia's established route. It was older than the master of the Eirene Constantia, which she hadn't expected. It was in its own space in the third structure, a space that bore the accumulated evidence of very long habitation in the way that the master's quarters on the ship had, the furniture from different eras and the objects that had been valuable for different reasons at different times.

It looked at her with the kind of eyes that had been looking at things for long enough to have the specific quality of accumulated looking, not tiredness exactly, something more like the weight of volume. A great deal had passed through those eyes.

It said: you're young.

She said: I know.

It said: I've been here since before your civilization reached the stars.

She held its gaze. I know that too.

It looked at her. How many of the others?

Nine, she said.

It was quiet for a moment. The weight of the accumulated looking doing whatever it did internally.

Then it said: I've been waiting for someone to come.

She looked at it. Why haven't you asked someone to?

A pause. Because what I am makes that impossible. In the way that what you are makes certain things impossible.

She thought about that.

Is there something you want me to know? she said.

It looked at her.

It said: the one above us. In the hierarchy. It's very old. Older than me. It doesn't know about the Keeper's Seal yet. If it did, what you found would already be gone.

She filed that.

It said: it's on Beowulf.

She said: why are you telling me this?

A long pause. Because I've been here long enough to be tired of what I am. And because you're the only one who could use the information.

She looked at it for a moment.

I'm going to give you a choice, she said, which surprised her as she was saying it and which the Keeper's accompanying presence received with something she could only call attention. You can keep existing. I know how to contain rather than eliminate. It would be �constrained. You wouldn't be able to feed. But you'd exist.

It looked at her.

No, it said. Thank you.

She thought about the master of the Eirene Constantia and the complicated last moments, the regret too old to be simple regret. She thought about what it was to have been something for long enough that not being it was more coherent than continuing.

She finished the work.

She stood in the third structure afterward and breathed through the rib and shoulder assessments and thought about Beowulf, which was a system she knew from the briefings, important, League-adjacent, the kind of place where something very old could be very established with significant resources available.

The Keeper's accompanying presence was very still in the way of something that had just received important information and was organizing its response.

She looked at the wall of the structure and thought about the scope of the thing ahead and thought about what she had and what she needed and the distance between those two.

She called Usher.

"The node is addressed," she said. "I have something else. When can you be here?"
