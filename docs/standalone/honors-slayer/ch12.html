Chapter 12 (3 of 54 in this book)

The third night she didn't go out.

She'd told Honor she would and then she sat in the shuttle at 2200 and her body wouldn't go. Not fear. She knew what fear felt like and this wasn't it. More like the particular heaviness that settled in after extended operations, the moment when the machine that was the Slayer's body registered the accumulated cost of three weeks of hypervigilance and decided to present the bill.

She sat with it for an hour and then she woke Fritz.

He was remarkably gracious about being woken at ten at night. He appeared in the shuttle hatchway with his hair disordered and his eyes already professional, which she was coming to understand was just how Fritz Montoya was built, professionally curious at all hours and in all conditions.

"Sit," she said, and he sat, and she said, "I need you to tell me what's happening."

"Medically?"

"Yes."

He looked at her the way he'd been looking at her for weeks, with the focused attention of someone cataloguing data and trying not to make it obvious. "Your baseline presentation has been improving steadily since you recovered full cognition. Motor function excellent. Reflexes significantly above human baseline and stable. Sleep quality�" he paused. "Less good."

"I'm not sleeping."

"You're sleeping four to five hours. That's less than your demonstrated need."

"Which is?"

"Seven to eight. Possibly more during periods of high physical demand." He paused. "The healing factor doesn't sleep. It's always running. It needs sleep-cycle biology to do some of its processing, the same way human immune function does. If you're not completing sleep cycles you're running the repair shop on reduced power."

She thought about the dreams. The Girl's memories. The running in the dark that felt more like joy than anything she'd felt in waking hours. "If I sleep more the dreams get longer."

"Are the dreams distressing?"

She considered how to answer that accurately. "No. They're someone else's memories. They're not bad. They're just�a lot. It's like being two people at once and I'm still figuring out where one ends and the other begins."

Fritz was quiet for a moment, making the small note in his personal log that she'd long since stopped trying to talk him out of. "The merging you described. The First Slayer's consciousness in proximity to yours."

"Inside mine. I think. I'm not a neuroscientist."

"Neither am I, technically, though I've done enough fieldwork." He looked at her steadily. "Has her presence affected your decision-making? Your sense of self?"

It was the question she'd been avoiding for two weeks. She sat with it and gave it the answer it deserved. "She's not making decisions. She's more like�a voice in the back. Very old. Very certain about some things. She gets louder when I'm in the field." She paused. "She was very loud the night I heard the prisoner."

"In what way?"

"She wanted me to go in and get them out immediately." She looked at her hands. "She doesn't really do the part where you sit with the question. She identifies a threat to the people she's protecting and she responds to it. The delay for planning is�" She searched for the word. "Uncomfortable. For her."

"But you waited."

"I've been doing this for two years. I've learned that the delay for planning usually saves lives that the immediate response would have spent." She looked up at Fritz. "But I want you to know that it's not purely academic. There's a voice in the back that's pushing. I'm managing it. I thought you should know."

Fritz nodded slowly. "Thank you for telling me." He made another note. "For what it's worth, from the outside you present as entirely coherent and in command of your decision-making. Whatever the internal experience is, it's not visible."

"Good." She stood. "Go back to sleep. Sorry for waking you."

"Don't be." He stood as well, and then said, in the same clinical tone he used for everything, which somehow made it easier to hear: "Lady Buffy. You've survived something that should not have been survivable and you're operating at a level that would be remarkable in a trained adult professional who hadn't been through any of what you've been through. The fact that you're managing it is�" he paused, and she saw him find the word� "not surprising to me, given what you are. But I want you to know that it's still a significant thing."

She looked at him.

"I'm keeping the notes," he said, "because I think someday someone is going to need to understand what happened here. To you. To all of us. And I want there to be a record that's accurate." He paused. "And because you're my patient and I pay attention to my patients."

She thought about doctors she'd known. The ones who'd patched her up in the dark in ways that couldn't be explained in the bright light of an ER. The ones who'd looked at wounds that healed wrong and pretended not to notice.

"Okay," she said. "Thank you."

She went back to her bunk and this time she let herself sleep and the Girl's memories took her running through a darkness so old it had never known a name and it felt like rest.

Four days later they went in.

The plan was this:

McKeon would lead a five-person team to the outer perimeter as a distraction element. Their job was to draw attention toward the south side of the compound at 0200, enough attention to pull the guard from the outbuilding, without triggering a full response. The method was controlled and deliberate and involved three of the camp's remaining explosive devices deployed at intervals to suggest a larger force than was actually present.

Buffy and Scotty would enter through the north perimeter gap at 0205, reach the outbuilding, extract the prisoner, and be back through the perimeter by 0220.

Fifteen minutes. Everything that happened in those fifteen minutes needed to happen quietly.

"Questions," Honor said, looking around the group.

"What's the signal if the distraction goes wrong?" That was LaFollet, standing at the back, who had not been included in the operational teams and whose feelings about this were visible if you knew how to read Grayson stillness.

"Standard abort. Three clicks on the communicator, everyone withdraws to rally point alpha." Honor's eyes moved to Buffy. "No exceptions."

Buffy didn't argue. She'd been thinking about the abort threshold since Scotty had asked her about it and she'd come to the conclusion that Honor was right, which was not a conclusion that came naturally to her but which she was developing a habit of reaching through a reasoning process that she could mostly follow.

At 0150 she and Scotty moved into position at the tree line north of the compound.

He moved well. She'd told him he was quiet and she'd meant it, but quiet in the camp was different from quiet in the jungle in the dark, where everything had a texture and pressure and moving through it without adding to the sum of its sounds was a specific skill. He was doing it. Not the way she did it, not with the unconscious completeness of two-plus years of training layered over whatever the Slayer sense added to the baseline, but with the deliberate competence of someone who had practiced and internalized and was now executing without the visible effort of execution.

She revised her assessment of him upward.

"Perimeter gap in four minutes," she said, barely voiced. The night-vision image through the gap in the canopy showed the compound in its ambient light, the regular sweep of the perimeter lights on their timer, the guards at their posts.

He nodded. He'd already seen it.

At 0200 the first of McKeon's devices went off, south side, far enough away that the sound arrived as a distant compression rather than a bang. The compound lights snapped to full and she could see the guards moving, turning south, the professional response overriding whatever they'd been thinking about in the quiet before.

The guard outside the outbuilding moved toward the south fence. Not all the way. He stopped at the corner of the nearest main structure and looked south and then looked back at the outbuilding.

She watched him make the decision. Stay or go.

He went.

"Now," she said.

They moved through the gap in a flat run and she was aware of Scotty beside her keeping pace better than she'd expected, and then they were at the wall of the outbuilding and she had her hand on the door and she could hear one voice inside, asking questions at the tired professional pace of someone running through a list they'd already run through many times.

She went through the door.

The questioner was between her and the prisoner, which was tactically convenient. She crossed the distance before the questioner had completed the rotation toward the door and put him down with the specific efficiency of someone who had learned very early that the goal was incapacitation rather than harm and who had trained the distinction into muscle memory. He went down. He would wake up with a headache and questions she did not plan to be present to answer.

The prisoner was a woman, as the voice had suggested. Mid-forties. The kind of face that had been handsome before the past however-long had taken their toll. She looked up at Buffy with the expression of someone who had stopped expecting anything good and was therefore fully available for surprise.

"Can you walk?" Buffy asked.

The woman looked at Scotty. Then back at Buffy. "Who are you?"

"We're the people who are leaving in about nine minutes. Can you walk?"

A beat. "Yes."

"Good. Stay between us. Do what I tell you immediately. If I say down, you go down. If I say run, you run. Questions after."

"Questions after," the woman repeated, in the tone of someone trying to determine whether they were hallucinating. "All right."

Scotty had already been to the door and back, taking in the compound's current state. "Guard's still at the south corner. Two more visible on the fence. No eyes on this building."

"Two minutes to the gap," Buffy said. "Move."

They moved.

The prisoner's walk was the controlled careful walk of someone with damage they were managing rather than recovering from, but it was functional and she kept up and when Buffy said down at the midpoint because one of the fence guards turned the wrong way she went down without argument and got up when Buffy got up without argument and that was exactly the right amount of cooperation.

They were through the gap with forty seconds to spare.

In the tree line she allowed herself one breath.

Scotty was watching the compound. The prisoner was watching Buffy. Nimitz, who had been left at camp over his extremely vocal objections, was not present to tell her what the prisoner was feeling, but Buffy had her own version of that read and what she got from the prisoner was something that was working through the transition from survival-mode to the possibility of actual relief, which was a transition Buffy recognized and which was going to take a while to complete.

"What's your name?" she asked.

"Surgeon-Lieutenant Theodosia Martingale," the woman said. "Grayson Space Navy."

Scotty made a sound. Buffy looked at him.

"I know that name," he said. "She was aboard the Austin Grayson. We listed her as�" he stopped.

"Dead," Martingale said. "I imagine you did." She looked down at her hands. "I've been imagining you did for about four months." She looked up again. "Who's in command?"

"Steadholder Harrington," Buffy said.

Theodosia Martingale closed her eyes.

Buffy watched her do it and recognized the specific quality of that closure, which was not grief but something adjacent to grief, the expression of a person receiving information that recalibrates something fundamental about how they've been organizing the experience of the past four months.

"All right," Martingale said. She opened her eyes. They were steadier. "All right. Let's go."

~~~

Honor met them at the camp perimeter.

She took in Martingale with the rapid assessment of a flag officer cataloguing tactical information, but there was something underneath the assessment that was personal in a way that Honor generally kept well-managed. Buffy had noticed that Honor's emotional life conducted itself in the register below her professional presentation rather than above it, which was unusual in her experience but which she was coming to understand was not suppression so much as architecture. The feelings were there. They were just load-bearing.

"Theodosia," Honor said.

"Milady," Martingale said, and then something happened to her face that was entirely at odds with the controlled voice, and Honor crossed the space between them and put her arms around the woman and Nimitz reached out from Honor's shoulder and laid one true-hand gently on top of Martingale's head and the whole thing was so unaffected and so complete that Buffy looked away.

She found herself looking at Scotty instead. He was watching with an expression she didn't have a word for. Something that acknowledged the weight of the moment without making it about him.

She thought about what he'd said. I'm good at after.

She thought maybe he was.

McKeon's team came in from the south at 0230, all five, with the tight expressions of people who'd run something close and were doing their post-operation inventory of themselves. "Perimeter response was faster than projected," McKeon said to Honor. "They've tightened up since last week."

"We expected that." Honor's arm was still around Martingale. "Debrief at 0800. Everyone get some sleep."

The camp dispersed with the quiet efficiency of people who'd learned to sleep in pieces around the edges of operations. Buffy sat down on the root of the tree outside the shuttle and watched the canopy and listened to the jungle settle back into its nighttime rhythms.

She was aware of Scotty sitting down a careful distance away.

She was aware that she didn't mind.

"Good work," she said.

"You did the work."

"You kept up. That was the work I needed from you."

He was quiet for a moment. "She'd been there four months."

"I know."

"We thought she was dead."

"I know."

The jungle breathed. Somewhere above them the bell-thing was silent for once, and the silence it left was its own kind of sound.

"Does it get easier?" he asked. "The part where you find out what you almost didn't get there in time for."

She thought about an honest answer. The honest answer was complicated, which was true of most honest answers worth giving. "It gets different," she said. "You stop expecting it to be easy and then it stops feeling like failure when it isn't." She looked up at the gap in the canopy where the stars were. Sirius was there. "The part that stays hard is the arithmetic. What you got there in time for versus what you didn't."

"Is the arithmetic ever in your favor?"

She'd thought about that question in various forms for two years. The Slayer was, by nature, a remediation. You arrived after something had already happened, after someone had already decided to open the Hellmouth or raise the Master or invite the demon mayor to ascend on graduation day, and the best possible outcome was that you prevented the completion of a plan that should never have gotten as far as it did. You were always cleaning up. You were never early enough to prevent the conditions that made the cleanup necessary.

"Sometimes," she said. "Tonight."

He looked at her. She looked back.

"Tonight was in your favor," he said.

"Yeah." She looked back at the stars. "I'm going to remember that one."

~~~

Martingale's debrief lasted four hours.

She had the compressed, precise recall of someone who had spent four months organizing information against the possibility of an audience, and she delivered it without editorializing, the dates and names and troop movements and supply estimates falling out of her in the ordered sequence of someone who had kept them alive in her head by treating them as a professional obligation.

Honor asked questions. McKeon asked questions. LaFollet asked questions that were specifically about the garrison's tactical capabilities in ways that suggested he'd already been running planning scenarios before Martingale was fully awake.

Buffy sat at the back and listened to the information she hadn't had before arrange itself into a shape.

The shape was this: the garrison was larger than they'd estimated but less coherent. There had been a shift in command structure approximately six weeks ago, the previous commandant replaced by someone Martingale described as younger, more ideologically committed, and less operationally experienced. The effect had been the tightening of some procedures and the neglect of others, the pattern of someone who'd been trained on theory and was discovering the friction of practice.

"The pinnaces," McKeon said. "You said one of them has been down for maintenance."

"The port landing leg took damage during the last resupply. Their maintenance crew has been working it for three weeks. I don't know whether they've resolved it."

"If it's still down�"

"We'd have one active pinnace as an intercept option if we tried to lift." Honor's voice was level. "One vs. two shuttles."

"Our shuttles aren't armed."

"No." Honor was quiet for a moment. "But one pinnace chasing two shuttles going in opposite directions is a different calculation than one pinnace chasing two shuttles going in the same direction."

Buffy watched the room absorb this. She watched the plan they'd been building adjust itself around the new information, the way a living thing adjusted around an obstacle. These people were very good at this. She'd been watching them do it for weeks and she was still impressed by how quickly they converted new data into revised strategy without the revision feeling like failure. On the Hellmouth, changed plans had always felt like setbacks. Here they felt like evolution.

She thought that was probably a healthier relationship with uncertainty.

McKeon pulled up the rough map again. "If we're going for the pinnace�"

"We may need to," Honor said. "The second shuttle's thruster concerns me. If it fails during the ascent�" She looked at Scotty.

"The variance on the warning signals has increased," he said. "Could be another two weeks. Could be next time we fire it."

"Then we plan for worst case." Honor looked at the map. "We secure the functional pinnace. That means someone has to be inside the compound to do it."

The room was quiet.

Buffy looked at the map. She looked at the compound layout she'd spent three nights learning. She looked at the landing pad and the pinnace and the distance from the outbuilding where she'd found Martingale to the landing pad, which was approximately two hundred meters of open ground.

"I can get to the pinnace," she said. "I need someone who can fly it."

"I can fly it," Scotty said.

She'd known he was going to say that before he said it. She wasn't sure whether that was the Slayer sense or just two weeks of paying attention.

"Of course you can," she said.
