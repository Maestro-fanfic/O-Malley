The second candidate's name was Yeva Ostrova and she was sixteen and she had been waiting for exactly this conversation for approximately two years without knowing what conversation she was waiting for.

That was clear from the moment Buffy sat down across from her in the small front room of the family's apartment, which was the apartment of a family that had done well enough to be comfortable and cared enough about it to show it, books on actual shelves and a rug that had been chosen rather than defaulted to. Yeva's mother had brought tea and then removed herself with the careful discretion of a woman who understood that her daughter needed this conversation without her in it.

Yeva sat across from Buffy with her hands in her lap and her spine straight and her dark eyes doing the thing that eyes did when they were paying complete attention.

She was tall for sixteen, broad-shouldered, with the look of someone whose body had been catching up to itself for the past year. She'd been in four incidents that the school records described as disciplinary issues and that Buffy read, between the lines, as what happened when someone with the physical profile was still developing the judgment to govern it.

She looked, in some specific way, like a version of someone Buffy had been.

"You're the Slayer," Yeva said.

Buffy looked at her. "You know that word."

"My grandmother was Russified," Yeva said, which was not a complete explanation and she seemed to know it wasn't. "She came from a family that kept old stories. The kind of old stories that serious people said were not serious." She paused. "She told me about the Chosen One. When I was small. Before I�" she stopped.

"Before you started noticing things," Buffy said.

"Yes."

"What did you notice first?"

Yeva looked at her hands. "That I was faster." She turned them over, the universal gesture of people looking at the instruments of something they didn't fully understand. "My classmates would react and I would already be done reacting. In gymnastics first. Then in other things." She paused. "I broke someone's wrist once. By accident. I misjudged." She looked up. "I've been very careful since then."

"I know that feeling," Buffy said.

"Are you careful?"

"I've learned to be. It took a while." She paused. "The dreams."

Yeva went still.

"Dark plain," Buffy said. "Women running. Old. Older than anything."

Yeva's expression did something that was relief and fear in proportions that shifted as Buffy watched, the fear decreasing and the relief increasing. "You dream it too."

"Not anymore. I have a�different relationship with the source now." She paused, because that was going to require a lot more conversation to explain. "But yes. I know what you're dreaming."

"What is it?"

"The lineage. The women who came before us. The line goes back a very long way." She held Yeva's gaze. "You're part of it. You were born part of it, the same way I was. It's not optional and it's not an accident." She paused. "What is optional is what you do with it."

"My grandmother said the Chosen One had no choice."

"Your grandmother was working from incomplete information." She kept her voice even. "The tradition your grandmother knew had a version of the truth. The version was�shaped by the people who maintained it. The shaping removed some things." She paused. "Choice was one of the things it removed."

Yeva was quiet for a moment. "What do you need from me?"

"Right now? Nothing." She let that land. "Right now I'm here because you deserve to know what you are. That's all. The knowing is yours. What you do with it is yours." She paused. "I'm not going to ask you to do anything with it. I'm not going to tell you what your obligation is. I'm not going to put conditions on the information."

Yeva looked at her. "That's different from what my grandmother described."

"Yes," Buffy said. "It is."

They talked for two hours. Yeva asked better questions than Buffy had asked at fifteen, which was partly temperament and partly the fact that Yeva had been quietly processing something for two years before the conversation arrived, and the questions that came out of two years of quiet processing were better than the questions that came out of a sudden ambush in a gym.

Buffy answered everything she could and was honest about the things she couldn't.

When she left she gave Yeva a communicator frequency and said she'd write, and she meant it.

Walking back through the spring afternoon she thought about the grandmother keeping old stories. The dispersal of knowledge. Two thousand years of the lineage's infrastructure dissolving into folklore and religious tradition and family memory, the full picture fragmenting into pieces that were each true and each insufficient.

She thought about what it was going to take to reassemble it. Not restore � you couldn't restore something that had been gone this long. Build something new that had learned from what the old version got wrong.

She thought about that all the way back to her quarters.

The Keeper, she was discovering, had opinions.

Not expressed as language. The Keeper didn't use language, or hadn't yet, and she'd stopped expecting it to and started paying attention to the other register it used, which was something between emotion and direction. It was how the Girl communicated when the Girl communicated directly rather than through the dreams, a mode that she'd been receiving without fully recognizing it for months.

The Keeper's opinions expressed themselves as a kind of pressure. Not insistent. More like the pressure of weather, the sense of something coming from a direction, which she'd learned to read as opinion about what she was doing.

It had opinions about Yeva. Positive ones.

It had opinions about the pace of the network search, which it found simultaneously appropriate and insufficient, a combination she'd come to understand as the Keeper registering the tension between the urgency of the underlying situation and the operational reality of what was possible.

It had opinions about the letter she'd written Carson that was still unsent on her desk, the one she'd been revising for four days. She wasn't entirely sure what its opinion was on that one. She suspected it was something in the vicinity of send it.

She sent it.

The letter said what it said, which was that she was thinking about the long work and thinking about what came after the long work and thinking about the shape of a life that was also the shape of a purpose, and that thinking about all of those things kept arriving at the same place, which was that the shape was better when it included the people who were already part of it, and that she wanted to say that explicitly rather than leave it as an implication.

She sealed it and sent it before she could revise it again.

The Keeper's pressure eased slightly in the way of something satisfied, which she found both reassuring and mildly annoying.

"You're not my Watcher," she said.

The accompanying presence did something she'd come to interpret as amusement.

"Don't," she said.

It persisted in the amusement.

She went to bed.

Three things happened in the same week in the seventh month.

The first was that Fritz's preliminary paper produced a response from a physicist at the Manticore Royal University named Dr. Haverstock who had been working on what he called dimensional interface theory for fifteen years and who had a framework for the scanner readings that Fritz had not anticipated and which required an urgent meeting.

The second was that the Grayson node of the vampire network moved. She'd been tracking it through the communication intelligence from the Eirene Constantia and through Usher's contacts on Grayson, and the movement said the node knew something had happened in Landing and was redistributing in response.

The third was a letter from Honor.

She read Honor's letter first because that was the order in which they arrived and also because it was Honor.

The letter was not long. Honor was not given to long letters when short ones said what needed saying, which Buffy had come to understand was characteristic. The relevant content was in three sentences in the middle.

The first said that things were developing in ways that she couldn't specify in a letter but which she expected would require her to be back in the Manticore system within the next two months.

The second said that she hoped Buffy was well and that Nimitz sent his regards, which was not the kind of thing Nimitz sent as a formality.

The third said that she had been thinking, over the months since Hades, about the conversation in the shuttle the night before the operation when Buffy had told her everything, and that what she'd been thinking was that she was glad Buffy had.

She read the third sentence three times.

Then she set the letter aside and went to meet Dr. Haverstock.

~~~

Dr. Haverstock was sixty and small and had the kind of energy that people had when they'd been working on something for fifteen years and had just received information that reorganized the entire project. He shook her hand with both of his and said, "The readings Fritz sent me are consistent with a stable dimensional interface of limited aperture, which I have theorized the existence of for fifteen years but have never had instrumental evidence for."

She looked at him. "Explain dimensional interface to me like I don't have a physics background."

He explained it. It took twenty minutes and several diagrams on the whiteboard behind his desk and two cups of tea, and what she came away with was this: the physics of this universe, the physics of two thousand years of scientific development in human civilization, had arrived at a framework that explained what the Keeper was in terms that didn't require the word supernatural.

The Keeper was a stable self-sustaining pattern of organized energy at a dimensional interface. The dimensional interface was not a metaphor. It was a real physical boundary between the experienced universe and a contiguous dimension that existed alongside it and that human physics had been theorizing about for decades without instrumental confirmation.

The case was a containment device for the interface. The Keeper was the pattern at the interface.

The pattern could, in theory, interact with physical reality at the interface boundary in ways consistent with what she'd been experiencing.

"It's not magic," she said, when he finished.

"It's physics we don't fully understand yet," he said, which was the kind of answer she'd been getting about the supernatural her entire Slayer career from people who were trying to be accurate.

"Can you talk to it?" she said.

He looked at her. "It?"

"The pattern. The Keeper." She paused. "The interface."

He looked at his whiteboard. "The interface itself, no. The pattern at the interface�that's a more interesting question." He paused. "The pattern has structure. Structure implies information organization. If the information organization can be addressed�" He stopped. "I would need significantly more data."

"You can have access," she said. "Within limits I'll specify." She paused. "Fritz is the point of contact. He's been working the biological angle and you'll need to coordinate." She looked at the whiteboard. "I want to understand it. What it is and how it works and what it can do." She paused. "It's been beside me for three months and I still don't fully know what I'm working with."

"Neither do I," he said. "But I'm very interested in finding out."

She left the meeting with the specific feeling of a thing having been placed in the hands of someone who was going to do something useful with it, the relief of delegation to competence.

The Grayson node was more immediately pressing.

~~~

The Grayson node's redistribution was not random. She'd spent two days with the communication intelligence mapping the movement and what she saw was a pattern consistent with a node that was contracting rather than fleeing, pulling itself into a smaller and more defensible configuration. Something had told it that the Landing operation had been targeted rather than accidental, and it was responding the way a professional operation responded to targeted pressure, not by dissolving but by consolidating.

Consolidation was both better and worse than dissolution. Better because a consolidated target was a locatable target. Worse because a consolidated target had resources pooled in one place.

She told Usher.

Usher had Grayson contacts that were the product of the Alliance relationship and years of intelligence cooperation, and the contacts produced, within forty-eight hours, a preliminary location for the consolidated node and a population estimate that was higher than she'd have preferred.

Eight to twelve.

That was not a one-person operation.

She sat with this for a day and then she called Carson.

He was still on Grayson. He'd extended his leave by two weeks to spend more time with his grandfather and the two newly identified lineage candidates, which was the right use of the time and which she'd told him so. He answered her call with the expression of someone who had been expecting it and had been waiting to see what form it took.

"I need help on Grayson," she said. "Not the lineage work. The other kind."

He looked at her steadily. "How many?"

"Eight to twelve. Consolidated. I can't do eight to twelve alone in a consolidated position."

"No," he agreed. He was quiet for a moment. "I can't do it either. Not with current capability."

"I know. I'm not asking you to fight them. I need your help with the approach." She paused. "You know Grayson. I don't. I need to understand the geography and the access and the political sensitivity of operating on Grayson soil without it becoming an official Grayson operation." She paused. "And I need to know if Castellan can come."

He thought about it. "The political sensitivity is significant. The Church of Humanity Unchained has specific positions on demonic entities and the methods of addressing them that may or may not be consistent with how this operation has to run." He paused. "My grandfather could help with that. He has relationships with the Sacristy that are� he's trusted. On the subject of the lineage specifically."

"Would he want to?"

"He would consider it a profound religious obligation and one of the great opportunities of his life," Carson said. "So yes."

She thought about Thomas Clinkscales, whom she'd still never met, who had kept the faith for thirty years and identified candidates the system hadn't known to look for and wept when his grandson came home from a prison planet with news that the lineage was real and present and in this century.

"Okay," she said. "Set up the meeting with your grandfather. I'm coming to Grayson."

His expression shifted slightly in the way it did when he was receiving something significant and not making a production of it. "When?"

"Five days," she said. "I need to coordinate with Usher and arrange Castellan and brief the Queen's office." She paused. "And I need to eat a very large dinner because the healing factor has opinions about operating on insufficient caloric reserves."

He almost smiled. "I'll have my mother cook."

"What's your mother's cooking like?"

"Better than mine," he said. "Which is not the comparison you want. She does a lamb dish that�" he paused. "You'll like it."

"I'll be there in five days," she said. "Tell her I eat a lot."

"I'll tell her," he said, with the specific quality of someone who had been telling people this for months and had stopped needing to explain why.
