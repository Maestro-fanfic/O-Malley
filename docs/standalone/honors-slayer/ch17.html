Chapter 17 (8 of 54 in this book)

Manticore came up on the sensors fourteen days later as a green light on a board she was learning to read, and by the time it was visible through the viewport as something more than a star it had already become several things simultaneously in her mind, which was a thing she was getting used to.

It was the destination. It was the next problem. It was the place where the after started in earnest.

She stood at the viewport in the mess hall at 0600 with a mug of chocolate and watched it resolve from a point into a disk and thought about Sunnydale coming up on the horizon from the highway, the specific way it looked from the bus the first time, smaller than she'd expected and greener than LA and already wrong in ways she couldn't have named yet.

Every new place was a version of that first look. The moment before you knew what it was going to cost you.

She drank her chocolate.

Scotty appeared beside her with coffee and the look of a man who had also not slept past 0600 and had made his peace with this. He stood next to her and they watched the planet together and after a while he said, "First time seeing it from the outside."

"You grew up here?"

"Sphinx. One of the other planets in the system." He nodded at the disk. "That's Manticore proper. I haven't actually spent that much time there. The capital feels�" he paused. "Like a capital. In ways that Sphinx doesn't."

"What's Sphinx like?"

"Cold. Forested. The treecats are from there originally." He paused. "Quieter than here. The sort of place where you notice when something changes because things don't change that often."

She thought about that. "Is that good?"

"When you're seventeen and restless, no. When you've been away for a while�" He looked at the planet. "I'd like to go back. At some point. When this is finished."

The war, he meant. She'd learned enough in six weeks to understand that the war was the kind of thing that structured everything around it, that when people said when this is finished they meant a thing that might not finish in any timeline she could currently see the edges of.

"I'd like to see it," she said. "Sphinx."

He looked at her. "Yeah?"

"Cold and forested sounds good actually." She looked at the planet. "I grew up in Southern California. I've never really done cold."

"Sphinx has cold you haven't imagined."

"That sounds like a challenge."

"It's a description." But there was something in his voice, something she'd learned to catch, the particular tone that his ordinary statements took on when they were also something else. "You'd need gear. The ambient temperature in the northern regions goes low enough to be medically significant for baseline humans."

"I'm not a baseline human."

"No. But even you would want a coat." He paused. "My family has a place in the northern range. When this is finished."

She looked at him.

He looked at the planet.

"Okay," she said.

He drank his coffee. She drank her chocolate. The planet grew.

~~~

The approach to Manticore took most of a day, the convoy threading through the system's traffic with the careful attention that a system at war paid to everything moving within it. There were warships on the passive sensors at intervals that Buffy couldn't have missed even before the Slayer sense had developed its instinct for the presence of organized force, large purposeful things holding their positions in the geometry of a defended system.

She watched them on the tactical display that Shaughnessy had, apparently on reflection, decided to make available to her without being asked.

Honor came to stand beside her at some point in the middle of the approach and they stood together looking at the disposition of forces in silence for a while.

"Is it what you expected?" Buffy asked.

"More than it was," Honor said. "Six months ago this would have been thinner." She looked at the icons moving on the display. "We've been building."

"Against what?"

"Haven has been building too." She paused. "The question is always who gets to a sufficient advantage first and whether the political situation allows them to use it."

"Does it?"

A pause that was not quite hesitation. "That's what I'm going to spend the next several months finding out." She looked away from the display. "There will be a reception. When we dock. I want to prepare you for it."

"Prepare me how?"

"There are people who will want to see you for reasons that have more to do with what you represent than who you are. Some of them will be important people politically. Some of them will be important people personally." She paused. "Some of them will be both."

"The Queen," Buffy said.

Honor looked at her. "Yes. Queen Elizabeth has been briefed. On the basic facts. She has�expressed interest."

Buffy thought about what that meant, interest from a head of state, and thought about how she'd handled the Mayor of Sunnydale, who had been an actual demon about to ascend to pure demon form, and decided the comparison was probably not usefully predictive. "What does she want?"

"To meet you. To assess you." Honor's voice was even. "She's a remarkable person. I want you to know that before you walk into the room. She is not going to be what you expect."

"What am I going to expect?"

"Ceremony. Formality. The performance of monarchy." Honor paused. "She'll do some of that because it's functionally necessary. Underneath it she is one of the sharpest political minds I've encountered and she will form an opinion of you in approximately ten minutes and that opinion will matter."

"No pressure," Buffy said.

"Considerable pressure," Honor said. "I'm telling you because I think you'd rather know."

She thought about that. "What's your read? On how she'll take me?"

Honor was quiet for a moment. "She's been queen since she was young. She knows what it is to have the weight of an entire civilization's expectations placed on you before you had the vocabulary to refuse them." She paused. "I think she'll recognize something in you."

Buffy looked at the display, at the warships holding their patient geometry in the dark. "And if she doesn't?"

"Then we navigate from there." Honor's voice was entirely certain. "But she will."

The docking happened in the late afternoon by ship's time, which corresponded to early morning on Manticore's capital continent, which meant that the people who came to meet them had been awake for some time and were doing that specific thing that official people did when they'd been awake before they wanted to be, which was performing alertness with slightly more effort than genuine alertness required.

The docking bay was large and decorated in the way of official spaces, which was with the symbols of the thing being represented more heavily than the symbols of the specific people involved. Manticoran standards and naval flags and the kind of light that was specifically designed to make people look appropriate for the occasion.

There was a crowd, which she'd expected. There were cameras, which she'd also expected. There was a moment, which she had also expected but which arrived with more weight than expectation provided for, when the hatch opened and the sound of the crowd came in and she was standing third in the line behind Honor and McKeon and the reality of the next thing was right there, immediate and irreversible.

She breathed.

The Girl was quiet, which was either a good sign or a sign that the Girl's category system didn't include this particular type of situation. Probably the latter. The First Slayer had not done a lot of press.

She walked out.

The crowd noise changed when she appeared, not dramatically, not the way it changed for Honor, but distinctly, a shift in the quality of the attention. She felt it the way she felt all attention, as pressure, and she walked through it with her chin level and her hands easy at her sides and her face doing the thing she'd learned to make her face do, which was present and composed without being closed.

A man in naval dress uniform appeared at her elbow with the smooth efficiency of someone who had been placed there for this purpose. "Lady Buffy. I'm Commander Hennessy, Admiral Caparelli's aide. I'll be facilitating your transition to temporary quarters and your initial briefings. If you'll follow me."

She followed him.

The temporary quarters were on the naval base adjacent to the docking facility, a suite of rooms that had been assembled for her with the particular kind of care that said someone had thought about what she might need without being entirely certain what that was. There was food. There was a bed that was larger than anything she'd slept in since Sunnydale. There was a window that looked out over a city at dawn, the specific blue-gray quality of early light on stone buildings that predated anything she'd seen in Sunnydale by several centuries.

Commander Hennessy ran through the schedule with the efficiency of someone conveying a great deal of information in the minimum necessary time. Initial medical assessment with the naval medical staff in the morning, which would include Fritz and Petrakis at her request and which she'd been told would be conducted with appropriate discretion. Preliminary debrief with Naval Intelligence in the afternoon. A rest day on the second day. On the third day, a meeting.

He said meeting in the tone that meant it was something more than a meeting.

"With whom?" she asked.

"Her Majesty has requested an audience," Hennessy said, carefully neutral. "At her convenience, which she has indicated is the third day."

She thought about what Honor had said. She thought about a woman who'd been queen since she was young and knew what it was to have the weight of expectations placed on you before you had vocabulary to refuse them.

"Okay," she said. "Third day."

Hennessy left. She stood at the window and watched the city wake up.

It was a real city, the kind she hadn't seen since Sunnydale, though everything about it was different from Sunnydale in ways that went beyond the obvious. The scale was different. The materials were different. The sky was a slightly different shade of blue, Manticore's sun being a slightly different kind of star, and she'd read about this in Fritz's initial orientation materials and knowing about it didn't make looking at it less strange.

But it was a city. People were moving through it with the specific purposeful randomness of people going to their particular version of wherever they were going, and that part was entirely recognizable, that fundamental grammar of human movement through urban space, and something in her chest did a thing she couldn't name precisely but which felt like relief.

Recognizable things. Even if nothing else was familiar, the shape of people moving through a city in the early morning was exactly what it had always been.

She watched until the sun was properly up and then she ate the food that had been left and went to sleep and slept, deeply and without dreams, for eight hours.

~~~

The medical assessment was not as bad as she'd expected, which was a low bar given what she'd expected.

Petrakis had prepared his colleagues with enough precision that none of them were surprised by anything, which she appreciated. Fritz stood slightly to the side through most of it with the expression of a man watching a process he'd largely designed and was monitoring for execution quality. When the two Manticoran physicians conferred in the corner over something she couldn't hear from the biobed she was sitting on, she looked at Fritz.

He gave her a small nod that meant proceeding correctly.

She relaxed fractionally.

The thing about being an unknown biological quantity was that the experience of being assessed depended entirely on the assessors. There was one version of this process that felt like being a specimen, categorized and quantified and reduced to data. There was another version that felt like being a patient, which was a person with a body that a professional was trying to understand in order to take care of.

Fritz had trained her to expect the second version. She'd been getting it consistently, which she attributed entirely to Fritz and partially to something in the culture of these people that she was still identifying but which seemed to include a default assumption of personhood that she found unexpectedly moving.

She hadn't always been given that assumption. The Council, in its less considered moments, had given her the other version. You were the Slayer. The Slayer was the mission. The person it was packaged with was administrative.

She thought about this while the Manticoran physicians did their work, and by the time they were done she had arrived at a thought she wanted to share with Fritz.

"Walk with me," she said, when the physicians had concluded and the room had cleared to the degree that it was going to.

He fell into step beside her with the comfortable ease of someone who had been walking beside her for two months. She went toward the window at the end of the corridor, the one that looked out over the base and a slice of the city beyond it.

"When I was called," she said, without preamble, because Fritz was the kind of person who did better without preamble, "the Council showed up the next day. My first Watcher. And everything changed. Not just what I did. How people looked at me." She paused. "They never stopped looking at me that way, the Council. Like I was the mission wearing a face."

Fritz was quiet, which was his version of active listening.

"These people don't do that," she said. "I've been waiting for it. Six weeks on the Wayfarer, the past two days. I keep expecting the moment when I stop being a person and become a category." She looked at the city. "It hasn't happened."

"No," Fritz said.

"Why?"

He considered this carefully, which was how he considered everything. "I think in part it's the people. Honor. McKeon. Shaughnessy. These are people who have a developed habit of treating people as people regardless of circumstance, and that habit shapes the environment around them." He paused. "In part I think it's your presentation. You've been consistently and clearly a person in ways that make the alternative framing difficult to maintain."

"And in part?"

"In part I think you've been fortunate in your initial context." He looked at her steadily. "That may not hold universally as you move into broader Manticoran society. There will be people who want to use you as a symbol. Honor warned you about that."

"She did."

"The medical community is going to be a particular vector for that. Not intentionally. But you represent something that doesn't fit any existing framework and the desire to build a framework will be strong." He paused. "I'll be writing papers. With your permission. With your review and approval before anything is published. I want to be transparent about that."

She'd known this was coming. She'd thought about it during the long weeks on the Wayfarer, about what it meant to be a documented thing, a thing that went into the record. About the ways that records helped and the ways they constrained.

"Yes," she said. "With review. And there are things that stay out."

"Of course. You determine the limits."

She looked at the city. A tram was moving along a street she could see from here, filled with people going wherever people went in the capital of a star system at war in the morning. "What's the Slayer lineage going to look like in two thousand years of historical record?"

"Fragmentary," Fritz said. "The Clinkscales family record is the most intact continuous documentation, but even that has significant gaps. The general historical record from your era is�" he paused� "poor, by current standards. The data degradation across two millennia is severe. What survives is mostly institutional records, physical artifacts, some genetic lineages. Personal documentation from your time is largely lost."

She thought about that. "Nobody's going to know who I was."

"Nobody is going to know the details, no. The Clinkscales record establishes the existence of the Slayer lineage but as mythology rather than documented fact, until now." He paused. "You are, in some respects, your own proof of concept."

She turned that over. "Carson said the family kept the records because they believed."

"Yes. Four generations of believing in something that the broader culture categorized as legend." He paused. "They were right."

She thought about Carson with his steady certainty, the young man from a religious tradition that had a category for her existence, who had said she would see Grayson with the confidence of someone stating a navigational fact. "He's going to have a complicated life," she said.

"He already has one," Fritz said. "He seems to be managing it."

She almost smiled. "Yeah. He does."

~~~

The debrief with Naval Intelligence was conducted by two officers who had the specific quality of people whose job was to receive information without contributing their own reactions to the room, which she found professionally impressive and personally exhausting. She told them what she'd told McKeon in the operational debriefs on the Wayfarer, the observation nights and the extraction and the garrison's state and the information Martingale had provided. She answered questions that were precise and that occasionally surprised her with what they were actually after, which was not always the obvious thing.

At the end the senior officer, a captain named Wainwright, looked at her directly for what felt like the first time in three hours despite having been looking at her the whole time. "Lady Buffy. One more question."

"Go ahead."

"The garrison commandant. The new one. Martingale described him as younger and more ideologically committed than his predecessor." He paused. "During your three nights of observation, did you form an assessment of him independent of Martingale's characterization?"

She thought about this. "I never saw him directly. But I saw the garrison's behavior and behavior reflects command culture." She paused. "The rotation tightening was real, you could see it, but it was uneven. Some posts were tighter and some were slacker than before, which is the pattern you get when enforcement is personal rather than systemic. He was riding specific people hard and those people were riding the people below them, but the ones who weren't in his immediate line of sight were taking the space he wasn't watching." She paused. "Ideological commitment tends to be personal. It inspires the people who already believe and it fatigues the people who are just doing the job. His garrison was mixed."

Wainwright made a note. "That matches other data we have." He looked at her. "Thank you."

She went back to her quarters and ate and slept again and woke up in the middle of the night with the Girl's running dreams and lay in the dark of a room in the capital of a star nation listening to a city that was nothing like any city she'd known and was also, in the ways that mattered, exactly like every city she'd known.

She ran through the dark on feet that had never touched a road.

She lay in a bed in Manticore's capital.

She was both of these things at once and she was getting better at holding both without either one displacing the other.
