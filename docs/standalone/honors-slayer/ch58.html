Chapter 58 (49 of 54 in this book)

Spring came.

She was in the capital when it came, the Manticore spring doing its specific thing, the city changing its quality in the way that cities changed their quality when the season shifted, the specific lightening that came with more hours of light and the specific texture of air that had stopped being winter air.

She ran in it and it was the spring air she knew now, the third spring she'd run in, and knowing it had the specific quality of knowing a recurring thing, the recognition that was different from familiarity and deeper than recognition.

She had a meeting with the Queen.

Not at the palace and not in the informal sitting room. At the Vigil's working space, which was where she'd suggested the meeting happen, because the Queen had expressed interest in seeing the current state of the institution rather than receiving a report about it.

The Queen arrived with Ariel and without ceremony and walked through the space with the specific attention of someone who was genuinely looking rather than performing the act of looking.

She took her time.

She looked at the candidate records and at the network map and at the research documentation and at the operational files and at the archive room that held the copies of everything that had been collected over four years.

Then she came back to the main room and sat down.

She said: "This is not what I imagined when you first told me what you were building."

Buffy said: "What did you imagine?"

The Queen said: "Something smaller. More centralized." She paused. "Something that looked more like the institution it replaced."

Buffy said: "That's what I was trying to avoid."

The Queen said: "I know. And you succeeded." She held Buffy's gaze. "What you've built is�" she paused. "It's genuinely new. Not in the sense of unprecedented historically. In the sense of being what it needs to be rather than what precedent suggested it should be."

Ariel made a sound.

The Queen looked at him and then back at Buffy.

She said: "He says you know what you're doing."

Buffy said: "Tell him I'm still learning."

The Queen conveyed this.

She said: "He says that's the same thing."

She held this.

She thought about the Constant, patient on Beowulf. She thought about Witness and the other presences. She thought about the record and the archive and the long thread running from the first Clinkscales entry to the data chip on the Hellmouth's floor.

She thought about what knowing what you're doing meant when you were doing something that was going to matter to people who weren't born yet for reasons they wouldn't fully understand until they were in the middle of them.

She thought: maybe that's what knowing what you're doing means. Doing it anyway.

She said to Ariel: "Tell him I'll take it."

Ariel's tail tip moved.

The Queen looked at her with the expression that had developed across four years of conversations, the one that was warm and precise and held its assessment lightly rather than as judgment.

She said: "The charter revision is producing the effects you expected."

She said: "Slowly."

The Queen said: "Haven is more engaged than I anticipated. The reform government is treating the Vigil's work as something they want to be part of rather than something they're tolerating." She paused. "Tolman has been effective."

She said: "Tolman is the right person for that work."

The Queen said: "Several League worlds are in preliminary conversations."

She said: "I know. Honor is managing them."

The Queen said: "Honor is excellent at managing them."

She said: "I know that too."

The Queen held her gaze.

She said: "You've built something that is going to outlast both of us."

Buffy held her gaze.

She said: "That was always the point."

The Queen was quiet for a moment.

She said: "What do you need from me now? Directly."

She thought about it.

She said: "Patience with the League process. It's going to be slow and it's going to require the kind of political capital that you have and that the Vigil doesn't yet." She paused. "And continued support for the research. Haverstock's work specifically. The interface framework is going to produce things in the next decade that are going to need institutional infrastructure to receive them."

The Queen said: "Done."

She said: "And one other thing."

The Queen waited.

She said: "The people on Old Earth. Whatever the lineage situation is there�we don't know yet. The window was a specific event and the next comparable window is eleven years away." She paused. "I want to prepare for that window. Not just the physics. The content. What we bring next time." She held the Queen's gaze. "We should be ready to offer something substantive when the window opens. Not just information. Connection."

The Queen held her gaze.

She said: "You're thinking about expanding the Vigil to Old Earth."

She said: "I'm thinking about what the Vigil is for. It's for the lineage, wherever the lineage is." She paused. "The lineage is on Earth. Whatever state they're in, they're there."

The Queen was quiet for a moment.

She said: "Eleven years."

She said: "Eleven years."

The Queen said: "Then we have time to prepare."

She said: "Yes."

The Queen said: "All right."

She looked at Ariel.

Ariel looked at her.

She thought about all the times the treecat had been in the room for the significant conversations, the specific continuity of it, the presence that had been there since the formal audience and had been reading her ever since.

She said: "What does he think of the eleven-year plan?"

The Queen conveyed the question.

She said: "He says it's correct."

She said: "Good. I think so too."

She looked at the room, at the Vigil's working space with its maps and its records and its evidence of the work that was ongoing, and she thought about eleven years and what they would contain and what the Vigil would be in eleven years and who would be carrying which parts of it.

She thought about Yeva at forty-one. She thought about Carson with the archive at a stage she couldn't fully picture. She thought about Allison's research in another decade of production.

She thought about what she would be.

Forty-one.

She thought: forty-one. Yes. I'll get there.

She thought: and then the window opens and we go again.

Not she. We.

The Vigil.

That was the difference. That was the whole of the difference.

~~~

The document she'd been writing was finished in the spring of her thirty-first year.

Not finished in the sense of complete. No document was complete that was also living. But finished in the sense that the record she'd set out to build was built, the full arc of it from the beginning to the going and the coming back and the landing, the thing that the people who came after would need to understand what had happened in this generation.

She read it from the beginning.

It took two days.

She read it the way she read things she wanted to understand rather than the way she read things she already knew, which meant she encountered it slightly differently than she'd encountered it in the writing, the completed thing having a different quality than the thing in process.

What she found when she read it was this:

It was a Watcher's journal. Carson had been right.

Not the Council's version, not the institutional document designed to serve the institution's interests. The other version. The one that was written for the people in it rather than about them. The one that said: this happened and here is what it meant and here is what I learned and here is what I wish I'd known and here is what you need to know when you're in the middle of your own version of this.

She read it and thought: yes. This is the right thing.

She told Carson.

He came to the capital and they sat in the archive room and she gave him the document and he read for four hours while she worked on other things and when he was done he was quiet for a long time.

He said: "It's the best record we have."

She said: "It's the most recent. The most complete is the archive."

He said: "The archive documents what happened. This explains why it mattered." He held her gaze. "That's different. And necessary."

She held his gaze.

She said: "Integrate it."

He said: "Yes."

She said: "And then write yours."

He held her gaze.

She said: "The record needs your perspective. The archive keeper's version. What it was like to be the person who kept the faith before the faith was confirmed and what it became after." She held his gaze. "Four years of watching this from the inside. Write it."

He said: "I'm not the Slayer."

She said: "I know. The record doesn't only need the Slayer's account. It needs everyone's account." She held his gaze. "The first Clinkscales entry is in the archive because he wrote it. The Vigil's founding is in the document because I wrote it. What it was like to be Carson Clinkscales in the years when all of this happened�that's yours to write." She paused. "Write it."

He held her gaze for a long moment.

He said: "All right."

She said: "Good."

She thought about all the accounts that were going to go into the archive over the next decades, the full distributed picture that no one person could provide and that required many people writing many versions of the same event from their own positions. She thought about what a hundred years of that would produce, the depth of record.

She thought: they'll know what we were doing and why.

She thought: they'll have what we didn't have. The full picture.

She thought: that was the point.

She looked at the archive room, at the accumulated record of four years of work and thirty years before that and eleven hundred years before that and two thousand years before that, all of it converging in this room in this specific form.

She thought: we kept it. We kept it and we're passing it forward.

She thought: forward.

Always forward.
