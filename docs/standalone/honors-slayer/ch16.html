Chapter 16 (7 of 54 in this book)

Manticore was six weeks away at the speeds available to a convoy that included a converted freighter and a shuttle with a dead thruster being towed on a tractor field.

Six weeks was a significant amount of time.

Buffy had known this intellectually and was now discovering it experientially, which was a different kind of knowing. On Hades the time had been structured by necessity, by the operation and its planning and the daily demands of staying alive in a jungle on a prison planet. On the Wayfarer the structure was different. There was routine � the ship ran on watches and meals and drills regardless of its passengers � but the routine was not hers. She was cargo, in the most benevolent possible sense. She was being delivered somewhere.

The somewhere was still abstract.

She dealt with this the way she dealt with most things, which was to find the parts she could work with and work with those while the parts she couldn't work with yet resolved themselves in the background.

She ran. The Wayfarer had a gym that was significantly more sophisticated than anything she'd trained on for the past two years and significantly more sophisticated than the jungle at dawn, and she used it with an enthusiasm that attracted attention she hadn't intended to attract. After the third morning when she'd had an audience of off-watch crew members standing in the doorway with expressions ranging from fascinated to slightly alarmed, she'd mentioned it to Scotty, who had mentioned it to the Wayfarer's XO, who had quietly suggested that perhaps Lady Buffy might prefer the gym at 0400 when it was unoccupied.

She'd agreed and 0400 had become her time, which she used the way she used all the time that was hers, completely.

She spent mornings with Fritz, who had been given a corner of the Wayfarer's sickbay by the ship's surgeon, a dour man named Petrakis who had looked at Fritz's preliminary notes on Buffy's biology with the expression of someone confronting information that was going to require significant professional reclassification of everything he thought he knew. Fritz and Petrakis had developed a working relationship that Buffy thought of as productive friction, two precise minds with slightly different frameworks arguing toward a shared understanding of the specific puzzle she presented.

She let them argue. She answered their questions when they asked them and ignored the rest.

She spent afternoons with different people depending on the day, which was its own kind of structure. Martingale was recovering with the systematic efficiency of a doctor who knew exactly what her body needed and provided it without negotiation, and on the days when Martingale was up to talking they talked. Honor was often occupied with the kind of work that came from being Honor Harrington returning to Manticoran space with a story to tell that wasn't fully tellable yet, and on the days when she had time Buffy was one of the people she spent it with, though not always the first. McKeon was running the recovered personnel through operational debriefs in preparation for the formal debriefs that would happen when they reached Manticore, and he included Buffy in these with the same professional completeness with which he included everyone else, which she appreciated.

Carson was healing faster than his injury warranted, which Fritz noted with professional interest and Carson accepted with the equanimity of a young man from a religious tradition that had a category for Buffy's existence.

Ibanez had asked, very carefully, whether she would be willing to spar with him.

She'd said yes and then discovered that Ibanez was considerably better at hand-to-hand than his unassuming demeanor had suggested, and that this was because he'd grown up with three older brothers on a working ranch outside Mayhew, the Grayson capital, and had learned to fight before he'd learned to navigate in the polite professional world of the Navy. He lost to her, consistently and by significant margins, but he lost in ways that cost her something and he was learning from each loss rather than from his wins.

She liked that. She liked it enough that the sparring became another regular thing.

LaFollet observed the sparring from the first session with the expression of a man watching something he wanted to join and who had decided that asking would be professionally undignified. On the fourth session she invited him in without making it into anything, and he joined, and was better than Ibanez and worse than Scotty, which she also liked because it meant she had a range, a spectrum of opposition that each asked different things of her.

The Wayfarer's commander, whose name was Commodore Ellen Shaughnessy and whose iron-gray hair came with iron-gray opinions about most things, observed all of this from what she probably thought was a discreet distance and which was not discreet to Buffy's Slayer sense but which she respected because Shaughnessy was being respectful in return and that was the deal.

On the eleventh day Shaughnessy requested a meeting.

Not an order, a request, which told Buffy something about how Shaughnessy had decided to handle her, which was as a person of uncertain but non-trivial status who warranted courtesy rather than an entity to be categorized and managed. She went to the meeting.

Shaughnessy's day cabin was the cabin of a person who had been doing a specific job for a long time and had reached an accommodation with the space it provided, which was to say it was functional and arranged with the precision of established habit and contained on the desk a photograph of two children and a man who looked like he found Shaughnessy as interesting as she clearly found most things, which was quite interesting indeed.

"Lady Buffy," Shaughnessy said. "Please sit down."

She sat.

"I've been reading Fritz Montoya's preliminary notes," Shaughnessy said. "With his permission, and yours, I understand, for the medical content. Not the personal content."

"I said yes to the medical content," Buffy confirmed.

"Yes. I want to be clear that I've not exceeded that permission." Shaughnessy looked at her directly in the way of someone who was comfortable with direct looking. "I want to talk to you about what happens when we reach Manticore."

"I've been thinking about that too."

"I imagine you have." Shaughnessy folded her hands. "The situation is, frankly, unprecedented. In several directions simultaneously." She paused, choosing her next words with visible deliberateness. "You are, as I understand it, a person of Old Earth origin displaced approximately two thousand years from your time of origin. You have no documentation, no citizenship, no legal standing in any jurisdiction I'm aware of, and a biological profile that is�" she paused again.

"Weird," Buffy supplied.

"Significantly outside established human baselines," Shaughnessy said, apparently deciding that was the appropriate register. "When we arrive at Manticore there will be questions. From people with authority to ask them. How you answer those questions will have consequences."

"I know."

"Do you understand the specific political context?"

She thought about what she knew, which was the briefings she'd attended on the Wayfarer and the things Honor had told her in direct conversation and the things she'd pieced together from observation. "Manticore is at war. Honor is coming home after everyone thought she was dead. The political situation around that is complicated. My presence adds a variable that nobody has a ready category for."

Shaughnessy looked at her. "Succinctly, yes."

"What's your advice?"

Shaughnessy seemed to consider whether to give advice. Then she decided. "Let Honor take the lead on how you're introduced. Not because you can't speak for yourself. Because the first introduction establishes the frame, and Honor Harrington has political capital that you haven't had time to accumulate, and how you're framed in the first forty-eight hours will matter for a long time after."

"Is that what you're doing? Framing me?"

"No." Shaughnessy held her gaze. "I'm talking to you so that I understand what I'm dealing with. I'm going to be writing a report. I want the report to be accurate."

Buffy thought about that. "What do you think you're dealing with?"

Shaughnessy was quiet for a moment. "A young woman who has been through a great deal and who is considerably more composed about it than is statistically likely. Who is also, by Fritz Montoya's account and by my own observation, something outside my existing frame of reference." She paused. "And who, I think, is trying to figure out who she is in a context that didn't exist when she was originally herself."

Buffy looked at her.

"That's a reasonably accurate summary," she said.

"I thought it might be." Shaughnessy unfolded her hands. "For what it's worth. You're welcome on this ship. Not because of what you are or what you did on Hades. Because Steadholder Harrington trusts you. That's enough for me."

Buffy thought about what that meant, the specific weight of being trusted by someone whose trust was legible as currency to people like Shaughnessy.

"Thank you," she said.

Shaughnessy nodded once, the nod of someone for whom the relevant business had been concluded. "Is there anything you need?"

She thought about it. "Books," she said. "History. I have two thousand years of catching up to do."

Shaughnessy almost smiled. "I'll arrange access to the ship's library."

The library was large and she went through it with an appetite that surprised even her, which was saying something given that the past month had recalibrated her sense of what her appetites were capable of. She read history the way she'd read patrol routes, looking for the shape of things, the recurring patterns that let you predict the next iteration.

History, it turned out, was mostly the same several things happening repeatedly with different names and improved technology. There was some comfort in that. The specific texture of human catastrophe had changed but the underlying grammar was consistent. She could read it.

She read for four days straight with breaks only for eating and sleeping and training and Fritz's appointments, and by the end of the four days she had a working outline of two thousand years that was more skeleton than flesh but had enough structure to hang things on.

On the fifth day she went to find Honor.

Honor was in the flag captain's briefing room, which Shaughnessy had made available for her use, with a stack of work that had been forwarded from Manticore through the courier system and which had the general character of a mountain that had arrived while the mountain's usual handler had been presumed dead and had therefore accumulated without maintenance.

She looked up when Buffy came in.

"You've been reading," she said.

"How can you tell?"

"You have the look of someone who has been reading for four days." Honor set down the document she was holding. "What questions do you have?"

Buffy sat down. "All of them," she said. "But I'll start with the Solarian League."

Honor looked at her. Then she set down the rest of her work and sat back. "Start from what you know."

"Big. Old. Technically neutral but structurally not. Internal politics are�" she searched for the word she'd read� "sclerotic."

"That's accurate." Honor's voice had the quality it always had when she was genuinely engaged with a subject, which was slightly different from her usual precision, slightly more alive. "What else?"

"Their Office of Frontier Security is the part I don't understand. It's not military exactly but it has military capacity and it operates in areas outside Solarian direct governance and the relationship between what it's supposed to do and what it actually does is�"

"Exactly as corrupt as it sounds," Honor said. "Yes."

"Which means they're a factor in the war even though they're nominally neutral."

"They're a factor in everything. The question is always how much of a factor and in which direction." She paused. "You've been doing this for four days?"

"I have a lot of catching up to do."

Honor looked at her with the expression that had the layered quality, the one that was several things at once and that Buffy had spent weeks learning to parse. Right now it was something close to what McKeon's face did when he thought something was genuinely impressive and was being professional about it.

"You're going to be formidable," Honor said. "When you have the context."

"I'm already formidable," Buffy said.

"Yes. But right now you're formidable the way a very sharp sword is formidable in a room where nobody knows which end to hold." Honor's voice was entirely even. "Context is the handle."

Buffy thought about that. "Okay. Tell me about the Solarian League's relationship with Haven."

Honor told her.

They talked for three hours and by the end of it Buffy had more skeleton to hang things on and Honor had the look of someone who had also gotten something from the conversation, though what she'd gotten was less clear. Something about the act of explaining, maybe. Something about the particular kind of thinking you did when you had to make your implicit assumptions explicit for someone who didn't share your frame of reference.

"You're useful to talk to," Honor said, which was not the kind of compliment Honor gave carelessly.

"Because I don't know things?"

"Because you ask the questions that people who know things have stopped asking." She paused. "The questions that feel obvious. They're usually the most important ones."

Buffy thought about two years of asking questions that everyone around her had found too basic to take seriously until the answers turned out to matter. "I have a lot of practice being the person in the room who doesn't know things."

"How was that?"

"Annoying. Then useful." She paused. "Mostly annoying."

Honor almost smiled. "It gets less annoying."

"Does it get more useful?"

"Considerably."

On the twenty-second day out from Hades, Buffy had a dream that was neither hers nor the Girl's.

She was standing in a library that wasn't the Wayfarer's and wasn't the Sunnydale High library either, though it had elements of both, the specific spatial logic of dreams that wasn't spatial logic at all. The books on the shelves were written in a script she couldn't read. The light came from somewhere without a source.

A woman was sitting at the table in the center of the room.

Not the Girl. Not anyone Buffy had seen before, though she had the quality of someone Buffy was supposed to recognize. She was old in the way that wasn't about wrinkles or white hair but about something behind the eyes, the density of accumulated time.

"You're doing well," the woman said.

Buffy looked at her. "Who are you?"

"You don't need my name. You need to know you're doing well."

"That's not a useful answer."

"Most useful answers aren't satisfying. Most satisfying answers aren't useful." The woman looked at the books on the shelves. "You've been reading."

"Apparently I do that in my dreams too now."

"You're trying to understand where you are. That's correct. That's the right response." The woman looked back at her. "Some of them are going to try to make you into a symbol. You'll feel it. The pull toward the thing they want you to represent."

"I know that pull," Buffy said. "I've been the Slayer since I was fifteen."

"This will be different. The Slayer is one kind of symbol. What you're going to be here is another kind." The woman paused. "A symbol of the past, to the people who believe the past means something. A symbol of possibility, to the ones who think it means the future is open." She paused. "You're neither of those things, of course."

"What am I?"

"A seventeen-year-old girl from Sunnydale who is also the Slayer who is also something the merging made you that doesn't have a name yet." The woman's voice was entirely matter-of-fact. "Being three things at once is inconvenient. It's also the only way to do what comes next."

"What comes next?"

"You'll see." She looked at Buffy steadily. "They're going to ask a lot of you. You don't have to give them everything they ask for. You get to decide what this is."

"The Slayer doesn't get to decide what this is."

"This Slayer does." The woman looked at the books again. "Two thousand years of change. The calling is still yours. So is the choice."

Buffy looked at her. "Are you the First?"

A pause. "I'm someone who has been watching for a long time and who wanted to say something before the next part starts." She stood, and the library moved around her the way things moved in dreams, with their own internal logic. "You found people worth fighting for. That's the important part. The rest is navigation."

"I'm not very good at navigation."

"You're better than you think." She paused. "You always were."

The library faded.

Buffy woke up on the Wayfarer at 0330 with the dream already losing its edges and the specific feeling of something settling into place, the way the last piece of a thing sometimes arrived without announcement and simply was there, completed, when a moment before it had been absent.

She lay in the dark and let it settle.

At 0400 she went to the gym and ran, and the running was not joyful the way the Girl's running was joyful, not free in that specific ancient way, but it was good, the body doing what it was built for in a gym on a Manticoran warship in a star system she hadn't known existed a month ago, and good was enough.

Good was considerably more than enough.

She ran until she felt like herself. All three of her selves simultaneously, which was the thing she was apparently going to have to work with.

Then she went and ate an enormous breakfast and watched the stars go by through the mess hall viewport and when Carson came in at 0700 with his arm out of its sling and his hair doing something optimistic, she moved over and made room and he sat down and they watched the wrong constellations together without needing to say anything about them.

The stars were wrong and she was here and Manticore was two weeks away and that was the shape of things.

She could work with that shape.

She was getting quite good at that.
