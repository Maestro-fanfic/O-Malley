Sphinx in autumn was exactly what Scotty had said it was, which was the spectacular version, and the spectacular version was not a figure of speech.

She stood at the edge of the property in the northern range on the first morning and looked at what the light was doing to the trees and understood why he'd used that word specifically. Not beautiful, which would have been accurate but insufficient. Spectacular, which was beauty that demanded something from you in return for being received.

The trees were not the same trees as any trees she'd known. They were the Sphinx version, old and enormous in the way of things that grew in cold climates where growing was slow and what grew did so with the accumulated weight of many careful seasons. Their leaves were the wrong colors for autumn in any frame she had, not the red and orange of California's inland hills or the New England colors she'd seen in photographs, but something stranger and more saturated, a range of colors that the pigment systems of Sphinx's specific biology produced and that had no precedent in anything she'd looked at before.

She looked at them for a long time.

Scotty came to stand beside her with coffee and she didn't say anything and he didn't say anything and the morning held them in the specific way of mornings in places where you were not a visitor.

"Okay," she said eventually.

"Yes," he said.

"The winter version has to earn this," she said.

"It does," he said. "In different ways."

She thought about different ways and about the word earn and about the specific project of coming to know a place through the different versions of itself that it offered across time.

She thought: I want to know this place through its different versions.

She thought: I want to come back.

She held both of those things and they were both true.

The house was the Tremaine family's property in the way of properties that had been family properties for long enough to have the accumulated character of that, the layers of different generations' decisions visible in the architecture and the garden and the arrangement of the spaces inside.

His parents were not in residence. They were on Manticore for the next month, which she'd been told was a coincidence and which she suspected was something closer to the considerate arrangement of considerate people who had heard about her and decided that the first visit to the place should not also be the first meeting with the family.

She filed this with appreciation and didn't mention it.

The property had a person who managed it, a woman named Sascha who was seventyish and had the specific quality of someone who had been managing something for long enough that the management had become indistinguishable from care. She'd received Buffy and Scotty with the comfortable authority of someone in their own domain and had produced dinner that first night that was the Sphinx version of what Allison's cooking was the Beowulf version of, which was to say serious and specific and made with the understanding that food was how you told people things you wanted them to know.

Buffy had eaten an enormous amount. Sascha had watched and said nothing and produced more, which was the correct response.

She slept better than she'd slept in months.

The Sphinx air was different from Manticore's air and different from Beowulf's and had the quality that Scotty had described as something you'd know when you breathed it, a specific character she didn't have a word for. Cold, obviously, but cold with a composition, the specific mixture of what Sphinx's atmosphere produced in autumn at this elevation making something that tasted like clarity when she breathed it.

She breathed it and let it be what it was.

The Keeper was present in the way it was always present and was also, she thought, doing something that she could only describe as appreciating the air. Which was a strange thing to think about a consciousness at a dimensional interface. And which she thought was probably accurate.

She spent the first three days learning the property.

Not systematically. She wasn't building a patrol route or mapping a territory for operational purposes. She was walking it the way she'd walked the waterfronts of Manticore and Beowulf, with the ambient version of the Slayer sense that was simply awareness rather than assessment, learning what a place felt like to be in.

The property felt like something that had been inhabited with care for long enough that the care had become structural. The garden that Sascha tended and which was between its season of growth and its season of rest, the specific beautiful stage of things winding down that had its own character distinct from the wound-down version that winter would produce. The outbuildings that had the purpose-built quality of things that had been added when the purpose became clear rather than in anticipation of it. The paths between things that had been worn by the repetition of actual use rather than designed.

Scotty walked with her on some of it and let her walk alone on the rest of it, which was the correct calibration and which she didn't have to explain.

On the fourth day she asked him about the treecats.

She'd known they were from Sphinx from Honor's description and from the briefings she'd read months ago, but knowing from description and being in the place were different things. She'd seen the signs of them in the edge of the property, the specific evidence of things that moved through the canopy with deliberate purpose.

"Are they here?" she said. "On the property?"

"The range that uses this area. Yes." He looked toward the tree line. "They generally avoid close contact with people they don't know."

"Generally."

"There's been one who comes to the edge of the garden sometimes." He paused. "Sascha leaves fruit. The treecat takes it." He paused. "Sascha says it watches the house."

She looked at the tree line.

The Keeper's quality was the quality it brought to things that were relevant in the lineage register rather than the operational one, the sense of something touching the channel in the way the dreams touched it.

"It's watching me," she said.

Scotty looked at her. "The treecat?"

"Yes." She kept looking at the tree line. "The Keeper's doing something I associate with � contact. Not the Constant's kind of contact. Something more like when Nimitz is paying attention to me." She paused. "Treecats feel things through their empathic sense. The Keeper operates through the channel. They're not the same thing but they might be�adjacent."

"Adjacent," he said.

"In the same register," she said. "Both of them reading something at the interface level." She looked at the tree line. "The treecat on this property has been reading me since I arrived."

Scotty was quiet for a moment. "Is that a problem?"

"No," she said. "I think it's�" she stopped. "I think it's curious. And I think if Nimitz's read on me is any guide, being read clearly by a treecat is a good thing rather than a concerning one." She paused. "I want to meet it."

"Treecats choose when they meet people."

"I know." She looked at the tree line. "I'm going to sit out here tomorrow morning and see what happens."

What happened was that she sat on the low wall at the edge of the garden at six in the morning with the autumn doing its morning version around her and the wrong stars fading in the specific Sphinx way, and at six-forty-two something moved in the canopy and a treecat came to the edge of the lower branches and looked at her.

It was not Nimitz's size. Slightly smaller, younger-looking, with coloring that was the Sphinx autumn colors, the camouflage of something that had evolved in exactly this landscape. Its eyes were the same green as Nimitz's eyes and they were doing the same thing Nimitz's eyes always did, which was see clearly and without apology.

She looked back.

The Keeper was very present.

The treecat looked at her for a long time. Then it made a sound she hadn't heard Nimitz make, something low and questioning, and she didn't know what the question was but she answered it as honestly as she could, which was to simply be present and readable, offering whatever the channel and the Slayer sense and the accumulated weight of who she was produced in the register the treecat was reading.

The treecat came closer.

Not all the way down from the canopy. But closer, close enough that she could see its whiskers and the small deliberate way it held its true-hands when it was alert.

It made another sound.

She said, very quietly: "Hi."

It looked at her.

Then it went back up into the canopy and disappeared.

She sat on the wall for another twenty minutes and thought about what had just happened, which was that a native Sphinx treecat had made a preliminary assessment of her and had not left immediately and had said something she didn't have a word for and had done so in the register that the Keeper was present in.

She was going to write to Nimitz's previous studies about this.

She was going to write to Honor.

She was going to tell Haverstock, who was going to have a theory.

She did none of those things immediately. She sat on the wall and let the morning happen around her and thought about the specific and wonderful strangeness of the universe she was in and let it be wonderful and strange rather than asking it to be otherwise.

She thought: this is a remarkable place.

She thought: I want to come back.

She went inside and ate an enormous breakfast and Sascha watched and said nothing and produced more.

The week in Sphinx had the specific quality of a week that was also a different kind of time, the time you were inside rather than moving through, where the days had texture rather than direction. She'd been operating in directional time for months, the time of someone who knew where they were going and was going there. This was different. Restorative in a way she hadn't known she needed until she was in the middle of it.

She and Scotty talked. The real versions of the conversations they'd been having in letters for four months, the conversations that the letter form could approach but couldn't fully contain. About the war and where it was going and what that meant for him. About the institution and where it was going and what that meant for her. About Sphinx and Manticore and Beowulf and all the places that were becoming the geography of a life in this century.

About after. The shape of it.

They talked about the after with the specific care of people who were building something they couldn't fully see yet and knew that was true and were continuing anyway, which was the only way you built anything real.

She told him things she hadn't fully told anyone else. Not because she'd been hiding them but because they required the specific kind of conversation that required being somewhere you weren't operational, somewhere you could be still without the stillness being a resource you were directing at something.

She told him about the loneliness. The specific texture of it in this century, which was different from the loneliness she'd known in the previous one because there had been the Council and the Scoobies and the sense of a world she knew even when she was alone in it, and this loneliness had been the loneliness of not knowing the world, of learning to be a self in a context that had no landmarks from the previous version.

She told him about building the new self from available materials, which was what she'd been doing, and how that process was the same as the physical healing in some ways, the body doing what it needed to do regardless of whether the conscious mind was on board with the timeline.

He listened.

He didn't try to fix it. He received it with the quality of attention that she'd come to understand was one of the most specific things about him, the capacity to receive something difficult without either resolving it prematurely or being flattened by it.

He told her things too.

About the Talbott Cluster and what had happened there and the specific kind of cost that those months had produced, not the physical cost which had been minor but the other kind, the cost of being in situations that required decisions faster than certainty was available and living with the decisions afterward.

She listened the same way he had.

They were, she thought, good at this. At the actual version of each other, not the managed presentation.

On the last night she said: "I want to tell you something."

He looked at her.

"I've been bad at this," she said. "The letting people in part. I've been better at it here than I was in the previous century, which is an indictment of the previous century as much as a credit to me, but I want to say it directly rather than leave it implied." She held his gaze. "You're in. If you want to be."

He held her gaze for a moment.

"I've been in," he said. "Since the terrace at Allison's."

"I know," she said. "I wanted to say it in words."

"Thank you for saying it in words."

She looked at him. He looked at her.

Then she leaned across the space between them and kissed him, which was the other way of saying it in words and which she'd been working toward with the same care she brought to things she'd decided mattered.

He kissed her back.

The Sphinx night was outside the window, cold in the way of the cold she hadn't imagined, the spectacular autumn doing its last act before it gave way to winter, and they were here, in the house, and this was the after.

Not everything she'd lost. Not a replacement for anything she'd lost. Its own thing, made of its own materials, with its own specific shape.

She thought: this is mine.

She thought: yes.
