Chapter 39 (30 of 54 in this book)

The letter from Allison arrived in summer.

It was long, which was characteristic of Allison in epistolary mode, and it was full of research updates that required concentration to follow and personal observations that required no concentration at all because they arrived with the directness that was Allison's mode in all registers.

The research update was this: the genetic analysis had reached the point of a complete map. Not just the modification but the full lineage genome, the specific sequence that every carrier shared and that varied only in its expression level. The map was the most detailed picture of what the lineage was, biologically, that had ever existed.

Allison's assessment of the map included a paragraph that Buffy read three times.

The paragraph said: the modification is not stable at the interface level in isolation. It requires something to maintain its coherence across generations. I have been working on what that maintenance requires and I believe the answer is the network. The connections between lineage members are not merely social or informational. There is a biological component. The channel functions better, maintains its calibration more precisely, when lineage members are in contact with each other. This is why the Vigil's work is not merely logistically useful. It is biologically necessary. The lineage maintains itself through connection.

She read that paragraph to Carson.

He was quiet for a long time.

Then he said: "That's why it survived."

"Yes," she said. "Not the archive. Not the Watchers' Council. The lineage itself. The connections between people who didn't know they were connected." She paused. "It maintained its calibration through contact that wasn't understood as contact."

"And now we're making the contact deliberate."

"And now we're making the contact deliberate," she said. "Which means the calibration gets better." She held his gaze. "Which means the fully expressed get better. And the channel in the subclinical carriers gets�" she paused, looking for the word.

"Clearer," he said.

"Clearer," she said. "Yes."

She wrote back to Allison immediately.

The letter she wrote back was full of questions that Allison was going to love and a question at the end that was different from the others.

The different question was: do you want to come to Manticore?

Not for a visit. The letter was specific about that. To stay. To be the Vigil's chief medical researcher, the person who continued the work in the place where the network was strongest because that was where the Vigil was.

She sent the letter and told Fritz what she'd asked and watched his face do the thing it did when he received news that required significant reorganization of his existing priorities and which he was going to reorganize around because the reorganization was correct.

"She'll say yes," Fritz said.

"I think so too," Buffy said.

"And Honor's mother coming to Manticore is going to�" he paused.

"Yes," she said. "Honor is going to have feelings about that."

"Good feelings," Fritz said, with the certainty of someone who had been paying close attention to the relevant people for long enough to have reliable data.

"Good feelings," she agreed.

She went back to Sphinx in the winter.

Not as a visit. She'd been thinking about what Scotty had said months ago, about the property and the northern range, and she'd been thinking about what the Vigil needed in the way of a place that was not the capital, not the institutional space of the Manticore system, but somewhere with the quality of the Tremaine property in autumn, the specific restorative quality of a place built for inhabitation rather than function.

The conversation about it happened on a Tuesday evening in her quarters with Scotty across the table and the city outside the window and the Keeper present.

She said: "I want to talk about the property."

He looked at her.

She said: "Not just for visits. For the work. Some of it." She paused. "Training. The physical and the other kind. The lineage candidates need a place that isn't an institution. A place that feels like somewhere they actually are rather than somewhere they're being processed." She paused. "Your family's property feels like that."

He was quiet for a moment, looking at her.

"That's the practical framing," he said.

"Yes," she said.

"What's the other framing?"

She held his gaze. "I want to be somewhere that's ours," she said. "Not the capital. Not the institutional spaces. Somewhere we chose because we chose it." She paused. "I want to plant a garden."

He held her gaze for a long time.

"Talk to my parents," he said. "When you're ready."

"Are you ready?"

"I've been ready," he said. "I was waiting for you to be."

She thought about the specific kind of patience that was not waiting-out but waiting-toward, the kind that was not passive but was the deliberate holding of a space for something to arrive in its own time.

She said: "I'm ready."

He said: "Then let's go to Sphinx."

The winter version of the Tremaine property was not the spectacular version. He'd warned her about this and she'd believed him and the believing had not prepared her for the specific quality of Sphinx in winter, which was a different kind of overwhelming.

The trees that had been spectacular in autumn were bare now, stripped down to their structural reality, the bones of things that had been full of color and were now the pure shape of themselves, and there was something in that she found beautiful in the way she hadn't expected, the beauty of things revealing what they were without the elaboration.

Snow. She hadn't been in snow before. Southern California and then this century's capital cities had not provided snow, and the snow that Sphinx produced in the northern range in winter was the kind that was not metaphorical, was not decorative, was the real thing in full weight.

She stood in it the first morning at six and let it fall on her and looked at the property under it and thought: yes. This.

She ran in it.

The running in snow was not the same as the running on the waterfront or the running in the gym or the ancient running of the Girl's dream-memories, but it was its own specific thing, the body working differently in a different medium, finding the adaptation that the adaptation required. She ran until she was warm from the inside in the specific way that cold-weather running produced and then she came back and her footprints were in the snow behind her and the property was the property and she was here.

She was here.

She ran her hands through a branch of the bare tree at the garden's edge and looked at the structure of it, the specific articulation of bark and smaller branch and where the growth had happened and where it hadn't, the whole decision-history of the tree visible in its winter architecture.

The treecat was in the canopy.

She'd known it was there before she saw it, the Keeper's orientation toward it the signal that had become reliable over the months since her first visit. It was looking down at her from a branch that gave it a clear angle and she looked up at it.

"Hi," she said.

It made the sound. The low questioning one that it had made the first time.

She answered it the same way she had before, by being present and readable.

It came lower.

Not all the way down. But lower, and when she turned back toward the house it came with her, moving through the canopy in the parallel way that treecats moved when they were accompanying rather than following.

She let it accompany her.

Scotty was at the kitchen window and he watched her come across the snowy garden with the treecat in the canopy above her and his face did something she didn't have a name for exactly.

She came in.

He said: "It's coming closer."

"I know," she said. "I'm not pushing it."

"I know you're not." He handed her coffee that she turned into hot chocolate by the process she'd developed which involved a packet from the supply she'd brought from the capital. "Sascha thinks it's been watching the property for a year."

"It has," she said. "Since the first visit."

"And it's coming closer."

"Yes." She looked out the window at the canopy. "I don't know what that means yet. I'm letting it tell me."

He looked at her with the expression he had when he was thinking about her rather than about the subject of the conversation. "Okay," he said.

She drank her chocolate and looked at the winter and let it be what it was.

Full. Specific. The bones of the trees and the snow and the treecat in the canopy and the kitchen and the man beside her and the work ahead and the life they were building in this place from available materials.

This was the life.

Not the one she'd planned. Not the one she'd lost. The one she'd built from what was here when she arrived, in this century, in this place, with these people.

The Keeper was present.

The Girl ran somewhere at the edge of awareness, patient and ancient and simply continuing as it had always continued.

She was here.

She thought: good.

She drank her chocolate and watched the winter and let it be good.
