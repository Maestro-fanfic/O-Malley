Chapter 19 (10 of 54 in this book)

Three weeks after their arrival on Manticore, Honor left.

Not permanently. The nature of being Honor Harrington in the middle of a war was that you were where the war needed you, and the war had found something it needed her for that required her to be elsewhere. She told Buffy two days before she went, in a conversation that was direct and brief in the way that Honor's important conversations tended to be.

"I'm not leaving you here alone," she said. "You have Fritz. You have Scotty and Carson while their reassignments process. You have Commodore Shaughnessy, who is staying in the capital for the next month at least. And you have the Queen's office, which will be in contact with you through Lieutenant Chen."

"I know," Buffy said. "I'm not worried about being alone."

"I know you're not." Honor held her gaze. "I'm worried about you being alone."

There was a distinction in there that Buffy worked out after a moment. "I'll be fine."

"I know that too." Honor was quiet for a moment. Nimitz was on her shoulder in the way he always was, the warm living weight of him, the green eyes tracking between them. "This is not a war that ends quickly. What I'm going to be doing for the next several months will determine things that matter for a long time. I need to be doing it."

"I know." She looked at Nimitz. He looked back at her. "Will you tell him something for me?"

"Tell him yourself," Honor said. "He hears you."

She looked at the treecat. "Tell her to come back," she said.

Nimitz reached out and laid his true-hand on her hand briefly. Just for a moment. Then he withdrew it and Honor's expression did the layered thing and she reached out and touched Buffy's shoulder once, a brief firm touch that said more than a longer contact would have.

"I'll come back," Honor said.

She went.

Buffy stood in the corridor outside Honor's quarters for a moment after she left, and then she went to the gym and spent an hour doing things that required her full physical attention and nothing else, because sometimes the body was the right tool for the emotion and sometimes the emotion needed to be temporarily outran.

She outran it.

Then she came back and ate an enormous lunch and went to her afternoon briefing with Naval Intelligence, which had become a regular feature of her Manticore days, and she was present and accurate and useful and when it was over she walked back through the capital city with the late afternoon light on the stone buildings and let the city be a city around her.

She was building context. Not fast enough for her preference, but steadily. The daily briefings were accumulating into something like understanding, the skeleton she'd assembled from six weeks of reading beginning to acquire flesh from direct engagement with people who were inside the situation rather than describing it from the outside.

The war was real in ways it hadn't been real from the Wayfarer's tactical display. It was in the faces of the people she talked to and the specific quality of the decisions being made and the things that weren't being said in the briefings because they didn't need to be said, because everyone in the room already knew them and lived with them.

Haven was real. Not the political entity, which she understood, but the human reality of it, the people who were prosecuting the war on the other side, the ideological machinery that had produced State Security and the Tepes and the garrison on Hades and the questioner in the outbuilding with his professional tired voice. She thought about that machinery with the specific kind of attention she'd learned for thinking about the things she was supposed to fight.

She'd always understood her opponents better by thinking about what they believed rather than what they did, because what they did was downstream of what they believed and if you understood the belief you could predict the action.

Haven believed in the People's Republic. Or the people running it believed in it, or believed in themselves, or believed in the logic of the system they'd built which required certain things to remain coherent and produced the Tepes as a coherent output.

She thought about this for a long time.

She wasn't sure yet what to do with what she concluded. But she filed it with the other things she was filing, the growing archive of this century that was becoming something she could work with.

Scotty's reassignment came through six weeks after their arrival.

She'd known it was coming. She'd known it from the day they arrived, the way she knew all the things that were going to happen that she didn't want to happen, with the specific anticipatory weight of impending rather than surprise. He was a naval officer in a war. The war had ongoing requirements. The navy was going to put him where it needed him.

He told her the day before it was official.

They were sitting on the roof of the building where her temporary quarters had become her quarters in the way that temporary things became permanent through the accumulation of inhabitation. The roof had a garden, formal by design and slightly wild at the edges, and they'd discovered it two weeks in and used it intermittently since.

The late afternoon was doing what late afternoons in the capital did, the specific quality of the light she was coming to know, not the same as California but its own thing entirely, and she was getting good at letting new things be their own things rather than measuring them against the things they'd replaced.

"HMS Hexapuma," he said. "Commander Terekhov's ship. We're being assigned to the Talbott Cluster."

She'd read about the Talbott Cluster. It was one of the places the war had spread to that had complicated the already complicated political situation in ways that the Naval Intelligence briefings returned to regularly. "That's not the front."

"No. It's something different from the front." He paused. "Possibly more complicated than the front, depending on how the Solarian situation develops."

She thought about what she knew about the Solarian situation, which was more than she'd known six weeks ago and less than she'd have liked. "When?"

"Three weeks."

She looked at the city. The late afternoon light on the stone. "Okay."

He looked at her. "Okay?"

"You're going where the navy needs you. That's what you do. I knew that." She kept her voice even. "It doesn't mean I'm glad about it."

He was quiet for a moment. "I'm not glad about it either."

She looked at him.

He looked back.

They'd been doing this for two months, building the thing they were building, the careful incremental thing that neither of them had named because naming it would change the rate at which it was developing and neither of them had wanted to change the rate. She knew what it was. She was fairly confident he knew what it was. They'd been proceeding at the pace of people who had other things to deal with and also had time, or had believed they had time.

Three weeks was time, but it was a different kind.

"I'll be honest with you," she said.

"Please."

"I don't know how to do this part. The part where someone goes and you don't know when they're coming back." She paused. "I've done it. I've had to do it more times than I wanted. Every time it was something I survived rather than something I was good at."

He was quiet for a moment. "I'll come back."

"Don't make me that promise," she said. "I don't believe that promise. I've heard it too many times and I know what it means when the universe doesn't cooperate."

"Then I'll make you the other one." His voice was steady. "I'll be as careful as the job allows. I'll think about what I'm doing and why before I do it. I won't spend myself on something that doesn't warrant the cost." He paused. "And I'll come back when I can, even if I can't promise the when."

She held his gaze.

"Okay," she said. "That one I'll take."

He reached out and she let him take her hand, and they sat on the roof of the building with the late afternoon going its particular way around them and the city below doing its city things, and she thought about the shape of this, the specific configuration of a person choosing to be present with someone who was going to leave without knowing the date of their return.

She'd always been bad at goodbyes. She'd learned to do them anyway.

She wasn't ready to do this one yet. She had three weeks. She was going to use them well.

"Tell me more about Sphinx," she said.

He looked at her.

"The northern range," she said. "The cold you said I hadn't imagined. Tell me what it looks like."

He told her.

Carson's reassignment processed two days after Scotty's.

His was different in kind. He was young and he'd been through what he'd been through, and the Navy had decided, with the particular institutional thoughtfulness that the Navy occasionally demonstrated, that what Carson needed was not an immediate combat posting but a period of consolidation. He was being assigned to the Naval Academy on Saganami Island as an instructor's aide, a posting that would keep him in the Manticore system and allow him to finish his recovery from the arm wound and complete certain formalities of his commission.

He told her on the same day she found out about his posting from Lieutenant Chen, which meant they had the same information at the same time and arrived at the conversation equally.

"Saganami Island," she said.

"Yes." He was slightly pink in the way that Grayson men were pink when they were processing emotions they'd been taught to keep well-managed. "It's here. In the system."

"I know." She looked at him. "Is that what you wanted?"

He was quiet for a moment. "I wanted to go back out. To be where things are happening." He paused. "And I also wanted to not go back out yet. And both of those were true at once and I didn't know what to do with that."

"So the Navy decided for you."

"In the best possible way," he said. "I think."

She thought about that. About the specific mercy of having a decision made for you when you were genuinely uncertain, not the relief of having your agency removed but the relief of having your ambivalence resolved by an institution that had thought about it independently and arrived at a reasonable conclusion.

"Saganami Island is not far," she said.

"A short trip by shuttle." He paused. "I could come back to the capital when the schedule allows."

"Yes," she said. "You could."

He looked at her with the steady clear look she'd come to rely on, the one that took things in without flinching and offered steadiness in return. "Are you all right? With all of this. The changing of things."

She thought about the honest answer.

"I'm practicing being all right with it," she said. "Things change. People go places. That's the condition." She paused. "I've spent two years trying to hold things still enough to protect them and things kept changing anyway and the trying to hold them still was some of what cost me." She looked at the window, the city beyond it. "I'm trying a different approach."

"Which is?"

"Letting things be what they are and staying anyway." She paused. "Being the thing that doesn't move when everything else does. Except that I move too, obviously. I just�" She stopped. "I'm still working out what I mean."

He nodded slowly. "I think I understand." He paused. "You're trying to be the anchor rather than the thing being anchored."

She looked at him. "Yeah," she said. "That's it."

"You'd be good at that," he said, with the same certainty with which he said all the things he was certain about. "You already are."

She thought about the Girl running through the ancient dark with the awareness that everything around her was measured relative to where she was. Not the center of things, not the most important thing, but the fixed point that other things could orient by.

"Working on it," she said.

He smiled at her. Young and from Grayson and steady in a way that had nothing to do with age, the earned stillness of someone who had chosen their ground.

"Me too," he said.

The three weeks before Scotty left were their own kind of weather.

Not dramatic. Not the concentrated urgency of the operation or the sustained compression of the Wayfarer passage. Just weeks, with their particular texture, the daily substance of people building something before they had to stop building for a while and trust that what they'd built would hold.

They explored the city. Not systematically, in the way Buffy had been exploring things since she arrived, with the deliberate intention of building understanding, but the other way, the way you explored when the exploring was the point rather than the product. He knew the capital better than he'd suggested, had spent time here in training and on leave, and he knew the parts of it that weren't on official maps, the specific small places that cities accumulated like sediment, the good ones and the strange ones and the ones that were good because they were strange.

There was a bookshop on a side street off the main commercial district that had been in the same family for four generations and which was organized according to a system that appeared to be entirely personal and which the current proprietor, a woman of about seventy with the look of someone who had been living in a happy world of books for fifty years, could navigate with complete confidence. Buffy spent two hours in it on a Tuesday afternoon and came out with four books and the feeling of having been somewhere that had its own very particular climate.

There was a park that was high enough on the hill that you could see both the harbor and the military base from the same bench, and they sat on that bench on a Thursday evening and ate food from a cart vendor that was not fancy and was very good and watched the two views simultaneously.

There was a concert of some kind on a Saturday that they found by following the sound through three streets and which turned out to be an informal gathering of musicians who played something that was technically traditional Manticoran folk music but which had absorbed enough influences over several centuries to be something that didn't need that description. She listened to it and thought about music she'd loved and music she was going to have to find equivalents for and thought that maybe this was a candidate.

On the last night she made him dinner.

Not in the formal quarters that the Navy had provided, which had a kitchen she'd been using with increasing confidence as the weeks passed, but in a configuration that was as close to her kitchen as she could get it, which was to say she pushed the furniture around and put the table near the window and found candles, which apparently still existed and still did the same thing to the quality of light they'd always done.

She made the rosemary chicken.

She'd found a recipe, something approximating the recipe, in the Manticoran culinary database that Fritz had helped her access when she'd explained what she was looking for. The ingredients were not identical. The herbs here were close but different. The chicken was chicken in the way that everything on Manticore was close but different, the same fundamental biology with two thousand years of evolutionary divergence creating variations that were usually imperceptible and occasionally significant.

She made it anyway.

He arrived at the time she'd told him, dressed for dinner without being formal about it, which was the correct read, and stopped in the doorway when he saw the table and the candles and smelled what was in the oven.

He didn't say anything for a moment.

"Sit down," she said.

He sat.

She cooked and they talked, the particular ease of a conversation that had two months of foundation under it, and she thought about how this was different from the previous versions of this kind of ease she'd had, which had been good and real and had asked less of her than this, or had asked different things. This version asked her to be present all the way down, not the managed version of presence that you deployed in professional contexts or even in friendships but the actual version, the one where the other person could see what was under the management.

She was getting better at the actual version.

The chicken came out of the oven.

He ate it in the concentrated way of someone receiving something seriously. She watched him.

"What is this?" he asked.

"Rosemary chicken. My mother's recipe, or as close as I could get it." She looked at her own plate. "The herbs are different. The actual taste is�not the same. But the shape of it is."

He looked at her.

"I wanted to make something from home," she said. "And this was the thing from home that I could most reconstruct from available materials." She paused. "I'm aware that's a lot to put on a chicken."

"It's not too much," he said. He paused. "It's good."

"It's not the same as hers."

"No." He looked at his plate and then at her. "But it's yours. The version you made here." He paused. "That's not nothing."

She looked at the candles. The light they made, the particular warm quality of it that was the same regardless of what century the candle was in.

"No," she said. "It's not nothing."

They ate. The city was outside the window doing its evening things. She thought about Joyce Summers in her kitchen, the specific way she moved in it, the sounds she made while cooking. She let herself think about it directly rather than sideways, which was the thing she was practicing, looking at the losses directly without letting them become the only thing in the room.

It was still her mother.

It would always be her mother.

She was also here, at a table with candles, with a person she'd found in the worst possible context and who had turned out to be exactly what she'd needed without having been designed that way, which was the only kind that counted.

She was both of those things at once.

She held both.

After dinner they sat at the window and she let him talk about Sphinx until he ran out of things to say about it and then she talked about Sunnydale until she ran out, and by the end the night was properly dark and the city was doing its nighttime configuration and neither of them had said the things that were waiting to be said.

She thought about whether to say them.

She thought about two years of not saying things until it was too late to say them. She thought about the habit of waiting for certainty before speaking, the way the wait had a cost that she'd paid more than once and hadn't accounted for fully until it was already paid.

She looked at him.

He was looking at her.

"I'm not good at this part," she said.

"The last night part?"

"The saying things part." She paused. "I always wait for the right moment and the right moment always passes."

He held her gaze. "What would you say? If the moment was right."

She thought about it. The honest answer was simple, which was usually the sign that it was the true one. "That the past two months have been better because you were in them," she said. "And that I don't know what comes next but I know I'd like you to be in that too. And that I'm aware that's a lot to say to someone who's leaving in twelve hours."

He looked at her for a long moment.

"That's not too much to say," he said. "That's exactly the right amount." He paused. "The past two months have been better because of you. What comes next�" he paused� "I'd like that too."

She looked at him.

He looked at her.

Neither of them said anything else for a while, which was the right amount of not saying anything else.

Outside the city did its nighttime things and the light was the wrong light in the right way and she was here, in this specific place, and it was enough.

It was, it turned out, quite a lot.

~~~

He left in the morning.

She walked with him to the transport that was taking his kit to the naval base, because he was traveling light and his kit was going separately and he was the kind of person who traveled light, which she'd come to understand was characteristic. They walked through the capital in the early morning with the city assembling itself around them and she let it be exactly what it was without trying to make it mean more than it did or less than it did.

At the transport bay she stopped.

He stopped.

"Sphinx," she said. "When this is finished."

"Sphinx," he said. "The northern range. You're going to need a coat."

"You're going to have to help me pick one."

"I can do that."

She looked at him. He looked at her. There were other people around them doing the business of a military transport bay in the early morning and none of it was relevant to this specific moment.

"Come back," she said.

"I'll be as careful as the job allows," he said.

She nodded.

He went.

She stood in the transport bay until the transport was gone and then she walked back through the city in the early morning light and the city was around her in its particular Manticoran way, full of people who were going to their various places for their various reasons, and she was among them.

She stopped at the waterfront on the way back and stood at the railing above the harbor with its working water in its wrong blue-green and she was here, in this place, and the shape of what she was building here was visible from where she stood in a way it hadn't quite been before.

A place. People in it. Something worth fighting for.

She was working on all three.

She turned from the railing and walked back into the city and the city held her the way cities hold you when you've been there long enough to start to belong to them, which is to say impersonally and completely, and she let it.

She was going to be here a while.

She was starting to think that was all right.
