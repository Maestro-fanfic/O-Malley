Chapter 14 (5 of 54 in this book)

At 0155 the camp was already empty in the ways that mattered.

McKeon's team had been in position for twenty minutes, working through the jungle on the route Buffy had mapped over three nights of observation. LaFollet's team had gone south, taking the longer approach that would put them in position at the east and south perimeter faces with enough margin to settle and breathe before the clock ran.

The two shuttles sat in the clearing with their engines in standby mode, Martingale in the lead and a young rating named Tomas Ibanez in the second whose hands Buffy had checked on the controls that afternoon and found steadier than his face suggested. Martingale had looked at Buffy doing this and not said anything, which Buffy appreciated.

Buffy and Scotty were at the tree line north of the compound at 0158.

Two minutes.

The compound sat in its nighttime configuration, the timer lights running their regular sweep, the guards at their posts. She'd watched this configuration for three nights and she knew it the way she knew the rhythms of things she'd lived alongside long enough to internalize. The guard at the northeast corner would complete his turn in forty seconds. The gap in the perimeter grid would open in sixty-three seconds. The guard inside the landing pad fence had a habit of checking his communicator at intervals of approximately twelve minutes and was due for one in about four, which would put his attention down for approximately thirty seconds.

She knew all of this and it was not enough and it was all she had.

"Clock," she said.

"Ninety seconds," Scotty said.

She breathed.

The thing about operations, the thing that nobody who hadn't done them could fully understand, was the quality of the time immediately before. Not the fear, which was present but manageable, not the tactical calculations, which were complete. The quality of the waiting itself. It had a texture unlike any other kind of waiting. It was the moment before the door opened, and what was on the other side of the door was not knowable and had never been knowable and you went through anyway.

She'd been going through anyway since she was fifteen.

"Sixty seconds," Scotty said.

She felt the shift in him beside her, not visible but present, the same way she felt weather fronts before they arrived. The compression of a person gathering themselves.

"Hey," she said.

He looked at her.

"You're going to be fine."

He held her gaze for a moment. "I know," he said. And then, because he was Scotty Tremaine and apparently couldn't help it: "Are you?"

She almost smiled. "I'm always fine."

"Thirty seconds."

McKeon's first device went off on schedule, south side of the compound, the compression reaching them two seconds after the light. The compound lights snapped to full. She watched the guards move, watched the specific choreography of a unit responding to a threat signal, professional and trained and directing themselves toward the south.

The perimeter grid shimmered and cycled and the gap opened.

"Now," she said.

They ran.

The gap was ninety seconds and they were through it in twelve, which left seventy-eight seconds of grace that she didn't use for comfort because the next obstacle was thirty meters ahead and grace ran out without warning. The inner fence was fixed rather than electrified, which she'd confirmed on the second night. She went over it in one movement, felt Scotty land beside her a beat later, kept moving.

The landing pad was visible ahead across one hundred and sixty meters of open ground that was currently unoccupied because everyone capable of occupying it had been pulled toward the south perimeter.

Currently.

"Move," she said, and they moved.

She counted as she ran. Not steps, not time exactly, something more integrated than either, the Slayer sense tracking the compound's attention distribution the way sonar tracked volume in water. The pressure was south. The compound's focus was south. It would not stay south indefinitely.

Eighty meters.

A second device went off, east perimeter, LaFollet's team coming online.

Fifty meters.

The guard inside the landing pad fence turned from the southeast, where the second explosion had drawn his eyes, and turned back toward the pad and saw them.

He was good. She gave him that. He didn't freeze and he didn't shout, he just raised his weapon and she was already past the point where changing course was an option so she committed to the line she was on and covered the last twenty meters faster than she'd been moving before.

The weapon discharged once.

She felt it as a line of heat across her left shoulder, nothing structural, flesh only, the kind of wound that registered as information rather than impairment. She put the guard down with the minimal efficient contact of someone who had learned that the goal was to stop the immediate problem without creating a larger one, and he went down, and the weapon didn't discharge again.

She was at the pinnace.

"Scotty."

He was already at the hatch. The guard's shot had missed him by enough that she could see him registering the near-miss in the way of a person cataloguing an experience for later, when there was time for it. Right now he was focused on the hatch mechanism, his hands moving with the quick certainty of a pilot who knew what he was looking for.

"Thirty seconds," he said.

"We have less than that."

"I know."

She turned and put her back to the pinnace hull and read the compound. The guard she'd put down was out. The shot had been heard, she could tell by the shift in pressure from the south side, someone breaking from the perimeter engagement and turning this direction. Two people. Maybe three.

"Scotty."

"Twenty seconds."

The hatch opened.

She went through it and pulled the guard's body inside with her because leaving it on the pad complicated things and Scotty was already in the pilot's seat and the hatch sealed behind her and the board was lighting up with the speed of someone who had done a preflight in conditions that didn't allow for thoroughness.

"The leg," she said.

"Reads green." His hands were moving. "Either they fixed it or the sensor's lying. We're going to find out."

The compound outside was audible even through the hull, a different kind of activity than before, more directed. Someone had found the empty guard post. Someone would find the guard in a few minutes. She had the gun she'd taken from him and the knife and Scotty's sidearm and eight minutes on the clock had become four.

"Talk to me," she said.

"Engines are up. Navigation online." A pause. "Comms are being jammed. McKeon got the array."

"Can you still coordinate with the shuttles?"

"Short range local, yes. Not going through the garrison system." He looked at the board and then out through the viewport at the landing pad and the two garrison personnel who had just appeared at the fence. "We need to go."

"The shuttles aren't at the rally point yet."

"I know. But if we wait for the shuttles�"

She looked at the fence. The two garrison personnel had been joined by a third. One of them was making the calculation she'd have made in their position, which was that the pinnace was the priority, and moving to the pad access gate with the specific purposeful walk of someone about to prevent a departure.

"Go," she said.

The pinnace lifted on thrusters, the landing leg holding, and she had a half-second of relief about the maintenance status before the compound's second pinnace began powering up across the pad and the calculation changed again.

"The other one," Scotty said.

"I see it."

"If it's combat-capable�"

"Is this pinnace combat-capable?"

"It has defensive systems. Whether they're active and loaded is a different question."

She looked at the board she didn't know how to read. "Tell me which thing to press."

"I need thirty seconds to get us clear of the compound before I can activate anything or we'll be firing into the garrison infrastructure."

"Then get us clear."

He got them clear. The pinnace lifted in the controlled urgent arc of a pilot who was doing this without the safety margins that made the maneuver comfortable and knew it and had decided comfort was not currently the priority. Buffy held the copilot's seat and watched the compound shrink below them and the second pinnace's thrusters light up and tracked the slope of their own climb against the slope of the intercept vector that was forming in her head.

"They're faster," she said.

"We have a head start."

"How much does that matter?"

A brief pause. "Some."

The communicator crackled. Martingale's voice, compressed and efficient: "Pinnace one, this is shuttle lead. We're lifting now. Rally point in four minutes."

"Copy," Scotty said. "We have a trailer."

A pause. "Understood. What's your status?"

"Moving. Defensive systems coming online." His hands moved across the board. "Buffy, the panel to your left. Red cover. Lift it."

She lifted it.

"Two switches. Top one is shields. Bottom is�don't touch the bottom one yet."

She hit the top one.

The board registered something. She didn't know what it registered but Scotty's shoulders dropped approximately two millimeters, which she'd come to understand was his version of visible relief.

"Shields are up," he said. "They won't stop a sustained engagement but they'll buy us time."

The second pinnace was climbing. She could see it through the aft viewport, the garrison vessel running hard on an intercept angle that she didn't have the navigational vocabulary to fully analyze but which her threat sense read clearly enough.

"The bottom switch," she said.

"That's weapons. I need another forty seconds to get target lock. If I fire before lock we're just lighting up the sky."

"They're going to close in forty seconds."

"I know." His voice had the quality she recognized from Honor, the quality of someone working a problem in real time without the performance of working a problem. Just doing it. "I need you to do something."

"Tell me."

"The panel on your right. Bottom row. Three green lights in a sequence. Tell me if the middle one goes amber."

She looked at the panel. Three green lights. "What does amber mean?"

"That their targeting system has a lock on us."

She watched the lights. "Still green."

"Their systems may be older than ours. Or their operator is still calibrating." A pause. "Or they're waiting until they have a clean shot."

"Which of those is worst?"

"The third one."

The middle light went amber.

"Amber," she said.

The pinnace jinked hard left, a movement that did things to her inner ear she hadn't known were possible, and something detonated in the space where they'd been that lit up the viewport in a blue-white wash that faded fast.

"That was a graser," Scotty said, very calmly.

"Is that bad?"

"It's a directed-energy weapon. If it had hit us it would have been very bad." His hands moved. "Target lock in ten seconds."

She watched the amber light. It was cycling, she realized, on and off, the garrison pinnace's operator recalibrating after the evasion.

"The shuttles," she said.

"I see them." The communicator crackled again and she could hear Martingale's voice but not the words clearly. Scotty answered in a clipped exchange she couldn't follow fully but which resulted in a change in his heading that moved them toward the shuttles' position rather than away from it.

"You're using us as cover," she said.

"I'm using the shuttles as a split targeting problem. Two targets is harder than one." He looked at the board. "Lock. Bottom switch."

She hit it.

The pinnace shuddered with the recoil of something she couldn't see, a compression that registered in her chest more than her ears, and the aft viewport lit up in a different color than before.

"Hit?" she asked.

"Hit." A pause. "Non-critical. Their shields are better than ours." He looked at the board. "But now they know we can return fire. That changes their calculation."

It did. She could see it in the way the garrison pinnace's approach vector changed, the flat aggressive line becoming something more cautious, buying range.

"How long until we're out of their effective engagement envelope?" she asked.

"Two minutes. Maybe less if they keep backing off."

The communicator crackled. Martingale's voice, clearer now: "Pinnace one, shuttle two reports thruster variance. Ibanez is compensating but we should be aware."

Scotty and Buffy looked at each other.

"The thruster," he said.

"I know."

"If it fails during the climb�"

"How far are we from the hyper limit?" she asked, which was a question she'd heard enough times in the briefings to know was relevant even without fully understanding what it meant.

"Hours. We're not going for the hyper limit. We're going for minimum safe distance to send an emergency signal and wait for pickup." He paused. "Or we were."

She thought about Ibanez, who had steady hands and a face that didn't match, sitting in the pilot's seat of a shuttle with a thruster that was running variance. She thought about the garrison pinnace behind them, backing off but not gone. She thought about the plan they'd made and the plans that plans became when contact with the actual situation occurred.

"What's the call?" she asked.

He was already making it, she could see it in the way he was working the board, running calculations she didn't have the background to follow. "If we push hard and the thruster holds, we can get enough distance in about forty minutes to be out of their practical interdiction range. If it fails before that�" He stopped.

"Say the rest."

"If it fails before that and they're still in the envelope, we're looking at a difficult conversation."

"Define difficult."

"The kind where Martingale and Ibanez and however many people fit in a crippled shuttle are sitting still while a garrison pinnace decides what to do with them."

She looked at the garrison pinnace, which had stopped backing off and was now holding at range, running its own calculations. She thought about what she knew about the new commandant, younger and more ideologically committed and less operationally experienced. She thought about what more ideological and less experienced meant for the decision to fire on a crippled shuttle versus board it versus let it go.

"They won't fire on a crippled shuttle," she said.

"No?"

"The new commandant wants prisoners. He's been running an interrogation operation for four months. He doesn't want to destroy assets, he wants to exploit them." She watched the garrison pinnace. "If the thruster goes, they'll board."

"And then they have Martingale back. And McKeon's team if they make the shuttles. And everyone else."

"Yes."

"So we need the thruster to hold."

"Or we need them not to have the option to board."

He looked at her. "The second option involves me doing something to a crewed vessel that I am not authorized to do and which would end the career of anyone who did it in a court martial context."

"I know."

"I'm not going to do it."

"I know you're not," she said. "I wasn't suggesting you should." She looked at the garrison pinnace. "I'm suggesting that if it becomes necessary, it won't be your call."

He held her gaze for a moment. Then he looked back at the board. "The thruster needs to hold," he said.

"Yeah," she said. "It does."

It held.

Not cleanly. The variance Ibanez had reported became a periodic fluctuation that put everyone on their respective comms at intervals, and once it became a full dropout that lasted eleven seconds during which the shuttle fell two hundred meters in the climb profile before the thruster caught again and Ibanez's voice on the communicator was the voice of a man who had just experienced something very clarifying and was processing it in real time. But it held.

The garrison pinnace tracked them to the edge of its practical envelope and then peeled off, the calculation having resolved in the direction Buffy had expected. No firing solution that didn't risk the target. No boarding option without coming inside the range of the other pinnace's weapons. A new commandant running his first actual operation, against opponents who had already demonstrated more capability than his briefing material had predicted, making the choice that preserved his remaining assets.

She watched it go.

"They'll report our vector," Scotty said.

"Can they catch us?"

"Not with anything that's currently in this system. If they get a signal out before McKeon's jamming effect lapses, they could have someone at our estimated position in�" he calculated�"sixteen to twenty hours. Depends on what assets are available."

"And McKeon's jamming."

"Bought us another hour, maybe two. After that their comms are back up."

She did the math. "So we need to be somewhere they can't reach in sixteen hours."

"We need to be picked up by someone they can't casually engage in sixteen hours," he corrected. "Which means we need the emergency signal to reach someone useful."

"Honor."

He nodded. "Honor. She knows who to call."

She thought about Honor in the second shuttle, which she hadn't been in when the plan went because the plan had her in the lead shuttle with Martingale and the lead shuttle was in the air and climbing. She pulled up the communicator. "Shuttle lead, this is pinnace one. What's Steadholder Harrington's status?"

Martingale's voice: "Aboard shuttle two with McKeon's team. They made the rally point with two minutes to spare."

She let out a breath she hadn't been tracking.

"Everyone?" she said.

A pause that was slightly too long. "LaFollet's team has one casualty. Non-critical. Ensign Clinkscales took a graze to the right arm during the perimeter withdrawal."

She closed her eyes for one second. Opened them. "Understood. Status of the casualty?"

"Walking, talking, and arguing with Surgeon-Lieutenant Martingale about whether he needs to sit down." A beat. "I'm going to suggest he sits down."

She almost smiled. "Tell him that's an order."

"Aye, pinnace one."

She put the communicator down and looked at the viewport, which showed the planet below them in its nighttime face, the continent they'd been on a dark mass shot through with the faint lights of the garrison compound and the landing pad and the diminishing spark of the garrison pinnace returning to its base.

"Carson took a hit," she said.

"I heard." Scotty's voice was even. "He's all right."

"He's seventeen."

"So are you."

She looked at the planet. "I know. That's why I said it."

He was quiet for a moment. The board hummed its various technical humming. The planet fell away beneath them at the rate of a stressed thruster working harder than its maintenance history recommended.

"First time someone you were responsible for got hit?" he asked.

"No." She watched the planet. "Every time feels like the first time though."

He didn't try to fix that. She appreciated it.

~~~

They found Honor on the communicator twelve minutes later.

Her voice was the same on a crackling short-range signal from a stressed shuttle as it was in a briefing room, which was to say level and precise and carrying its priorities in the order of their importance. "Pinnace one. Good to hear you."

"Good to be heard," Scotty said. "We have a window of approximately ninety minutes before the garrison gets their comms back. After that we're visible."

"Understood. I'm putting together the signal package now. Do you have a preference for who picks it up?"

"Anything Manticoran," Scotty said.

"That may not be the fastest option depending on disposition of forces." A pause that carried the sound of Honor doing several things simultaneously. "I have a contact. She'll be in range to receive inside the window if we boost the signal."

"Who?"

"Someone who will come." The simplicity of it was complete. "Get your people comfortable. We may be waiting a few hours after contact."

"The shuttle's thruster�"

"I know. I heard Ibanez's reports. If it becomes critical we redistribute personnel to the pinnace and the lead shuttle and Ibanez puts the second shuttle in as stable an orbit as it'll hold." Another pause. "How's Scotty?"

Buffy looked at Scotty. He raised an eyebrow. "Tell her I'm fine," he said.

"He says he's fine," Buffy said.

"He would." A warmth in Honor's voice that didn't soften its edges, the way the warmth always operated in her register, foundational rather than surface. "And our other crew member?"

"Also fine," Buffy said. "Left shoulder's already dealt with."

A brief silence. "You were hit."

"Graze. It's been healed for an hour."

"Fritz is going to want to know that."

"Fritz is going to have to get in line." She looked at the planet, now visibly curved at the edges, the atmosphere a thin skin of light at the horizon. "Honor. Your signal contact. She'll actually come?"

"She'll actually come." No hesitation. "Get some rest if you can. I'll call when we have a response."

The channel closed.

Scotty set the pinnace to its minimum maintenance orbit, a long ellipse that kept them moving without requiring active thrust, and then he leaned back in the pilot's seat and looked at the overhead and exhaled.

"Good flying," Buffy said.

"Good shooting."

"I pressed a switch."

"At exactly the right moment." He looked at her. "How's the shoulder actually?"

"Fine actually." She rolled it experimentally. The wound was closed. She could feel the deep tissue still knitting, that faint internal pressure that was the healing factor working through the layers, but the surface was sealed and nothing was compromised. "It'll be fully done in another hour."

He shook his head slowly, not for the first time, in the way of someone who had not yet finished adjusting to the specific reality of her biology. "Does it hurt?"

"While it's healing, a little. Like a bruise." She considered. "Less than it should, probably. I think the pain response is dialed down somehow. I've never been sure whether that's a feature or a design flaw."

"Sounds like a feature to me."

"Unless it means I miss something that's actually serious because it doesn't hurt enough to register." She paused. "That's the argument Fritz makes. That the reduced pain sensitivity is an operational advantage that creates a diagnostic blind spot."

"Is he right?"

"Probably. He usually is." She leaned back in the copilot's seat and looked at the overhead alongside him. The pinnace hummed. Through the viewport the planet turned. "Scotty."

"Mm."

"Thank you. For the after thing you mentioned. For being good at it."

He turned his head to look at her. "We're not in the after yet."

"I know. But I wanted to say it before we get there, in case it's harder to say then."

He held her gaze for a moment with an expression she was getting better at reading, the one that was taking something in without making a production of receiving it. "Okay," he said. "You're welcome."

She looked back at the overhead.

"Buffy."

"Mm."

"For what it's worth." He paused, choosing words with the care of someone who'd thought about how to say something and was now doing the saying. "Whatever comes after this. The Manticore part, the figuring-out-what-you-are part, all of it. You don't have to do that alone either."

She was quiet for a moment.

"That's a significant offer," she said.

"I know."

"You don't fully know what you're offering yet."

"I know that too." His voice was even. "I'm offering anyway."

The planet turned. The pinnace hummed. She thought about the long shape of the thing ahead of them, the after that was building itself on the other side of this operation, all the versions of herself she was going to have to negotiate between on the way to whatever she settled into.

"Okay," she said.
