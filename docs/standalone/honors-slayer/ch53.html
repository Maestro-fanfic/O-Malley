Chapter 53 (44 of 54 in this book)

The decade didn't arrive as a decade.

It arrived as days, as it always did, the accumulation of specific days with specific work and specific people and the ongoing texture of a life that was full enough to be present in rather than merely waited through.

She turned twenty-two.

She turned twenty-three.

The Vigil's network reached a hundred confirmed candidates. Fifty-three in active engagement. Twenty-three operational at various levels.

Yeva led the highest-complexity node operation the Vigil had attempted, in a Solarian system that had required six months of preparation and three weeks of careful diplomatic groundwork by Honor before the operational approach was possible. She executed it with a team of four, two of whom were candidates who had been in the Vigil's operational track for less than a year.

The team performed exactly as Yeva had assessed they would.

Buffy received the operational report and read it and thought: there it is.

The ceiling had been visible for some time. Now it was visible to everyone.

She told Yeva: "The next highest-complexity operation is yours if you want it."

Yeva said: "What's the next highest-complexity operation?"

She said: "The thing in the Solarian League that Honor has been building toward for two years."

Yeva held her gaze.

She said: "That's not mine yet."

She said: "No. But it will be." She held Yeva's gaze. "I'm telling you it's coming so you can prepare for the kind of operation it's going to be."

Yeva said: "How long?"

She said: "A year. Maybe less."

Yeva said: "All right."

She thought about herself at seventeen being told things she wasn't ready for yet and how the telling had shaped the readying and how she was doing the same thing for Yeva that nobody had done for her in quite that way.

She thought: this is what the Vigil is for. Among other things.

She turned twenty-four.

Allison's publication had been out for two years and the response in the scientific community had moved through the stages that significant publications moved through, from initial skepticism to cautious engagement to the beginning of active research by people outside the Vigil who were interested in the lineage modification as a biological phenomenon.

She'd been careful about the relationship with the outside researchers. Not closed. The Vigil wasn't a secret anymore and the lineage wasn't a secret and the research was better with more people contributing to it. But the candidates' privacy was protected and the operational work was not discussed and the balance was managed with the careful hand of someone who had thought about it from both sides.

Fritz had published four papers. Allison had published seven. Haverstock and Prieto had published eleven in four years, each one moving the interface framework a step further along the path that the Constant had been pointing toward since before Beowulf's civilization existed.

The mapping array now covered eighty percent of explored space.

The interface variation was measurable with a precision that two years ago hadn't been possible, and the precision was revealing detail that Haverstock described in his letters as beautiful in the specific way that physicists found things beautiful, which was precise and inevitable and pointing toward something large.

She read his letters and wrote back and thought about beautiful and inevitable and what those words meant for the shape of what was coming.

The presences were coordinating in ways that were new. She felt it through the channel, the specific quality of multiple very old things working in concert, the slow and deliberate alignment of capacities that had been operating independently for centuries.

Witness communicated through Eris that the coordination was progressing.

The Constant communicated through the channel that the threshold was moving.

She didn't ask for timelines. She'd learned not to ask the presences for timelines, which were a human category that the presences accommodated politely and which they found imprecise in ways they didn't fully articulate.

She turned twenty-five.

Scotty's operational role in the Vigil had become what she'd known it was going to become when he'd made it formal, which was essential in the way of things that had been missing from the structure until they arrived and then were impossible to imagine the structure without. He coordinated the operational logistics with the specific capability of someone who had spent a career coordinating complex things under pressure and who brought that capability to a context that used it fully.

He was also, in the Sphinx garden in the spring of her twenty-fifth year, the person who handed her a cup of hot chocolate and said: "Look at that."

She looked.

The rosemary equivalent was in its fourth year. What it had become in four years was not what it had been in the first year, not the cautious tentative growth of something finding its ground, but the established thing, the thing that had found its ground and was doing what it was built to do with the confidence of roots that went deep enough.

She looked at it for a long time.

She said: "Mom would have liked this."

He said: "Yes."

She said: "She would have had opinions about the companion planting."

He said: "Your mother gardened?"

She said: "She tried. She was better at cooking than gardening. But she tried." She looked at the plant. "She would have wanted to know about Sphinx. The climate. What grows here." She paused. "She would have asked your mother a lot of questions."

He held her gaze.

She said: "I think about that sometimes. What she would have made of all of this. Not in the grieving way. Just�" she looked for the word� "imagining."

He said: "What do you imagine?"

She thought about Joyce Summers. She thought about her mother's specific quality, the combination of fear and love that had been Joyce's relationship to the Slayer and how that relationship had evolved in the time they'd had and what it would have become in the time they'd been denied.

She said: "She would have been proud. And scared. And she would have made friends with your mother at some point and the two of them together would have been� a great deal." She paused. "She would have loved the Vigil. Not for the work specifically. For what the work meant. That her daughter had found something worth doing and people worth doing it with." She paused. "She was always more interested in the people than in the mission."

He was quiet.

She said: "I think she and Allison would have understood each other."

He said: "Both of them feeding people constantly."

She almost smiled. "Yes. That." She looked at the plant. "I miss her in a way that's�" she stopped. "It's not the same as it was. The missing. It's become something else. Still there. But different." She paused. "Part of what I'm doing. Like the rosemary." She looked at it. "Different than what I planted it from. Still the same thing."

He stood beside her and didn't say anything that needed saying.

She drank her chocolate.

The spring was doing what spring did and the garden was doing what established gardens did and she was here, in it, and the missing and the present were both true simultaneously and they held.

She turned twenty-six.

The operation in the Solarian League that Honor had been building toward for two years was ready.

Yeva led it.

She was there as backup, present in the operational sense of someone who was available and not required, and what Yeva produced was the confirmation of what she'd seen coming.

The operation required forty-eight hours rather than the projected thirty-six, which was not a failure of planning but a feature of a situation that had more variables than the pre-operational intelligence had captured and which Yeva navigated with the specific capability of someone for whom adapting to live information was natural rather than stressful.

She led the team through the forty-eight hours with a quality that Buffy recognized from the inside, the quality of full engagement that was also full presence, the thing that made the difference between someone who was doing the work and someone who was the work.

The debrief afterward.

She said: "Tell me what you would do differently."

Yeva told her. Three things, precise and accurate.

She said: "I'd add a fourth."

Yeva said: "Tell me."

She told her.

Yeva considered it. She said: "Yes. You're right." She paused. "I'll have it next time."

She held Yeva's gaze.

She said: "I want you to lead the Vigil's operations going forward."

Yeva held her gaze.

She said: "Ongoing?"

She said: "Ongoing. Not all of them. The highest-complexity ones remain mine for now. But the operational track, the training, the deployment decisions�I want that to be yours." She held Yeva's gaze. "I'm going to be spending more of my time on the interface work and the presences coordination and the long-work preparation. The operational capacity needs a consistent leader and you're it."

Yeva was quiet for a long moment.

She said: "This is preparation."

She said: "Yes."

Yeva held her gaze. "For what you told me about. The door."

She said: "Partly. And partly because it's the right move regardless." She held Yeva's gaze. "The Vigil was always supposed to be bigger than me. I want you to make it that."

Yeva held her gaze for a long moment.

She said: "All right."

She said: "Yes."

They held each other's gaze.

Yeva said: "What do you need from me while you're still here?"

She almost smiled at the while you're still here, the specific directness of Yeva accepting the full frame without flinching.

She said: "Everything you've always given. And the specific thing that you're going to have to do, which is be the person I can think alongside about both things simultaneously. The operational work and the long-work preparation." She held Yeva's gaze. "The Vigil at twenty-six is not the Vigil at twenty-one. The next five years are going to make it into something else again. I need you to be the one who sees that happening and helps shape it."

Yeva said: "I can do that."

She said: "I know." She paused. "That's why I asked you."

Haverstock's letter arrived in the autumn of her twenty-seventh year.

It was the letter she'd been expecting and not expecting simultaneously, the letter that arrived when the physics had caught up to the theory and the theory had proved out and the estimate had become something more solid than an estimate.

He wrote: I have been running the calculations with input from the presences for six months. The threshold is reachable. Not in forty years and not in twenty. The combination of the interface variation's progression and the presences' coordinated capacity has compressed the timeline significantly from my original estimate. My current best estimate for the threshold being reachable is three to five years. The uncertainty remains, but the direction is consistent across every model I've run.

Three to five years.

She was twenty-seven.

She would be thirty to thirty-two when the threshold was reachable.

She sat with this for a long time.

Then she went to find Scotty.

He was in the Sphinx kitchen, which was where he often was on the days they were on the property, the specific domestic territory of someone who cooked as a form of thinking and who produced good food as a byproduct of working things out.

She sat at the table.

He looked at her face and turned down what he was working on and sat across from her.

She said: "Haverstock's letter came."

He said: "Tell me."

She told him.

Three to five years.

He held it.

He said: "We have time."

She said: "Yes."

He said: "Three to five years is time."

She said: "It is." She held his gaze. "There are things I want to do in that time. Things that need to be in place before�" she stopped. "Before the question becomes the decision."

He held her gaze.

He said: "Tell me what you need."

She said: "The Vigil to be what it needs to be without me. Yeva in the operational lead. The research in a state where it can continue." She paused. "Carson in a position where the archive is what it needs to be." She paused. "Honor and the diplomatic structure solid enough to stand." She paused. "Allison continuing the biology work." She paused. "Haverstock and Prieto continuing the interface work."

He held her gaze.

She said: "And us."

He said: "Us."

She said: "Three to five years of this. All of it. The work and the property and the garden and�" she held his gaze� "the things we've been building." She held his gaze. "I want that time to be full."

He held her gaze.

He said: "Yes." He said it without the weight of concession and without the performance of bravery. He said it the way he said all the important things, which was directly and without qualification. "Yes. Full."

She held his gaze.

She said: "And then I'll decide. When the threshold is reachable and the decision is real rather than theoretical." She paused. "I'm not deciding now. I'm�" she looked for the word� "preparing for the decision."

He said: "I know."

She said: "You're all right with this."

He held her gaze. He said: "I'm not all right with the possibility of you going and not coming back. I want to be honest about that." He held her gaze steadily. "But I'm all right with you being who you are and having this question be part of who you are." He paused. "Those are different things."

She held this.

She said: "Yes. They are."

He reached across the table.

She took his hand.

They sat in the kitchen with the Sphinx afternoon outside and the established garden and the treecat in the canopy and the work that was always there and the question that was now also always there, and it was all true simultaneously, and it held.

She thought: three to five years.

She thought: full.

She thought: yes.

The kitchen smelled of what Scotty was cooking and outside the autumn was doing its work and inside the life was doing its work and she was here, present in all of it, the question and the life and the work and the people, all of it held.

Forward.

Always forward.

Both directions.

She held his hand and the afternoon moved through its stages and the Sphinx autumn came in through the window in its spectacular way and she was here, twenty-seven, alive and building and asking the question she needed to ask and not yet answering it because the answer needed three to five more years to become real.

That was enough.

That was, as it always turned out when she looked at it directly, exactly enough.
