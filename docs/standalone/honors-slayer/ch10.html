Chapter 10 (1 of 54 in this book)

The dreams had started three days after the merging.

Buffy knew they were the Girl's memories and not her own because everything in them was wrong in small specific ways. The sky was the wrong shade. Fire was a miracle rather than a convenience. The men around the fire were short and dark and spoke in something that wasn't quite language, more like language's rough first draft, and Buffy understood every word of it the way you understand a dream, which is to say completely and then not at all the moment you wake up.

In the dreams she was the Girl. Not watching her. Being her.

She ran on bare feet across ground that had never seen a road and the running was joy, pure and uncomplicating, her body a perfect instrument tuned to exactly one purpose. The darkness around her breathed. She breathed back. The things that lived in the dark knew her smell and gave her a wide berth, not from weakness but from something older than fear, a recognition of hierarchy that didn't need to be argued or demonstrated. She was the thing that hunted the things that hunted men. The food chain had a roof and she was it.

She woke from these dreams feeling better than she had any right to.

That bothered her more than the dreams themselves.

"You're doing it again," Honor said.

Buffy looked up from where she'd been sitting cross-legged on the shuttle's deck, staring at nothing in particular. Honor stood in the hatchway with two mugs, Nimitz draped across her shoulders like an extremely opinionated scarf. The treecat's green eyes tracked to Buffy and his tail tip moved in what she'd come to understand was the equivalent of a nod.

"Doing what?"

"Going away somewhere behind your eyes." Honor crossed the space between them and sat, handing over one of the mugs. Hot chocolate. It had taken Scotty Tremaine approximately four hours after Buffy's return to full personhood to figure out that she shared Honor's feelings about coffee. He'd shown up with a small brick of compressed chocolate powder with the expression of a man presenting tribute to uncertain royalty, and Buffy had decided then that she liked him. "Fritz is calling it dissociative episodes. I'm calling it thinking very hard about something you don't want to talk about."

"Fritz needs a new hobby."

"Fritz's current hobby is writing seventeen pages of notes about your biology that he knows he can't show to anyone." Honor wrapped both hands around her mug. The morning outside the shuttle was the color of old bruises, the Hades jungle pressing against the canopy in every direction. Somewhere out in it something screamed, brief and final. Neither of them flinched. "He asked me to ask you whether the healing factor is always active or whether it requires some kind of trigger."

"Tell him it's always on." Buffy looked into her mug. "Like a pilot light. Most of the time you don't even know it's there."

"And when you're injured?"

"It turns into a bonfire." She paused. "That's not exactly right either. It's more like�" She stopped, frustrated. The vocabulary for this didn't exist in any language she'd grown up with and apparently hadn't been invented in the two thousand years since. "You know how your body normally handles damage? Like if you cut yourself, there's this whole process, clotting and scabbing and new skin, and it takes a specific amount of time and there are specific steps?"

"Yes."

"Mine skips the steps. Or does them all at once. I don't know which. I've never been able to watch it happening from the outside." She thought of Fritz's expression when he'd described watching her arm knit itself back together around the bones he was still trying to set. The memory made her a little queasy. "It costs something, though. Energy. That's why I eat so much. My body's running a repair shop twenty-four hours a day whether there's anything to repair or not, and when there's something big to fix it drafts every available calorie for the job."

"That explains the E-rats." Honor's voice was carefully neutral. The camp had gone through three weeks of rations in the first five days of Buffy's lucidity. Nobody had said anything directly to Buffy about it. She'd overheard Alistair McKeon doing some creative accounting with the supply inventory to paper over the gap, and had made a note to find some way to square that particular debt.

"Yeah." Buffy drank her chocolate. Outside the screaming had stopped. "Sorry about that."

"Don't be." Honor said it in the tone that meant the subject was settled rather than the tone that meant she was being polite about it, which Buffy was learning to distinguish. "If the caloric cost scales with the severity of the repair, then the transfusion you gave me cost you considerably more than a deciliter of blood."

Buffy hadn't thought about it in those terms. She turned the idea over. Fritz had cut off the transfusion when she lost consciousness, which meant the blood loss itself hadn't been what dropped her. Her body had been running the equivalent of a stadium's worth of repair work on Honor's arm and face and eye and then had simply run out of fuel mid-job and shut down non-essential systems, of which consciousness was apparently one.

"Huh," she said.

"Yes." Honor looked at her steadily. "So. Thank you."

The directness of it landed differently than Buffy expected. She'd been thanked before. Giles had thanked her with tea and careful understatement. Willow had thanked her with enthusiasm and hugs. Xander had thanked her by pretending she hadn't done anything particularly impressive and then going quietly and saying something in private that he'd never repeat in public. Each of those had felt like a language she was fluent in.

Honor's thank you was a different language. It had weight to it. It arrived like a handed object, something you were meant to hold onto.

"You're welcome," Buffy said, and found she meant it without qualification for maybe the first time in her life.

Nimitz made a small sound and Honor's expression shifted slightly in the way it did when the treecat was feeding her something through their link. Buffy watched the process with the same fascination she always felt. It was the closest thing to magic she'd found in this century, which was either deeply comforting or deeply ironic, she hadn't decided which.

"He says you were awake at three in the morning sitting outside the shuttle counting stars," Honor said.

"I wasn't counting them. I was looking for constellations." Buffy kept her voice even. "They're all wrong. I know that's stupid. I know it's been two thousand years and I know the stars move, I paid attention during that unit in ninth grade, I know why they're different. Knowing why doesn't make it less�" She stopped.

"Wrong," Honor said.

"Yeah."

Honor was quiet for a moment. Nimitz made another small sound, more like a question this time, his head tilting toward Buffy. "He wants to know if any of them are the same."

Buffy looked up despite herself. The canopy blocked most of the sky, but there was a gap to the east where something had fallen years ago and the jungle hadn't fully reclaimed it yet. A wedge of pale morning, a few stars still visible against the brightening edge. "Orion's still there," she said. "Mostly. The belt's shifted. And Sirius. I think that's Sirius." She pointed. "It's supposed to be the brightest star in the sky and that one's the brightest star I can see, so."

Nimitz bleekd softly. Honor said, "He thinks Sirius is a good anchor."

"Tell him I think he's a pretty good anchor too."

The treecat's tail curled at the tip. Honor's expression did something complicated and fond.

The camp was waking up around them. Buffy could hear it before she could see it, the specific sounds of military people moving through a morning routine with the compressed efficiency of people who'd done this on ships and stations and now were doing it in a jungle and had simply recalibrated the motions without particular complaint. She'd spent enough time watching them to have assigned everyone a distinct sound signature. Alistair McKeon's footsteps were deliberate and evenly spaced, a man who moved through space like he was always on a quarterdeck. Jamie Candless was quieter, hunter-quiet, which she respected. Andrew LaFollet was the quietest of all when he was focused, which should have impressed her more than it did, because she could still hear him coming from thirty meters.

She could not hear Scotty Tremaine until he was approximately four meters away, which was either the result of some deliberate technique he'd never mentioned or just the natural consequence of being a person who moved through the world assuming it was basically friendly. She hadn't decided which. Either way it was annoying.

He appeared around the shuttle's bow with his hair doing something structurally ambitious and a ration pack in each hand, offering one to Honor with the exact degree of deference that managed to communicate respect without being stiff about it, then turning to Buffy with the other.

"Still no coffee," he said. "In case you were worried."

"I was not worried."

"Good. There's something in the ration packs that claims to be a breakfast cereal but I think that's aspirational." He dropped to sit cross-legged a comfortable distance away, not so close as to crowd her, not so far as to signal discomfort with her presence. She'd noticed he always calibrated that distance carefully and never made a production of it. "How'd you sleep?"

"Fine."

"She was outside at three in the morning," Honor said pleasantly.

Buffy gave her a look. Honor's expression conveyed absolute innocence. Nimitz's expression conveyed that he found human social dynamics endlessly entertaining.

"Couldn't sleep," Buffy said. "I do that sometimes. I used to patrol at night to deal with it." She tore open the ration pack and looked at the contents with the pragmatic resignation she'd developed over the past few weeks. "Back home, I mean. Walking helped."

"You can walk the perimeter," Scotty said. "We've got a rotation but there's no regulation against extra sets of eyes. Especially�" he stopped.

"Especially eyes like mine," she finished.

He met her gaze directly. "Especially eyes like yours. Which can see in the dark and process threat data about four times faster than a human baseline. Yes. I was going to say that." He didn't look apologetic about having said it. "I'm not trying to make you feel like a weapon."

"I know." She ate whatever the cereal was claiming to be. "I am one, though. That's not something I can unknow." She looked out at the jungle, which had brightened now to its daytime shade, that particular green that was too green, too fecund, too alive, a green that hadn't learned any restraint. "I came to terms with that a long time ago. Being a weapon that makes her own choices about what to aim at. It's basically the whole job description."

"What happens when there aren't any targets?"

It was a better question than it probably sounded. She considered it seriously. "I've never actually had a gap long enough to find out. There was always another apocalypse." She paused. "That's not a figure of speech, by the way. Literal apocalypses."

"Multiple."

"I was seventeen. It was a busy year."

Scotty looked at her for a moment with an expression she couldn't entirely read. Not pity. Not disbelief, which she appreciated. Something more careful than either of those. "How old are you now?"

The question dropped into a silence. She was aware of Honor going still beside her.

"Seventeen," she said. "Technically." She ate another spoonful of the not-cereal. "I'll be eighteen in January, assuming anybody around here knows what January is."

"Grayson still uses the old calendar for liturgical purposes," Jamie said from somewhere behind the shuttle, which meant he'd been there for at least part of the conversation and had been too Grayson about it to announce himself. He appeared around the bow a moment later, looking somewhat unrepentant. "January is the first month of the year. You were born in January?"

"The nineteenth."

"Then in approximately six months you will be eighteen years old." He said it with a gravity that suggested this was important information which had been successfully captured and filed. "Steadholder Harrington, the Ensign asks whether you'll need him before the morning briefing."

"Tell him half an hour." Honor stood, gathering Nimitz, and looked down at Buffy with an expression that had some component of the same careful thing Scotty's had held, but older, more seasoned, something that had been through more rounds of the same fight. "The morning briefing will include you. Such as it is. We're still in the planning stages of getting off this rock and you should know what the options are."

"Options plural is encouraging."

"It's approximately one and a half options. But we're committed to optimism." She paused. "Are you all right?"

The directness again. Buffy had noticed that Honor asked that question the same way she issued orders, without hedging, without the social softening that turned it into mere courtesy. It was a real question that wanted a real answer.

"I'm working on it," Buffy said. Which was the most honest answer available and also probably the most correct.

Honor nodded once and left. Nimitz looked back over her shoulder at Buffy as they went, his green eyes holding something that wasn't quite readable but felt distinctly like encouragement.

Scotty was still there. He'd finished his ration pack and was now looking at the jungle with his hands loosely on his knees.

"You don't have to stay," Buffy said.

"I know."

She ate the rest of the not-cereal. Somewhere in the canopy above them something was making a sound like a distant bell, rhythmic and incongruous. She'd heard it every morning since she'd been coherent enough to notice things. She still didn't know what made it.

"What's that sound?" she asked.

He tilted his head, listening. "No idea. We looked for it the second day. Couldn't find the source." He glanced up into the green. "McKeon thinks it's some kind of bird. Fritz thinks it might be an insect colony. Andrew thinks we shouldn't be spending resources on finding out." He paused. "I just like it."

"Me too," Buffy said.

She hadn't known that until she said it.

The morning briefing was held in the larger of the two shuttles, which had been stripped of most of its passenger fittings to create something that functioned approximately like a planning room if you were willing to extend the definition of planning room to include exposed conduit runs and the persistent smell of recycled air and jungle damp.

There were eleven of them, counting Buffy. She'd met most of them in the scattered and nonlinear way of someone who'd spent the first part of her time here as a feral apex predator, which meant some introductions had already happened but bore reviewing under better circumstances. She sat slightly outside the main cluster, not by anyone's direction but by her own habit, the position that gave her sightlines to all the exits and let her see everyone's face.

Nimitz, from his perch on Honor's shoulder, watched her do this with what felt like professional appreciation.

Alistair McKeon ran the briefing with the concise competence of someone who'd been distilling complex problems into clear points his entire adult life. The problem was, as Honor had described, approximately one and a half options.

The camp was located on Hades, a prison planet at the center of a gravity well that made casual departure impossible without a ship capable of compensating for it. They had two shuttles. Shuttles could not compensate for the gravity well. The Peep garrison controlled the planet's only functional orbital facility. The garrison was not small.

"What we have," McKeon said, "is an asymmetric advantage in the form of one asset that the garrison is not currently accounting for in their threat calculations."

Everyone looked at Buffy.

She raised one hand slightly. "Hi."

"The question," McKeon continued, "is how to use that advantage most efficiently, which means we need more information about the garrison's current posture than we have. Their patterns have changed in the past three weeks. Regular sweeps have become irregular. Communications traffic we can detect has dropped." He looked at Honor. "Which suggests either they've become more disciplined about emissions or something has changed in their strategic situation."

"Or both," Honor said.

"Or both," he agreed. "What we need is a closer look at their main compound. Close enough to get accurate headcounts and observe their rotation patterns." He paused, which was the pause of a man about to say something that he'd already thought through carefully and had decided was the best available bad option. "Closer than we can get with our current personnel without unacceptable risk of detection."

The silence that followed was not a surprised silence. Most of the people in the room had clearly already followed the same reasoning to the same destination.

"How close?" Buffy asked.

"The outer perimeter is four kilometers from the main compound. The inner perimeter is eight hundred meters. We'd want eyes at approximately two hundred meters to get reliable headcounts through the structure."

"Okay," she said.

"There's a night-vision grid on the inner perimeter. Thermal as well."

"Does the thermal measure above human-baseline body temperature?"

A small pause. McKeon glanced at Fritz Montoya, who looked up from the notes he'd been making throughout the briefing. "Her resting temperature runs approximately half a degree above baseline," Fritz said. "In the field, moving, it could be as much as a full degree. Depending on the sensitivity calibration of their equipment�"

"Is still within the range of natural variation that their system would likely filter out as noise rather than flag as a target," Honor finished. She looked at Buffy. "Probably."

"Probably is better odds than most of my Tuesday nights back home," Buffy said.

Jamie Candless, standing to her left, made a sound that was almost certainly not a laugh and which he immediately converted into a cough.

"We'd want a three-night observation window before we commit to anything," McKeon said. "Pattern analysis. Looking for gaps, rotation timing, any indication of what's changed in their posture." He looked at Buffy steadily. "This would mean three solo nights inside their perimeter."

"Solo's fine. I work better alone."

"That's not�" he started.

"I know it's not what you want to hear," she said. "But it's true and you know it's true. Two people moving through a perimeter is a different calculation than one, and the second person doesn't add anything to my capability that outweighs what they'd cost in detection risk." She looked around the room. "I'm not trying to be a cowboy about it. I'm being accurate."

The room was quiet. McKeon looked at Honor.

Honor looked at Buffy. Through Nimitz she was presumably getting some readout of Buffy's emotional state that Buffy couldn't curate or edit, which she'd decided some time ago to simply accept as the price of being around treecats. Whatever Honor was receiving, her expression settled into something resolved.

"Three nights," Honor said. "Observation only. No engagement with garrison personnel under any circumstances unless your cover is blown and you have no other option."

"Understood."

"And you will have a communication device that you will check in on at regular intervals, and if you miss a check-in we will know within an hour." She met Buffy's eyes. "I am not negotiating that part."

Buffy thought about seventeen-year-old herself, alone in the dark, with no one who would know within an hour whether she'd lived or died. She thought about how that had felt and how it had become ordinary and how she hadn't recognized until right now, in this converted shuttle smelling of jungle and old recycled air, that it had cost something that she hadn't accounted for at the time.

"Okay," she said. "Check-ins. Got it."

Nimitz made a small sound that she was increasingly able to interpret as satisfaction.

The briefing moved on to supply inventory and water purification and the state of the second shuttle's port thruster, which had been giving intermittent warnings since the escape. Buffy listened with partial attention, filing away the information that was directly relevant to her and letting the rest wash past, watching the room instead. The way these people moved around each other. The specific grammar of how they communicated rank and trust and history without saying any of those words directly.

She'd always been able to read rooms. It was a Slayer thing, that physical hyperawareness that included social dynamics as one of its inputs. But this room was doing something she hadn't encountered before.

These people had been through something that had taken pieces of all of them and they had not subsequently fallen apart. They had instead reconfigured around the losses with a kind of disciplined grief that didn't hide itself but also didn't let itself become the loudest thing in the space. She'd seen grief before. She'd lived in it. She knew the version that calcified and the version that leaked constantly and the version that a person carried so long it stopped feeling like grief and started feeling like just the way things were.

This was different. This was grief that had been given its due and then put to work.

She thought about Willow and Xander and Giles and all the losses they'd accumulated over two years of standing next to her and she wondered whether she'd done right by them. Whether she'd given them enough room to process the weight of it or whether she'd needed them so badly as functional parts of her support structure that she'd accidentally treated their capacity for grief as a logistical problem.

Stop. She pushed it down. Not now. File it. Come back to it when there was time to do it properly.

She looked up and found Scotty Tremaine watching her from across the room with that careful expression again, the one she still hadn't entirely decoded. He looked away when she caught him at it, but not guiltily. Like someone who'd been watching a process they found interesting and didn't mind being noticed watching.

She filed that too.

Carson Clinkscales was in the habit of rising before everyone else. On a ship this had meant the hours before first watch when the corridors were quietest and he could walk them alone with his thoughts. On Hades it meant the jungle before full light, which was a different kind of alone entirely.

He didn't go far. A hundred meters past the camp's outer perimeter, which he could cover and return from quickly enough that he was back before anyone registered his absence. He wasn't supposed to go alone. Ensigns were not supposed to go alone. Carson was aware of this and made a private theological accommodation for it on the grounds that his morning prayers required a degree of solitude that the shuttle interior did not reliably provide.

He prayed for the souls of the people they'd lost. He prayed for guidance. He prayed, in terms considerably more specific than he usually permitted himself, for them to get off this planet alive.

And then, this morning, he prayed about Lady Buffy.

He was aware that praying about a person was something he needed to be careful about. The Church of Humanity Unchained had reasonably specific things to say about the obligations of men toward women and the specific temptations that came packaged with those obligations, and Carson was young enough and honest enough with himself to recognize that what he felt toward Lady Buffy was not entirely composed of reverent theological awe.

She was beautiful. He could notice that without it being a sin. She was also approximately his age, or had been before whatever had happened to her, and that approximation of shared temporal context was something he found himself reaching toward in ways that probably needed examination.

But beyond the beauty and the age and the theological significance was something else, something that he thought about while he knelt on the roots of a tree that had no name in any language he knew and looked up at the edge of the sky between the canopy gaps.

She was lonely in a way he recognized.

Not the loneliness of someone without companions. She had companions now, was acquiring more of them by the day as the survivors of the Prince Adrian worked out their individual relationships with her. It was the loneliness of someone whose actual context was missing. Whose reference points had all been subtracted.

On Grayson, Carson had a grandfather, parents, two sisters, a community of faith, a world that had formed him. He understood himself in relation to all of those things. Take them away, put him somewhere two thousand years removed from everything that had constructed his sense of self, and he would have the same look that lived in the back of Lady Buffy's eyes when she thought no one was watching.

He wasn't arrogant enough to think he could solve that particular problem. But he thought perhaps he could be one of the things she oriented herself toward while she built a new set of reference points. Something steady. Something that saw her clearly and didn't flinch.

He could do that. He was good at steady.

The bell-sound from high in the canopy began its morning sequence, the same rhythm it made every day, and Carson looked up trying again to locate it and failing again. He thought about what Scotty had said this morning about simply liking the sound.

Then he thought about Lady Buffy saying she liked it too.

He was still thinking about that when he heard her.

Not footsteps. There were no footsteps. There was simply a transition from her not being present to her being present, the way certain large predators apparently could move when they chose. He'd read about hexapumas doing it. He hadn't expected to experience it with a seventeen-year-old girl from Old Earth.

"You're going to give Andrew LaFollet a heart attack if you keep leaving the perimeter alone," she said.

She was sitting on the root of the tree nearest his, her knees pulled up, a knife he'd never seen before balanced flat across her palm. Not doing anything with it. Just holding it the way some people held worry stones.

Carson recovered his composure and noted that he was relatively pleased with how quickly the recovery happened. "I could say the same to you."

"I told Jamie where I was going."

"I told no one," he admitted.

"I know. That's why I followed you." She looked at the knife rather than at him. "The perimeter isn't your responsibility. You don't have to work the watch."

"I wasn't working the watch. I was praying."

She was quiet for a moment. "Does it help?"

"Yes." He paused. "Mostly."

She turned the knife over. The blade was the same matte black she'd had since the Tepes, the one that didn't catch light, the one she'd produced from somewhere on her person during the ship's medical bay and that Fritz had been quietly fascinated by ever since. "What do you pray for? Specifically."

It was a direct question. She asked a lot of those. He'd noticed that she became uncomfortable with conversations that worked around the perimeter of what they actually wanted to say. "This morning I was praying about you," he said.

She looked up at that.

"I was asking for guidance," he said, "about what my obligations are. What I'm supposed to do with�" He gestured vaguely, which he immediately recognized as a cowardly substitution for accurate language. "With the fact of you. What you represent. What you might need from me."

"The fact of me," she repeated, something careful in her voice.

"You are the Slayer," he said. "My family has kept that record for a very long time. I am sitting approximately two thousand years after any version of events my family ever contemplated and you are sitting three meters away from me holding a knife and asking me what I pray for." He kept his voice steady. "I am trying to be a person who responds to extraordinary circumstances with clear thinking rather than with either reverence or dismissal, and I will be honest with you that the first is easier than the second in this particular case."

She looked at him for a long moment. Then she looked back at the knife.

"Merrick used to look at me like that," she said. "My first Watcher. Like I was proof of something."

"Was that bad?"

"It was lonely." She tilted the blade slightly and a faint glimmer moved along its edge despite the coating. "Not because he was wrong. More because it was the first thing he saw when he looked at me. Before I was a person I was a proof." She paused. "I got used to it. I don't want to get used to it again."

Carson heard this. He let it sit for a full breath before he answered.

"I cannot unknow what I know about you," he said. "I won't pretend otherwise, that seems dishonest. But I think what I actually want is�" he searched for the word�"to know the person the Slayer is packaged with. If that distinction makes sense."

It was the most he'd articulated that thought to anyone including himself. He felt somewhat exposed by it.

She turned the knife over again. Then she said, "Buffy Summers. Sunnydale, California. I'm a Virgo, I like shoes, I'm afraid of being buried alive, and my favorite food is anything my mother makes, specifically her chicken with the rosemary, and I will never eat it again." She said the last part in the same tone as the rest, which was not quite flat but was the tone of someone who had decided to treat a wound as a fact rather than a wound. "Your turn."

"Carson Clinkscales. I'm from Grayson. I'm the third of four children. I joined the Navy because my grandfather served and because I wanted to do something my sisters couldn't, which in retrospect was not a particularly sophisticated reason but I was sixteen." He paused. "I'm afraid of being responsible for the death of someone under my command."

"Has that happened?"

"Not yet." He let that sit the way she'd let the thing about her mother sit. "I'm aware the 'yet' is doing a lot of work in that sentence."

She nodded slowly. Then she tucked the knife somewhere, one fluid motion with no visible destination, and unfolded from the root in a single movement that shouldn't have been as graceful as it was given how thoroughly her feet were tangled in the ground cover.

"Morning briefing," she said.

"Yes."

They walked back to the camp in a silence that had somewhat less empty space in it than the silence they'd walked out in, though Carson couldn't have said precisely what had changed to account for the difference. The bell-sound from the canopy followed them in its particular rhythm, unanswered and not requiring an answer, simply there.

Something steady, he had thought. He could be that.

He thought maybe she was already doing the same thing for him, and hadn't told him, and probably wouldn't.
