Chapter 62 (53 of 54 in this book)

She was sixty when she gave the document to Mara.

Not the full archive. The document, her document, the one she'd started in the working space on Manticore and finished in the Sphinx kitchen and which had been in the archive for twenty-nine years.

Mara was thirty-five.

She'd come to Manticore at eighteen, which had been a year after the third window meeting, and she'd done what she'd done with everything, which was to take it in completely and then do with it what could only be done with it from the inside, which was to become what she was in the context of the people who already were what they were.

She was thirty-five now and the Vigil was her home in the same way it was Yeva's home and in the same way it was Buffy's home, the place that held the work and the people and the meaning.

They were in the Sphinx kitchen.

Mara had been to Sphinx many times. She'd been coming to Sphinx since she was twenty, the first time Buffy had brought her to see the garden, and she'd taken to the planet with the specific recognition of someone who was encountering something they hadn't known they'd been looking for.

She had opinions about the garden.

Strong ones.

She and Sascha had developed a relationship around the garden that Buffy found both gratifying and slightly intimidating, the two of them agreeing about things and disagreeing about things with the mutual respect of people who took the same subject seriously from different positions.

The garden in its twenty-fourth year was something that Buffy looked at with the specific feeling that established things produced, the feeling of something that had found its full form, the accumulated weight of two decades of growing.

She sat across from Mara in the kitchen with the garden outside the window.

She put the document on the table.

Mara looked at it.

She said: "I know what this is."

Buffy said: "Yes."

Mara said: "You're giving it to me."

Buffy said: "I'm giving it to the Vigil. I'm giving it to you to carry."

Mara held her gaze.

She said: "Why me specifically."

Buffy said: "Because you know both ends of the thread." She held Mara's gaze. "You're from Old Earth and you've been in the Vigil for seventeen years and you carry both of those things simultaneously in a way no one else currently does." She paused. "The record needs someone who can see it from both sides. The origin and the work." She paused. "That's you."

Mara held her gaze for a long moment.

She said: "You're not done."

Buffy said: "No. But you're the next carrier. The document doesn't belong to one person. It passes forward." She held Mara's gaze. "I'm passing it."

Mara looked at the document.

She said: "Adisa should have it too."

Buffy said: "Adisa has a copy. The archive has a copy. Mara�" she held her gaze� "this is the original. The carrier version. The one that continues to be written." She paused. "Add to it. What happens in your time. What you know that I don't know. The thread continues."

Mara looked at the document.

She put her hand on it.

She said: "I'll carry it."

Buffy said: "I know."

She said it with the specific quality of something settled. Not resigned, not elegiac. The specific quality of someone who had built what they'd built and was now watching the building continue without requiring her hand on every part of it.

She thought about Thomas Clinkscales who had kept the record for thirty years and handed it to his grandson. She thought about the first Clinkscales entry and the sitting-down of a boy to tell him what the family kept.

She thought about the document and the seven things and all of it in Mara's hands now alongside her own.

She thought: forward.

She thought: yes.

Vigil was in the canopy.

She felt the bond, warm and specific and present, the specific quality of it after twenty-four years of being what it was between them.

She looked out at the garden.

She thought about sixty and what it looked like and what it felt like and what the distance was between here and the jungle on Hades where she'd come back to herself in the mud with Honor's voice reaching her.

The distance was very large.

She was not the same person.

She was recognizably the same person.

Both of those were true and she held them without needing to resolve the contradiction.

Mara said: "What do you need? From me. Now."

She looked at her.

She said: "Keep writing." She held Mara's gaze. "Don't let the document close. It's not finished. It's never finished while the work is ongoing." She paused. "Add to it. Your version. What you know. What you learn." She held her gaze. "And when the time comes, find the next person to carry it and hand it forward."

Mara said: "How do I know who the next person is?"

She thought about it.

She said: "You'll know. The same way I knew it was you." She held her gaze. "They'll fit. Differently than anyone else. But they'll fit."

Mara held her gaze.

She said: "Okay."

She said: "Yes."

She looked at the garden.

She thought about what was ahead. The sixty years that had been and the years that were still coming, the work that was still ongoing and the things that were still building and the long work in its geological patience moving toward whatever it was moving toward in the centuries ahead.

She thought: I'll be here for some of it.

She thought: they'll be here for the rest.

She thought: that's the shape of the Vigil.

That's what it was always supposed to be.

She breathed the Sphinx air and the garden was outside and Vigil was in the canopy and the kitchen held them both, Mara with the document in her hands and Buffy across from her, and this was the passing of something forward that had been passed forward before and would be passed forward again.

She thought: forward.

She thought: the thread continues.

She thought: always.

She was sixty-seven when Vigil died.

Not dramatically. Not in the way of things that were lost suddenly. In the way of things that had lived well and long and were ready, the specific quality of a very old treecat in the winter of a long life, the bond's quality changing over the months of the last year, becoming quieter and more inward, and then one morning in February in the Sphinx garden simply not there.

She was in the garden when it happened.

She'd known. Not precisely when, but the imminence of it, the way the bond had been for the past several weeks, the specific quality of something that was completing rather than continuing.

She'd been in the garden every morning.

She'd been in the garden when it went quiet.

She stood in the February garden and held the quiet.

The bond was not there.

It was not there the way a sound was not there when the room that had held it became empty, the specific shape of an absence that was defined by what had been present.

She stood in it.

She breathed.

She thought about Nimitz and what Honor had described, years ago now, about the treecats who chose and about the bond and about what the bond was.

She thought about twenty-four years of Vigil.

Twenty-four years of the garden and the canopy and the approaching and the bond's ambient quality and the specific communication of the touch and all of it, the accumulated specific weight of a relationship that didn't have a human category but was real in the way that all real things were real, present and specific and gone.

She stood in the garden for a long time.

Then she went inside.

Scotty was in the kitchen.

He'd been getting up early for months, the specific way he'd been present in the early mornings during the last year, the unspoken quality of someone who knew what was coming and was choosing to be there for it.

She came in and he looked at her face.

He said: "Yes."

She said: "Yes."

He came to her and she held on to him in the kitchen in the February morning and let the grief be what it was, the full grief of a real thing that had been present for twenty-four years and was now not present, the specific and irreplaceable quality of what had been there in the bond that would not be there again.

She held on.

He held her.

After a while she said: "Twenty-four years."

He said: "Yes."

She said: "I thought I knew what the bond was. I didn't fully know until it was gone."

He said: "You'll know it again. Treecats choose."

She said: "I know." She held him. "Not soon. Not immediately." She paused. "The grief needs time to be what it is."

He said: "As long as it needs."

She held him.

She thought about the bond and what it had been. The specific gift of it, the being fully seen and not alone in the seeing. She thought about what it had given her across twenty-four years and what it had shown her about herself, the things she wouldn't have known without the reflection.

She thought about what she'd owed Vigil, the carrots she'd thought of on the day she'd first understood what the bond was, the specific small thought she'd held.

She thought about being sixty-seven and the grief of a treecat and what grief felt like now compared to what it had felt like at seventeen in Sunnydale or twenty-one in Manticore or thirty on the portal's eve.

The grief was grief. It didn't get smaller with age, the individual griefs. What got different was the knowing that grief was a form of love and that having it was the evidence of having had the real thing.

She thought: twenty-four years of the real thing.

She thought: yes. It was worth every piece of it.

She held Scotty in the kitchen in February and the garden was outside and Vigil was not in the canopy and would not be in the canopy and the garden was still the garden and the work was still ahead.

She thought: forward.

She thought: yes.

She thought: I'm going to need some time.

She gave herself the time.
