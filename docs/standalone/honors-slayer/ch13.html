Chapter 13 (4 of 54 in this book)

They spent six days planning.

Not because the plan required six days. It probably required four. The extra two were Honor's decision, made explicit on the second morning when McKeon had suggested they were approaching operational readiness and Honor had said, calmly, that they would take the time to make the plan right rather than making it fast, and the room had understood that this was not open for revision.

Buffy used the time.

She ran the perimeter in the mornings, the camp's perimeter rather than the garrison's, a fixed route that Jamie had mapped in the first week that covered approximately eight kilometers and included three significant elevation changes and two water crossings and enough technical terrain to keep the Slayer sense engaged rather than idling. She ran it alone in the early dark while the camp slept and then she showered with the camp's meager water ration and was present for breakfast and looked, she suspected, irritatingly fine.

She spent afternoons with Martingale.

This had not been planned. It had happened on the second day when Martingale had sought her out in the way of someone who had decided to address something directly rather than wait for an opportunity to develop, and sat down across from her with a mug of tea and said, "You went in for me. I'd like to understand what you are."

Buffy had looked at her. "You could ask Carson. He has the family records."

"I did ask Carson. He was very thorough and very reverent and I came away knowing what the Clinkscales family thinks you are. I'd like to know what you think."

That had been a better question.

They talked for three hours that afternoon and two hours the next day and a variable amount on the days after, the conversations ranging widely in the way of conversations between two people who are discovering the shape of each other's minds. Martingale was a surgeon who thought about bodies the way engineers thought about systems, with a preference for precision and an impatience for imprecision that she'd learned to manage professionally if not personally. She was also, underneath the precision, someone who had spent four months alone with her thoughts in a situation that did not invite optimism, and had come through it with a kind of earned clarity about what mattered.

"What kept you going?" Buffy asked, on the fourth afternoon.

Martingale thought about it seriously, which Buffy appreciated. "Habit, mostly. In the early weeks. The habit of being a professional, which means continuing to function regardless of conditions." She looked at her tea. "Later it was something else. Information. I kept cataloguing the information because it seemed like the information would be useful to someone eventually, and that required me to be present to deliver it, so." She paused. "And I prayed. I'm not particularly religious by Grayson standards, but it turns out that when you're in a small room with limited options you discover what you actually believe, and what I actually believe is that random chance alone doesn't account for everything I've seen."

Buffy thought about a universe that had included the Hellmouth and the First Slayer's dreamscape and a portal that had spat her out two thousand years from everything she'd known. "Yeah," she said. "I have some feelings about random chance myself."

On the fifth day she found Carson sitting outside the water reclamation unit at midday with the expression of a person who had finished a task and had not yet found the next one, a state of unscheduled time that appeared to make him mildly anxious.

"Sit down," she said, and sat beside him.

He sat. He had the quality she'd noticed in him from the beginning, which was a steadiness that wasn't passivity. He didn't fill silence with noise. He didn't need to. She'd grown up surrounded by people who needed to fill silence � Willow with words, Xander with humor, Giles with tea-making sounds � and she'd loved them for it, that specific warmth of people whose anxiety expressed itself as sound. Carson's quiet was a different thing. It wasn't anxious. It was occupied.

"Tell me about Grayson," she said.

He looked at her. "What would you like to know?"

"Everything. Start from what it looks like."

He thought for a moment and then he began, and she listened, not to the information specifically but to the quality of his voice when he talked about it. The way it changed when he described the particular angle of the light in the late afternoon, the color of the sky when the storms came in off the northern ocean, the smell of the orchards at harvest. The way he described his grandfather's hands.

She thought about Sunnydale in the early evening, the specific quality of the California light on the palm trees, the sound of her mother's voice from the kitchen. She did not say any of this. She just listened to Carson describe home and let it fill the place in her chest that was shaped like home, not replacing what was there but sitting alongside it.

"What are you thinking?" he asked, after a while.

"That it sounds like a place worth getting back to."

"It is." He paused. "You'll see it."

She looked at him. He said it with a certainty that was not bravado, not the forced positivity of someone trying to encourage themselves as much as her. It was just certainty, the way you'd state a fact.

"You sound sure about that."

"I am." He met her eyes. "We've come this far."

She thought about this. Two thousand years and a prison planet and a trauma she was still cataloguing the full dimensions of and an escape plan that was going to require everything they had and then some. She thought about the Girl running through an ancient dark with nothing pursuing her, just the joy of the run itself, the body doing what it was built for.

"Yeah," she said. "We have."

On the sixth day Honor called the final briefing.

The plan had three phases.

Phase one: McKeon's team would neutralize the garrison's communications array. Not destroy it. Neutralize it, long enough to prevent them from calling for support during the window of the operation. The array was on the north side of the compound, far enough from the main guard positions to be approachable but not undefended. McKeon had volunteered himself and two others. Honor had looked at him for a long moment and then said yes.

Phase two: simultaneous with phase one, LaFollet would lead a four-person team to create a perimeter engagement on the south and east sides of the compound, enough to draw and hold garrison attention. The objective was confusion and commitment of resources rather than direct engagement.

Phase three: Buffy and Scotty would enter the compound, reach the landing pad, secure the pinnace, and be in a position to lift within eight minutes of phase one executing. The two shuttles would lift simultaneously from the camp, minimum crew, and converge on the pinnace extraction point outside the compound perimeter.

"The timing," McKeon said, which was where he always started when he was about to raise a concern.

"I know," Honor said. "Phase one and phase three have a six-minute overlap. If phase one takes longer than projected�"

"The garrison's response patterns, based on Martingale's information and Buffy's observations, suggest initial response to an external perimeter engagement is approximately two to three minutes before elements are allocated to internal security," LaFollet said. "That gives phase three a window."

"That gives phase three a very precise window," McKeon said.

"Yes." Honor looked at Buffy. "Can you work a precise window?"

Buffy thought about how to answer that. The honest answer was that she'd been working precise windows since she was sixteen, that the Slayer's entire operational existence was built around precise windows because the things she dealt with didn't give you the luxury of imprecise ones. But she'd learned that the way she expressed confidence about her own capabilities required calibration based on her audience, because there was a version of that answer that sounded like arrogance and a version that sounded like information and the difference was mostly delivery.

"Eight minutes from phase one execution to ready-to-lift," she said. "That's my commitment."

Honor nodded. "Lift coordinates?"

Scotty unfolded the map they'd built from Buffy's observations and Martingale's knowledge and three weeks of looking at the available satellite data. "There's a clearing approximately two kilometers northeast of the compound. Large enough for both shuttles and the pinnace with margin."

"That's the rally point," Honor said. "We don't wait. If someone doesn't make the rally point within fifteen minutes of lift�"

"You go," Buffy said. "I'll find my own way up."

The room looked at her.

"The planet isn't the problem," she said. "I can survive down here indefinitely. If something goes wrong and I miss the window, you go. Come back if you can. Don't stay trying to fix a situation that can't be fixed from the ground."

"Buffy�" Scotty started.

"That's the plan," she said. "I'm not negotiating it." She looked at Honor. "You know I'm right."

Honor held her gaze for a moment. "Yes," she said. "I know." She looked around the room. "That's the plan. We execute at 0200 four days from now. Rest, prepare, be ready." She paused. "Questions."

Martingale, who had been cleared for the briefing and who had the expression of someone who had been filing information under 'things that will be useful when I'm out of here' for the past six days, raised her hand slightly. "The pinnace's maintenance status is still unknown."

"Yes," Honor said. "We're assuming it's been resolved. If it hasn't�" she paused for one fraction of a second�"we adapt."

The room absorbed this and moved on, which was, Buffy thought, the right thing to do with unknowns that couldn't be resolved in advance. You named them. You accepted them. You went anyway.

She'd been doing that for two years.

She was getting better at the accepting part.

Two nights before the operation she had a dream that was hers.

Not the Girl's running memories. Not the ancient dark and the fire and the men who spoke in proto-language. Her own, with her own sky over her own town, the specific amber of Sunnydale streetlights on the sidewalk outside Revello Drive. Her mother in the lit window.

She woke up and the grief was there in the way it always was, present and exact, the specific weight of a specific absence. She'd learned not to fight it. You didn't fight grief, that was the lesson that took the longest to learn, you just let it be what it was and breathed around it and waited for it to become background again.

She lay in the dark and breathed and thought about Joyce Summers who did not know where her daughter was and might never know, who had worried, who had been the one reliable thing in two years of unreliable things.

She thought: I'm okay, Mom. I'm doing something important and I'm okay.

She didn't know if that was true. She thought it was becoming true.

She got up before the camp was awake and sat outside and Nimitz appeared from somewhere, having apparently decided at some point that she warranted the same supervision as Honor, and sat beside her on the root with his tail wrapped around his feet and his eyes steady.

She looked at him. He looked at her.

"Do you actually know what I'm feeling?" she asked. "Or is that a Grayson belief system thing that happens to be correct?"

He tilted his head and made a sound she didn't have words for.

"Right," she said. "Fair."

She thought about the Girl, who had also not had words for a lot of things, whose world had been prelinguistic in some fundamental way, who had navigated existence through a kind of direct knowing that Buffy had access to in flashes and which the merging had made more consistent if not entirely fluent.

She thought about what it meant to be the Slayer, at this particular remove from everything the Slayer had originally been for. The Hellmouth was gone, or not gone but two thousand years and several light-years in every direction unreachable. The Council was gone. The infrastructure of her calling had been built for a specific context and that context no longer applied.

What remained was the thing itself, under all the context. The capacity. The speed and the strength and the sense and the healing and the two-thousand-year lineage of women who had been exactly this specific kind of instrument.

And the choice about what to do with it.

She'd always had that choice, technically. The Council had spent considerable effort convincing her otherwise. But she'd known it was there, the knowledge you couldn't unknow once you'd seen it, that the Slayer's power was the Slayer's power and not the Council's, and the only authority that could direct it was the one that lived inside her skin.

Here, with the Council gone and the Hellmouth irrelevant and two thousand years of accumulated consequence fallen away, the choice was all that remained.

She thought about what she was choosing.

Nimitz made a sound she thought she could almost interpret as approval.

"Don't read my mind," she said.

His tail tip moved.

"I'm serious."

He put his head under her hand and she scratched between his ears because there was apparently no version of herself that could resist a small warm creature requesting that, and the grief was still there but it was background now, doing the thing grief did when you gave it space, becoming part of the texture of things rather than the loudest thing in the room.

The camp began to wake up.

She felt it before she heard it, the shift in the pressure of occupied space as people moved from sleep to wakefulness, the small sounds of morning routine beginning, someone in the second shuttle heating water for the tea that was running low and would be gone in another week, the specific footstep pattern of LaFollet starting his perimeter walk.

And then, softer than LaFollet and quieter than McKeon and quieter still than Jamie but not quiet enough, the footsteps she'd learned to recognize at four meters.

"You're getting better," she said, without turning around.

Scotty sat down on the other side of Nimitz, who regarded him with green-eyed neutrality. "Or you're still just better than me."

"Both can be true."

He looked at the gap in the canopy where the stars were fading. Morning was coming in shades of gray, the jungle's nighttime sounds giving way to the daytime ones. "Two days," he said.

"Two days."

He was quiet for a moment. Nimitz, apparently satisfied with the situation, settled his weight more firmly against Buffy's side. "Can I ask you something?"

"You keep asking that."

"And you keep letting me ask it."

She considered this. "Yeah. Go ahead."

"What happens after we get off the planet?"

It was the question under the operation, under all the planning and preparation and tactical consideration. The operation had an end state. The end state was getting off Hades. After that was a different kind of question.

"Depends on what Honor decides," she said. "We get back to Manticoran space, there are about a thousand things that have to happen before I figure out what comes next."

"But you have a what-comes-next."

She thought about it. "I have a direction," she said. "That's different from a plan but it's more than I had six weeks ago."

He looked at her. In the early morning light his face was the face of someone who'd been awake for a while and was not trying to hide it. "What's the direction?"

She looked at the fading stars. Sirius, still there, still the brightest thing in that part of the sky. "Something worth fighting for," she said. "I've been fighting to prevent things for two years. That's important, I'm not saying it isn't. But�" she paused, looking for the word. "I want to fight for something. Not just against things."

He was quiet for a moment. Then he said, "That seems like a direction worth following."

She looked at him.

He looked back.

Neither of them said anything else. The morning came in fully and the camp woke up around them and Nimitz eventually extracted himself and went to find Honor, and the day began, two days before the operation, ordinary in all the ways that days were ordinary and extraordinary in the way that all of them were, now, which was that they were still here and still themselves and still choosing.

That, she thought, was enough to work with.

~~~

The night before the operation nobody slept much.

She could tell by the quality of the camp's silence. Not the deep-cycle silence of genuine sleep but the lighter, intermittent silence of people moving in and out of rest, surfacing and submerging, the body's attempt to restore itself running up against the brain's insistence on running the operation forward and backward and sideways.

She didn't try to sleep. She sat in the shuttle with Martingale, who had the operational role of staying with the shuttle as flight crew, and they talked about nothing of consequence for two hours, which was its own kind of preparation.

"You're not scared," Martingale said.

"I'm always scared," Buffy said. "I've just been scared enough times that it doesn't feel like new information."

Martingale looked at her. "You're seventeen."

"I know."

"That's�" Martingale stopped.

"I know," Buffy said again. "It was a lot."

At midnight she checked every piece of equipment she'd be taking in. The knife. The two pieces of cord that had more uses than their appearance suggested. The communicator. The small pen-light she'd never used but which Scotty had pressed on her with the expression of someone who had thought carefully about contingencies. She checked them with the focused attention of ritual, not because she was uncertain about their condition but because the checking was its own kind of preparation, the body doing something concrete in advance of the thing itself.

At 0100 she went to find Honor.

Honor was awake. Of course Honor was awake. She was sitting in the forward section of the lead shuttle with Nimitz asleep across her legs and the operational map open on the display in front of her and the expression of someone who had already run every scenario available to them and was now simply waiting.

Buffy sat down across from her.

"Did you need something?" Honor asked.

"No." She looked at the map. The compound in its rough outline, the perimeter positions, the landing pad with its two pinnaces. "Just didn't feel like sitting alone."

Honor looked at her. Then she looked back at the map. "Tell me about Sunnydale," she said.

Buffy looked at her.

"You've told bits of it. To different people. I've heard pieces." Honor's voice was even. "Tell me the whole thing. From the beginning."

So Buffy told her. From the beginning, from the fire at Hemery and the first Watcher and the first time she'd understood what she was and what it was going to cost her, through the move to Sunnydale and the library and the Scooby Gang and the Master and Angelus and all of it, the whole two years of it, the apocalypses and the losses and the thing with Angel at the end that she hadn't fully told anyone, that she hadn't even fully told herself, and she told it to Honor Harrington on the night before the operation in the forward section of a shuttle on a prison planet two thousand years from everything she'd been talking about.

Honor listened. She asked questions that were exactly the right questions. She didn't flinch at the parts that warranted flinching. She didn't offer comfort she didn't have. She listened the way Buffy imagined good commanders listened when they needed to understand the full picture before they made a decision, with the complete attention of someone who understood that the information being offered was being offered at a cost.

When Buffy finished it was 0145.

Honor was quiet for a moment.

"You've been doing this since you were fifteen," she said.

"Yes."

"Alone, mostly."

"Mostly."

"And you're here."

Buffy looked at her. "I'm here."

Honor held her gaze. Nimitz opened one eye, regarded them both, and closed it again.

"Then we'll do what comes next," Honor said.

Buffy nodded.

It was time.
