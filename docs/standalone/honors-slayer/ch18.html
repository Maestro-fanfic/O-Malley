Chapter 18 (9 of 54 in this book)

The rest day was not restful in the way that rest days were supposed to be restful.

It was restful in the way that days without scheduled obligations were restful when you were someone whose baseline operational mode was hypervigilance, which was to say it was slightly less activated than the days with obligations but remained in the register of alert rather than relaxed.

She dealt with this by going for a run.

Not in the gym. Outside, which required a conversation with Commander Hennessy about the security implications of the Slayer going for a morning run in the capital city, a conversation that resolved in her favor when she made the point that her personal security capabilities significantly exceeded anything the naval base could provide and that the run was non-negotiable.

Hennessy had looked at her for a moment and then said he would arrange a route.

The route he arranged was along the waterfront, a kilometer of paved path above a harbor that had the specific character of working water, functional rather than decorative, the kind of harbor where things happened rather than where things were displayed. Ships at the quay, smaller than warships but serious, the kind that moved goods between planets and asked no particular questions about what the goods thought of the arrangement.

She ran.

The city woke up around her as she ran, the early-morning population of people with somewhere to be assembling itself from doorways and transit stops, and she was aware of being looked at in a general way, the way anyone was looked at who was running on a public path, and also in a specific way that said her face was beginning to be recognized.

She'd expected this. The cameras at the docking bay had been there and the information about who she was had been released in some form, she didn't know exactly what form, that was one of the things Honor was managing. She ran through the looking and let it be what it was.

At the far end of the waterfront path she stopped at a railing and looked out over the harbor.

The water was the wrong color. Not dramatically wrong, not alien-wrong, but the specific shade of blue-green that said the mineral content and the biology of the water here were not the same as any water she'd looked at before, and knowing why it was different didn't make the difference less present. She looked at it.

"Lady Buffy."

She turned.

The woman who had come up behind her was about fifty, with close-cropped gray hair and the specific build of someone who had been physically active their entire life and whose body showed the accumulated decisions of that rather than the accumulated decisions of a desk. She was wearing civilian clothes that were the kind of civilian clothes that a person wore when they were trying to look like they weren't being formal about something that was actually formal.

She had a treecat on her shoulder.

The treecat was larger than Nimitz, older-looking, with a scar across one true-hand that had healed to a white line against the cream-colored fur. He looked at Buffy with green eyes that were doing something she couldn't precisely read but which felt like assessment.

The woman looked at Buffy with gray eyes that were doing exactly the same thing.

"Your Majesty," Buffy said.

A beat. Then Queen Elizabeth III of Manticore almost smiled. "Honor said you'd figure it out."

"The treecat is the tell." She glanced at the large cat. "No offense."

The treecat made a sound that was probably not offense.

"This is Ariel," the Queen said. "He was going to be insulted regardless of what you said, so don't trouble yourself." She came to stand at the railing beside Buffy and looked at the harbor. "I wanted to meet you before the audience. I do that sometimes, when I want to see how someone is before they know they're being seen."

"I knew I was being seen," Buffy said.

"You knew someone was there. You didn't know it was me."

"Fair."

The Queen looked at the water. She had the quality that Honor had described, the thing underneath the performance of her role, the sharpness that coexisted with everything the position required of her. Looking at her directly, outside the context of an official audience, it was entirely apparent.

"How are you?" she asked.

Not as ceremony. As the question it was.

"Working on it," Buffy said. Which was the same answer she'd given Honor on Hades and which was still the most accurate one available.

"Good answer." The Queen was quiet for a moment. Ariel's tail moved in a slow pattern that probably meant something. "I've been briefed extensively. I want you to know that. I'm not going to make you go through the whole thing again."

"I appreciate that."

"I have two questions. The rest can wait for the audience or not happen at all." She looked at Buffy directly. "First. What do you want?"

Buffy held her gaze. It was a better question than most people led with. "A place," she said. "Not just somewhere to be. A place that I'm part of. A reason to be in it that's mine rather than assigned." She paused. "Something worth fighting for."

"And the fighting. That's not optional for you."

"No. I've tried the optional version. It doesn't work."

The Queen nodded slowly. "Second question. What are you afraid of?"

She thought about it. She thought about the honest answer, which was complicated, and she thought about the version of the honest answer that was also useful.

"Being made into something I'm not," she said. "I've been the Slayer since I was fifteen and the Slayer is a real thing, I'm not arguing with it, but it's not all of me and there are people who look at it and stop seeing anything else." She paused. "I don't want to be a symbol. I want to be a person who does something real."

The Queen was quiet for a long moment. Ariel was very still.

"I can work with that," she said.

Buffy looked at her. "Most people who say that mean they'll work with it until it becomes inconvenient."

"I know." The Queen looked at the water. "I've been the Queen since I was eighteen. I know the difference between the symbol and the person it's packaged with, because I've been both simultaneously for a very long time." She paused. "What I'm offering is not a promise that I won't need the symbol. I'm going to need the symbol. The political situation requires it and pretending otherwise would be dishonest." She looked back at Buffy. "What I'm offering is that I see both. The symbol and the person. And I won't pretend one doesn't exist to get use out of the other."

Buffy held her gaze for a long moment.

"Okay," she said.

"That's not a yes to anything specific."

"No. It's a yes to the conversation." She paused. "What is it you actually need from me? Specifically."

The Queen almost smiled again. "Honor said you asked the direct question."

"Honor said a lot of things about me, apparently."

"She trusts you. In a way I've rarely seen her trust anyone this quickly." A pause. "That means something."

Buffy thought about Honor on Hades, in the shuttle, asking her to tell her about Sunnydale and listening to the whole thing. About the way Honor's trust worked, the way it was given with precision and held with commitment. "Yeah," she said. "It does."

"What I need from you," the Queen said, "is time. To understand what you are and what you can do and where those things fit into a war that we are working very hard not to lose." She paused. "I need you to be patient with a political process that is slower than you are. And I need you to be honest with me when the political process is asking something of you that you can't give."

"And in exchange?"

"Resources. Support. People who will help you build whatever life you're building here." She paused. "And my personal word that I will not use you as a symbol without telling you I'm doing it."

It was, Buffy thought, an unusually honest offer.

She thought about what Giles had taught her about deals and about reading the person across from you and about the difference between what someone promised and what they actually intended. She thought about what Honor had said: she'll recognize something in you.

"I'll take it," she said.

The Queen nodded. "Good." She looked at the water one more time. "The formal audience is tomorrow. Ceremony. Dress uniform and a room full of people. I wanted this first."

"I know. Thank you."

Ariel made a sound she couldn't interpret but which felt like approval. The Queen glanced at him with the expression of someone receiving information through a channel no one else could access. Then she looked back at Buffy. "He thinks you're all right."

"Tell him I think he's all right too."

"He knows," the Queen said. "That's rather the point."

She walked back along the waterfront path the way she'd come, unhurried, and Buffy watched her go with the specific feeling of having been genuinely assessed and found sufficient, which was different from being found useful and different from being found impressive and was its own category that she didn't have a precise word for.

She turned back to the harbor.

The water was still the wrong color. She looked at it for a while longer, the wrong blue-green of water that had never been touched by her world, and let it be what it was.

Then she ran back.

The formal audience was what Honor had said it would be, which was ceremony and formality and a room full of people.

She wore the closest thing to appropriate she'd been able to assemble with help from a very efficient woman named Lieutenant Chen who had shown up the previous afternoon with a collection of options and a tape measure and the expression of someone who had been briefed on unusual circumstances and was not going to make them more unusual. The result was something that was technically Manticoran formal civilian dress in a cut that worked for someone with her particular proportions and in a color that Chen had described as appropriate without being presumptuous.

She didn't know enough about Manticoran fashion to evaluate this independently. She trusted Chen's assessment.

The room was the kind of room that had been designed for exactly this function, which was to make the ceremony feel like it meant something through the accumulated weight of architecture and light and formality. It worked. She could feel it working on her even knowing how it worked, which was the thing about well-made ceremonies that she'd learned from watching Giles manage the Council's occasional formal moments.

There were perhaps sixty people in the room. She recognized a portion of them from the Wayfarer's passenger complement. The rest were the kind of people who attended formal royal audiences, which she was learning to read, the specific grammar of rank and relation that expressed itself in Manticoran society through bearing and position and the subtle semaphore of uniform and decoration.

She read it and filed it and walked through it with her chin level and her hands easy.

Honor was three places ahead of her in the receiving line, in full dress uniform with every decoration she was entitled to, which was a significant number. McKeon was behind her. Scotty was somewhere in the room in his own right, she'd seen him earlier, and he'd caught her eye and made a small gesture that she'd parsed as you look correct, which she'd returned with a look she hoped conveyed the same thing because he did.

The audience proceeded with the formal efficiency of a process that had been done many times before and was being done correctly now. Honor received what was coming to her, which included things Buffy didn't have full context for but which the room received with the kind of reaction that said they were significant.

Then it was her turn.

She stepped forward and did the thing Lieutenant Chen had coached her through, the specific Manticoran formal acknowledgment that split the difference between a bow and a curtsey by being neither quite either, and looked up at Queen Elizabeth on the throne that was exactly what a throne was supposed to be.

The Queen was the same person she'd met at the harbor railing. She was also simultaneously not the same person, or rather she was more of the same person, the symbol and the substance both visible at the same volume. She looked at Buffy with the gray eyes that were doing the same assessment they'd been doing at the waterfront, with the additional weight of sixty witnesses and several centuries of ceremony behind them.

"Lady Buffy of Sunnydale," the Queen said. The room was quiet. "On behalf of the Star Kingdom of Manticore and the Grayson Space Navy, we extend our welcome."

Buffy held her gaze. "Thank you, Your Majesty."

"You have come a very long way."

"Yes." A pause. "I intend to make it worth the trip."

The room held a particular kind of silence that she could feel as pressure, the specific silence of sixty people who had just witnessed something they were going to be thinking about for a while.

The Queen's eyes held something that was not quite a smile but was its closest formal approximation.

"We believe you will," she said.

After the audience there was a reception, because there was always a reception.

She moved through it with the specific skills she'd developed for the school dances and Bronze nights of her pre-Slayer life, the ones she'd been good at before everything changed, the ability to be in a social situation and read it and navigate it without letting the navigation show. It was a different kind of competence than what she'd been using for the past several months and she was surprised by how readily it came back, the social version of muscle memory.

People wanted to talk to her. She talked to them. She deflected the questions that needed deflecting and answered the ones that could be answered and moved through the room with the fluid purposefulness of someone who had a destination even when the destination was just the next conversation.

She found Carson near the food table.

He was wearing Grayson formal dress, which she gathered from his expression was not his preferred environment but which he was managing with the composed dignity of a young man from a culture that prepared you for formal occasions whether you wanted to be prepared or not. His arm was out of its sling. The color in his face was good.

"How's the arm?" she asked.

"Full function." He flexed it slightly to demonstrate. "Surgeon-Lieutenant Martingale is very good."

"She is." She took a piece of something from the table without looking at it, because the reception food had been consistently excellent and she'd stopped being cautious about it. "How are you doing? With all of this."

He looked around the room. The assembled weight of Manticoran formal society doing what formal society did, which was circulating. "It's a great deal," he said.

"Yeah."

"My grandfather is going to�" He stopped, shook his head. "I don't have words for what my grandfather is going to do when I tell him."

She thought about what it meant to have a grandfather who had kept the faith in a lineage for thirty years and was now going to find out that the lineage was literally present and had been through a series of events that made the family records look understated. "Is he going to be all right?"

"He is going to be the most all right he has ever been in his life," Carson said, with a certainty that was affectionate and specific. "He's been carrying those records since he was a boy. He'll cry. He'll pray. He'll probably reread every document in the archive within the week." He paused. "And then he'll want to meet you."

"I want to meet him," she said.

He looked at her. "You mean that."

"He kept the story alive," she said. "That matters." She paused. "In my time the Watchers' Council kept the records. They had the institutional framework and the resources and they still lost things, misinterpreted things, turned things into doctrine that should have stayed as data." She looked at her food. "Your grandfather kept it as something true. Without the institutional framework. That's harder."

He was quiet for a moment. "He would be very moved to hear you say that."

"Tell him." She paused. "When you write. Tell him from me."

He nodded with the gravity of someone accepting a charge. "I will."

She ate her food. He ate his. The reception moved around them with its noise and its circulation and its sixty people sorting themselves into the conversations that were going to matter and the ones that were going to be forgotten by morning.

Scotty found them eventually, with Ibanez in tow and the slightly loosened look of two people who had been at a formal reception long enough that the formality had started to feel comfortable rather than constraining. He caught her eye and she read in the catching a question she answered with a slight nod, because the past two months had given them the vocabulary for that level of nonverbal exchange, and he came over.

"Carson," he said.

"Ensign Tremaine." The formality between them was light, a courtesy rather than a distance.

"How's the arm?"

"People keep asking me that."

"People who were there tend to," Scotty said.

Ibanez had a plate of reception food and the expression of a young man who was deeply grateful for the existence of better-than-functional cooking in a formal setting. He caught Buffy's eye and said, "Lady Buffy," with the particular inflection he'd settled into, which was respectful without being deferential in a way she appreciated.

"Ibanez. You flew clean."

He looked down at his plate. "I had help."

"You had a failing thruster and a garrison pinnace and a good instinct." She paused. "That's not the same as help. That's you."

He looked up. He was eighteen and from a place she couldn't pronounce and had been through more in the past two months than most eighteen-year-olds encountered in a decade, and he was standing in a formal reception at the palace of the Queen of Manticore eating food that was better than functional and processing it all with the careful thoroughness of a person who had decided to take it in properly rather than let it pass over them.

"Thank you," he said.

"You're welcome."

The reception continued. She moved through it and came back and moved through it again, the social muscle memory doing the work while the rest of her ran quieter calculations. The shape of the situation she was in. The deal she'd made with the Queen. The things that were going to be asked of her and the things she was going to ask in return.

The woman in the library dream had said they were going to ask a lot of her.

She thought that was accurate.

She thought she was going to ask a lot in return, which was something she'd never been particularly good at and which she was developing a determined intention to get better at, because this version of her life was not going to be structured around giving everything and not asking what the giving was for.

She was going to ask what the giving was for.

She was going to require an answer.
