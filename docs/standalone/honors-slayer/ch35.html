Chapter 35 (26 of 54 in this book)

The ship back to Manticore was the same class as the Wayfarer, which she noticed and then let go, because the Wayfarer was the Wayfarer and this was a different ship and the comparison served nothing.

It served nothing and she made it anyway because that was still how her mind worked, the constant triangulation against previous experience that was the specific mode of someone who had lost a frame of reference and was building a new one piece by piece.

The new frame of reference was more complete than it had been.

She sat at the viewport on the first day out of Beowulf and watched the system recede and thought about what she was carrying back.

The Constant's communication, which she'd written down in the document and which was going to be the most significant addition to the Clinkscales archive in its history.

Allison's research, which was going to change the understanding of the lineage on a biological level.

Haverstock's interface data, which was going to change the understanding of what the supernatural in this universe actually was.

The open channel, which was available now in a way it hadn't been before the contact, a resource she hadn't had and now did.

And the shape of what she was building, which was clearer than it had ever been. Not a finished plan. She'd learned not to wait for finished plans. A direction, clear and specific, the kind that let you navigate even when the exact path wasn't visible yet.

Scotty was at the seat beside her.

He had his own work, the debrief materials from the Talbott Cluster that needed organizing into the formal reports that the navy required, and he was doing it with the focused efficiency of someone who had learned to work in transit. She watched him work for a while and then went back to her own.

They worked in the same space in the comfortable silence of people who had the same relationship to silence, which was that it was a form of company rather than an absence.

She wrote in the document.

She wrote about Beowulf and the Constant and what she'd received and what it meant. She wrote about Yuen Sharra and toward the light between and the founding documents. She wrote about Allison's research and the channel and the biological substrate of the lineage.

She wrote about the thing that had made the Hellmouth and the long timeline and what the preparation required.

She wrote carefully and completely because the document was going to be read by people who came after her and those people needed the full picture.

The Keeper was present while she wrote, attending in the way it attended to the document work, and she wrote with that attention alongside her and thought about the document becoming part of the archive alongside the Clinkscales records and what the combined picture looked like for the people who would eventually need it.

She wrote until she ran out of what she could articulate for that day and then she put the document aside and looked at the stars, which were the transit stars, the specific quality of stars in motion, different from the fixed configuration that ports produced.

She thought about Manticore and the work waiting there and then she thought about Sphinx after Manticore, the autumn version, the cold she hadn't imagined.

She was looking forward to the cold she hadn't imagined.

She was looking forward to a great many things.

~~~

Manticore from approach was different from Manticore the first time. The first time it had been the destination, which had made it enormous with the weight of what it represented, the new place, the new life, all of it arriving at once.

This time it was home.

She noticed this as the planet resolved from point to disk to the specific configuration she'd spent ten months learning. Home. Not in the sense of Sunnydale, not in the sense of her mother's house and her mother's kitchen and the specific smell of rosemary chicken. In the sense of the place she returned to. The place with the shape of her life in it.

Both could be true.

She'd learned that both could be true.

The docking and the transition back to her quarters and the immediate meeting with Chen and then with Usher were efficient and she moved through them with the accumulated competence of someone who knew this system and how to operate in it.

The Queen met with her on the second day.

The informal sitting room again. Ariel on the chair back. Tea. The afternoon light that she knew now, the specific Manticoran quality of it.

She told the Queen everything. The complete version, not the summary. Two hours of it, detailed and organized, the way she'd learned to brief people who needed the full picture rather than the navigable excerpt.

The Queen listened.

When Buffy finished the Queen was quiet for a moment and then she said: "The timeline."

"Centuries," Buffy said. "Well outside anyone's lifetime currently."

"But within the lifetime of the civilization."

"Yes."

"Then what I build in the next decade matters for something that will happen in three hundred years." The Queen held her gaze. "That's an unusual way to think about governance."

"I know." Buffy held her gaze back. "But you've been governing with an eye toward what your civilization will be in fifty years. A hundred years. The extension is the same logic."

"It is," the Queen said. She looked at Ariel, who looked back at her with whatever he was telling her through their link. "The institution."

"Yes."

"I've been thinking about what it needs to be officially," the Queen said. "The Survey of Lineage Continuity is accurate but insufficient. What you're describing is more than a survey." She paused. "It needs authority. Legal standing. The ability to operate across sovereign systems by agreement rather than by tolerance." She paused again. "That's a negotiation with every member of the Alliance at minimum and ideally with Haven and the League eventually."

"I know it's not immediate," Buffy said.

"No. But it's the direction." She looked at Buffy. "I'll take it to the Alliance Council. With your full briefing. What you received from the Constant, the timeline, the nature of the threat. All of it." She paused. "Some of them are going to have difficulty with the framework."

"I know."

"Not me," the Queen said. "Ariel hasn't been wrong about you yet."

Buffy looked at the treecat. He looked back at her with green eyes that were doing the thing they always did, which was see clearly and without apology.

"Tell him he hasn't been wrong about you either," she said.

The Queen conveyed it. Ariel's tail tip moved.

"Good," Buffy said.

~~~

Three days after the meeting with the Queen she sat down with Carson in her quarters, the two of them across from each other at her desk with the accumulated evidence of the work around them.

He had the Survey's current status. Twelve confirmed lineage candidates across the Alliance. Seven in contact. Five with some form of ongoing support structure around them. The Grayson candidates integrating well. Yeva sending letters at increasing frequency that had shifted from questions about the physical profile to questions about the history to questions about the institution and whether she could be part of building it, which was a development Buffy had not expected at the timeline it arrived on but which she received as exactly right.

She told Carson what she'd received from the Constant.

Not the summary version. The complete version.

He listened with the complete attention he always brought to things she told him, and when she finished he was quiet in the way of someone whose entire frame of reference for a thing they'd spent their life committed to had just been confirmed and expanded in ways that required time to hold properly.

"The founding," he said finally. "My great-grandfather's grandfather. The man who started keeping the archive." He paused. "He said he started because something told him to."

She looked at him.

"He wrote it in the first entry," Carson said. "My grandfather showed me when I was eleven. He said: something told me this was necessary and I could not say no to the telling." He paused. "We thought it was a metaphor. A way of describing conviction." He paused. "It wasn't."

"No," she said. "It wasn't."

He was quiet for another moment. Then he said, with the specific steady quality of someone who had committed to something thirty years ago by inheritance and was now recommitting to it by direct knowledge: "Tell me what the institution needs and I'll help you build it."

She looked at him.

She thought about Sunnydale and the Council and Giles, who had given her everything he could within the constraints of what he was and had wanted to give her more and hadn't always known how. She thought about what it would have meant to have what Carson was offering, the direct specific commitment of someone who saw the whole thing and was in it.

"I know," she said. "That's why I'm here."

He nodded once.

They got to work.

~~~

She wrote to Scotty that evening from her desk with the city outside the window and the work spread across the surface and the Keeper present in the specific way it was present when she was doing something it considered correct.

The letter was shorter than usual. She'd said the significant things in person and the shorter letters were, she'd found, sometimes the more complete ones.

She said: Manticore is good. The meeting with the Queen was good. Carson and I are moving forward on the institutional structure and it's beginning to have the shape of something real rather than something planned.

She said: I've been thinking about what you said on the terrace. About choices and significant variables. I wanted to say again that I'm glad you said it the way you said it. Directly. I've been learning that direct is better and I'm glad you already know that.

She said: how are the leave arrangements looking. I want to know when we're going to Sphinx.

She sealed it and sent it.

Then she turned back to her desk and the work and thought about the shape of the days ahead.

Full. Specific. The long work and the immediate work and the people who were part of both and the city outside the window that was home in the sense she'd found for home in this century.

She'd built something.

Not finished. Not settled in the sense of done. Settled in the sense of having found its footing.

She'd found hers.

She looked at the desk and the files and the map with its nodes and Carson's letters and the copy of the founding document of the Biological Survey Institute with toward the light between underlined in her own handwriting, and she thought about all of it as one thing, the accumulated specific whole of nine months in a century she hadn't expected.

The Keeper's quality was the quality of satisfaction.

Not completion. The work was too large and too long for completion to be a present-tense experience.

But satisfaction. The specific satisfaction of something that had been waiting and was now watching the thing it had waited for doing what it was built to do.

She felt that.

She let herself feel it.

Then she turned back to the work, because the work was what she was for and the work was also, in the deepest sense, what she wanted, and those two things being the same thing was the best version of what a life could be.

She worked until midnight.

Then she went to sleep and the Girl ran through the ancient dark and the running was joy, pure and uncomplicating, the body doing what it was built for, the lineage continuing forward as it had always continued, patient and specific and without requiring expectation to go on.

Forward.

Always forward.

She ran and the dark held her and she was entirely herself, all three versions of herself at once, and the running felt like beginning rather than ending, the specific quality of a threshold crossed.

She ran.

Tomorrow there was work.

Tonight there was this, the ancient joy of the body and the dark and the forward motion, and it was enough.

It was, as it turned out, exactly enough.
