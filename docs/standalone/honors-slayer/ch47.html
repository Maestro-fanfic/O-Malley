Chapter 47 (38 of 54 in this book)

The Sphinx autumn came.

She was on the property when it did, which meant she saw the transition from late summer to the beginning of the spectacular version, the specific days when the trees were starting to decide and the decision was visible in them before it was complete, the first hints of color in the higher canopy while the lower leaves were still the summer green.

She was in the garden on one of those days when Scotty's parents arrived.

Not dramatically. An air car came down in the field adjacent to the property that served as a landing area and two people got out of it with the ease of people returning to somewhere they knew well, and then they saw her in the garden and stopped.

She stopped too.

They looked at her and she looked at them across the space of the garden in the early autumn light.

His mother was small, dark-haired going gray, with a look that was specific and kind in the way of people whose kindness was a practice rather than a mood. His father was taller, with the bearing of someone who had served and whose service was in how they held themselves rather than on display, and with eyes that were doing the same calibration that Scotty's eyes did when they were assessing something they cared about.

She crossed the garden.

His mother said, when she was close enough: "You're Buffy."

She said: "Yes."

His mother looked at her for a moment.

Then she said: "Carson told us about you."

She looked at her. "He's talked to you."

"He came to Sphinx two months ago," Scotty's father said. "He came to tell us about the Vigil. About what it is and what it does and what you've built." He held her gaze. "He didn't ask us to think well of you. He just told us the truth about you and let us reach our own conclusions."

She thought about Carson, who had said that seeing someone clearly and honestly was how you helped them be known. She thought about what it meant that he'd done this for her without telling her he was going to.

She was going to have to write him a letter about this.

She looked at Scotty's parents.

She said: "What conclusions did you reach?"

His father held her gaze for a moment.

He said: "That our son chose well."

She held this.

It arrived with the specific weight of something that had been waiting for delivery, the thing that parents of people you loved could give that nobody else could give, the specific recognition that was theirs to offer.

She said: "Thank you."

His mother said: "Come inside. I want to see what you've done with the garden before the light changes."

She came inside.

His mother walked the garden with the specific attention of a person who knew gardens and was reading this one, the decisions in it, the things planted for function and the things planted for other reasons and the way the two intermingled.

She stopped at the rosemary equivalent.

She said: "This isn't Sphinx."

"No," Buffy said. "It's a Grayson variety. The closest equivalent I could find to something I grew up with."

His mother looked at it. She touched one of the stems and rubbed the leaves between her fingers and smelled it.

She said: "It's close."

"Close enough," Buffy said.

His mother looked at her. Something in her face was doing the assessment that the Vigil made Buffy think of as the slow kind, the kind that came from living alongside someone rather than looking at them directly.

She said: "Scott told us about the work. What it is. The long work." She paused. "He explained it well. I want you to know that I understand what you're doing and why and that I think it matters."

Buffy held her gaze.

"Thank you," she said.

"Don't thank me," his mother said. "It's just accurate." She looked at the garden. "What else are you planting?"

They talked about the garden for an hour.

Not about the Vigil or the timeline or the operational work or any of the things that were the visible content of her days. About the garden, which was the other content, the underneath content, the soil of a life rather than its superstructure.

His mother had opinions about companion planting. She had opinions about the specific Sphinx winter challenges that came with the elevation they were at. She had the particular directness of someone who had been making things grow in difficult conditions for decades and who found the conversation about it more interesting than most other conversations.

Buffy found it interesting too.

She thought: this is what the after looks like. This specific thing, in this garden, with this person who loves someone I love, talking about what grows.

She thought: yes.

The afternoon moved through its stages and became evening and the spectacular version was continuing to begin in the canopy around them, the decision spreading from tree to tree, and Vigil was present in the canopy in the way Vigil was always present when she was on the property, the bond's ambient quality, the knowledge of accompanying.

Scotty came out of the house at some point and stood at the garden's edge watching her and his mother and not interrupting, and when she caught his eye he had the expression she had no precise name for, the one that was several good things at once.

She held that.

She held all of it.

The garden in the autumn and the wrong stars that were hers and the treecat in the canopy and the man at the garden's edge and his mother telling her something useful about the soil composition at this elevation and the Keeper present at the channel and the Vigil in its collected distributed reality doing the work that was theirs to do.

She was here.

She was building something.

She was, as it had kept turning out every time she let herself look at it directly, exactly where she was supposed to be.

Not supposed to in the sense of destiny or the sense of design, not the Council's sense or the sense of any authority external to herself. Supposed to in the sense of having made the choices that brought her here and finding that the choices were right. Supposed to in the sense of this specific fit between who she was and what she was doing.

She'd spent a long time not having that fit.

She had it now.

She let herself have it.

She stood in the garden in the Sphinx autumn and let herself have the life she'd built and the people in it and the work and the wrong stars and the treecat and all of it, fully and without the reservation she'd learned to hold things with, the protective distance that had kept her from being too disappointed if they went away.

Some things went away.

Some things stayed.

This was staying.

She let it stay.

She turned back to the conversation about soil composition and was present for it and the evening came in around them and it was the Sphinx autumn and it was home and it was good.

It was very, very good.
