Chapter 11 (2 of 54 in this book)

The first night she went out alone.

Not because she hadn't considered taking someone. She'd considered it the way she considered all tactical questions, which was thoroughly and without sentimentality, and the conclusion was the same as the one she'd presented in the briefing. One person moved differently than two. The math didn't change because someone was worried about her.

She'd told Jamie at 2200. He'd looked at her with the expression of a man whose entire professional training was colliding with a situation that training had not anticipated, and then he'd done the Grayson thing, which was to hand her the communicator and tell her the check-in intervals and then stand very still while she went.

She appreciated that. She'd had guardians before who couldn't do the standing still part.

The jungle at night was different from the jungle in the day in ways that went beyond the obvious. It wasn't just the light. The whole sonic landscape shifted, the daytime cacophony of birds and insects and the distant bell-thing replacing itself with something lower and more continuous, a deep background hum that she felt in her sternum more than heard with her ears. The predators that slept through the heat of the afternoon were awake and moving, and she could track them by the pressure-change their movement sent through the understory, the way smaller creatures went quiet in a ripple around anything large enough to be dangerous.

She moved through it like water through water.

The Slayer sense was not precisely vision or hearing or smell. It was all of those and also something that had no name in any language she'd encountered, a direct readout from the world's threat landscape that her brain processed as a combination of knowing and feeling. In the daylight it was useful. In the dark it was something close to supernatural.

She reached the garrison's outer perimeter in forty minutes. In the daylight, moving carefully, it would have taken twice that.

She stopped at the tree line and simply looked.

The compound was larger than the briefing's rough estimates. Four main structures, two smaller outbuildings, a landing pad with two pinnaces and what looked like a modified civilian shuttle that someone had bolted additional equipment onto with more enthusiasm than elegance. The perimeter lights were on a timer rather than a sensor grid, which was either an efficiency measure or a sign of complacency. She filed it.

The night-vision grid McKeon had mentioned was visible once she knew to look for it, the faint ghostly shimmer of the projector arrays mounted at intervals along the inner fence. She watched the shimmer for ten minutes and confirmed the gaps. Four of them, each approximately ninety seconds in duration, cycling in a pattern that was either random or pseudo-random. She'd need a second night to determine which.

She counted personnel. Eighteen visible in the first hour. She suspected the total garrison was somewhere between forty and sixty based on the size of the structures and the number of vehicles, but she'd need more time to see the full rotation.

She checked in at midnight and at two and at four.

At four-thirty she turned back toward camp with information she hadn't had before and the particular quiet satisfaction of a job done cleanly.

She was back in her bunk at five-fifteen. She lay in the dark listening to the camp breathe around her and ran the numbers.

The gaps in the perimeter grid were consistent enough to be exploitable. The rotation timing was regular enough that she could predict position. The question was not whether she could get in and out of the compound. She could. The question was what came after, which was the question that kept her awake until the bell-sound started up in the canopy outside and the camp began to stir.

She thought about what Honor had said. No engagement unless your cover is blown.

She thought about what she knew about herself, which was that she was seventeen years old and had been ending lives since the night a vampire had dropped out of a tree at her in a graveyard and she'd staked him with a branch she'd grabbed on reflex. She'd stopped counting after a while. She'd stopped counting a lot of things after a while.

The people in the garrison compound were not vampires. They were not demons. They were human beings who had done terrible things in service of a regime that valued the terrible things they were willing to do, but they were human and that meant the math worked differently.

She thought about that until the camp was fully awake and then she put it away and went to the morning briefing.

"Forty-eight to fifty-two," McKeon said, looking at the notes she'd delivered. "That's higher than projected."

"Could be lower. I only got eyes on the rotation, not the full complement. Some of them were probably asleep."

"Still." He looked at Honor. "Our original planning assumed thirty-five to forty."

"Revise it," Honor said. "What's the practical effect on the approach?"

"It means the distraction element needs to be bigger." He looked back at Buffy. "The perimeter gaps."

"Ninety seconds, four gaps, cycling. I need one more night to confirm whether it's random or pseudo-random. If it's pseudo-random I can predict the next cycle. If it's actually random we work with the known gap duration and accept the uncertainty."

"What's your confidence level on the gap duration itself?"

"High. I timed eleven cycles. The variance was under three seconds." She paused. "I want to go back in tomorrow night and get closer."

Silence.

"You said two hundred meters," Honor said.

"I said we'd want eyes at two hundred meters. I was at the tree line last night, which is four hundred. I can do two hundred. I want to see the guard positions at the inner fence up close before we commit to anything."

"And the night after?"

"Depends on what I find."

McKeon looked at Honor again. This was something they did, the two of them, this rapid silent consultation that covered in a glance the same ground that other people needed five minutes of argument to traverse. Buffy watched it with something that was not quite envy and not quite longing. Whatever it was had something to do with the particular kind of trust that only accumulated through shared catastrophe, and she recognized it because she'd had it once with Willow and Xander and she knew what it looked like from the outside.

"Three nights," Honor said. "Then we plan."

~~~

The second night she went to two hundred and fifty meters, which was close enough to see faces.

This was not tactically necessary. She'd told herself it was, that she needed to assess individual guard alertness rather than just rotation patterns, that McKeon's plan required granular information about the personnel themselves. Some of that was true.

Some of it was something else, a need to look at the people she might be asked to help neutralize and make a decision with full information. She owed them that much. She owed herself that much.

The guards were young. Not universally, there were older personnel who moved with the watchful economy of people who'd survived by being careful, but the majority of the faces she could see clearly at two hundred meters with Slayer eyes were young. Mid-twenties. Some of them younger. Kids who'd joined an organization that told them they were the sharp end of history and paid them accordingly and never mentioned that the price was becoming the kind of person who could work a camp like the Tepes without losing sleep.

Or maybe they did lose sleep. She couldn't know that from here.

She watched one of them, a young woman with her hair scraped back under her cap, stand at her post for forty minutes in the humid dark without moving more than was strictly necessary. Professional stillness. The kind you had to learn. The kind that didn't come natural to anyone in their twenties and meant she'd been at this long enough for it to become a habit.

Buffy thought about herself at the same age. The same professional stillness. The graveyard at midnight, perfectly quiet, waiting for something to emerge from a fresh grave so she could put it back.

The comparison was imperfect in every possible way and still landed somewhere uncomfortable.

She checked in at midnight and at two.

At two-thirty she heard the thing that changed the calculation.

It came from one of the smaller outbuildings, the one she'd tagged as a potential storage facility or communications relay. She'd been planning to get closer to it on the third night. She was getting closer to it now because the sound coming out of it was a human voice saying something in the tonal flatness of someone who had run out of ways to scream and had gone somewhere quieter and simpler.

She was at the wall of the outbuilding before she'd made a conscious decision to move.

The window was shuttered but not sealed. She could hear through it clearly enough.

Two voices. One asking questions. One answering in that flat depleted register, a woman's voice, answering in the way that people answered when they'd concluded that answesty or non-answer had the same consequence and they'd simply stopped caring which.

The questions were about the escaped prisoners.

About Honor.

Buffy stood with her back to the wall of the outbuilding and her hands flat against the composite and breathed through the combat calm the way she'd learned to, slow and deliberate, feeling her heartbeat settle rather than race. This was information. This was tactically relevant information about the garrison's knowledge state and the source of their changed posture and the thing she was supposed to do with this information was note it and withdraw and report it.

She stayed for eleven more minutes, until she had everything that was being said, and then she withdrew through the dark without making a sound and ran the numbers back to camp.

~~~

She woke Honor at three in the morning.

Not the way she'd have done it a month ago, which would have been simply appearing next to Honor's bunk without announcement, a habit that had caused three separate incidents involving Honor's sidearm in the first week. She knocked on the shuttle wall and said "it's me" in a normal voice and waited.

Honor appeared in the hatchway thirty seconds later with her hair down and her expression alert in a way that suggested she hadn't been deeply asleep. Nimitz was a dark shape on her shoulder, his eyes catching the ambient light.

"Second night," Honor said.

"Yes. You need to hear this."

They sat in the shuttle with the lights at their lowest setting and Buffy told her what she'd heard.

The summary was this: the garrison knew someone had escaped from the Tepes. They did not know how many. They did not know identities. They had a prisoner who had been on the Tepes and who was being questioned about the same, and the prisoner was not currently in a condition to provide much useful information, but the questioners were not finished.

Honor's face did very little while Buffy talked. This was different from having no reaction. Buffy had spent two years reading faces in crisis and she could see what Honor's face was doing under the stillness, which was processing the tactical situation and the moral one simultaneously and not letting either interfere with the other until she had the full picture.

When Buffy finished, Honor was quiet for a long moment.

"How many guards on the outbuilding?"

"One outside. One inside with the prisoner. Could be more inside that I couldn't locate."

"The prisoner's voice. Could you determine condition?"

"Alive. Aware enough to answer questions. Beyond that I couldn't tell." She paused. "The questioning had been going on for a while. You could hear it in the way they were talking. Both of them. The questioner was tired of asking and the prisoner was tired of the whole situation."

Honor looked at Nimitz. He looked back at her, and whatever passed between them stayed between them.

"I need to think," Honor said. "Can you give me an hour?"

"Yes." Buffy stood. "There's something else."

Honor waited.

"I can get them out," Buffy said. "The prisoner. I know the perimeter gaps. I know the guard positions. I can go back in tomorrow night and get them out before they provide anything useful." She kept her voice level. "I want you to know that's an option before you spend the hour thinking."

"I know it's an option," Honor said. "Go sleep. Come back in an hour."

Buffy went.

She didn't sleep. She sat outside in the dark with her back against the shuttle hull and thought about the woman's voice in the flat exhausted register and thought about the question of what she was for.

She was the Slayer. She was for fighting things that wanted to eat the world. She was for the thing that went bump in the night and the thing that wore a human face over something that had never been human and the specific category of evil that the rest of humanity wasn't equipped to address.

The garrison on Hades were not that category. They were the other kind of evil, the kind that was entirely human, and the conventional wisdom was that the other kind of evil was someone else's problem.

She'd never entirely believed the conventional wisdom.

She thought about Giles telling her that she needed to stay in her lane, that the Slayer's mandate was specific and that she'd burn out trying to solve every version of the problem instead of just the supernatural one. She thought about how she'd argued with him and how she'd lost the arguments and how she'd sometimes done what she thought was right anyway and how the track record on those occasions was sufficiently mixed that she'd learned to at least sit with the question before acting.

Sitting with the question.

She sat with it.

The hour passed.

She knocked on the shuttle wall again. Honor was already awake, or still awake, and had been joined by McKeon, who had the look of a man who'd been briefed while she'd been sitting outside thinking.

"We go in," Honor said. "Four nights from now. The prisoner changes the schedule."

McKeon pulled up the rough map they'd been building. "The distraction element becomes more complex if we're extracting a prisoner who may not be ambulatory."

"I know."

"We're going to need more people on the inside than we originally planned."

"I know that too." Honor looked at Buffy. "Can you take someone with you on the extraction run?"

"One person. Small. Quiet." She thought. "Scotty."

McKeon blinked. Honor didn't.

"He was top of his class in small unit tactics," Honor said, to McKeon rather than Buffy, answering the unasked question. "And he's been watching Buffy move for two weeks."

"I've been watching him move for two weeks," Buffy corrected. "He's quiet enough. And he won't do anything I don't tell him to do."

She wasn't entirely certain that was true. She was certain he'd try.

"Brief him in the morning," Honor said. "Full picture. He needs to understand what he's walking into."

"Yes, ma'am."

She said it without thinking and Honor looked at her with something that might have been surprised and might have been pleased, and Buffy wasn't sure where the instinct had come from either, except that she'd spent enough time watching these people to have started absorbing the grammar of how they spoke to each other and apparently some of it was sticking.

~~~

She told Scotty over breakfast.

Not all of it. She gave him the tactical outline, the perimeter, the gaps, the guard positions, the prisoner. She watched his face while she talked the way she'd learned to watch faces, for the tells that separated the people who could handle what they were being told from the people who thought they could and were wrong.

His face did something she hadn't quite expected, which was that it didn't perform anything. It didn't perform calm or resolve or the anticipatory excitement that some people couldn't keep off their faces when they were handed an operation. It just received the information and processed it and when she finished he asked three questions that were exactly the right three questions in exactly the right order.

"What's the extraction route?"

"What condition do we assume the prisoner is in?"

"What's our abort threshold?"

She answered all three.

He nodded. "Okay."

"That's it?"

"Did you want more?"

She thought about it. "No. That's the right amount."

He drank his coffee. She drank her chocolate. Around them the camp was moving through its morning rhythms, LaFollet doing his perimeter walk, Fritz making notes in the medical bay shuttle, Carson doing something with the water reclamation unit that involved a great deal of careful attention and one carefully controlled expression of frustration.

"Can I ask you something that's not tactical?" Scotty said.

"You can ask."

"Are you scared?"

She thought about giving him the answer that would reassure him. She thought about the habit she'd developed over two years of being the person everyone looked to for the assurance that things were going to be fine, the particular performance of confidence that a Slayer was supposed to project because if the Slayer looked scared then everybody looked scared and that didn't help anybody.

"Of going in?" she said. "No. I know how to do that part."

"Of what comes after?"

She looked at her mug. "The after is where I always had trouble."

He was quiet for a moment. Then: "For what it's worth, I'm good at after. Ships are mostly after. You spend six hours on a combat op and then you spend six months dealing with the paperwork and the inquiry and the people who weren't there asking you to explain your decisions to them in a way that makes sense in a room that smells like furniture polish." He said it lightly, without self-pity. "I'm not saying that's the same thing you mean. I'm saying I have some practice at coming down from things."

She looked at him. He was watching her in that way he had, interested and undemanding.

"I'll keep that in mind," she said.
