Chapter 29 of 43

Arrival

"New setting, new arc, and would you look at that," the voice from the gallery said, settling in with real appetite. "Another montage. Third one, by this narrator's count, and this narrator is now formally on the record as keeping a count."

"You promised a second act, not a hat trick."

"I contain multitudes. So does the structure, apparently."

The first month taught him more about failure than the entire war had.

Not the dramatic kind. Nobody died because Xander said the wrong thing in his first few weeks in the field, nobody's plan collapsed under the weight of his mistakes the way Faith's had. It was smaller than that, quieter, the grinding humility of discovering just how much of what made him good at his job back home didn't automatically transfer somewhere entirely new.

He'd shown up with a rough itinerary Giles had assembled from scattered reports, three villages where credible sightings of newly-Called girls had come through, a translator arranged for the first week who turned out to have other, more urgent obligations after day four, and an assumption, mostly unexamined, that steadiness itself would be enough to bridge whatever gaps opened up along the way.

It wasn't. The first girl he found, a fierce, terrified fourteen-year-old named Amara who'd been fending off her own village's fear of her with nothing but raw nerve and a kitchen knife, looked at him with open suspicion the entire first conversation, understanding maybe one word in five of what he was trying to say, and Xander went back to his rented room that night with the humiliating knowledge that his best tool, his voice, his ability to find the right words at the right moment, was mostly useless here without a shared language to carry them.

This is harder than the vineyard, he admitted, lying on a mattress that smelled faintly of dust and diesel, staring up at a ceiling fan that turned with a rhythm he still hadn't gotten used to. At least at the vineyard I knew what I was doing.

You knew the shape of the danger, Coulson said. You've never claimed to know everything. This is a different kind of problem, that's all. It's solvable. It just requires a different tool than the one you've been leaning on.

Which is?

Time. And, I'd suggest, actually learning the languages instead of hoping charm carries you across the gap. It won't, not reliably, not for the kind of trust this work actually needs.

"'Hoping charm carries you across the gap,'" someone in the anonymous rows repeated, savoring it. "That's just a diss track with better vocabulary."

"He'd never dignify it with the word 'diss.'"

"Fine. A formal grievance. Same energy."

Xander took that seriously in a way that surprised even himself. He found a local teacher in the second village, a retired schoolmaster with more patience than Xander had any right to expect from a stranger, and spent every spare hour he had, evenings mostly, sometimes mornings before the day's work started, working through vocabulary and grammar with the same dogged, unglamorous persistence he'd once put into a popsicle-stick model of a vineyard. It was slow. It was humbling in the way that being a genuine beginner at something always was, a grown man stumbling over basic phrases a five-year-old native speaker could manage without thinking.

You hate this, Coulson observed, one evening, watching Xander mangle a tone he'd already been corrected on twice.

I hate being bad at things in front of people. Kind of a lifelong condition. Xander tried the phrase again, got it slightly less wrong. Doesn't mean I'm gonna stop doing it, though. Some things are worth being bad at for a while.

"There's no awards committee."

"There should be. I'd nominate the ceiling fan too, honestly, it's been very consistent."

That's a healthier relationship with failure than you had a year ago.

Yeah, well. Losing an eye and getting a permanent roommate does wonders for a guy's perspective.

The weeks went by in a slow accumulation of small, unglamorous competencies. He learned enough of the local language to hold a real conversation, halting and imperfect but genuine, the kind that let a scared girl actually explain what she was afraid of instead of trying to read it off his face and hoping she'd guessed right. He learned the rhythms of villages that didn't run on Sunnydale's clock, learned to wait when waiting was what the moment needed, learned that the same steadiness that had worked back home worked here too, once he'd built the actual bridge for it to cross on.

Coulson helped, in the ways he could. His trained eye for reading a room, for catching the tell that meant a village elder's welcome was more performance than genuine trust, for noticing the hesitation that meant a girl was hiding an injury she didn't want treated in front of her family, added real, tangible value to work that would otherwise have relied entirely on Xander's own slower, more instinctive read. It wasn't a competition anymore, hadn't been since the fight under the school. It was just two sets of eyes, doing what each of them did best, folded into one steady, patient presence.

"Fast forward," someone in the anonymous rows said. "We all know how this part goes. Let's see the good part."

By the end of the second month, word had started to travel, the way word did in places where a stranger's reputation mattered more than his paperwork. The American who showed up quiet, who learned the language properly instead of expecting everyone to meet him in his, who didn't rush a scared girl or her family into a decision they weren't ready for. Villages that had been wary of the first knock on the door started sending word ahead of his arrival instead, an invitation rather than a surprise, and Xander found himself, for the first time in longer than he could remember, doing work that fit him so precisely it barely felt like work at all.

You're good at this, Coulson said, one quiet evening, watching a sunset spill orange and gold over a landscape that had started, slowly, to feel less foreign than it had two months ago.

We're good at this, Xander corrected, easy now, no longer needing to defend the distinction. Different skill set than the vineyard. Same basic idea, though. Somebody's gotta be the steady one nobody else has the patience to be.

I've noticed you say that a lot. "Somebody's gotta."

Yeah, well. Xander watched the light fade slow over hills he was starting, despite everything, to think of as almost familiar. Turns out it's kind of my whole thing.
