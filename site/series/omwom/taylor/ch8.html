Chapter 8 of 29

Traffic Alert

The staff changed first. That was the tell McClane caught before Taylor did, a shift so small she'd have written it off as nothing if he hadn't flagged it the second it happened.

Two more of them by the east doors, he said. Watch how they're standing. Not serving anybody. Not clearing plates. Just standing.

Taylor found them without turning her head, a skill she was getting better at by the hour, two men in the same black-and-white catering uniform as everyone else, positioned at the east doors with trays they weren't offering to anyone. One of them had a build that no amount of formal wear quite managed to disguise, shoulders too wide, stance too settled, weight on the balls of his feet in a way waiters simply didn't stand.

Muscle. Somebody just doubled the muscle on that door.

Or somebody's short-staffed and grabbed whoever was closest.

Kid. No heat in it, just certainty, the patience McClane seemed to keep in reserve for arguments he'd already had a hundred times with a hundred less careful people. I've worked enough details in my life to know what a bored waiter looks like versus what a guy pretending to be one looks like. Those two aren't bored. They're watching the room the exact same way we've been watching it. Different training, maybe. Same job tonight.

One of the real waiters reached them carrying a tray of empty champagne flutes. He was young, sweating lightly beneath a collar starched past comfort, moving with the exhausted speed of someone who had spent five hours being told that rich people should never see him hurry. The wider man murmured something without turning his head.

The waiter stopped.

For a second his expression was blank. Then he looked down at the tray, looked toward the kitchen, and handed it over.

The wider man accepted it without shifting his stance. The waiter removed a folded service cloth from his belt and passed that over too. Last came the small black order pad from his breast pocket. The false waiter tucked it away without opening it, fully equipped now with the props of a job he still had no intention of performing.

"Break rotation changed," the second man said.

The real waiter glanced toward a woman supervising the coffee service. She had heard. She kept arranging cups and did not meet his eyes.

"Nobody told me."

"They just did. Kitchen until somebody comes for you."

There was no threat in the words. That made the obedience worse. The waiter took one look at both men, lowered his gaze, and walked back the way he had come with empty hands.

That wasn't extra staffing, Taylor thought.

No. That was a uniform changing owners without anybody taking it off.

The false waiter lifted the tray and began turning slowly with the room. From a distance he looked useful. Up close, every glass on it was empty.

It kept happening, small and quiet, over the next twenty minutes, each new piece slotting into a shape neither of them liked. A third man near the coat check, positioning himself where he could see the main entrance and the service corridor both at once. A fourth drifting a slow, unhurried loop through the crowd that took him past every cluster of guests without ever once stopping to actually serve anyone. A fifth stationed at the base of the grand staircase, angled to watch both the mezzanine above and the atrium floor below at the same time. None of them looked at Taylor twice. None of them looked at anyone twice, not obviously, which was, McClane said, exactly the problem.

Taylor stopped counting people and started counting positions.

The atrium had six routes out if she treated every door as equal. The main entrance opened into the coat-check lobby and the covered drive. The east doors crossed a glass vestibule to the garden terrace. The kitchen connected to a service corridor, then forked toward the loading dock and staff parking. A fire door beneath the mezzanine stairs led somewhere her insects had not fully mapped. The stairs themselves reached the second-floor conference suites. The bank of three elevators could reach every public floor and, judging by the buttons Taylor's bugs had felt beneath the plates, two levels the guests had no reason to visit.

Six routes. Seven if she counted the service elevator separately. Eight if the kitchen's dry-goods door reached the alley instead of another storage room.

McClane made her do it again without assuming any door opened where its sign claimed it did.

Main entrance gets you to a lobby, he said. Lobby isn't outside. East doors get you to glass. Glass isn't outside until the next door opens. Kitchen gets you into a corridor we know forks. We don't know where both forks end. Elevators get you into a box somebody else controls. Stairs go up, which isn't out. Count destinations, not promises.

Taylor revised the map.

Two confirmed exterior routes. One probable. Five ways to move inside the building. No route that carried both her and Danny out without crossing a position Anders's people had quietly occupied.

That did not mean they were trapped. Not yet. It meant someone had decided every departure should have a witness.

The real staff reacted without understanding why. A waiter carrying coffee reached the east doors, received a quiet word, and turned his whole route around. The coat-check attendant surrendered her stool to the third man and moved behind the counter. A MedHall employee tried the mezzanine staircase with a stack of auction sheets and was redirected toward the service lift by the man at its base.

The employee tried to laugh it off. "I only need the board packets."

"They're bringing them down."

"Since when?"

The man smiled at her and touched two fingers to the radio wire disappearing beneath his collar.

"Since just now."

She stood there long enough for the interruption to become visible. Then a board member called her name from across the room, and she seized the excuse to turn away. By the time she reached him, her own smile had returned. She bent close and spoke. The board member glanced at the stairs.

The man at their base was helping an elderly guest find the restrooms. He looked attentive and harmless. The board member nodded before the employee had finished explaining, a tiny surrender offered to avoid making a scene at a charity gala.

That's how the room helps them, Taylor thought.

Yeah. Everybody in here has spent their whole life learning not to be the first person who calls something ugly.

The replacements did not merely watch the exits. They controlled who was allowed to approach them.

Taylor sent insects to each door. The main entrance still opened whenever a late guest arrived, but only after the coat-check man looked toward the mezzanine and received a nod. The east doors had gained a steel floor pin that had not been there earlier. At the loading corridor, somebody had moved a rolling rack across the fire exit, not blocking it completely, not yet, but close enough to finish the job in seconds.

She tested the routes without moving herself.

A moth above the coat-check desk rode the pressure change when the outer doors opened for a late-arriving couple. Cold air crossed the lobby, but the inner doors remained closed until the new man had compared their names to the guest list. He asked for identification from the husband and not the wife. He looked past both of them at the drive, counting the valet, the sedan, and whoever might still be sitting inside it. Only then did he welcome them into the gala.

At the east vestibule, a spider clinging behind the brass push plate felt the inner door move less than an inch before the floor pin caught. Someone had installed it from the atrium side. It could be lifted in a second by a person who belonged there and would turn an ordinary departure into a visible request for permission for anyone who did not.

The kitchen route was worse. Taylor sent two roaches beneath the swinging door, one along each wall so she could read the corridor from both sides. A security man stood beside the employee time clock, apparently checking a clipboard. Another waited beyond the loading-dock fork with one hand in his jacket pocket. Between them, cooks and servers still moved normally, but every worker leaving the kitchen crossed one man's field of view and approached the other.

The rolling rack at the fire exit held boxed wine. Too heavy for one hurried person to move quietly. Light enough for two of Anders's men to shift once they wanted the door.

They haven't closed it, Taylor said.

They don't need it closed yet. They need to know who tries it.

They're building a lock, she thought.

Yeah. One piece at a time, slow enough nobody tries the door while it's changing.

A room full of people would notice being locked in.

A room full of people notices a locked door. Nobody notices six unlocked doors becoming watched doors until somebody needs one.

Taylor looked at the smiling guests, the quartet, the servers circling with coffee, and understood that the takeover had begun while the party was still happening. The only missing element was anyone admitting it aloud.

She found Danny beside the silent-auction table studying a framed aerial photograph of the waterfront. Someone had marked the current bid at four hundred dollars, which seemed excessive for a picture Danny could have taken from the ferry if he owned a camera and remembered to bring it.

"How much longer?" Taylor asked.

Danny checked his watch. "Auction closes in eighteen minutes. Then we can go."

"We don't have to wait for it."

He turned fully toward her. That was the problem with Danny. He could spend half an evening missing what she was doing because he assumed she wanted the space, then hear one wrong note and give her his complete attention.

"What's wrong?"

Taylor could feel three of Anders's people from where she stood. One at the coat check. One looping behind Danny. One at the stairs, too far away to hear ordinary conversation but looking in their direction whenever his route allowed it.

"It's loud."

"We can leave now."

He meant it immediately. No irritation, no reminder about the invitation or the review meeting or how rarely they did anything together anymore. He reached into his pocket for the coat-check ticket.

Taylor nearly said yes.

McClane did not stop her. That mattered. He laid out the facts instead.

Main door is still opening. Your dad gives them the ticket, you both put on your coats, you walk out. Might work. Might be the cleanest chance you get.

But?

But you asked to go three minutes after Anders put the bug line in his toast. If the coat-check guy's reporting sudden departures, you and your dad go on the list. If they decide to delay you, your dad asks why, and then this becomes a confrontation with him standing in the middle of it.

Danny had the ticket between two fingers. Waiting.

Taylor hated that staying required less visible courage than leaving. She hated more that visible courage was exactly what Anders's people were positioned to notice.

"Eighteen minutes is fine," she said. "I just wanted a number."

Danny did not put the ticket away.

"You sure?"

"Yeah. We leave when the auction closes."

He searched her face, then tucked the ticket back into his pocket. "Eighteen minutes. Even if Anders announces a second chocolate fountain."

"Especially then."

He smiled. Taylor did too. Over his shoulder, the man completing the slow circuit moved past them without altering his pace.

The decision created a deadline. They only needed the room to remain normal for eighteen more minutes.

How many's that now, she asked, once the fourth one had drifted past for the second time.

Counting the two by the east doors? Six that I've clocked. Probably more I haven't. No alarm in the delivery, just an accounting, careful and precise. Six is a lot of bodies to burn on watching a party. Whatever he's protecting, it's worth more to him than looking normal tonight.

That's supposed to be a comfort?

It's supposed to be information. Comfort's not really on the menu right now, kid. Wish it was.

"I don't like this chapter," Gladly said, and for once there was no lightness at all underneath it. "I liked the toast chapter better. The toast chapter had jokes."

"This one's got jokes too. They're just worse jokes."

Is he doing this because of us? Because of what happened with the bugs?

Maybe. Maybe not. McClane's voice had gone careful again, weighing a read he clearly didn't love the shape of. Could be standard practice for a guy who runs whatever he actually runs, tightens the room a little every night just on principle. Could be him reacting to a hunch he can't name yet. I can't tell you which. What I can tell you is it doesn't matter which, not for what we do next. Either way, this room's getting harder to move through unseen by the minute, and we don't have forever to figure out why.

Taylor watched the fourth man complete another slow loop, and felt something settle in her chest that wasn't quite fear, not exactly, something colder and more useful than fear, the same feeling she got right before a fight she hadn't chosen but couldn't avoid either. Above them, the chandelier's light caught the crystal in long, slow ripples every time the air handling kicked on, and for one strange, suspended second the whole grand room felt less like a party and more like a stage set built at genuine expense to be torn down later, all of it beautiful, all of it temporary, none of it built to survive whatever the night still had left to throw at it.

He still doesn't know it's me.

No. Careful again, provisional, a certainty McClane clearly knew had an expiration date on it. Not from anything I've seen. But knowing something's watching him and not knowing who is a problem he's actively trying to solve right now, in real time, with real bodies on real doors. That's not a man settling back down after a false alarm. That's a man narrowing his search.

And when he finishes narrowing it?

McClane didn't answer right away, and the silence told her more than any answer would have.

Then we're not playing defense anymore, he said finally. Then it's a different kind of problem, and I'd rather we weren't standing here waiting to find out what kind when it happens.

Across the room, Danny laughed at something the councilwoman said, easy, unaware, a man having a perfectly ordinary evening at a party his daughter had stopped being able to enjoy the moment a stranger's voice had arrived in the middle of it. He'd loosened his tie an inch sometime in the last hour, the closest thing to letting his guard down he ever managed in a room like this, and the sight of it, small and domestic and utterly unaware, made something in Taylor's chest hurt in a way none of the men by the doors had quite managed yet. Taylor watched him for a long moment, and felt the shape of the night rearrange itself around one simple, unbearable fact: whatever came next, he was still standing three feet from the center of it, with no idea at all that there was a center, or a moment, or anything at all worth being afraid of.

"Anybody want to bet whether she gets those eighteen minutes?" the newer voice asked.

"With what money?" Fury said. "None of you have wallets."

"I contain multitudes and gambling debts."

Taylor didn't smile this time. She was watching the fourth man complete his loop again, watching him pause, just slightly, just for a second, near the spot where she'd stood twenty minutes ago sending three cold bugs under a service door, and felt, for the first time all night, total certainty that whatever window they'd had to walk away from this quietly had already, somewhere in the last hour, closed behind them without either of them noticing it happen.

McClane.

Yeah, kid.

I think we're out of time.

Yeah, he said, and for once there was no dry humor in it at all, just a flat, professional readiness that felt, underneath the fear, almost like being handed something solid to hold onto. Yeah, I think you might be right.
