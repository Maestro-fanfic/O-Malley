Chapter 17 of 29

Near Miss

Cricket was faster than the service-level crowd could account for.

Taylor felt it before she saw it, the wrongness of a straight line closing where a curved one should have been. She looked up from the stream of cooks, servers, and displaced guests just in time to watch the woman cut through a service alcove that should have taken her four times as long to reach, moving like the panic in the corridor was a current she'd already learned to read and use.

Cricket had not followed Taylor's false swarm routes. She had followed the human bottleneck. Every usable stair from the basement fed into one of three corridors, and Cricket had chosen the one with bodies pouring through it.

She didn't need the bugs, Taylor thought.

No. She needed the building. Same thing we've been using all night, except she knows how to hunt in it.

She's cutting you off. McClane's voice had gone flat, immediate, no room in it for anything but the next three seconds. She's not chasing the crowd. She's chasing the shape of where the crowd's thinnest, which means she's chasing you specifically now, whether she's got a face to put on it or not.

I can't get to the chute. Not this way.

Then we don't go this way.

Taylor cut left instead, hard, into the narrow gap between a collapsed dessert table and the wall, and felt Cricket adjust a half second later, already anticipating the correction before Taylor had fully committed to it, and that, more than the blade at her hip or the coiled economy of her movement, was the thing that finally made the fear in Taylor's chest go sharp and clear instead of formless.

Taylor vaulted a fallen chair. Cricket stepped around the place she would land before Taylor's feet reached the floor.

She ducked behind three fleeing guests. Cricket did not push through them. She moved parallel on the other side, watching shoulders and hips, and arrived at the end of the human screen at the same moment Taylor did.

Taylor threw a handful of silverware from an abandoned table. Not to hit her. To create six moving objects with six paths.

Cricket's blades flickered. A spoon split against the wall. Two forks rang off steel. She let the rest pass without looking and continued forward.

She's sorting threats, McClane said. Anything aimed at her goes in one box. Anything that isn't gets ignored.

Then I can't distract her.

Not with anything she can classify.

Taylor sent a wasp at the back of Cricket's neck.

Cricket moved before it came within a foot. One blade cut the insect from the air. Her eyes never left Taylor.

The route to the chute vanished behind her.

Taylor backed into the service alcove. A tray rack blocked one side. A decorative wall closed the other. Cricket slowed because speed no longer mattered.

"You made him nervous," she said.

Her speaking voice was quieter than Taylor expected.

"Good."

The answer came out before Taylor decided to give it. McClane's satisfaction flashed through her fear.

Cricket smiled without warmth. "He'll want to know how."

She shifted her weight.

Taylor felt the attack begin before the blade moved, not through precognition, only because McClane had taught her where to watch.

She's not just fast. She's already three moves ahead. That's not just training.

No, McClane agreed, grim. That's a power built for exactly this. Reading a fight before it happens. Which means straight lines are a loser's game against her. We need something that doesn't have a straight line to read.

Like what.

Like everything you've got.

Taylor didn't have time to be careful. She reached, hard, for every straggler still drifting loose across the ground floor, dozens of them now, warmed and awake and desperate for direction, and threw the whole ragged mass of them not at Cricket's eyes or her hands but at the air around her, a churning, formless cloud of motion and sound with no single point in it worth tracking, no blow to anticipate, no attack to read, just noise, close and total and impossible to parse into anything a combat instinct could sort cleanly.

Cricket's head snapped sideways, hands coming up on pure reflex, blades catching the light in two bright arcs that cut nothing but empty air, and for the first time all night Taylor watched a parahuman flinch.

"You cannot out-fight a combat reader," the gallery said. "You can out-weird one."

Go. Now. While she's still recalibrating.

Taylor went, cutting past the alcove and into the service corridor beyond it, lungs burning, the cloud of bugs already scattering behind her, their job done, their small, brief usefulness spent. She didn't look back to see whether Cricket recovered fast enough to follow. She didn't have the seconds to spare for finding out.

"That deserved slow motion," Gladly said.

That, McClane said, somehow breathless despite having no lungs of his own, was the single best piece of improvisation I've seen out of a rookie in thirty years on this job, and I include myself in the pool of rookies I'm comparing you to.

I got lucky.

You got smart. Different thing. Luck doesn't know to throw noise at a power built to read clean signals. Something almost like pride crept into the flatness. You didn't fight her. You broke the rules of the fight before she could start one. That's not luck, kid. That's the whole trick. That's the trick I spent thirty years learning and you just found in about four seconds flat.

The corridor spat her out near the kitchen, steam and shouting and a line cook already halfway out a service door with a fire extinguisher clutched to his chest like a life preserver, and Taylor let herself be carried along in his wake for a dozen steps, one more panicked body in a building full of them, before she peeled off toward the linen closet and the chute beyond it.

She didn't make it three steps before she heard the sound.

Small, sharp, entirely human, coming from a supply alcove tucked behind the kitchen's walk-in freezer: a woman's voice, thin with panic, and underneath it, the rattle of a door handle that wouldn't turn.

Keep moving, McClane said, and then, a half beat later, quieter, No. Wait. What's that.

Somebody's stuck.

Taylor was already changing course before she'd finished the thought, some old, stubborn part of her refusing to let the sentence finish any other way. She found the alcove door exactly where the sound had placed it, a service closet with an old push-button lock that had, sometime in the chaos, jammed itself solid, and behind it, muffled and increasingly frantic, a woman who sounded younger than Danny, older than Taylor, and entirely alone.

"Hello? Is someone out there? The door's stuck, I can't..." A sharp, indrawn breath. "Please, I heard people running, is there a fire, what's happening..."

You don't have time for this, McClane said, and there was real conflict in it, not coldness, a man weighing a cost he clearly hated weighing. Cricket's still out there. Every second you spend on this door is a second she gets to close the gap.

I know.

I'm not telling you not to. I'm telling you to know what it costs before you pay it.

Taylor already knew. The old privacy latch had an emergency pinhole beneath the handle. She pulled a bobby pin from her hair, straightened it against the metal strike plate, and sent two tiny insects through the gap around the latch. They could not move the mechanism for her. They could tell her where the inner cam sat and whether the wire passed above or below it.

"I'm here," she called through the door, keeping her own voice as steady as she could make it. "The lock's jammed. Give me a second. Push the door toward me when I say."

"Oh, thank god, thank god, please hurry..."

It took eleven seconds, the longest eleven seconds of the whole night. Taylor missed the cam once, bent the pin, turned it, and felt through the insects when its tip finally settled into the release notch.

"Now."

The woman pushed. Taylor pressed the pin. The latch gave with a soft click, and the door swung open on a woman in a catering uniform, mascara streaked, hands shaking. She stumbled into Taylor, caught herself against the wall, and stared toward the shouting in the kitchen.

"Service stairs," Taylor said, pointing back the way she'd come. "Down one level, then follow the mechanical corridor away from the laundry room. Don't use the loading dock."

The woman seized Taylor's wrist. "Come with me."

For one second the offer was almost enough.

Then Taylor felt Cricket enter the far end of the corridor, slower now, testing every branch.

"I can't. Go."

The woman ran. She looked back once before the stair door closed, long enough to see Taylor turn toward the threat instead of following her away from it.

"She's not just surviving anymore," Gladly said.

"No," Fury agreed. "She's not."

Taylor didn't have time to feel what she was feeling about that. She was already moving toward the chute, not to hide in the burned laundry room again but because the shaft offered the fastest route past the search teams closing on both service stairs. She carried the eleven seconds with her like a small, warm weight she hadn't earned the right to put down yet.
